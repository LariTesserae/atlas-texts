Someone sleeps in the farther shelter. You can tell by the bench. The stone at the raised end is dull where a head has lain, and below it there is a little heap of leaf ribs sorted by length. The shortest is no longer than a finger. None would be much use in mending the roof.

The sleeper comes out on both hands. A foot follows, laid sideways across the threshold, and then the rest of the body. It takes a while. There is nothing wrong with the foot. She is listening.

At the first white stone she stands up. Her clothes are made of the broad leaves, split and worked until you can fold them without breaking a rib. The hems are white. She has a strip of the same stuff wound round each wrist, where a sharp edge would otherwise catch her as she puts her hands down. She could pass for an old woman on the path. Off it, when she puts her cheek against a slope and waits with her mouth a little open, it is harder to say what she could pass for.

She eats the pale folds from low down in the beds. She takes them from the middle, where there is no need to pull a whole fan aside and let the light in. Where she has eaten, the remaining leaves close over the gap. By the next visit she has to feel for the cut with a thumbnail to know she has been there. Once, finding a fold she had already cut and set aside, she chewed it for most of the way home before she noticed it had gone on growing. There was a thin green blade between her lips.

The basket in the shelter is hers. It will still carry anything wider than a fist. What she generally carries in it is somebody's supper.

There is another sleeper, under the path between the shelters. In one place the stone is sufficiently thin that, with your palms flat, you can hear this one turn over. The noise is slight, a drag and then a knock, such as a heel makes when a blanket has got tight across it. If you shift either hand to get a better purchase you lose the sound. The woman takes the basket a little farther on, to a fissure that opens sideways into a bed of leaves. She sets it down with the hole toward the fissure. In the morning she picks it up. Now and then a leaf has been pushed back through the hole, all its soft parts eaten, the ribs still joined at the stem.

They did not always live on different sides of the stone. There was a winter when the bench was just wide enough for two. If one wanted to get up, the other had to get up as well. The woman still rolls toward the wall before she rises. It is a small movement and she makes it even when she is awake enough to know better.

She did not cut the scratches. She remembers lying on the bench while they were cut, each stroke quite distinct although the hammerstone was on the other side of the wall. When the work was done she crawled out to see it. From where she knelt there was nothing on the wall but the ordinary tooling of its stones. Someone took her by the elbows and moved her a little to the left.

There are things in the valley whose lives she knows less well. At the western rim she once found a root stretched taut across a gap. Far below it, where a dropped stone would have been lost, there was another length of the same flat ribbon. For three visits she thought she was looking at two roots. On the fourth, the upper one drew through the rock by about the breadth of her hand, and the lower one slackened. She set the basket down and watched until the light went. In all that time nothing else moved. When she came back the gap was gone, though the root was still there, lying loose on a new shelf with a little twist in it.

She does not mark every piece of ground she can stand on. Some is too young to keep a cup. You can peck one in the morning and find in the evening that there is scarcely enough of it left to catch a nail. Nor does she put a marker where she has seen the roots pull tight. The white stones beyond the lip are older than any she has laid. She knows the second by a chip on its near edge. She used to put her bare toe into that chip on the way past it, even though it was much easier to tread in the middle of the stone.

Lately she has been going to the lip without the basket. She lies on her belly beside the new leaf, with both hands on the ground, and listens. There is a sound under the leaf now. Not the tearing, and not the sleeper turning over. Something brushes once against the underside of the new stone and is still. After a long while it brushes again.

She brings one of the short ribs from the shelter and slips it beneath the tip of the leaf to keep it from chafing on the edge. By the next visit the leaf has lifted clear of the rib. The rib has not fallen. One end lies on the ledge beside her thumb; the other rests on the pale knuckles of a hand. It is a small hand, or very far away. She can see that the nails have been bitten. She remains where she is for a while, then moves her thumb so that there is room at her end of the rib.
