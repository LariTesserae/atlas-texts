The chair is occupied for part of every day. Seen from the square, it appears to be empty even then. The woman who sits in it is the color of the wall behind her, and she rests her chin on the sill so that only her eyes come above the lower sash. An old woman can sit this way for hours. A young one keeps having to get down and go out to see whether something she saw was really there.

This one is old. The skin at her temples has gone thin enough to show the little fans of bone that open when she swallows. She keeps a dish of water on the sill and wets them with two fingers. In the dry rooms of the city, this is something everyone does without thinking, the way they take hold of a railing on a steep stair. The dish is a saucer that came with the building. Her fingers have grown knotted in the years she has been dipping them into it. The saucer still has the little point on its underside where it was lifted from the mold.

She watches the avenue because there are children on it.

They have been sent to fetch a man from the building with the bright roof. There is a telephone in her room, and another one in his, but what he is wanted for is to hold a baby while its mother sleeps. The children were told this plainly. They took a blanket in case, on hearing why he was wanted, he decided to bring his own baby too. A child will often provide for a difficulty nobody else has foreseen and then forget to take anything to eat.

They walk in the gutter, which is just deep enough to keep the wind off their ankles. Their feet are broad and gray, with a seam up the back that closes when they are on dry ground. At the first patch of turf they lie down side by side and open the seams. Water moves up their legs. It takes less time for the smaller child, who gets up first and walks around lifting leaves. She does not expect to find anything under them. She likes to put her palm where a leaf has been and feel the small difference in temperature before it goes.

There are animals here, though the children have never caught one in the turf. Along the cliff, where the seep comes out, little translucent bodies lie crosswise to the flow. They catch what the water carries: shed skin, a thread of wool, the soft part of a leaf that has died under its elders. When they have eaten enough, they let go. Most are taken by the wind and set down elsewhere along the seam. Sometimes one comes up over the curb into a street, where it appears to be a drop of water making its way uphill. People put a hand down in front of it. It gathers itself into the crease of the palm, and can be carried back to the edge.

The people themselves eat the old leaves. You have to get an arm under the green ones, almost to the shoulder, and feel for a blade that comes away without pulling. It is a disagreeable food until it has hung for a while above a motor. Each public building has a place for this. In the pump house there are racks just inside the door, where the leaves curl in the warm draft and give the whole room a smell like a coat drying. Nobody knows quite how much is on the racks at any time. If the building is far from home you eat what is ready and put up what you brought. A person who has had a baby may go a year without gathering a leaf.

The babies are born in water, but must be brought out of it to sleep. For the first few weeks they make a brief swallowing noise at the bottom of each breath. A woman hearing it from across a room cannot always say why the last one sounded different from the one before, only that someone ought to go and pick the baby up. After a while she begins to hear it when there is no baby in the room. That is when they send for somebody who has slept.

By noon the children are on the other side of the cleft. The old woman loses them there, though she knows where to look. The motors go on conveying their separate burdens through the rock. Under the nearer one she can sometimes feel the steps of a person coming up the stairs, but it is too early for that.

Late in the day she sees the blanket. The man has it over his head, with the two corners hanging down beside his face. He has not brought a baby. He has brought a pot with a lid, and is carrying it in both hands, level, while the children walk on either side and tell him how much farther there is to go. He has lived on this street longer than either of them. At each of the ramps he lets them take his elbows. The last ramp is in full view of the window. He takes it a little more slowly than he needs to, so the smaller child can do her share.
