The keeper has a name for each of the three pans, though on the plan in the pump house they are numbered. One is the Good Pan. It is the worst of the three. It was the first to fill the year the keeper came, and for a week there was no reason to call it anything else.

The keeper sleeps behind the pump on a bed just long enough to lie flat in. In winter, ice grows on the heads of the bolts above the pillow. In summer, a dun bird comes in under the sheet-metal shade and takes the dead flies off the windowsill. It will do this while the keeper is eating, but not while the keeper is looking at it. The keeper has got into the habit of turning the page of a book without raising their head.

It is a bird of that year, with a little yellow still showing at the corners of its mouth. When it first learned to knock a beetle against a stone, it knocked the beetle out of its own beak and stood peering into the seam where it had fallen. Now it carries the larger ones to the roofless room. There is a stone in the wall with a shallow notch that takes the beetle’s body and leaves the head sticking out. The bird uses this stone even when there are other birds on the wall. If one comes too close, it turns sideways and opens a wing over the notch. There are six or seven little black heads in the dust below.

At night something comes for what is left of them. It is one of the pale animals from under the stones, hardly longer than a grain of rice, with feelers it can lay right along its back. On bare rock it moves in short runs, stopping with its front legs raised. In the wall it goes without stopping. The stones of the room were laid with their broad faces out, and behind those faces are passages a finger cannot enter. The animal can go from the foot of one wall to the top of the next without coming into the open. Where a stone has fallen out, it takes the long way round.

There used to be a door. The keeper knows this not from the plan, which stops at the last pan, but from a hole in the sill polished on one side. In the lee of the sill there are a few grains of barley. More come to light when the wind takes the dust down far enough. The first time the keeper found them, they brought three back to the pump house and set them in a saucer of fresh water. Nothing grew. One grain swelled and split, and by the third day the room smelled faintly of bread.

By then the rain pools were going dry. In the deepest one, the little swimmers had spent the first morning grazing the rock and the second out in the open, where their shadows moved on the bottom ahead of them. On the third day they crowded into the last water under the lip. A bird could take several at a dip. Those left when the pool dried went white so quickly it was hard to distinguish them from the salt. But some had already left eggs in a rough patch on the underside of the lip. When the keeper passes that pool in dry weather, there is nothing in it that moves.

The black lizards go down into the pavement at dusk. They choose slabs with room beneath for the whole body, though sometimes a tail remains out on the warm surface long after the lizard has disappeared. The swifts come up from the valley late. Seen from the pump house, they seem to arrive singly, then all at once there are dozens of them, every bird taking a different crack. For a little while there is a sound from inside the cliff like someone shaking out a heavy cloth. After that the keeper can hear the grille rattle in the wind, even with the pump off.

The cup on the wall has a chipped rim. The keeper sets the chip toward the room, where the wall is easier to reach, and in the morning turns it away before pouring. Once, finding a pale animal in the cup, they tipped it out onto the coping. It lay still on its back. The keeper started to reach for it, then drew back a finger that was white with salt from the sluice. By the time they had rinsed their hand, the animal was under a stone.

On mornings after a storm, the keeper leaves the cup where it is and washes their spectacles at a pool. They have to get down on both knees to do it without stirring up the red dust. With the spectacles off, the far shore disappears. Close to their face the water is full of small, clear bodies, working their legs. The keeper waits for them to move away from the edge, then dips one lens and then the other. When they put the spectacles back on, the little bird is usually somewhere on the pavement behind them. It has learned that when the keeper kneels like that, they often turn over a stone.
