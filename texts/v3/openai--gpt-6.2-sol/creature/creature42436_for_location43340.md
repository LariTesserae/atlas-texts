A woman named Sela lives on the western side of the river with six cups and no table. She keeps one cup on each windowsill. If she wants to drink in the bedroom she has to wait until late afternoon, when the cup there is cool enough to pick up.

She is the city’s enumerator. On the first day she was given a list of addresses. On the third day she began another list, of places where someone would mind if you didn’t come back. The lists are about the same length, but they have very little in common.

The night baker, for instance, sleeps in a room behind the ovens and has an address of his own all the way out by the southern ridge. A boy goes there every morning to open the shutters. He was asked to do it once, while the baker fetched his shoes, and no one has thought to tell him to stop. He has begun to bring things to the room: a piece of honeycomb with nothing in it, an umbrella that opens on one side. He has put the shoes together under the bed. Sela has asked him if he wants to live there. He doesn’t. He likes the walk home.

Sela’s daughter, who is eleven, has taken charge of the things found on the tram. She keeps them on a towel in the station nearest their building. So far there are two hairpins, a jar of salt, a wet glove, and a photograph of a man standing in water up to the tops of his boots. When someone comes to claim a thing, she asks them to describe it first. This worked for the glove. It didn’t work for the salt, which three people described perfectly.

There are small brown frogs in the south, under the beans. They spend the day pressed into the damp sides of the planting holes, and at dusk some of them climb the stems. A woman who came out with a colander to pick beans found one sitting in the curled end of a leaf. She set the colander down and went along the row lifting leaves with a finger. By the time she’d counted thirty she’d gone farther from the avenue than she meant to. She had to call to her husband to find the colander. Now he comes with her in the evenings, though he has yet to see a frog. He looks wherever she points, a little too late, and holds the colander.

The oldest person Sela has met is a man who comes out of the eastern trees to buy bread. He pays in coins he washes before he leaves home. He told her he lived past the cistern, so the next day she went past the cistern, and when she couldn’t find him there she went on to the avenue. He was waiting for her when she came back. He had brought out a chair. It stood on the bricks inside the foundations, and behind it, at the height of a seated person’s head, a shirt hung from a branch to dry.

There were two people in the household, he said. His sister was out. Sela wrote down his name and asked when his sister might be back. He said she usually came in when the bricks got cold. They sat for a while without speaking. From the chair Sela could see why it had been put just there: the low wall took the weight of both her feet.

She went back the following morning. The chair was gone, but the sister was there, sweeping the bricks with a short broom. Rain had washed grit into the hollows and she was working it loose with the corner of the broom, one brick at a time. She knew about the list. She wanted to know whether, once their names were on it, people would start coming through.

“People come through now,” Sela said.

“Yes,” the woman said. She turned the broom and picked a twig out of it. “But they don’t knock.”

On the ninth day Sela’s daughter left the towel at the station and came home with the photograph. A man had come for it and described, without being asked, the bridge out of shot, the friend who’d taken it, and the hole in the left boot. He’d carried it as far as the door. Then he came back and laid it on the towel again, face down this time.

On the back he’d written his address. He had the upper room in a building so new that Sela hadn’t been able to get to the door. That evening, going past, she saw a light in it and a second light lower down. The man was sitting on the top stair with a lamp beside him. He had put another lamp at the bottom, where the stair began, for anyone coming up after dark.
