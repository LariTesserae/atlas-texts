The six-footed animal is called a listener, though it has no ears on the part it lifts. Its ears are in its feet. When it stands still with its head up, it is taking the weight off its mouth, which otherwise scrapes the ground and makes a noise that travels all the way through it.

A listener has to learn where to put each foot. A young one crossing its first joint will get five feet over and stand indefinitely with the sixth in the air. The joint is full of sounds. Some mean the sides are about to draw together; others, almost exactly like them, mean this happened a long time ago. An adult can tell the difference with one foot while using the other five to dig. What it digs for is not the fringe itself but the soft, colorless creatures that lie end to end inside it. They make the fringe brittle by eating the part that bends.

Under a curb where listeners sleep, there are usually a few leaves of foil-leaf laid dark side up. The animals bring them in without detaching them, backing into the shelter with the tips in their mouths until the stalks bow. A leaf gives a little click when it turns. A listener asleep on its belly can feel that click through the floor. It is possible to watch one sleep through the turning of eleven leaves and get up at the twelfth.

Not all the combs in the joints are alive. The live ones are easiest to distinguish when something goes wrong. An old comb with a bead stuck in it will wait as readily as a living one, and for much longer. But underneath a living comb, out of the light, a second row of teeth will be growing to fit around the obstruction. Sometimes a bead that has not moved for years comes through with a little clear collar on it. Farther down the seam the collar catches. The bead passes on, and the collar stays and begins to grow teeth of its own.

There are also inhabitants of the standing shadows. You can find them by laying a strip of white material across an empty posthole at noon. From above, the strip darkens where you would expect. Turn it over and there may be a little dark fork on the underside, facing the other way. That is an animal. It is about the weight of a foil-leaf root and leaves a faint greasy mark if you touch it. It spends the bright hours feeding along the foot of the shadow and moves up into the wider part as the light fails. Nothing much eats it. Listeners sometimes pick one up, work it around in their mouths, and put it down intact.

The broken circles belong to something larger. It is tempting to take the whole basin for one creature, but two basins a long way apart may be the same individual, and two halves of one rim may belong to different ones. A useful place to look is where an old paving layer has peeled. There, between the cramped squares, you can see a flat brown strand with a twist in it. The strand is softer on the inside of the twist. On a cold evening the soft part travels, a little at a time, toward the surface. By morning a shutter that has been stuck all season may turn over, and a shutter that has never stuck may stay open.

The young begin in the smallest squares. They are scarcely wider than a hair, with no twist in them yet. Most come up under unbroken pavement and die there. A few reach a seam and grow along it until they find a vacant shutter. For several seasons a young one may have just that shutter, and all it can do with the evening is make one bright mark in a place where there was none. A listener will visit the mark when the gas drains. It leaves behind crumbs of fringe, and in the crumbs there are almost always some of the colorless creatures still alive.

In the hollow with the loosened curb, the animal is a nursing female. There are two young beneath her, tucked so far up between her legs that in daylight she appears to have only an unusually deep chest. The curb has been settling all season. Each night she scrapes a little dust out from beneath it and pushes the dust ahead of her when she comes out to feed. Tonight, while she sleeps, the last shutter turns. The youngest lifts one foot and sets it against the underside of the curb. It leaves it there even after the small vibration has passed.
