The biggest animal on the reef is usually mistaken for a number of small ones. Its mouths sit in separate cracks, little puckers the color of the white stone. There is one under the chipped leaf and another almost at the rim. A shrimp can put its forelegs into one and go on picking at the stone with the others. The mouths are joined by a ribbon of flesh so thin that where it crosses a patch of black glass you can see the glass shine through it.

You can tell which mouths belong together by what happens when one gets something too large to swallow. The others close in order, starting with the nearest. A while later they open in the same order. It once took most of an afternoon for the mouth at the rim to learn that the one under the leaf had caught a crab.

The crabs are long-legged only in the cracks. Out on a slab they draw the joints up under their bodies and walk on the last pale hook of each foot. A female with eggs walks higher than the others. She has to leave room for the eggs to swing clear of the glass, and when she meets another crab in a narrow place it is the other crab that backs up. In the milky cleft there are females so thick on the walls that when the water clears you can see which way it went by the slant of the eggs.

The white fish eat the young crabs. A fish will hold its place through a dozen good chances, then turn on its side and take one off the rock with a small sideways bite. It always returns to the same slot. Even two fish of the same size do not seem to fit the same slot equally well. Where one hangs with its fins almost shut, another has to work its whole body. There is a fish with a notch in its tail that spends the hot part of the day in a gap behind the chipped leaf. At each knock the tip of its nose touches the glass. It has been doing this long enough to wear a pale spot on its nose.

In the inner hollow the transparent animals grow from one another. A new one starts as a clear bud on the shoulder of an old one, and for a time the two share a gut. Later the bud opens at both ends and begins to strain its own water. You can pick out the youngest by the first dark speck lodged in them. The oldest are not clear at all. They have turned the dull brown of what they eat, and the little ones are all that hold them up in the current. When an old one tears loose, the fish let it pass.

The long animal takes some of these castoffs. It has no apparent head until there is food near it. Then a short stretch of the forward end thickens, and a seam opens along the underside. The seam can open farther back if the food has already gone by. Once, on a ledge scarcely wider than the animal itself, two such openings worked on the same scrap from opposite sides. Neither got it. The scrap went over the edge, and both mouths slowly shut.

There are animals below the lip, too, though not many. At the lower limit of the light, white stalks stand out from the mountain and bend under the weight of sticky cups. Most of what falls from the reef goes straight past them. Now and then a crab catches a stalk. It rests there awhile with its legs around the stem, and then begins the climb, taking hold of the glass one hook at a time. Long before it reaches the rim it comes to a place where the chipped leaf can be felt through its feet. There is no scrape down there. Just the two knocks, and a little farther up, the two knocks again.
