The woman in the market has nine pear trees. They grow in tubs on the old cab rank, where the roots can be lifted once a year and cut away from the pavement. If she leaves them longer, the trees begin to put out leaves at the same time. She likes to have a little interval between one pear and the next. The six on the felt are what she has ripe today. Before she goes home, she’ll take whatever is left to the tram shed, where there’s a cupboard for things that won’t keep until morning. The drivers leave her the empty plates. None of them can remember who supplied the plates in the first place.

Most people live within a few doors of somebody they know, even on streets where there’s no one for the next three blocks. They could spread out. There are houses with sound roofs and enough glazed frontage to grow beans in winter. But you want somebody near when a door sticks, not because a stuck door is much trouble, but because of the noise you make getting it open. From the next house it sounds exactly like somebody knocking. In the evening, on certain streets, you can hear two neighbors going about their separate business and calling out, “Only me.”

In the houses with children, there is generally a saucer upside down on the outside sill. This is for the combs. A comb is an animal about as long as a little finger, with a row of clear legs down one side and a gray sleeve it can draw over its head. It walks hanging down. Come morning, the legs have collected enough water that a comb crossing a window leaves a series of fine wet commas overhead. Children like to put a finger in its path and let it walk out along the finger. It weighs less than the water it brings with it. An occupied saucer can be carried from house to house, but the child has to go slowly and keep it upside down, which is difficult when there is something to show at the other end.

There are bigger things in the wood. The fitters know them by the clean places they leave. You take out a bearing that has run hot all week, expecting a paste of oil and fibers, and find the whole lower half of the seat licked clean, sometimes with a narrow, wet chin laid in it. The animal withdraws without turning around. The fitters call them uncles. They have never found the far end of one. In a shop that’s been shut overnight an uncle will occasionally draw out a strip of oily waste, work it over, and put it back, neatly pleated. This is unpopular with new apprentices, who have to be told that nobody is teasing them.

In the open country the combs pass winter in the mouths of the clipped posts. The uncles go on doing whatever they do. There is also a white animal that comes out when it rains hard enough to put water in the wheel ruts. It has broad feet, a drooping nose, and hair that parts all the way down its back when it gets wet. In town it’s known as a calf, though the ones that come out smallest are often followed by even smaller ones and it’s hard to say which are young. They don’t graze. They go from rut to rut putting their feet in, standing awhile, and taking them out. The pads come up bluish, with the fine grain of the road impressed on them. In the morning you find those prints on the undersides of planks and in the high corners of the freight sheds, where there ought not to have been room to turn an animal of that size.

An old woman who lives at the farther of the two tram stops used to keep a calf. She says it wasn’t tame. It simply came back to her kitchen each time it rained, and by the fourth or fifth visit it had learned to knock a pan off the draining board if she wasn’t there. She would put water in the pan and it would stand in it with its front feet while she ate her supper. She says its hind feet never got warm. She found this out in the years when she was still in the habit of putting a towel down for it.

The pan is under the draining board now. The woman has moved to a smaller house at the other stop, where there are people she can play cards with after supper. On wet evenings she sometimes takes the tram home the long way. She has a key to the old kitchen and uses it, though all she does there is put the pan out and wait for the next car. From the uphill end of the platform you can see the kitchen light. From the downhill end you can just make out the window of the room where they’re dealing her in.
