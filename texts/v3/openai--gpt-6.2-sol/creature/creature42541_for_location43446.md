Follow the wet line from the cup and it goes over the lip of the ledge. On the underside it runs uphill for the width of a finger. Put a fingernail to it there and the whole uphill part draws back.

The animal is about as long as the handle of the cup. Most of it lies folded in a seam under the ledge, where there is room for it to turn over but not to turn around. Its skin is clear enough that you can see a meal going through it: a little succession of green knots, with a black one now and then. In a good seam there will be two animals, one facing each way. This one has had a good seam to itself for years. At its far end there is a white nub where a second mouth is growing.

It feeds on what it can draw through the crack. There is very little to eat in the rain itself, but a cup that is never quite rinsed grows a fine green fur. The animal lays a lip along the wet line and works it slowly toward the foot. You could watch for a long time and see no movement, then find that the last inch of the line had gone dry. The inside of the cup is green except for a clean stripe up to the crack. It looks as though someone has been getting at it with the corner of a cloth.

There are young under the stair. The first thing they grow is that little shell, no bigger than the clipping from a child’s nail. It covers them entirely so long as they tuck the mouth in. They can walk on wet rock perfectly well, but they choose the dry patches because there they can feel what is coming through the stone. Watch the one under the tread when a surge goes round the blind bend. It lifts the front of its shell a fraction before you hear the blow. That is how the young cross the basin: not in the gaps between showers, which are too short, but in the gaps between one piece of rock moving and the next.

Most never cross. They go down the wall channels and make a living in the bends, where they grow flat and broad and lose their shells a grain at a time. The one beneath the cup still has its shell. It is buried in the lime ring, with the cup resting on one edge of it. When a drop strikes inside the cup, the animal unfolds a little. It does this even when it has just eaten, even when the drop is too small to reach the crack. There is a corresponding movement under the stair: sometimes one shell lifts, sometimes three.

You can pick up the cup. There is no grip on it other than the lime, and that lets go with a little grit. Underneath, among the old shells, is a green shoot with two leaves the size of pinheads. The animal has been washing silt into the ring, and the shoot has been living on it. Its stem lies flat in the shape of the cup’s foot. While you hold the cup, the leaves begin, very slowly, to come apart.

The mouth reaches the place where the crack was. It goes on ahead of the water for a little way, feeling across the empty ring. If you put the cup back with the handle turned the wrong way, it will find the crack before morning. The shoot is the thing to mind. There is a low spot in the foot where the two leaves fit. Whoever set the cup down last took the trouble to find it.
