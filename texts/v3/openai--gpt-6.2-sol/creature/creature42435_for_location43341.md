The woman who clears the north ditch is called Iven. She lives over the tram stop with her son and her father, who used to clear the ditch when it was on the other side of the tracks. He still wakes when she goes out. In the kitchen he puts her cup upside down on the hot pipe so that it won't take the heat out of her tea when she comes back.

The pale animals in the ditch know Iven by the way she lifts a grate. A tram sets them all combing faster, but at the first scrape of her hook they fold their legs and let the current take them. She keeps a bucket downstream to catch them. They look badly made in the bucket, with long legs coming out at angles that would be of no use on land. In the ditch a single animal can strain nearly the whole width of a narrow run. What it catches it rolls into a pellet and passes back between its feet. There is usually a smaller animal waiting there. It is hard to tell whether the little one is being fed or robbing the large one very gradually.

Iven's father says there were none of these when he began on the drains. He remembers a red animal with a blunt head that kept the runnels clear by eating the scale off them. There is one in a jar at the works depot, but he says the jar has taken all the red out of it. He has been saying this for years. Last winter Iven found one alive in the bend of a disused pipe and brought him down to see. He looked at it for a long time and said he'd remembered the head being bigger.

On the roofs the young are born with their feet shut. An adult can spread each foot to the size of a saucer and get a grip on stone a person would swear was smooth. The young have to be held until their feet open. On a crowded roof there may be forty adults lying head to tail, each with a young one on its back, and when one turns over to warm its other side it gives the young to its neighbor. The colonies are quietest toward the end of the cold season, when the last of the young can hold on independently. People in the rooms below wake at the stillness. For a few nights they miss the sound and then they forget that they missed it.

There are two rooms to let directly beneath the oldest colony. The landlord will show them only when the animals are moving. He says a person ought to hear the ceiling before agreeing to live under it. He raised three children in the smaller room himself and can still point to where each bed stood by the pale squares on the floor. The animals on the roof have outlasted his children’s interest in coming home, though one daughter sends him a parcel of dry socks every year.

Beyond the tram stop, something lives in the pools before the ice closes. It is dark and flat and has a white edge that shows when it turns. Iven's son found one by lying on his stomach and breathing on the ice until there was a clear spot. The animal rose under his face and waited there. He breathed on a second spot, farther from the edge, and it came to that one too. He thought it was following the warmth. Then his grandfather put a cold saucer on the ice and the animal came up beneath the saucer. It wanted the shadow, or whatever cast the shadow. They left the saucer there, and in the morning it had gone down through the ice and the animal was sitting on it.

Her son asked if they could take it home. Iven said he could if he could bring the pool. He spent some time looking for a seam around it. The next time she went out with him he had found one, but by then it ran through three more pools and went on under the ditch.

When the large breath comes, Iven's father takes his cup off the pipe. It rattles otherwise. At the depot there is a man whose job is to take down the time of the breath, and he has asked people at the north end to note whether it comes before or after the water backs up in their sinks. Iven sent him six times, very neatly written. Her father made her take the seventh back. What she had heard that evening, he said, was the upstairs tenant dragging his bath away from the wall. The tenant had not had a bath in twelve years. They went up together to ask him, and afterwards her father stayed for supper.

The new ditch has grown almost to the first of the pools. Iven goes out before the first tram now, not because there is more work but because her son likes to come with her and look through the ice. She lets him carry the hook. Where the last grate used to rock under her boot, there is enough stone to bed it flat. This morning she beds it and finds, on the other side, two pale animals standing crosswise in a run of water no wider than her thumb. She lowers the hook without touching them. There will have to be another grate. On the way home she tells her son to remind her to put the order in, and he does, three times before they reach the stairs.
