The surveyor at the end of the line is named Iven. She lives three stops back with her father, who used to fit doors and now keeps finches. When she comes home she takes her boots off on the landing. The dust from the cuts gets into the birds’ feet, and then they spend all evening picking at their toes instead of eating.

Her father has lived in the same rooms since before there was a station. In those days they were on the ground floor. Now there are two floors beneath them, one occupied by a dentist and the other too shallow for anyone but the finches, which go down through a grille to look for grit. He still calls the front room the shop. Some of the old door samples are there, including a fine tall one that no longer fits through the entrance. He turns it a little every few months so that it keeps the ceiling from growing down around its top edge.

The finches have never been outside. They came in an egg case on the freight, with air holes and a picture of a branch. For their first winter they slept on a curtain rod. Then the wall behind the stove put out a fine root, and they moved to that. Iven’s father used to trim it back to keep it from reaching the stove. Now he moves the stove.

Most households in the district have some such arrangement. You can tell which neighbors are on good terms by the plugs in their walls. If a root comes through from the next flat, you don’t pull it out without asking. It may be holding up a shelf. There was a bad year when the new tenants upstairs kept paring off a ridge beside their bath; Iven’s father had to go up and show them the long pale hinge of the cupboard below.

On workdays he meets Iven at the station, though she has asked him not to come when the stairs are bad. He likes to walk home with somebody who knows what the district will look like in the morning. She generally tells him she can’t say. She can tell him how far the two sides of the new street moved between noon and four, or how much weight the thin floor of a platform took before it began to bend. In their own street there is a tree with a narrow crown, and for six years it has done nothing anyone has thought worth measuring. Last month a branch went through the bakers’ sign. Now you can see it moving from the station, carrying the tin letter B out over the tracks.

In the unoccupied floors there are other tenants. The commonest are little jointed animals that look like pieces of the stiff white seam on a cut leaf. They feed where the oil is fresh, standing quite still with their mouths to it. When they have had enough they back away and leave a row of empty places in the wax, each about the size of a grain of rice. Finches will eat them, but only if somebody turns them over first. From above, even a finch takes them for part of the floor.

Iven sometimes brings one home in the fold of a survey strip. Her father puts it on the table and waits for it to show itself. It can take a long time. They eat their supper in the meantime, and the finches come down to pick at the crumbs. Usually one of the birds steps right across it.

There’s a girl in the next flat who has never known a building to come to an end. Last week she asked Iven when they could move into the new street. She didn’t mean when would it be finished. She wanted to know the first day there would be room for a person. Iven said she would take her out on the tram and show her the survey papers. The girl has been leaving her coat on the landing so they can go as soon as Iven gets back.

Yesterday Iven found a strip she’d set in the morning drawn so far into the seam that she had to lie flat to reach it. A small hand would have done better. She made a note to bring longer tongs, then got up and brushed the dust from her coat. Down in the opening a gray hunter was walking away from her, setting each foot with care on a floor still soft enough to keep the prints. She watched until it went around a corner. That was the first she knew there was a corner.
