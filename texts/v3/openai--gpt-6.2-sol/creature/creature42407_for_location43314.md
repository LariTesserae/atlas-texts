The blind animal lives behind the cupboard. The board that shows in the shaft is the back of the flour shelf, and when somebody sets the flour jar down hard, a little flour shakes through a nail hole onto its bed. It does not eat the flour. It pushes it out, along with loose mortar and the skins it sheds from its feet. This accounts for the pale streak below the cupboard, which the woman who keeps the flour jar has twice reached out with a broom to remove.

The animal is about as long as the thumb hollow. On the way up to feed it looks white. Coming home, with a meal in it, it looks yellow from the jaw to halfway down the belly. It takes one bite from a leaf, however large the leaf may be. A second bite too near the first would let the oil run out over its face. On the wall beneath a particularly good fan there are little heaps of white dust where it has stopped to clean its mouth with the backs of its wrists.

For much of the year there is just the one. Its young are born in the old nail holes, sometimes in the very hole from which the flour falls. There are two or three of them, and before their feet have hardened they can be told from loose pieces of plaster by the way they cling to one another. Their mother goes on feeding in the garden. Coming back she lies flat across the holes, and they lick the oil from the grooves along her belly. The youngest generally gets very little. It feeds longer than the others, after they have gone to sleep, going over the same groove until it is clean.

When they are old enough to go down the wall, they follow her in a row. At the leaves she stops, and they pile up behind her. It takes several nights for them to learn to go around. The first leaf a youngster bites will often have a whole fringe of nicks in it, and the youngster will go home with its chin furred white and nothing in its stomach.

They leave at different times. One might go up past the oven and take a week to get back down, finding nothing to eat in the whole hot height of the shaft. Another goes straight to the inside edge of a leaf and puts its forefeet over. There it waits. You could watch it through most of an afternoon and think it had fallen asleep. Then a root breaks. For a moment, in the quiet, its feet move separately, testing the air in two directions. If there is an answer from below, it lets go.

The mother has a toe missing on her left hind foot. There is a mason who knows this from the prints, although he has never seen her. Years ago he found the same print in a garden two streets over, when he was small enough to be sent headfirst under a landing to retrieve a chisel. He cannot be sure it was hers. He has never found out how long these animals live. But when he lifts the step he puts his finger down carefully, clear of the old trail, and when he is done he brushes the grit from his new mark out onto the passage, not into the middle.
