The mouth beneath the drip is at the narrow end of an animal about as long as your arm. It has no lip to close. When a drop lands, six little pads rise out of the mouth and meet around it, and the water disappears between them. Between drops the pads lie flat again. You can tell how long an animal has been under a good drip by the black grit on its back. On a thirsty one the grit lies loose. On a full one it has sunk into the skin, and you could pass a hand over the back without feeling a grain.

These are the animals that look like ledges. They move on a broad sole that they put down a little at a time, beginning at the mouth. The bellow comes from the other end. Every so often you can see a slit open there and a small puff of grit go out at ground level. Put two of them end to end in a runnel and they will call for hours, the one ahead raising its hind edge a little at each bellow from the one behind. Sometimes a third has wedged itself crosswise between them. It takes the whole night for any of them to get past.

The white-legged animals can run in the space beneath a sole because their knees fold sideways. On open stone they ride high and look almost round; under a ledge they go flat enough to pass one another. Their rattle comes from two ridges on each knee. A single animal walking makes only a few ticks, and stops if you put your hand near it. In a crowd one will brace itself on four legs and work the other four without going anywhere. Its neighbors take this up, sometimes while standing on its back. When the rain comes, the ones underneath keep rattling until water reaches them down the legs of the ones above.

They eat the ledge animal where its skin is thin, along the seam where the sole turns under. A crowded seam has the scalloped look of something cut with blunt scissors. Farther up the flank, beyond reach of that seam, there are little blunt animals with no legs at all. They pass their lives in the pleats. Their young have legs and can be seen after rain hurrying over the stone, trying the folds of every animal they meet. Most folds are already occupied. A young one will push its head in beside a resident and wait there with its legs sticking out. By morning it has either been pushed out or has gone far enough in that you cannot tell which body the legs belong to.

The swimmers are easiest to see when a pool is about to go. With a finger’s depth of water they stay low and show their white sides only when they turn. In the last of the water they keep breaking the surface. They are scarcely wider than the grit, but each has a dark hinge across the middle. As the pool shrinks they fold at that hinge. You can watch one make half a turn and fail to finish it, then settle with both halves shut. The white-legged animals come down to the wet line and pick them up. A swimmer that has closed all the way is generally put down again. Often it is put down a little higher on the stone than the water ever reached.

There are nights when a ledge animal comes upon one of those old pool rims as it feeds. The mouth opens over grit and empty shells and whatever has shut itself in time. Some of the swimmers lodge between the drinking pads and go on with it. At the next drip a few work free. There may be no pool there yet, only the stone darkening in a circle beneath the mouth. They lie on their sides until enough water gathers to lift them. The first one up turns by itself. The next turns with it.
