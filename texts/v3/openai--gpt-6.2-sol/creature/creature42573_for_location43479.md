The woman at the zinc counter is writing to her son, who lives eleven streets away. She could tell him what she has to say on Sunday, when he comes to sharpen her scissors, but there is a photograph to send. It shows him at six, holding a perch over a white basin. He remembers the fish as something he caught. She remembers buying it alive from a man with a bucket and letting the boy lower a bent pin into the basin. She has decided to tell him. The difficulty is that the longer she looks at the photograph, the less certain she is that the basin was white.

Her name is Ada Pell. Until this morning she sold buttons and kept a kettle on for the men who maintained the belts. The shop will open again on Thursday with the same stock and counter, two doors down and one floor up. The kettle has already gone ahead. Someone working on the stairs has plugged it in.

People who have lived here a while give directions by things that are difficult to move: an old pear root, a jack sunk crookedly into the ground, the warm patch where a steam line runs too near the surface. Ada's son says this makes it hard to get anyone to come to dinner who hasn't been before. He is trying to train his daughter to say the street and number first. She can do it if prompted, but left to herself she says they live where the pigeons walk uphill.

They do. On cold days the birds come out of a broken ventilator at the bottom of a sloping yard and walk all the way to the top, picking oat husks out of the joints. At the top they fly down and begin again. The girl spent the better part of a Saturday trying to teach them to start at the top. She laid a trail of oats from one end of the yard to the other. By evening the oats were gone and the birds were still walking uphill. She respects them a little less for this, though she continues to feed them.

There are other animals in the ventilator. A cat goes in as far as the first bend, where there is a dry flange big enough to sleep on. Beyond the bend there are white slugs. The fitter who found them thought at first that they had blanched from living in the dark, but in a jar on his windowsill they stayed white and ate the printing off the paper he'd put under them. His husband made him take them back. They had eaten Tuesday's football results, which he hadn't read.

In the raw lots live small brown voles with exceptionally clean feet. Their runs are not in the soft ground but along the undersides of things set down on it: a slab, a sleeper, a bath with both taps still in. Stand the bath upright and there will be a shallow trail of clipped grass, with a little heap at either end. If the bath is put back, the voles will return to it even if the taps leak and there is a better bath twenty feet away. One of the yardmen has taken to moving it with a vole still beneath it. He lifts one end, waits until he sees the grass twitch at the other, and calls to his mate to lift. The vole generally runs in the direction they're going. The men have grown fond of this arrangement, which saves no time at all.

By the time Ada finishes her letter, the counter is out on the pavement and the man at the rear of the trolley is holding it level with his knee. She apologizes. He says he's all right, though when he sets it down he has to stamp his foot several times.

She has written that the fish came from a bucket. She has also written, on the back of the photograph, that her son let it go. This much she remembers quite well. They took it down to the loading yard, where water had collected in the bed of a truck that hadn't moved for months. The boy climbed onto the running board and tipped the basin. Then he went back every day with crumbs until the truck was taken away.

At the new shop there is no letter slot yet. Ada's granddaughter comes by on her way home, and Ada gives her the envelope to carry. The girl puts it inside her coat and takes the long way across the pigeon yard. There are no pigeons now. At the foot of the slope she stops to move a snail out of the track of a closing gate. There is a place for it on the wall, among others she has put there, though of course they don't stay.
