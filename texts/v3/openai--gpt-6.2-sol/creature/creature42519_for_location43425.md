There are people in the town. They come down out of the houses after dark, when the curbs settle and leave enough room to put a foot between the pavement and the wall. In daylight they sleep in the walls themselves, in long pockets of dry bark. A person getting out of bed leaves a dark shape there, like the shape a body leaves in grass. By morning the bark has risen again.

They don't use the stairs. Across from the house with the landing, a woman sets a dish of mashed fruit on a high sill. By evening the dish is clean. She puts it out even in the season when there is almost no fruit, scraping what she needs from the inside of the husks. Nobody has seen what comes for it. Once, on a dry day, there were prints on the landing: a heel and four little toes, with a fifth set so far to one side that it might have belonged to something walking alongside.

The children catch lizards. They lie flat with their chins on the curb and slip a hand out at the first knock, not in the lull. By the lull the hand is already waiting. They let the lizards go in the houses, where the little feet make a dry noise on the floors that can be felt through a cheek laid against the wood. Almost everything the children know about one another they learn at that distance. A girl with a loose tooth clicks it against the floor until somebody clicks back. The reply sometimes comes from a house two streets away.

There is an old man who keeps a garden under the square. He grows white mushrooms in the sediment the roots have picked clean, and brings them up in the few seconds the drain is open. He has to work around the bird. It will let him put a hand into the water, even rest a wrist against its standing leg, but if he takes hold of the leg it bears down so hard that his knuckles go into the mud. In a good rain he can bring up enough mushrooms for everyone. In a poor rain he eats them down there.

The bird has a nest. It is outside the far gate, in a patch of sedge so close that you can see the eggs from the square when the light is right. Sometimes there are two eggs, sometimes a pair of young with black skin showing through their down. Once there was only a shallow dish of flattened grass. The bird went on fishing. A child carried a fish out to the nest and laid it in the grass, and the next day ants had taken everything but the eyes.

People do leave. They go through the near gate, usually before sunrise, and come back with salt or a new knife wrapped in cloth. It is the far gate they have trouble with. There is a woman who has been coming home through it for years. On clear days you can see her put her basket down to change hands. She does it once on the road outside and once in the short street, though the second time the basket is on her other side. Her sister waits for her at the sill where the two grooves disappear. She doesn't call. At noon she puts her palm flat on the wet sill, and from underneath, quite distinctly, someone takes hold of one of her fingers.

There are days when she is busy elsewhere and sends a child to do it. The children don't mind whose finger it is. They have learned to bring a stool, because the hand under the sill is often a long time letting go.
