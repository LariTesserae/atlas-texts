There is a skin under the rowan. If you put your hand in the water and follow the largest root, you can get two fingers beneath it. It feels much like the underside of a mushroom. There is a little give, and then something hard. Most people take the hard thing for the root and let go.

The hard thing is a tooth. There are others along the wall, though not all at the same height. One has grown around a grain of quartz. The animal was small enough once to lie in the pool at the foot of the tree, with its whole back out of the water. Now the greater part of it is pressed up against the slate. It has worn the stone smooth by breathing. You can stand in the hollow a long while without seeing a breath. A spider has fixed one end of a thread to the stone and the other to the animal. By the end of winter the thread will be taut.

It eats what comes down the mountain in the water: the white ends of grass roots, threads of sheep dung, the almost weightless bodies of insects once the air has gone out of them. On the side nearest the entrance there is a fold in its skin where grit collects. A crayfish keeps this fold clean. When you see the crayfish with its claws out, it is waiting for the fold to open. It has waited there before. On the far side of the root is a heap of grit it has carried out, with its own little slope and a path up it.

The moths came later. A few folded themselves against the rowan during a week of rain, and others came to the places where the first ones had been. They can put a foot down on the back of another moth without waking it. The frogs are less skilful. Every so often one takes a place too far out on a frond and, over the course of several days, tips it into the water. There is a frog down there now. Its throat is bright where the frond has turned it over. It will come up when it needs to.

A shrew should be dead in the time it takes this animal to close its mouth. The one on the ledge has been there since the berries were green. There are two neat punctures in the slug, on the side you cannot see. The shrew has already bitten it. It is drawing back for another bite, its forefeet close together, its hind feet braced against a flake of slate. By the time it has finished eating there will be young slugs under that flake. It may eat those too. Nothing has promised them anything.

The animal is not asleep. Just inside the entrance, beneath the good flat scraper, is a soft part as wide as the heel of your hand. This is where it feels the weight come off the mountain when a stone falls. It feels your weight go, too. The walls ease apart a little when you step out from between them, and a fold you could not have got a fingernail into opens near the tree. Water enters it. The crayfish lifts one claw. There is room now to turn it over.
