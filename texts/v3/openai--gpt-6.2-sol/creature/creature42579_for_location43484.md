There are mouths under the lips of the far wall. At low water they look like flaws in the mortar, short seams with a little clay caught in them. If you put a finger against one, the clay draws in. It is a surprisingly deep movement. You can feel it through the stone with the heel of your hand.

For most of the year that is as much of the animal as comes out. In warm weather a mouth will put down a clear thread, which the current takes sideways along the wall. Grit passes through it. The soft parts of things stick: the meat of a drowned fly, the white pulp from the middle of a seed. The thread shortens a little at a time as it feeds. The wagtail takes the threads when the pump leaves them hanging in the air. It has learned to pull sharply and step back. Pulling slowly gets it a beakful of clay.

There must once have been separate animals in the wall. There are still separate ones in the ironwork: pale, blunt little things, each no longer than the hole in a bolt head. Lift a loose bolt and the one inside will fold itself around the wet end. It holds that shape for the whole time you have it out of the water. The ones in the wall have grown into one another behind the mortar. A mouth may close at the ladder and a mouth halfway down the bank open to let the water out.

When there was a mill, the drop in the pond drew water through the wall twice a day. The animal fed on what came through. Now there are long spells when the pump gives it scarcely anything, and it draws for itself. The movement begins well back from the pond, under the road, where there is still a bed of loose stone laid for the old millrace. After a rain there is more water in that bed than the drains can carry. The animal takes it in along its buried length. The mouths on the pond side stay shut until it has taken all it can hold.

That is what it was doing in the bright spell. When the last bowl filled, a little wedge of clay came free beneath the ladder. It appeared to go down, caught the light once, and went sideways into the white cup. The silt went with it. For the rest of the afternoon the cup was clean inside.

By the next morning there will be three or four small mouths along the rim. Most will dry when the water falls. One may reach the handle, where there is a patch of glaze missing and the clay stays damp. The cup has been there long enough for this to have happened before. If you turned it over, you would find the handle joined to the submerged beam by a seam that looks like mortar until you touch it.
