Something is taking the mortar off the fallen bricks. It works from the edges, where the mortar is thinnest, and leaves the pieces in little fans on the gravel. You can find a brick it has finished with by putting your palm on it. Even on a cool morning the cleaned face is warm.

It lives behind the pale slip of clay. There is room back there for a hand, if you put it in flat. The animal goes in on its side. It has a long mouth and two short forefeet, with nails worn down on the outside toes. When it comes out, it draws its feet up against its chest and waits for the clay to stop moving. A starling can stand on the root just above its head and not notice it.

Most days it comes down while the laundries are running. It does not seem to mind the noise, but it has trouble walking when the extractors stop. Its feet go on feeling for the tremor after it is gone. You can see it then on the open gravel, lifting one foot and setting it down in the same place several times. If a tram comes round the bend it gets going again, sometimes so abruptly that it runs into a brick.

It eats the pale things it finds under the mortar. Some have legs. Some are no more than a wet line that disappears when its mouth passes over them. It drinks from the pump house pipe with its chin laid along the metal. The nettles part low down as it goes through them; their tops hardly move. In June the rabbit began using the same passage. The two have met in it, though neither seems to have found a satisfactory way of getting past the other.

The first tap from the bank is an echo. The second is made with a nail on the underside of the alder root. Not every sound gets an answer. A wheel squealing does not, nor does a stone kicked along the platform. The noises it answers are the ones that end cleanly: a latch, a dropped spanner, the blackbird breaking a snail. Once, when the laundries were shut for a holiday, a depot worker stood in the square snapping his fingers. For the rest of the afternoon the animal came to the mouth of its hole and looked down the bank. Ordinarily there is something to eat where a sound like that has been.

By late summer there are two smaller ones behind the slip. They come out dusty along the back and dark underneath. They cannot strip a brick yet. They turn over the pieces their mother has loosened and lick whichever side she has left uppermost. At the pipe one climbs onto the other to reach the drop, although there is another drop farther down.

When the slip falls, the hole behind it will be in daylight for the first time. The clay will be smooth where their bodies have been against it, with a shallow step at the entrance worn by their feet. By then they will have begun to sleep under the cobbles. The smallest has already found a way in. It goes down headfirst between two alder roots and for a moment its hind feet stick straight up, working at the air. Then it catches on something damp and pulls itself out of sight.
