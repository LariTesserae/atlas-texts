The woman who sleeps in the garden has a room two floors down. There is a narrow bed in it, and a row of hooks at a height that was convenient to the person who had the room before her. She goes down to change her socks. On the hottest nights she goes down to sleep, too: after midnight the condensate comes through so warm that the corner by the grille is like the inside of a glove.

Her name is Iona. She was sent up to attend to the floats. For the first week she carried a folding stool from one end of the bay to the other, setting it down wherever there was something to watch. Now that the ferns have covered most of the places where it will stand level, she leaves it by the door. She sleeps there on a slatted panel laid across two empty pumice crates. At night she can tell by the small changes in the sound under her which beds have filled. It took her longer to learn the sound of an empty bed. There was nothing to listen for except the water going on somewhere else.

She has a pale pink line across the knees of her work trousers. It comes from kneeling at the bottom basin to clean the float guide. There is always a little grit in the guide, though less than there ought to be, considering how much of the block comes away on her rag. She used to wipe out the whole basin while it was low. Then one afternoon she watched the strider come down off the dry block and try to stand on the film of water left by the rag. Its feet kept touching bottom. Since then she has cleaned only the guide.

When she eats an orange, she does it out on the bare step. She has learned that if she puts a piece of peel down among the leaves she may not find it again, even when she can see the exact place where she put it. The bare blocks are good for eating on. They warm the backs of her thighs through her trousers. She sets the peel in a single curled piece beside the capped pipe and takes it away when she goes. She does not water the two ferns. Once, when the dark line in the mortar shortened, she brought a cup out and sat with it for a while. By the time she finished the orange, the line had reached the ferns again.

Below the slats, the roots of the reeds have begun to grow through the holes in the lifting tray. When the tray tilts, some come with it and some stay where they are, threaded through holes on the other side. Iona discovered this by putting her hand under a bed that wasn't coming all the way up. She had expected a stone or a bent pin. Instead something fine and surprisingly strong drew tight across her knuckle. Now, when she makes her rounds, she takes the little inspection mirror from her pocket and looks at the roots from below. She is trying to see which ones the bed can spare. The first time she cut one, a length of it a foot long slid out of a hole and lay in the gutter, white except for a red stain where it had rested against the block.

On the inside of the door there is a card for recording faults. In the first few weeks she wrote down everything she found: the drip at the bolt, the uneven lift, the wet joint under the capped pipe. A man came up from the floor below and asked her to show him the drip. She showed him the vine instead. At that point it only had one leaf past the bolt. He held the leaf out of the way with the back of a finger, looked at the hole, and said he could clear it while he was there. She said she had been mistaken about which hole it was. He waited for her to find the right one, and after a minute she pointed to one that was dripping exactly as it should. He put his hand under it. Neither of them could think of anything more to say.

The card is still there. On the back, where it lies against the door, she has started keeping the dates on which the pepper opens its flowers. They are small enough that she missed the first batch and found them only when they fell, several to a leaf, looking like something shaken out of a pocket. She has never seen what comes to them. In the morning some have a little dust on the petals and some are clean. She turns them with a wet fingertip to see.

A swift has begun coming past twice in the evening, once in each direction. The first pass is usually while she is still at work. By the second she has brought her blanket out and spread it over the slats to air. Sometimes she hears the wings with her head down and looks up too late. She can see the blanket lift a little at a corner, but it does that all evening, bird or no bird. What she waits for now is the interval after the first pass, when there is still a chance of seeing it come back. She sits on the edge of the panel with her feet on the wet floor. Under her heels the lowest bed begins to fill. By the time the counterweight clicks, the red block at the edge of the basin is bare.
