The thing that lives there can make a body out of very little water. A drop is enough for a mouth. In the red light under a fern you can see the two clear lips work around the rim of a bead that has not yet fallen. They do not drink it. They draw it out until it touches the next bead, and for a moment there is a straight wet line between two leaves that do not touch.

Most of the time this is as much of the animal as the room will support. A lip under one frond, the joint of a leg in the hollow of another. The pieces move in concert. Put a finger in the damp soil and, a yard away, a little transparent heel will draw up sharply. Where there is dust between them they have to wait for a root to cross it. A fine root will do, provided it is alive all the way through.

When the pipe cools, the parts come down. They are hard to distinguish from the water running off the ferns until they reach the basin and begin to take weight. First there is a long back, too narrow for the ribs that rise out of it. Then the ribs fold down and the legs find their places under them. The head is the last part to fill. It has small eyes set far forward and a soft, broad muzzle. Standing in the basin it comes a little higher than the workbench. It has never had room to straighten its neck.

It does not step out of the pool. It lowers its mouth to the patch of light and feeds. The grass bends toward it in handfuls, and there is a small tearing sound quite different from anything the pipe makes. It is particular about what it takes. It will push a broad blade aside with its upper lip to get at the pale new growth behind it. Once it drew up a whole plant by mistake and stood for some time with the roots hanging from its mouth. Soil fell from them into the pool. By the next cooling there was a new fern in that place, though the plant it had pulled was not a fern.

There is another thing it does while it has a head. It puts one ear to the surface. In that position its muzzle points directly at the recess with the shelf. On some days the grass goes on moving and the ear stays down until the water is too shallow to hold it. On others it lifts its head almost at once. You can see water run from the ear, and the skin along its back will jump as though a fly had landed there.

The old cup used to stand open side up. A man who ate his lunch on the worn pier would tip the last of his drinking water into it on his way out. Even when the basin was dry, there would be enough in the cup for an ear. He used to wait on the green step until it appeared, then say good night in his ordinary voice. He couldn’t have heard an answer from there. He waited a little after he said it all the same.

Now, as the pool goes down, the animal takes itself apart in much the order it came. The mouth is gone early. The legs last long enough to lower the back without a splash. When the basin is empty there is still a little water caught between the porcelain handle and the root that holds it. On a quiet day in the recess you can hear the cup shift once against the brick, well after the roar has fallen.
