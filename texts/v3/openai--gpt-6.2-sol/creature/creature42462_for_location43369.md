The warmest place in the basin is a crack no wider than a hand. The bats pack themselves into it belly to back. The ones at the mouth have room to turn their heads; farther in there is only room to take a foot off the stone and put it down again. If one comes back late, it works its way in under the others. The whole band stirs for a little while, as if the rock were ticklish there.

In summer there are young in that crack. A female returning among several thousand others goes on calling after she has landed. From somewhere inside the mass a smaller voice answers. She crawls toward it, over feet and folded wings, and the two go quiet together. For the first weeks the young know the basin by what reaches them in that crevice: the bodies going away at evening, the cold after they have gone, the feet coming back before the rock has had time to get warm.

Underneath, the mounds are being taken apart as fast as the bats can make them. Fresh droppings keep their shape for a day or two. Below those is a loose black meal that shifts when a beetle pushes through it, and at the bottom, where the mound touches the limestone, there is a damp layer full of white specks. Some of the specks are eggs and some are mites. A beetle that comes out of this layer may have half a dozen mites riding in the seams of its body. It can fly to the next mound. The mites cannot.

The clean stone around a mound is not a boundary to everything. At night the crickets cross it on their way up the slope. They do not all leave the cave. Some go only as far as the fern and feed in the clay seam; others get out among the roots on the ridge and come back so late that the returning bats pass them at the entrance. The great fringe under the ledge is a day’s worth of arrivals. A small spider lives just behind it, in a pocket the crickets have to step over. It does not need a web. It waits with its front feet on the lip.

There is less to eat in the cups. A gnat falls in, or a fragment of a bat dropping lands near enough for the next splash to carry it over the rim. Most of what the pale animals eat comes in with the water and is too fine to see. They comb it off the gravel. When a drop hits, they flatten themselves against the stones, and then, one by one, rise on their legs. In a cup a hundred yards away, the animals are smaller and almost clear. The two populations are nearer each other than either is to daylight. There is no way for them to meet across the floor.

Even on the dry shelf there are things that live from one drop to the next. They are inside the stone. On a freshly lifted flake, the underside sometimes has a faint brown stain that does not wipe away with a thumb. The stain follows pores into which a little moisture has withdrawn. Nothing visible moves there. A wet season can pass before the stain advances the width of a grain.

Toward evening one bat wakes out of time with the others. It stretches a wing, folds it badly, and stretches it again. Down in a cup, a fleck that fell with the last drop has lodged between two grains of gravel. Two feelers find it from opposite sides. The bat lets go. On the shelf nothing crosses the dust, though a moment later you can feel the air move against the backs of your hands.
