The grey things are called listeners, though they have nothing anyone has been able to find that could be called an ear. If you put one on your palm it goes on grazing for a moment, working its mouth against the place where the leaf ought to be. Then it sets all six feet down and waits. On a sleeve it will walk about. The trouble with a hand is that it has a pulse.

In their first season the listeners are flat and almost transparent. They live under the old stairs, in the narrow spaces where the sap has hardened across a cut without filling it. Their young can be seen through the white, moving one at a time from step to step. A stairway with young in it is not necessarily a good one. Sometimes the whole flight is standing on edge before they come out. They emerge perfectly well fed, onto a slope nobody will walk again.

There is a woman who goes round with a shallow box looking for them. She doesn't collect the animals, only the skins they leave behind. The skins are grey until she lays them in water, when they open into little nets, each with six closed ends. She sews them inside the toes of children's shoes. A child will wear through the sole in a month walking on new ribs. With a listener skin inside it, the worn shoe will hold together for the walk home.

The woman's name is Eda. She lives with her brother above the bench with the two cups, in a shelter so low that a visitor has to lie down before being introduced. Her brother, who is nearly blind, goes out more than she does. The light hurts him less. He can tell by the backs of his legs when he has come into the air that spills off an edge, and he takes his time about going any nearer. He is the one people ask to find things they have dropped. Seeing a buckle slide away, you remember the direction it took. He wants to know how it sounded, and whether it was still making that sound when you lost sight of it. Often the buckle is only two ribs down, standing in a seam on its narrow end.

Eda keeps her finds on a slat just inside the entrance. Long ago someone left a small shoe there, with the laces knotted together. She has mended it twice. In the full light outside you can see the first owner's toes outlined in the hide, and across them the toes of somebody much smaller who wore the shoe on the other foot.

Most people who stay any length of time near the bench take a turn keeping the wet cup clean. It draws its water through a hairline crack in the rib underneath. In the coldest part of the year you have to lift the disk before the next one forms, or the water creeps out beneath it and freezes down the bench leg, where it is no good to anybody. The dry cup is for setting things in while you do this. There is nearly always a loose button or a bit of good fibre in it. Once Eda found a tooth, still bloody at the root. By evening someone had come back for it.

Far out along the underside of the upper leaf there are colonies of something the people below take for stains. Each colony grows from a single dark point and puts out threads that lie flat against the ceiling. When the ceiling moves, the threads peel away. For days they hang down, so fine that you only see them where a bead of water has caught. The beads travel upward. In a still spell you can sit with your back against a rib and watch a whole scattered shower climb, one drop at a time, into a stain no bigger from where you sit than a mark left by a dirty finger.

Once every few years one of the threads comes within reach of the floor. The children are allowed to put a wet finger to it. It dries the finger so quickly that the skin draws tight, and for the rest of the day the child will keep touching things with that finger, testing the difference. When the thread pulls away again, all the loose skin of the leaf underneath it lifts and follows. Some of that brown dust reaches the ceiling. On mornings when the light is especially hard, even Eda's brother can make out the faint interruption it leaves there.

There are people on the lower leaf. Eda knows because a long slat once drifted up on a current and wedged beneath the bench. It had been pierced for sewing, but the holes were paired in a way nobody near the bench used. Her brother said that didn't prove where it came from. Then he drew his thumb down the slat and felt the worn place at the end where someone had rested a hand to rise. He went out and stood by the opening for most of that afternoon. He didn't expect to see anybody. He wanted to know how far the slat could have come.

The opening has narrowed since then. In another season there may be no view at all. On days when it is wide enough, Eda takes the little shoe out and sets it on the lip, with a pebble inside to keep it there. From below it will be a dark speck, if it can be seen at all. She puts it out chiefly on days when she can see the other speck. At night she brings the shoe in, takes out the pebble, and puts it back beside the door.
