The snail is the largest thing that lives wholly in the tray. Its shell is the brown of a wet apple seed, except at the mouth, where the new growth is nearly clear. In the open it keeps both feelers out. At the edge it draws one in and goes on with the other, feeling its way among the sedge stems. There is a place under a root where the shell will pass if it goes sideways. It usually does.

On the wall just below the waterline are three clear patches that look like places where a drop has failed to run. They are eggs. In the oldest patch each egg has a dark curl in it, and now and then one of the curls turns. The young snails come out small enough to graze the face of a single grain of brick. Their first tracks are too narrow to pick out from above. Some reach the outlet and go over it. Others spend their first weeks on the underside of the same root.

A fallen leaf may look whole from the stair long after it has ceased to be whole underneath. The underside goes soft first. Among the veins are little grazers that feed on what grows there, and worms so fine they can retreat into the thickness of the leaf. If you lift it, they draw up short. If you hold still, they lengthen again. By the time the upper surface splits, most of what was between the veins is gone.

There is a fly that comes out of the root mat with its wings folded. It walks up a stem and stops just above the water, where it stands for a while on legs that seem too long for it. Once its wings have opened it can leave by any of the gaps in the stair. A spider has a few threads across one of those gaps. The threads are hard to see except early in the day, when enough water has collected on them to pull them out of line.

The oldest sedge has a dead center. It looks like several plants now, green at the tips of a ragged ring, but if you put a finger down among the shoots you can feel the hard crown they share. Brick has worked into it. A root enters the joint at the foot of the wall, disappears for the length of a thumb, and comes out white through another joint. There is almost always a little moving water there. The newest root ends are finer than the hairs on the back of your hand.

By afternoon the snail has reached the far side. It climbs the rounded lip until the front of its foot is in the overflow, then turns and follows the edge. For a little way the shell rises clear and loses its shine. It settles again under a leaf. The track across the middle is still there, broad at the beginning where the snail stopped and fed, then narrow and almost straight. Tomorrow it will be harder to find, except at the place where it crosses the black pebble. The film takes a little longer to come back on the stone.
