In the old splits above a grove there are skins so like the stone that you can put your hand on one without knowing it. The stone is cold all the way down. The skin is cold at first, and then, in one place under the heel of your hand, it is warm.

This is where the long-winged animals spend the night. The wings fold twice, first at a joint well out from the body and then again near the tips. Folded, they make a flat packet with a ragged edge that fits the mouth of the split. The body hangs behind it. In the morning the rock takes the sun before the animal does. There may be six of them along a ledge, with their folded wings showing, and by the time the last one is warm enough to move the first is already far out over the valley.

They do not hunt much in the first hours of light. The grazers are still under the fans, eating the thin edges off scales that have fallen in the night. It is only when a grazer has cleared the ground just inside the grove that it ventures out to the older scales beyond. It turns these over with its two front feet. A grazer with its head down and all six feet at work can walk backward as readily as forward. What stops it is having to choose which way to go when the scales run out.

At noon the hunters come in on the cold air. The leaves turn their dark faces up and the white back of a grazer stands out even under the first rank of fans. The hunter folds one wing to get in among the copper stems. This is a narrow passage for an animal that, out in the open, can span three body lengths of its prey. Most of the time it comes out with nothing. You can follow a miss by the fans opening again in the wrong order, the outer ones first and then one deep in the grove where the grazer has stopped.

There is another animal that makes a living out of these passages. It is smaller than the scales and lies under them, flat on the grit with its legs tucked into grooves along its belly. It feeds on the crumbs the grazers leave, but has no means of crossing the open ground while the air is moving. When a hunter strikes the margin of a grove, a little spray of scales goes up behind it. The flat animals open their legs in the air. They cannot fly, but they can keep a scale from turning over by holding on to one edge of it. Sometimes, well downwind of a grove, there is a single scale lying hollow-side up. The animal under it has starved. The ones that come down among the fans may live long enough to be carried out this way twice.

The hunters rear their young on ledges below the oldest groves. They bring shed scales to the ledges, not the hard ones from out in the wind but the ones that still bend. Over these they lay threads pulled from broken stems. The threads stay clear, and from below, if the light is right, you can see the feet of the young through the floor of the nest. For most of a season the feet are the largest part of them. Even after the wings have begun to grow, a young one that is startled grips the floor and puts its head down. The parent has to pull it loose to move it.

When a seam gives out, the grazers go first. They leave at night and mostly go downhill, so that a young grove at the foot of an old one may have grazers before it has shed enough scales to feed them. In the brass-colored grove above, the hunters continue to bring food to the ledges. Their flights grow longer. A bird-sized shadow passes over one bench and the next, and there is no movement underneath it but the slow lifting of loose scales. In the bottom of the old nest, the resin threads are as clear as ever.

The young do not need to be shown how to open their wings. They need to be shown where there is room. In the last light the parent stands on the outside of the nest, facing in, and steps backward off it. The young one comes to the edge and looks down. By then the parent is already below the first bench, and for a while nothing happens. Then the young one lets go with one foot, and hangs there long enough to feel the air coming up under the wing it has not quite opened.
