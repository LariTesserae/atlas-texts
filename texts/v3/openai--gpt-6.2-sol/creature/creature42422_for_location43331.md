The woman who clears the windows lives in the house with the boulder’s shoulder in its back room. She sleeps with her cheek against the curve. The tree is thick there and takes a long time to cool when the snowmelt comes over the summit. In the morning she puts her knife in her mouth and goes out to see which windows have grown shut.

She does not clear them all. The house next to hers has a minnow nursery in the front room, and its window is allowed to knit itself down to a slit. The minnows pass through one at a time. A pollack spent most of last summer outside that window, arriving earlier each morning until it was there before the minnows woke. Her grandson used to go around by the door and put his face to the slit. The pollack would draw back a little and wait for him to go away.

The boy is old enough now to take a turn in the market. He sells clay from a bowl set into the root floor. It is good clay for taking impressions. People bring him a broken pot or a worn latch, and he presses the pieces into a lump and sends the lump home with them, so they can make another without giving up the old one. His grandmother brought him her knife once. The edge had been sharpened so often that there was scarcely room left to lay a thumb against it. He made the impression she asked for, then widened the blade when she wasn't looking. She has been using the new knife for three winters. It takes two hands to get it out of the sheath, but she has not said anything to him about that.

Most of the square is given over to things that need to be set apart from one another: baskets of live crabs, bowls of clay, a pair of young trees being taught to meet across the top of a doorway. The people who mind these things sit at the edge, where there is something to put a foot under. They come and go by the walls, pulling themselves along by little knobs left for that purpose. Their toes are broad and pale on the underside. Children who have not learned to hook them properly go sailing out into the square whenever they laugh hard enough, and have to make their way back in full view of everybody.

There are fewer people than doors. Two households have joined by letting their eaves grow together and putting a room in the join. Several of the doors open on places where nobody has slept for years, though someone still goes in to pick the buds out of the cupboards. In one of these houses an old man spends the afternoon listening to the roots. He used to judge which stems could be bent and which would split. Now he leans against the wall with his mouth slightly open. When the trees knock on the exposed slope, he can tell which roof will answer first. Occasionally he gets up and goes across the street to move a cup.

A few of the inhabitants came from the lower street. The window woman was small when they moved up. She remembers a shelf where her mother kept salt stones, and the way she could reach it from the stair without going indoors. On the upper street her mother put the stones at the same height above the stair, but the current came the other way and ate them down to nothing before winter. There were a number of mistakes like that. It was a long time before anyone planted a door that opened easily in the north current.

When the water clears, the boy takes his grandmother to the south stair. He goes down ahead of her and braces himself on the last tread. She holds his shoulder while she looks. From there the new handrail seems close, and each year he asks whether he ought to go down and see if it will bear him. Each year she says not yet.

Last time, there was a crab on it. The crab went up the rail as far as the growing tip, which was nearly white and too soft to hold on to. It felt around for a while, then backed down out of sight. The woman took the knife from her mouth. She had brought it to cut away a shoot that was crowding the top step, but she put it back in its sheath and asked the boy to help her up. At the market she borrowed a thumbful of his clay. She went back alone and pressed it over the shoot, leaving just enough of the tip showing that she could come down in the morning and see which way it meant to grow.
