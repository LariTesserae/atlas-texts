On the lee side of one ridge, below the last of the sponges, there is a row of small holes. The holes are spaced about a finger’s breadth apart, though the row bends where a stone lies under the mud. When the current runs along the ridge, nothing comes out of them. When it turns and runs across, a gray lip rises from each hole and folds open on the downstream side.

The lips are almost the color of the bed. A pale scrap going by makes them easier to see. It catches on one, doubles over, and is drawn down. A flake of mica catches in the same way, but after a moment the lip puckers and lets it go. In clear water the lips open wider. In a heavy run of silt they close, though the first one to close is seldom the first to have opened.

The holes belong to one animal. Under the loose mud it has a flat, branching body, no thicker along most of its length than the shell of an egg. Its underside lies against the hard bed. At intervals a muscle runs crosswise through it, and a contraction there can draw several branches a little closer together. It cannot pull itself loose all at once. To move the width of a hole, it must let go at one edge, take hold again, and wait for the rest of its body to follow. All summer it may be working its way around a stone that a fish could pass without turning a fin.

Where two branches meet there is a soft knot in which the scraps collect. Some of the older knots lie under holes that have been shut for years. If a fold of mud comes down the ridge and stops over the row, these knots keep the outer branches alive while they work upward. The shortest way to the surface is not always the one they take. A branch that meets a buried shell follows the inside of its curve, sometimes coming out through the hinge. Down on the plain there are shells with three or four gray lips in them. A brittle star can walk over one and close every lip in turn without breaking its stride.

Once in a while the current strips a branch bare. It lifts and lies flat and lifts again until the narrow place where it joins the body comes apart. Most of the loose branches fetch up in deep mud and are buried there. One may lodge under the edge of a stone with a single lip still in moving water. That lip feeds the whole length of it for a time. Then a swelling forms on the underside, and a little way off, where there was no hole before, another lip comes through.

The largest animal in the valley feeds on both sides of a ridge. Nobody looking at the two rows would put them together: one is low among the sponges, the other well out on the plain, and there is bare rock between. The connection goes through a crack near the crest. At the mouth of the crack the rock has worn smooth, but farther in it is still sharp. The animal is pinched there to a gray thread. When mud covers the lower row, food passes through that thread from the higher one, a scrap at a time. Some years the flow is the other way.
