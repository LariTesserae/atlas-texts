The last animal to reach the pool is a broad-footed doe with a young one riding under her chest. The young one is too old to fit there properly. Its hind feet drag when she lowers her head, and twice on the way to the pool it has had to let go and catch up. Finding no water, the doe licks the dark line the pool has left. The young one licks the same place after her. Then she starts into the wind, and this time it goes beside her.

In a herd these animals keep just far enough apart that one can turn without making the next turn. A doe knows her own young chiefly by the sound of it putting a foot down. For the first few weeks the outer toes are folded in, and the two middle pads strike the stone separately. The second sound is very small. It is the one she listens for when she stops. By the time the pads have spread and the young animal walks as softly as the rest, it generally has no need to be found.

A little hunter has been following the pair since before the pool. It is scarcely heavier than one of the doe’s feet. It could take the young one if it could get it onto the loose gravel, where quick feet matter more than broad ones, but the doe keeps to the shelves. What the hunter gets instead are the amber grazers she dislodges when she noses under a leaf. It eats them from the back, leaving the mouth and forelegs still holding on. At a plant with only three leaves, you can sometimes find one of these empty bodies under each leaf.

The wings that come out of the ground belong to an animal that spends nearly all its life without them. In the fissures it is soft and about as long as a grain of grit. It lives in the wet furrows of a nodule, grazing the film that holds the metal together. The furrows run deeper as it feeds. An old nodule, cracked open, is full of passages that stop just short of one another, with a live animal at most of the ends. When the pressure begins to rise, those nearest the mouth of the crack come out first. Their wings have been folded dry under their skins. For the length of a breath after they emerge, they can still go back down.

The doe can feel the bed working a long way off. She turns aside from the rain smell and stands with her forefeet on either side of a seam. The young one stands across a seam of its own, a dry scratch in the rock. When the winged things come up, she catches them with little sideways movements of her head. They stick to the wet hair around her muzzle. The young one comes to lick them off, and she lets it, though she moves a foot when it leans too hard against her.

There is another animal at the seam, so flat against the stone that the doe has been standing on part of it. It has no mouth on its upper side. All through the venting it feeds underneath, where the first moisture spreads along the rock and the soft bodies of the latecomers are still working their way out. In a good place it may stay for years. When the fissure seals, it lifts a little at the edges, draws itself together, and crosses the stone on short wet feet. This is mostly when the hunters find it. They eat the feet and leave the rest, which can live a surprisingly long time where it falls.

By the time the doe and her young reach the country where it rained, there is no standing water there either. The rain has gone into a bed of fine gravel, and the doe scrapes until the stones come up cool. She lies down and draws them toward her with the insides of her forelegs. The young one noses into the pile. There is enough water on the stones to wet its mouth, though it has to keep turning them over. Presently the doe gets up and goes a little way off. The young one lets her go. For the first time in its life it has found something worth staying behind for.
