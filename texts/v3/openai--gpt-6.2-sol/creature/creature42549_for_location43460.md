People sleep in the dry patches over the warm animals. A good patch will take three beds in winter, though by spring the animal may have drawn itself into a different sleeve and the person on the outside will wake with a wet blanket. Nobody owns the warmth. You own your bed boards and the pegs you drive in to keep them from slipping. If you find a dry patch wide enough to lie down on, you can lie down there, unless there are already pegs.

The people who bring the jars from the west seldom stay the night. They find the basin noisy in the wrong places. A woman can call to her son from the next lane and he won’t hear her, but somebody turning over in bed three partitions away sounds as though he’s getting in beside you. The people born here know which noises have come through clay and which have come through air. A child will sit up at a cup falling off its ledge under the floor and sleep right through the jar the cup drains into.

There is a woman in the eastern district who keeps a white thing in a broken jar. She feeds it rims knocked off cups that can no longer be balanced. When she has company she sometimes lifts the cover. Usually there’s nothing to see except a strip of bluish clay with a row of small bites out of it. If she waits, two of the animal’s legs come round the strip and feel along it, one from each side. It has more joints near the feet than you’d expect. She says it knows her. Her daughter says it knows when the cover’s off because that’s when the clay comes. Both of them put the cover back very gently.

The warm animals are harder to keep. An old man who’d had one under his bed for most of a winter tried to follow it when it moved. He took up three tiles before his sister made him stop. Under the second tile he found a smooth place on a rib where the animal had rested its weight, and beside it a little drift of white legs. The legs were dry all the way through. He brought one up for his sister, who turned it over in her hand and asked him where he meant to sleep now he’d taken up the floor.

On the aprons, the children play a game with two hollow tiles and a clay figure. The figure is generally a person sitting down, since those are easiest to make and hardest to knock over by accident. Each child sets one tile, and they leave the little person between the two mouths. You win if your tile carries the person off. Often both tiles get there and the person is squeezed flat. Sometimes in the morning the tiles are still a handspan apart and the person is gone. This is usually because somebody’s little brother has been out before daylight. There is one apron where it happens even when the little brothers are accounted for. On that apron the children make their figures with long legs, so they can get away.

Most of those children will lay clay for a while. They start with the short runs because a short roll can be lifted without letting its middle sag. The heelprints are made by the smallest workers, who can step between the runs without putting a foot through them. As they grow heavier they have to learn to fill a gap with the side of the heel, leaning on someone’s shoulder. From the wall you can see a place where a child got too big for the work over the course of a single strip. The first prints nearly touch; at the far end there are four long smears, and then another child takes over.

The woman turning the wet roll at the edge has a bed in the old quarters, but she hasn’t used it lately. Her mother no longer likes to cross the aprons in the dark, so they’ve put two sets of boards down near the work. The mother can still judge a roll by the sound it makes coming off the board. She’s the one who taught her daughter to turn a wet one instead of leaving it until the top will take a print and the underside won’t. Tonight she’s lying with her good ear against a folded coat. When the roll peels away, she raises her hand a little, palm up: give it longer. Her daughter has already set it back down. She sees the hand and waits for her mother to lower it.
