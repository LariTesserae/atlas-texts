Something in the basin husks the snails. It leaves their shells standing mouth upward in the joints between the paving stones. A current might do this, but a current would not put a chip of brick into each shell to keep it from rolling. The shrimp climb in among the chips and feed. Whatever took the snails does not take the shrimp, though they will feed almost against its mouth.

Most of the year there is very little of it to see. Under the bench, where the lime has made a skirt around one of the iron feet, a strip of skin lifts and settles. The skin is the color of the wet mortar. At its outer edge it is thin enough for the red of the brick to show through. When the water falls, the strip gathers itself into folds. The folds take a long time to go anywhere. A snail put down beside them could cross the width of the bench in the time it takes one fold to pass over another.

It can move quickly if it has to. Once, when the attendant dropped a washer, the washer stopped short of the bottom and went sideways under the bench. She took off her shoes and went after it. Feeling along the paving with her toes, she found a patch that seemed warmer than the rest. It contracted under her foot, not away from the pressure but toward it, with the slow firmness of a hand closing in sleep. She pulled her foot back. Before she had got her sock on, the washer had been set on the submerged curb.

In winter it comes farther out. There is a pale seam down its back and on either side of this several small dark spots. They might be eyes. At any rate, the spots on the side nearest the stairs darken before the inspection lamp comes on. The creature is broader than the bench and surprisingly thin for its breadth. It can drape itself over the curb without lifting any part of its underside off the stone. In this way it noses along the old gutter to the pipe of the street that is gone.

That pipe brings down things the other pipes do not. In April there are scales from opening buds. In October there are little red stalks with the leaves already off them. Once there was a whole plum, so ripe that it burst when the creature drew it through the mouth of the pipe. It went on searching the place where the plum had been long after the flesh had washed away. For several days there was a clean fan of paving there, with the grains of sand pushed out to its rim.

It seldom crosses the reflected window. It goes around the panes by way of the lowest stair, even when this means going partly out of the water. But during the summer when the window stood open, it lay there every afternoon with a narrow part of itself in the opening. The caretaker saw that much. What he did not see, because by then he would have gone upstairs for his tea, was how the creature came away at dusk with small white petals adhering to its back. It carried them to the bench. Some floated free on the next rise of water; others stayed in the folds until they wore through and fell apart.

The lime is thickest where it rests. There are older outlines higher on the wall, one of them no bigger than a palm. The present outline takes in both feet of the bench and part of the submerged curb. On inspection days it draws itself into the darker of those two feet. It can no longer get all of itself in. A little of the pale seam is always left showing, and if the attendant stands quite still after she has wiped the gauge, she can watch the seam slowly lengthen as the animal decides she is done.
