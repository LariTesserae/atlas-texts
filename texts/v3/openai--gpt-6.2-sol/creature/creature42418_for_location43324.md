Under some of the shelves there are marks on the dry stone like the prints of a child’s hand. They are scarcely wider than a raindrop. You find them in pairs, with a smudge between, going from the wet floor of a passage up the wall and out of sight. The prints last longer than you would think. The animal that makes them has mud on its belly even when its feet are clean.

It is a kind of salamander, gray on the back and the color of an apricot stone underneath. A big one could lie along your forefinger without having to curl its tail. By day they fit themselves into the space between a shelf and the first loose flake beneath it. Several may use the same flake, but they don’t bunch together. Each has a place where the stone is just far enough from the wall to take the thickness of its head.

They come out for the overflow. A pool can stand full all morning with nothing at its edge but the small crustaceans working over the grit. Then water crosses the sill, and a salamander comes down headfirst to where the spill thins enough for its feet to hold. It eats what is carried past. You can watch one for a long while without seeing it strike; the giveaway is a little kick of clear water ahead of its nose. When the spill dwindles, it follows it down, sometimes over two or three ledges, until it reaches a pool with no outlet. It walks back up the wall. The young ones try to ascend by the falling water and have to make several attempts before they learn to go round.

The bird that drops into the courts takes them, though not as often as it takes the things they eat. It hunts by walking the margins and turning its head to look under the stone. A salamander caught out on a bare floor goes flat, with its feet spread. On gray stone this works fairly well. On the dark mineral stain it leaves the animal looking very much like a pale hand laid there in the open. There are a few courts where the salamanders are almost black. Their bellies are still the same warm color, which you see only if one has to go up a wall in front of you.

The eggs are laid out of the water. Behind the low willow there is a slanting crack you could put the edge of a coin into. All spring a little water seeps down the back of it. The eggs hang from the dry side, in groups of four or five, each on a clear stalk. If you look into the crack late in the season you can see the young folded inside, already with feet, their tails brought up past their eyes. The outermost eggs sometimes dry without hatching. The innermost are often lost to a flood. Between them there is room, in a good year, for twelve or fifteen young to get out onto the wall.

The willow court seldom holds water long enough to raise them. They leave it through a thumb-wide notch at the foot of the north wall, where the seepage keeps a strip of mud wet even in sunlight. At the end of that strip they have to wait. In a rainy year they go on the same night, scattered down the lower passages by the first flush. In a dry year you can find them a week after they hatch, under the last wet chip, so thin that the slow movement at their throats seems to lift their whole bodies.

Out beyond the marsh the same animals live in cracks too fine for a fingernail. It would take one of them a long time to walk there from the willow court, and for most of the year there is no continuous wet way across. But in a storm the marsh rises over its low ribs of stone. For a few hours the water runs level with ledges that usually stand a foot above it. The little ones can go straight across. They do it in daylight, too, while the water is brown and the bird is elsewhere.

By the time you come back in the afternoon, the marsh has dropped below the ledges. In a passage that was dry that morning there are three small tracks leading away from the old waterline. Two disappear under a shelf. The third goes a hand’s breadth farther, to a drop still hanging from the underside of the stone. The animal is there with its head in the drop. Its hind feet are planted wide on the dry wall.
