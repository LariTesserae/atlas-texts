Something lives in the bare crescent. It is there when you kneel, though there is nothing between your knees and the stone. The first sign of it is that the note in your teeth goes away. You think you have found the one position in which your bones will not carry it. Then you lift a knee, and the note stays gone until you set it down again.

It has no fixed size. In the dark behind the drum it occupies the difference between two leaves whose edges ought to touch. Farther up it is spread through enough rootwork to hold a shelf together. The roots there do not close the crack. From either side they put out short blunt ends, and between the ends there is just room for a fine white powder to lie undisturbed. When the mountain cools, and the shelf draws in by an amount too small to see, those ends take the strain one after another. Each gives up a little of the load before the next takes it. This takes most of the night.

The plants are not its body in any simple way. A black leaf will go on being a leaf if you break it off. It will keep its gloss and its oil; on the plain it may lie for years without curling. What it will not do is make room for another leaf. Up here they do that continually. If one edge takes a little more of the vibration, the edge beside it thickens. A root taking weight from a bolt lays down a band of softer tissue. It would be possible to cut a section through that root and count the occasions when the bolt was tightened. The most recent band is the widest. It grew after the last person to tighten the bolt had gone, when the rock took back his weight.

There was a time when the wheel turned. The leaves nearest it have a smooth side and a side rough enough to catch a thread. The smooth sides all face the direction from which the air used to come. They have not changed sides. But along the underside of the ledge, the silver line is made of edges that grew after the wheel stopped. They answer to the stone, and no longer to the air. With your ear close to them you could hear the coarse sound divide into little strokes, with a rest after every ninth or tenth. It is not always the same number. Something down there is still finding out how long it can wait.

The garden makes a poor record of the person who worked in it. It has kept no shape of his face and nothing of his voice. It has kept the pressure of two knees, unequally borne, and a frequent light knock at the bend of the rail. He used to set something down there before he put his hand on it. Whatever it was, it was small enough to carry out through the seam without turning sideways. The leaves stopped short of that spot for a long time. One has grown over it now. The rail is bare underneath.

Your pack does not weigh what his weighed. While you are looking at the washer, a little oil moves in a stem below the ledge, and a leaf turns its plated face toward the floor. This is enough to lose the upper note. When you take the pack away, the leaf does not turn back at once. There is a brief interval in which the room is fitted to a weight that is no longer there.

Outside, the strap lifts and falls. Inside, at the edge of the crescent, a root finer than a hair begins to carry its own weight again.
