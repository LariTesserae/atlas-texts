The animal that holds up the lip is not the largest in the crack. She is the widest. Her back has spread out over her legs in a low rim, like wax that ran a little before it set. At the lip she puts her front feet on the underside of the glass and her hind feet on the upper side. The good scraps lodge just beyond the reach of her mouth. She draws them in a little at a time with two short hooks. A piece of fly wing can keep her there through several journeys of the cars below. There is nothing she can eat in the clear part of the wing, but the joint at its base is worth the work.

Behind her, traffic does not so much wait as come apart. The small ones go down under the lip and try the hotter wall, where they can take two or three steps before turning back. One kind can get by her altogether. It is long rather than round, with a waist so soft that the front half can be well into a passage before the rear half has chosen to follow. It goes under her while she feeds. Sometimes it comes out carrying a scrap she has dropped; sometimes it comes out carrying one she still has hold of. Then both animals back up. Usually the scrap breaks.

The long ones hatch on the crystal shelf. Their eggs are laid loose in the angles, not fastened down like the broad mites’ eggs. When one hatches, the shell opens at one end and stays whole enough to roll. The young animal comes out with its feet stuck to its belly. It has to rub them free against whatever it happens to be lying on. On a rough crystal this takes very little time. On the broad face of a flake it can work a long while and free only the feet on one side, with which it rows itself slowly around.

Down in the corner there are mites that scarcely ever meet a living animal larger than themselves. They eat the thin parts first: the joints of legs, the soft lining left inside an old skin. A whole back can arrive in the corner and keep its shape long after everything underneath it is gone. Several generations may feed under it. There is one down there with a dent in the rim that matches the rim of the broad animal at the lip. It is hers from an earlier skin. The dent came from a grain she grew against, and she no longer has it.

She has an egg to lay now. When she leaves the lip she goes backward, feeling with her hind feet, until she finds a grain that will give a little without coming loose. She leans against it and lays the egg in the space she has made. When she takes her weight off, the grain settles back. The egg is not quite concealed. A small part of it shows, dull at first, then clear enough that the two dark hooks of the young animal will eventually be visible through it. They are the first parts to darken. Well before it can feed, it has the tools.

When the rock moves at the blind end, none of the animals now at the lip will be alive. The first to enter the new space will likely be one of the long ones, and it will not get far. It will put its front feet down, draw them up, and back out with its rear half still on the old glass. After the stone has cooled a little, a mite will go in after a scrap shaken loose by the break. It may carry the scrap straight out again. For a while the only sign that anything has been there will be a shed skin caught by one foot on the new edge, hanging into the strip of dark where there was no room for it the night before.
