The pump keeper is not the first person up. In the house nearest the yard, a woman named Bess makes bread before the first run. She keeps the flour in a tin hung from the ceiling, not on account of mice, though there are mice, but because anything left on the floor acquires a damp underside. Her grandson sleeps in the next room. When the motor starts he turns over without waking. His bed has one short leg, and the floor rises just enough in winter to steady it.

There are people in most of the houses. You would guess fewer from the street. Much of what they do can be done sitting down, near a window: mending a harness, taking a clock apart, picking stones from the lentils brought in from the fields. The clock mender has six clocks that belong to him and nine that belong to other people. He keeps the latter on a shelf with a little space behind it. Once he left a clock against the wall and had to cut it free. There is still a bit of wall in the hole where the winding key goes.

Children know which parts of the ward will take a nail and which will push one out again. They make use of both facts. Along the tram bed they have a game that begins with a row of bent nails set in the pavement on Monday; by Friday some have been tipped far enough to touch their neighbors. Nobody is allowed to help them. There are fierce disagreements over whether stepping on the ground between two nails counts as helping.

Under the eaves live a kind of small gray snail. Its shell is not much bigger than a lentil, with a lip so thin it breaks if you pick it up. The snails come out where the walls have been cut. The morning after the doors are trimmed, they can be seen in a neat line across the fresh wood, eating the clear beads that have gathered there. Bess brushes them into her apron and shakes them out beneath the goods shed. She has done this for years. There are always more snails at her house than at any other.

The roots are not all of one plant. In the old loading yard, when a piece has to be lifted, there is usually a dark, stringy layer beneath the close pale grain. It smells of wet wool. Small white creatures uncoil in it and go down out of sight. The yard was laid in strips, originally, and an old foundry hand who lived long enough to see it cover the rails used to say you could tell who laid which strip by the way it took a patch. No one now can do that. The strips themselves can still be seen on a cold morning, when the dew holds longer on some than on others.

The keeper is a narrow man named Pell who wears his good shoes on his rounds. He says he can feel a stopped gutter sooner through a leather sole. At the fourth threshold he sometimes stops to talk to a woman whose front room is a step lower than the street. She wants the water, just not all at once. She grows onions in shallow boxes along the inside wall, and when the evening run comes right, she can dip a cup at her doorstep without going out. Pell turns a slate on its edge for her. In the morning her daughter lays it flat again on her way to school.

At the last house there is an empty room where a man used to repair bicycles. The hooks are still in the wall. Through the low open window, field mice bring in grass heads and husk them on the bench. A wren has begun carrying the husks away. It goes in under the sill and comes out over it, taking a slightly longer route each time as the sill puts on its spring growth. From the kitchen the man’s sister watches it. She has put off asking anyone to trim that window. The bird has not nested yet, and she wants to see where it goes.
