A little mouse lives under the oldest part of the city. You can stand with both feet over her nest without standing on earth. The basalt there is two sheets thick, and the upper sheet stops just short of meeting the lower one. She goes in sideways. By the time she has turned around, she is in a space where the rock has never been warmed directly by the sun.

She has lined it with the white insides of stalks. To get these she stands a stalk against the wall of a lane and works down it with her teeth. The outside falls in curls. She leaves the curls where they fall, and the insects take them. Sometimes they take the stalk too. She comes back to the place where she left it and finds a low, roofed passage where there was a corner she could see into the night before. She noses along the new work. If the stalk was a good one she digs it out. The insects close the hole behind her.

At dusk she comes out under the leaves. In the first few minutes she makes a great deal of noise. This is partly because the leaves are dry underneath and partly because she is hurrying. There is a snake that lies along the wall here. It cannot get into the crack where she sleeps, but it can get into most of the places she could reach in a hurry. She crosses one open lane and then another and stops in a court where the birds have been feeding. There she sits up and cleans the dust off her nose. The snake is still in the same place. Its back is the color of the wall in daylight, but at this hour the wall has gone gray and the snake has not.

The mouse does not drink from the leaves. The drops on them, when there are drops, are too small to be worth getting out into the open for. She eats the leaves themselves. She opens a thick one along its edge and scrapes it hollow, leaving the skin attached to the stem. By the next afternoon the empty skin has drawn tight and brown. A ground bird works at it awhile, thinking there is something inside.

There are young in the crack for part of the year. At first they fit in the hollow between the mouse’s forelegs when she lies down. Later there is so little room that one of them sleeps in the entrance, with its tail sticking out. The insects build across the tail one morning. They get only a little way before it moves, and for several days afterward there are two blunt ends of a wall there, with just enough room for a tail between them.

When the young begin to come out, they follow the older mouse to the feeding court. She stops at the edge of each lane. They stop when she stops and sometimes when she doesn't. One of them gets into a roof through a rain hole and spends most of a night inside, where the galleries run straight for a surprising distance and then narrow all at once. By dawn it has found a way out through a rubbish opening. It comes out backward, dragging a dead beetle too broad to turn in the passage.

Farther out on the plain there are mice that have never lived under a roof. They sleep in the lava folds with the roots against their backs. On nights when the wind drops they can hear one another across stretches of bare stone that take them a long time to cross. After the seed falls, they carry some of it home. A mouse coming back with its mouth full has to put the seeds down to get around the sharp turn into its crack. It gathers them up again in the dark, as many as it can find.

The first plant at one of those cracks is often a long way from the nearest plant of its kind. Before it is big enough to cast shade, there will be a little ridge of sand on the sheltered side of its stem. The mouse steps over it on her way out. In a few years, if the plant lives, there will be a low roof over that whole end of the fold. The crack will still be there. From above, there will be no way to tell where she goes in.
