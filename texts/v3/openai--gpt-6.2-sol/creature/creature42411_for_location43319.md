The middle tuft is two tufts. The line between them runs from the pale notch to the underside of the cup, where it is lost in the rough clay. One edge has grown a little way over the other. It must have done so when the cup still stood somewhere rain could reach it. There is no longer even the width of a hair between the overlapping edges.

The two outer tufts are dead. They have kept their colour, but on the broken side of the cup one of them shows a row of empty chambers, small and regular as the bubbles in a bread crust. The middle tuft has empty chambers too. Farther in, under the overlap, a few are still green. Not grey-green like the surface: the green of a pea split with a fingernail. All the colour in those living chambers would scarcely tint a drop of water.

Something else lives in the cup. It is a mite, pale enough that the brown in its belly shows through. There have been mites there for a long time, though not continuously. In a hollow in the clay are several of their old skins, folded flat, and one skin that never opened. Sometimes, when rain stands in the courtyard overnight, the chalk at the back of the room darkens. It dries again before the clay does. In those few hours the mite can feed along the ragged edge of a dead tuft. It has never needed to cross the floor.

At present it is tucked beneath the handle. The flake of glaze above it has a white face and a raw face, and on the raw face there is a shallow pit left by a grain that came away in the firing. The mite fits in the pit with its legs drawn up. The whistle is particularly clear there. It goes on whether the mite is awake or not.

The thumbprint is on the other side of the room. Nothing lives in it. There is a fork in one of its ridges where the mason had a small scar, and a grain of chalk at the fork. Had the room been left open, the grain would have fallen out the first time someone brushed the wall. Here it has worn down until it is exactly level with the ridge. In the light from the tile you can still tell the two whites apart.
