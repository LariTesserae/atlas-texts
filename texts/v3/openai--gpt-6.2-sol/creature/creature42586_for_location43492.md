Ada, who takes the night shift at the works, lives behind the oldest door with her brother and a long-backed animal that comes and goes by the drain. Her brother has made it a bed out of his winter coat. It sleeps on the hook where the coat used to hang, with its hind feet tucked into the crack behind the peg and its nose nearly touching the floor. Sometimes, when Ada gets home, it is drinking out of the cuffs.

Her brother is called Len. He used to cut hair upstairs and still puts a board across the arms of a chair when a child comes for a trim. The children down here won't wear the cape. They like to see where their hair goes. Some of it lies where it falls, and some starts across the floor before Len can fetch the broom. There is a crack under his washstand where the short dark clippings are taken in first. The long ones may lie about for days, though if he knots one into a loop it will be gone by supper. He leaves a few loops on the washstand on purpose. In return, the legs of the washstand have not been eaten above the first joint.

Ada is the one people send for when a door has grown too far in the night. She has a good hand with the knife, though in her own house she cuts a little more than she needs to. Len likes to sit at breakfast and watch the light narrow between the door and the jamb. He can tell, to within a chair or two, how many haircuts he will do before the door wants shaving again. They have had this argument for thirty years. On her way out Ada cuts it wide enough to bring in a chair sideways.

There are people behind seven of the eleven doors. Behind the eighth are the spools, and behind the ninth the pale grazers stand with their heads together until it is time to come out. The tenth is a narrow door with a brass letter slot. Something in there folds whatever is put through, patiently and very small. A woman who went upstairs to live used to send letters to her sisters through that slot. She had the wrong door, but the sisters got the letters. Every few weeks they took a saucer to the sill and waited. The letters came out as hard little squares, which could be opened by soaking them. For a while one sister could read them and the other could not, since she always put in too much water.

The eleventh door opens on a room no wider than the door itself. It is where the animal from the fountain goes after it has set down its leaf. Len once watched it go in and thought he could hear it climbing, though there was nothing in the room to climb. Another evening he found one asleep on the threshold. It was small enough that he could have shut his hand over it. He went to get Ada, and by the time they came back it was still there, but too big for either of them to step over. There was no moment he could point to when it had moved or woken. Its breathing had the same slight catch in it. They left the door open and went home by the other stair.

Ada has never seen what lives above the flywheels. When the belt is still, she can get her lamp high enough to see a shallow hollow over the nearer bearing. The rim of the hollow is worn slick on one side. She thinks there must be a foot that hangs over it, or a tail. She has been offered a taller ladder and has declined it. There is plenty to do at closing without making something get up.

On evenings when the fountain animal is late, Len brings his chair out and waits for it. He used to tell Ada he was only getting the sweepings out of his shop. Now he takes his tea. The animal pays no attention to him until it comes to the place it wants. Then it stands a little sideways, with the leaf still in its mouth, and waits for him to move his feet.

The new lip has reached the height of Len’s smallest knuckle. After the last rain he found a little water backed up against it, not enough to drink, but enough to wet a comb. He dipped his comb and was about to put it in his pocket when he saw the marks in the bowl: three fine tracks coming down one wall, stopping just short of the water. He tipped the comb and let what was on it run back. By the time he fetched Ada, the water was gone. One set of tracks went all the way down.
