A woman comes out from beneath the sixth garden, walking backward. She has a terrapin in each hand. She sets them down in the sun and waits for them to choose a direction. When both turn toward the plinth, she picks up the smaller one and sets it a little farther off.

She has a piece of breadfruit stuck to her elbow. She must have been eating under there. Her hair is cut close on one side, where a swelling behind her ear is going down, and on the other it reaches her chin. If you ask her who lives here, she wipes her elbow and says, “My brother, for one.”

Her brother is a child when he comes for the dipper. By the time he brings it back he is old enough to have the white hairs on his wrist that she teases him about. He is not always her brother. They have been trying to settle this between them, and he has conceded that she may call him whatever she likes when there is company.

They sleep in the gardens, up in the forks of the trees. At night the birds come down and stand on the plinths, and the woman and her brother stretch out where the birds have left the wood warm. There are other sleepers. You can see where someone has lashed a pair of sandals to a branch so the lizards won't get into them. In the first garden a man has been coaxing a vine to grow around the handle of a broken jug. He wants to be able to take his shade with him when he goes to visit his daughter. His daughter lives in the next garden. She comes every morning to see how much more vine he thinks he will need.

The people are not numerous. The difficulty in counting them is that you remember people you haven't met. At the eighth garden you may find yourself looking up to see if the girl with the split lip has got her kite down. The lip healed a long time ago. You know this when you see her, although you have never seen her before. She has the kite rolled around a stick and is using it to knock breadfruit out of a tree. She asks you to catch one. It is heavier than you expect.

The woman with the terrapins looks after the brick. Nobody asked her to. She used to come to the wall to sit out of the wind, and once, finding the cloth dry, she wet it. Now she comes twice a day. She turns the clay over so it won't take the grain of the board, and picks out the white hairs the cloth leaves behind. When her brother asks why she doesn't finish it herself, she says she has one in the wall already. She can show it to him. It is low down, and the print is so small she has to use the tip of her little finger to reach the bottom of it.

Someone is expected. No one agrees on whether this is the same person who left the brick unfinished. The woman says the person who left it was in a hurry. Her brother says you can see by the care with which the cloth was tucked under the lump that they weren't. They have this argument in low voices because the man in the first garden is apt to hear and come over. He was there, he says, though he never gets any farther than saying so.

It is the woman who puts the dust in the cup. She brings it from the gardens in the crease of her palm. Her brother once washed the cup for her, and she went all the way back to the sixth plinth to fetch another pinch. A person should be able to see, she told him, whether anyone had wet their hands.

When you leave, she is sitting on the sheltered side of the wall with the board across her knees. She has put the cup on the paving so you won't step around her and knock it over. A terrapin has made it nearly to the end of her shadow. Without looking down, she moves her foot a little, giving it another inch.
