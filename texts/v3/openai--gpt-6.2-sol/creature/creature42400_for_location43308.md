Under the overhang there is an animal about as long as a forefinger. In water it is flat and dark and has no discernible edge. When the water lifts, it takes a breath. The stain pales as the animal draws its wet skirt in toward the middle, pleat by pleat, until what hangs from the ceiling is a little body the color of an apricot stone. It has two black eyes on the side nearest the rock. It seems to be looking into the rock.

It eats in the dry. The grain that strikes the floor is not its food but the husk of its food. If the water stays up long enough, the animal lets down a thread with a bead of clear gum at the end. The bead goes sideways in the still air. Sometimes it goes uphill. It touches the floor well away from where the grain fell, and comes back with a little meat in it. Once it brought up something with a jointed leg that kept kicking after the rest of it had gone into the mouth.

There used to be two of these animals. You can see where the other one lived, a handspan away, because the rock there dries first. For a while after it disappeared, the remaining animal still took in its skirt with a notch on that side, leaving room. It no longer does this.

There is a larger inhabitant of which less can be seen. At the low note, something bears against the saddle from beneath. The flat slabs settle on it in succession. At the high note the weight goes off them, but not in reverse order: the third wall is always last, even when one of its stones has fallen and lies flat on the ground. In the grit under that stone there are shallow impressions, close and numerous at one end, opening out at the other. They could have been made by the fringe of a very large mouth. They could also have been made by something attempting, over and over, to get comfortable.

The fish know one spot where the weight comes off the rock. They come to it alone. On some crossings the fish vanishes head first, though it has made no forward motion. On others it comes through again under the lip of the smaller peak, swimming briskly and trailing from one gill a filament much too long to have been inside it. The filament doesn't break. It draws out finer and finer until the fish is gone into the ordinary water and there is nothing left to see. The small animal under the overhang will not put its thread down on those occasions. It closes so tightly that the rock around it dries.

The limpet is doing something else. With each sweep that empties the saddle, it moves a little toward the side of the bowl where there is no wall. Its trail is visible only while the rock is dry, a dull, overlapping series of ovals. It has taken years to get this far. At the last oval the trail forks. One branch is old and has grit in it; the other is fresh. The old branch goes right up over the rim.

Outside the bowl, on the bare slope, there is a small clean place where a limpet could have stopped. At every coincidence of the two notes, water stands off it just long enough for a foot to come down. So far, every time, the foot has felt the stone and withdrawn.
