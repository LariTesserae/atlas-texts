There are three things the snail eats: the green skin on the water, the soft part of a reed where it has been bent, and whatever is left on the clay by a thumb. It will go a long way for the last of these. On the underside of the bluff it has licked the whorls out of the weaver’s print, leaving the little crescent where her nail went in.

It is not quite as still as it looks. At noon its shell was empty as far back as the first turn. By dusk the animal has withdrawn from the shell altogether and is lying lengthwise on the water, so thin that a reed seen through it looks merely a little wider. The dents are where it grips. When the rain rises, it lets go one grip at a time and gathers itself under the roof. By morning it is back in the shell. Sometimes it puts the shell on crooked, and spends the first warm hour turning it against the clay.

There are others in the jars, though they have not made shells yet. The woman knows them by the way a jar pours. A jar with nothing in it gives a steady thread even when its roots have tipped it nearly on its side. A jar with young in it takes a mouthful and holds it. Then they all let go, and the thread strikes the next jar hard enough to make its lid jump. The smallest ones come over with the water. They catch on the underside of the rim and hang there, eating the clay a grain at a time. She used to smooth the rims with a wet rag. Now she leaves them rough.

What lives deepest in the wall is harder to account for. A reed root will reach the end of its tray and turn back, except at one place behind the first pool. There the tips go straight into a brick. Over the course of a night they come out of it stripped of their fine hairs. They go in again the next night. The woman has tried a slip of fired clay between the brick and the tray. In the morning the pool was low, the slip was warm on both sides, and there was a line of root hairs along its upper edge, neatly laid with their white ends all the same way. She took the slip out. Whatever is there has not taken a whole root yet.

The new bank has brought something of its own. Late in the evening, after the opening narrows, a reed near the fresh edge begins to stoop. Nothing weighs on its tip. It bends from the base, slowly enough that the drop on it travels down the leaf without falling. In the fold where the leaf clasps the stem there is a small brown head, with a bit of the old mat still caught between its jaws. It has lived in the mat since before the mat was mud. There is a second head in the next shoot, and a third in the next. The woman put in six shoots because she thought two or three might fail.

On the stair she waits until the low note comes back. It tells her the first pool has filled and is passing water on. Then she puts her bowl under the joint to catch what she can before it freezes. Once she found a shell in the bowl, no bigger than the hollow in a grain of barley. She warmed it in her mouth on the way up the stair and set it under the bluff. It was gone the next day. For a week afterward, whenever she leaned in to work, she felt a small cold place on the inside of her lower lip.
