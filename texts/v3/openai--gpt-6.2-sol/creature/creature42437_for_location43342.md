The animal at the back has one foot in each room.

The foot in this room is broad and gray, with a seam down the middle. Water pools on it. The little white grazers cross it without checking their pace, though they will turn aside at a fresh root. The other foot is visible through the curve of water between the lips: five clear pads spread against the far wall. They look too small to hold anything until one comes loose. The whole wall pulls inward with it before letting go.

Between the feet, the animal is no thicker than a ribbon. For most of its life it could draw itself back through the seam. It doesn't. It feeds on the soft rim where the two lips are coming together, reaching first to one side and then the other. Where it has fed, the green goes dark and hard. The lips can meet there without knitting. All around it they close as usual.

There are other such animals in the hollow. The oldest is over the entrance, in a seam so wide that its middle hangs in a slack loop. The gray foot on this side has a fringe of roots growing into it. The foot outside is warm on certain days. When it warms, the animal takes up the slack a little at a time, and the entrance narrows. Water that ordinarily runs out over the grit backs up into the floor. The small animals in the leaf go on eating; they can be heard quite distinctly while the water is rising. By the time it reaches them, most have gnawed through to the next layer.

The one at the back is young. Its gray foot still has an eye on it, right in the seam, which closes when a grazer walks over it. Sometimes, when a bud opens, the foot lifts to intercept the patch of afternoon. The clear pads let go one at a time. A narrow swelling travels down the ribbon and disappears between the lips.

A little later the far wall lights up. There is no patch of sunlight there, only a flicker coming and going very fast. It shows the water that has been making the noise. In a room scarcely bigger than a hand it has found a slit down which to fall, and the slit is deep enough that the bottom does not light up. The pale grazers are all on the wall above it. They have eaten the young tissue down to its veins, leaving themselves a branching set of narrow roads. One comes to the end of a road and feels out into the dark with both forelegs. The clear foot moves under it. The grazer steps across, and the foot sets it down where the green begins again.

Eventually the seam will be too tight for the ribbon. You can see what happens then in the older folds: a gray foot on one wall, and opposite it, beyond a dark line in the leaf, the five shallow prints of another. It would be easy to take those feet for dead things. But when a pool spills in one room, the foot in the next lifts before the water gets there. In the coldest part of the hollow several lift together, leaving dry ovals behind them. All the little creatures that had been grazing around their edges move in to eat what has grown underneath.
