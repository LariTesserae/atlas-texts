The people who live at the upper stop know which car has come in by what gets set down on the platform. In the morning it’s the empty milk cans, which ring even when the driver puts them down gently. At eleven it’s the mill sacks. The last car brings up the depot laundress, who sets down nothing but has to stand for a moment with both hands on the rail. She’s been on her feet since six. Her grandson meets her there and takes the cloth bag with her wet shoes in it. All summer he’s been tall enough to carry it without letting it bump on the stairs, but he likes the sound and lets it bump.

They live in three rooms behind the orchard wall. The best room in winter is the smallest one, where the warm return pipe goes through. In summer they eat on the landing with the family opposite, passing plates across the head of the stairs. The boy does his arithmetic there. If he drops a pencil, somebody will bring it up eventually, though not necessarily to him. There are four children on those stairs and a blue pencil has been going around among them for most of a year.

Something eats the old figs after the parrots have opened them. The boy thought it was the bats from the freight shaft and went down with his grandmother’s laundry lamp to see. What he found was a brown mouse on the underside of a branch. It had its hind feet hooked over the wood and was eating upside down, very fast, with both front paws in the fig. A second mouse waited in the trough. When a piece fell it didn’t go after it. Only when the first mouse came down did the second climb, by exactly the same route, to eat at the hole its companion had made.

The woman who keeps those trees grew up at the settling tanks. She still judges distances downhill badly. Going to see her sister, she’ll carry a hot dish wrapped in a towel and arrive with it cold. She knows every tree in the upper orchard by touch, though. When she thins the leaves she puts a hand beneath a branch before she looks up into it; she can tell from the weight where the fruit has set. This year she has been going out before breakfast to look at the oldest tree’s roots. One has flattened itself against the expansion joint and put out a pale lip on the far side. The surveyor marked the lip with chalk. By his next visit the mark was still there, but it was no longer at the edge.

In the disused shaft the bats have their young in a band about two arm lengths high, just below a place where the old freight doors leak warm air. Higher up there are plenty of ledges, all empty. The sweeper knows when the young have begun to fly because that week there are droppings all over the stairs instead of in the usual neat fall. He has never seen one make its first flight. He comes after they’ve gone out and works with the door propped open, listening to dishes being washed in the rooms across the gap.

The keeper has decided to take off the branch that reaches toward the cable. She’ll do it after the fruit is picked. It’s the branch that shades the driver’s bench, so the driver and the laundress’s grandson go along the platform one evening to see where the shade of the other branches falls. A little way down, beyond the end of the rail, they find a patch wide enough at four and wider at five. The bench unbolts. They move it on a Sunday, with the boy carrying the bolts in his grandmother’s shoe bag. By the first car on Monday a swift has gone through the space where the bench stood, low enough that the driver, eating the last of her breakfast in the new place, sees the pale underside of its throat.
