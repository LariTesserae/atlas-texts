The bodies under the shelf are clear enough that you can see the pebbles through them, but not quite clearly enough to see what they are doing. If you lie down with your cheek against the stone, you can make out two lips at the upstream end of each body. One stays flat against the pebble. The other lifts and settles, admitting a little water. The clean patch is not where the animal feeds. It is where it has to keep a seal.

When a pebble works loose, the animal does not let go at once. It goes on lifting its lip even as the pebble turns over and presents it to the air. Before long the lip sticks. The body puckers, lets go, and travels downstream as a small white knot. It may come to rest in one of the shallow dishes. By the next morning there will be nothing in the dish that you can distinguish from grit.

There is another animal in the stone beneath the gravel. You can find a piece of it where a seam has broken open: a brown skin drawn across a row of holes, with a loose edge that looks as if it ought to lift in the current. It never does. If water is drawn out of one of the holes, the edge pulls *in*, and a little more of the skin comes into sight from under the rock.

The skin goes a long way in some beds and hardly a handspan in others. It takes the shape of the spaces that are open to it. Here it is a sheet with pillars through it; here it is five or six cords, their ends spread in the stone like the ends of a frayed string. In the tighter beds there is no room for so much as a grain of sand to pass alongside it. The water passes through the skin itself. It comes out with less in it than it had going in, though not much less. The animal has a great deal of water to make a living from.

At the dishes that knock, the skin has grown across the narrow end of a chamber. Water heaps up behind it until it gives. The sound comes from a tuck in the skin snapping inside out against the rock. The tuck fills again. In one dish the knock is so regular that the shadow of a hand waved above the water seems erratic by comparison. In the next dish there may be two knocks close together and then a wait.

The little animals under the shelf depend on that wait. While the tuck is filling, the seep there is scarcely strong enough to stir the gray deposit. When it gives, a pulse travels through the gravel, and the lifted lips take in what the water has picked up on the way. If you watch by the knock rather than by the clock, you can see them all thicken at once.

Not every knock has such a shelf below it. At one of the loudest dishes the water goes straight back into bare rock. There are pebbles in the bottom, but they are fitted tight in their sockets. Nothing is folded around them. Now and then a white knot comes through this dish and circles against the direction of the ripples before it goes down the seam. Most are caught at the first narrowing. Some make it far enough to lodge against a skin. They soften there. A few stick, and the skin grows around them without covering the lips. In time the lips widen, and the body between them flattens. The pebble animal and the animal in the stone are the same kind, though most of those that live on pebbles remain small and clear all their lives.

Where two skins meet, neither can draw water through the other. The edges thicken and lie pressed together, sometimes for years, with no more life in the join than in the stone beside it. One may die back, and the other take its place. There is a dish near the middle where a knock stopped for most of a summer. The pool did not shrink. The skin had split, and water was going through the split without having to wait. By the time the knock returned it was coming from a place a foot farther down, where the seam narrowed again. The animals under the old shelf did not move. They kept their mouths open into the weaker seep. Their clean patches grew smaller, until some of the pebbles showed only a bright line beneath the lower lip.

On the far side of the rise, where the sound thins out, there are skins in the rock that never knock. One of them comes near the surface beneath the dripping ledge. At each fall the ring crosses a pale spot in the bed of the pool. The spot darkens a little later than the stone around it. If you stay to watch, you can see it whiten again, from one edge to the other, while the next drop is still gathering.
