What lives there has a place for its thumb.

Most of the time it lies in the seam, spread so thin that the two shelves could come together without crushing it. The water belongs to it in the way saliva belongs to a mouth. Draw some away and it does not fetch more from deeper in the mountain. It wets itself, all along the place you touched, in one small involuntary act.

It is not the whole seam. Beyond the pinched pebble, the stone goes on a good way without it. Nor is it the little jointed animal. That animal feeds on the whitish skin that forms along the water and is left to get on with it. When it dies, the next one will have the flake. There have been several.

The inhabitant thickens at the rubbed patch. Given a long quiet, it puts out something like the heel of a hand and rests it there. A narrower part curls over the lip. This is how the stone got smooth. The lip stayed sharp because the narrow part never bears weight; it hangs, with its end in the seam. There is no corresponding hand on the other side. The rest of the creature has never needed to take a shape you could name.

The cup was there before the patch. A person brought it in empty and spent some time setting it level, trying one chip and then another. The inhabitant knows this much of the person: how a chip feels when it is rejected and put down, and how it feels when the weight of the cup is let onto it. It knows the pause between those two things. It would know that person again by the pause, though not by the sound of their voice or the touch of their skin.

It leaves the cup alone. Once, on drawing itself back into the seam, it brushed the rim and took a little water with it. The lightening of the cup made the smaller chip shift. It spent a long while putting the chip back, moving it no farther than the width of a grain in the clay. Since then it has kept the cup full by passing water to the rim from below, a little at a time. There is always a dry interval afterward, too narrow for a finger to find, between the clay and the wet stone.

Your swallowing is a considerable event to it. So is the effort you make not to swallow again. It draws itself in under your cheek, not because it is afraid of you but because the weight is in the wrong place, and it cannot tell whether you mean to keep it there.

After you leave, quite a while after the rock has ceased to carry the noise of your leaving, it comes up onto the shelf. It settles, takes its weight off the lip, and feels for the two chips. Neither has moved. It feels for them once more before it lies down.
