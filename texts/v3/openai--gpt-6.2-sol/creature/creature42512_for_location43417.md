The cobbler lives behind his shop, though he has a flat four stops up the line. There is a bed behind the curtain where customers suppose he keeps the leather. He keeps the leather in the flat. It goes soft down here, and the little white insects get at the cut edges. He brings down what he expects to use, rolled with the good side in.

The insects live mostly on paste. You can find them under the old notices on the service doors, in the thin space between the paper and the metal. They leave the printed side intact. When a notice is taken down it looks perfectly sound until somebody tries to fold it. Then it comes apart along lines that have nothing to do with the folds.

There is a woman who gardens on Tuesdays and Fridays. Her name is Ada, and she is the one who puts the bowl under the pipe. The bowl was her father's shaving bowl. The chip is where he used to rest his thumb, and when she picks it up she puts her thumb there too, although it is sharp now. She empties it into the ginger. On Fridays there is sometimes enough left over for the fern.

Ada says the lizard went into the wall. The cobbler says it went upstairs. Neither has much evidence, but they have been having the argument long enough that each knows which part of it the other needs. Ada needs there to be a way into the wall. The cobbler needs a creature that has lived here to be able to leave.

In fact there are lizards in the square again. They are small enough to sit between the raised letters on the pump cabinet. In the daytime they lie behind the conduit, with their bellies on the rock and their toes resting on the white roots. It is not clear that they are kin to the one from the fountain. That one was a large, badly scarred animal that came out when people ran water. These wait for the lamps to dim. Then they go down the wall, stopping at each bracket, and take the insects that have come out to feed at the damp edges of the notices.

The ginger has been divided so many times that there is no longer a first plant anyone can point to. Some of it lives in a pot on the cobbler's landing four stops up. Some is in the staff lavatory at the tram terminus, where it is pale and has never flowered. Down here it lifts the paving. Every year Ada takes out the growth nearest the brake door, and every year the new shoots come up a little closer to the hinge. She has a narrow trowel for that job, made from a bread knife the baker left behind. The baker herself lives by the river now. On cold days she comes down and sits on the step of her shop, saying she has only come to get warm. Sometimes she brings a loaf. She never brings a knife.

Under the bowl is a woodlouse with one white patch on its back. The first time Ada saw it she thought she had hurt it with the rim. The patch was still there the next week, and by then she could tell it from the others. She lowers the bowl slowly now. Most of the woodlice run for the joint in the paving. The white one waits until the shadow reaches it and then moves just far enough. Ada has begun to leave the bowl a little crooked.

The cobbler is awake when the last tram goes over. He feels it through the bed before he hears it, a movement so slight that on his nights in the flat he sometimes lies waiting for it and misses his sleep. Down here he puts out his lamp after the middle lamp dims. For a while, through the gap in the curtain, he can see the green underside of the leaf. He has never seen the drop fall. Once he stayed up to watch, but a lizard came down the iron leg of the machine, put its forefeet on the leaf, and drank the drop without shaking it loose.

He told Ada in the morning. She asked how big it was. He showed her the length of the last joint of his thumb. They went over to the fountain together and looked into the dry basin. Then Ada reached in and took out a dead leaf that neither of them had noticed from across the square.
