The little shell animals live in the joints of the causeway. There are plenty out on the clay, but those are feeding. To sleep they come back to concrete and fit themselves into the cracks, small ones first, then larger ones, until the last shell lies flush with the slab. You can run your hand over a joint packed that way and mistake it for a good repair. When a slab tips, the animals on the low side move out. The ones on the high side stay where they are and grow a little more shell.

The broad animal sleeps with its head off the causeway and its hind feet braced against a joint. It has five toes on one foot and four on the other. The missing toe is an old loss; the little shell animals graze the scar along with the rest of the foot. It lets them. There is very little on the plain that requires it to wake all the way up.

It feeds during the pause between strokes. A fold opens low on its neck and the flat fish come off their shelves and go in. Most of them come out again. They rest afterward with their mouths downstream, which they do at no other time. If you put a hand into the water among them, you can feel what they have brought out in their gills: a fine grit, warmer than the water, that works into the creases of your fingers. The little shell animals eat it before it settles. Around the sleeper the concrete is bare even underneath.

A woman lives in the pump house nearest the ladder. She has a bed made up on a plank between two pipes. One pipe stays warm; the other warms only when she has been in bed for some time. She hangs her socks on the first one. Under the bed she keeps a crock in which the cupped leaves will grow if she stands them root-down in a piece of old felt. She eats the youngest leaves and leaves the old ones to thicken. A thick leaf makes a good washer for the little cocks on the gauge lines.

She has worked at the other houses, though not in the order you would come to them on the causeway. At one of them her name is cut into the handle of a valve she has never been able to turn. At another there is a shelf at just the height she likes, but it was put up for somebody who could reach farther. She takes a stool when she goes there. She has stopped moving the shelf.

Twice she has found young in the house, in the shallow pan beneath the RETURN coupling. They were long enough to lie across her palm and had the same heavy hind feet as the animal on the causeway. Their mouths were at the ends of their heads then. They sucked at the pad of her thumb hard enough to hurt. The water in the pan was only a finger deep, but when she tried to lift the first one out it put its feet down and stood on something well below the pan. She let it be. By the end of her shift there was only a little grit where it had been.

The second time she brought a flat fish in a cup. The young one took it by the tail, held it for a while, and gave it back. Since then she has brought two fish. The first is usually the one it wants.

She is the one who wipes the ladder. She starts at the top, where the mud has begun to toughen, and works down with a twist of sacking. The little shell animals pack the space beneath the lowest rung, though there are better joints less than a yard away. She used to clear them out with the end of a spoon. Now she passes the sacking over their backs and leaves them in place. They have brought the rung almost level.

On her way home she passes the sleeper. For years she went around it on the side with the fish. Lately it has drawn one hind foot up when she comes near, leaving a strip of concrete wide enough for her boot. Sometimes it does this before she reaches it, at the sound of the footfall that gets there first. She steps through without touching it. By the time she gets her key into the pump-house door, the foot is back in its joint.
