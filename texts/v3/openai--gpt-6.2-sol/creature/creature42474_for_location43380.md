The black animals are narrow enough to stand in the shadow of their own legs. At noon this is of little use to them, but later in the day you can see one stop and gather itself into the six fine lines it casts. Its feet are broad and soft. When it runs, it lifts them high, as though the rind were sticky.

They are hunting the pale grazers, though a grazer outweighs one of them by a hundred times. They want what falls from its mouth. A grazer feeds with its chin against the ground, working a loose edge of rind between two grinding plates. Every so often it raises its head to let the dust out. There are small amber grains in the dust. The black animals come in under its chin and pick them out before they harden. A grazer with young will put up with this for hours. One without young is apt to lie down suddenly.

A newborn grazer has no grinding plates. It puts its mouth to the underside of its mother’s jaw and takes the crumbs there, along with any black animal too slow to get out of the way. For the first few days its feet are curled. It rides in the fold between her throat and chest, where the passage of her feet over the rind is so loud that it cannot hear the root pumps. When it is old enough to walk, the first thing it has to learn is which of the noises underfoot it can ignore. It lies down at every change in the beat. The mother goes on feeding, a little farther away each time.

There are things in the warm seams that never learn to walk at all. They begin as flat white specks, easy to mistake for scuffs, and grow downward into the rind. In an old one the mouth lies several feet below the surface. The only part left in the daylight is a thin ring that lifts and falls as the animal breathes. The black runners shelter in these rings when a storm comes. After a bad storm you can find all forty or fifty packed into a single ring, their feet drawn in, with the white rim moving gently up and down around them.

At a cut bank you can see what the buried animals eat. They have their mouths against the little valves, not the big ones that knock, but the ones that make the sound you can only hear when you lie with your cheek to the ground. They take a film of oil each time a valve closes. The plant grows a collar over the mouth. The mouth grows through it. In the older layers there are collars nested a dozen deep around animals that have been dead for centuries. Their bodies are gone, but the space each one occupied has kept the shape of a soft thing pressing back.

A leaf carries a different company. Its hunters have hooked feet and almost no weight; when they die, they generally stay where they died until the leaf itself comes down. The living ones pass them on the way to the rim. They hunt by putting one foot against the leaf and taking the other five off it. A runner on the upper surface makes a tremor they can follow through the thickness of the leaf. Where a vein intervenes they lose it, and you can watch them feeling along either side of the vein, raising and setting down that one foot.

The hunters are among the animals that disappear into the folds of a falling leaf. There is often a brood in there, each young hanging by a pair of hooks, each so slight that the rocking scarcely reaches it. When the leaf lodges and starts to come apart, the hunters carry their young out one at a time. They have never walked on the plain. They keep trying to get underneath it. For a night or two, if you follow a fresh break, you can see them setting their young down to try one edge after another, even edges no thicker than a shaving. Some find a lifted place. Some go back to the leaf.

By the following noon the grazers have come to the wreck. They feed along its torn edge, where the oil has set in beads. Farther down, each gust raises a little cloud of fibers. The black animals turn up as the first grazer lifts its head. Under the wreck there is shade enough for all of them, and for a while none has to run. Then a broad piece of leaf peels back in the wind. A hunter carrying its young is left standing upside down against the sky. It walks a little farther out, to where there is still an underside.
