The smallest of the grazers can get its whole head into a seam in the stone. Its mother cannot. She opens the wet leaves with her tongue and eats what she can reach, and the young one goes in after the pale stems. For a few weeks it can feed where she has fed without eating anything she would otherwise have had. By the time its head is too broad, it has learned the pans.

They don't go to the pans in order of nearness. After rain they will walk past a sheet of water broad enough to show the sky and drink from a little black hole at the foot of a slab. The sheet tastes of the droppings of the birds. The hole is fed from underneath, and in a dry spell, when there is nothing to see in it, a grazer will put its mouth to the stone and wait. You can tell when it has got something by a single movement in the white of its throat.

The young stay in the beds while the mothers feed. They don't lie curled. They lie with their legs folded flat against the body and their chins on the ground, so that at a little distance the stripes look like the edges of thin stones lying one on top of another. A mother returning to a bed puts her nose down before the young one moves. Sometimes she puts it down in an empty bed. The hair and the warmth are still there, and she stands over it for a while, working her nostrils. Then she goes to the next outcrop. There are enough beds that a young one which has got up and moved out of the sun can take most of an afternoon to find.

A long, low animal hunts among those outcrops. It has fur the color of the stone where the stone is wet, and paws broad enough to spread over two sides of a ridge. It eats chiefly the creatures under the leaves. Where it has fed, the plants are laid open in little fans, and by the following morning the exposed leaves have gone the color of old paper. It will take a young grazer if it finds one away from its mother. More often it goes to a bed after both animals have left and pulls the hairs out of the rough stone. In the deep cracks where it raises its own young, the hairs make a lining scarcely thicker than the fur on a living flank.

At the rim you can sometimes see that animal stop with one forefoot lifted. It has felt something through the other three. A grazer coming downhill puts its feet down hard, even when it is in no hurry. A bird alighting makes almost nothing of itself on the stone. But when a bird comes in under the lip and takes hold upside down, the animal can feel the claws scrabble if it happens to be standing on the right piece of rock. It puts its foot down and goes to look. Most of the nests are beyond it. There are a few it can reach by going into cracks well back from the edge and coming out lower down. The birds keep using those recesses. In a good year there are more birds ready to lay than there are places to lay in.

The mouths of the nestlings aren't all one color. There are two bright spots far back in each, one on either side of the tongue. Out in the light you would hardly notice them. In the recess the returning bird can see them before it can see the young. A chick will open to a passing shadow all day when it is small. Later it waits for the push of air from a parent checking its flight. Later still it comes to the threshold to meet the bird and has to learn to get out of its way.

On the first flights the plain brown backs are particularly conspicuous. The young stay right side up long after the others have turned. One will go a great way out like that, dwindling against the opposite summits, and seem to have committed itself to crossing. Then it drops a wing. The white shows, and several others turn almost at once, though none of them is close to it. There are days when you can follow the whole flock by those flashes and never be sure you have seen a bird travel between two of them.

Where the slanting water draws away from the wall, there are shelves that don't dry. A soft black growth covers the stone there. In it live little flat creatures with gills along their sides. The birds bring them back stuck together in wads; now and then a live one drops on the lip of a nest and makes for the damp behind it. The white thresholds are not quite bare of life. Look closely at one in a recess near the top and there are minute dark tracks through the white, which end wherever the stone gets dry. The creatures can go no farther. A good many of them spend their lives within the width of a bird's foot of the open air.

There is a grazer you may take at first for two animals standing very close together. Its winter coat comes off in long pieces, and the new stripes under it do not quite meet the old ones. It is an old female. She drinks standing, with her feet apart, from a pan sunk at the downhill end of a slab. The younger animals kneel at the other end. When the water gets low they have to move around to her side, and for a day or two there will be several of them there, all standing awkwardly on the slope. Then the pan is dry. The others stop coming. The old one comes once more and licks the dark ring, starting at the lowest point and working up one side as far as she can reach. Under her tongue the ring is dark again for a little while.
