The things that carry the beetles are about the length of a thumb. The old ones look longer because the skin at the base of the tail has loosened and trails behind them. They live in the newest breaks, where very little else can get a purchase. Their toes are soft on top and hard underneath, and they can go down an edge that would open the belly of a lizard. In daylight, if you turn over a loose flake of stone, you may find one with its eyes closed and its four feet planted on the flake, upside down. It will stay like that until you put it back. If you put it back wrong, it will leave the following night.

They do not carry every beetle. The big black ones are too heavy, and there is a red kind that makes them rub their mouths on the ground. What they take is the small swimming sort, which folds up almost round. One comes to the pool and puts its lower jaw in the water. The beetle fastens on. The little animal lifts its head and walks across the dry place with the beetle hanging from its lip. Sometimes it puts it down in the next pool. Sometimes it goes on past three or four pools and into a crack nobody would think had water at the bottom. There it rolls the beetle onto its back and eats the pale eggs from under the wing cases. It does not eat the beetle. By the next rain the same beetle may be carrying eggs again.

There are dry cracks in which they raise their young, and wet ones in which they keep beetles, and for a few nights in the year these are the same cracks. That is a bad time to be a young one. A mother bringing a beetle home may find the nursery full of water and her young clinging to the ceiling with their noses just clear. She can take only one in her mouth. The others have to wait while she finds a place to put it. A root will do, if there is a hollow on the upper side. After a hard rain you can find young ones lined up along a root, each with its chin laid over the back of the one in front. They stay there after they are quite dry. It is the mother coming back, and putting a foot on the root, that makes them let go.

When two pieces of stone still answer one another, these animals use both. One may live on the underside of a slab and feed its young through a narrow opening halfway up a ridge. It goes the long way round to reach them, but before it sets out it scrapes a toe against the slab. The young stop moving. They know the scrape that comes before food and the scrape their mother makes when a striped animal has got its nose into the opening. You could sit beside her and not know she had made either sound.

Sometimes the opening closes while she is away. Sometimes it is still there and the sound no longer reaches it. Then she walks the ridge with a beetle on her lip, trying each slit in turn. The beetle weighs almost nothing, but after a while she sets it down. On dry stone it doesn't try to get away. She goes a little farther, putting her head into places where her head has never been. In the morning there will be a beetle under a leaf, or in the hollow of a footprint, waiting for enough water to swim in. If she has found her young, she comes back for it. If she hasn't, she comes back for it anyway.
