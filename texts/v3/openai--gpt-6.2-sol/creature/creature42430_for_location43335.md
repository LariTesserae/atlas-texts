The little animals have gray backs and white bellies. You can see the white when one goes over the lip. It turns in the first few feet of its fall and goes on belly upward, with its chin tucked in. The four feet are small and look blunt until they spread. Then each shows a fringe of clear hairs, longer than the foot itself. Grains caught in the fringe travel from root to tip and float off. An animal at rest, with all four feet doing this, appears to be standing in a very slow fall of dust.

They eat the pale growth beneath the black leaves. They do not eat much of it. At a good patch an animal will go around the stone several times, taking a little at each opening, and leave the leaves lying neatly in place. The bitten tissue goes brown by morning. If you lift the leaves a week later you can find the marks, each with a pale raised rim, and in the cleft of one of them there may be a seed. The seed did not come from that plant. The animals carry them pressed into the soft corners of their mouths. The plants under the lip would have no other way of getting seed from one ledge to the next. Anything that came loose there would fall for most of a day.

The young spend their first season under the overhang. They are born with the fringes folded flat against their shins and can get about only where there is stone. In the hottest part of the day they lie on the undersides of the ledges, toes curled into the cracks, while an adult goes out to feed. When it returns, they nose at its mouth for food. They sometimes come away with a seed stuck to the forehead. An adult can groom the top of its own head with either hind foot; the young cannot, and for several days you can tell one from another by what it has there.

Their first journeys on the summit are made through the low stone room. The slant is shallow enough for them to climb, and there is a worn place on the inner wall where they wait. From there they can see the ground below the lip without going near it. When an adult passes through that view, a young one follows it along the wall until the wall runs out. It puts a foot into the swept part of the floor. Often it snatches the foot back. If it puts all four down, the hairs begin to open, and it traverses the room in one startled bound. That is why the outward tracks are so much farther apart. On a calm morning you may find that the last four dots are on the outside of the wall.

There are things in the dust that are easier to see through the room than in the open. Against the shaded rock below, you can make out threads with little dark intervals along them. A thread may be longer than a person is tall, although laid on a slab it would scarcely cover a finger joint. The intervals draw together when the dust thins and move apart as it thickens. In a low plume the threads are short and numerous. Higher up, where the plume begins to fray, several will join end to end. The joined threads turn crosswise in the air. Grains collect along them until they sag, and stream off the low end in a sheet. A large sheet can shut out the stars. What is left of the thread afterward is very small. It goes on rising.

The columns between the posts bring these things down. At the foot of a column, just clear of the earth, the threads wind round one another. As the dust drains away you can see the winding grow tighter. Finally there is a gray tuft about the size of a burr, going round and round at the height of your knuckle. It holds there until dusk, even on days when the column has gone by early afternoon.

An animal arriving at a pair of posts does not go straight to the tuft. It hunts for a stone, nosing several over before it takes one in its mouth. It brings the stone to the center and sets it down. Sometimes the stone is too low, and the tuft keeps turning above it; the animal removes it and tries another. When the stone is high enough, the lowest threads brush it and stick. The animal waits a little, then takes hold of the tuft with its teeth and pulls. A few threads come free. The rest contract against the pebble so closely that you would take them for a smudge on the stone.

The animal carries what it has pulled away back to the lip. The young chew the threads but do not swallow them. They draw them through their mouths, with a great deal of bobbing of the head, and wipe them down the insides of their forelegs. By morning the clear fringes are standing out. The smallest young can open them only a few hairs at a time. It may stamp one foot repeatedly on a ledge while the others sleep.

At a broken pair of posts the dust still comes down, but it comes down broad and touches the ground. The threads work their way into the grit and by evening there is nothing for an animal to take. In those parts of the summit there are old plants with leaves as broad as fingernails. You can lift leaf after leaf without finding a bite. At the intact pairs, on the other hand, there are often so many pebbles in the middle that a returning animal has to move the old ones aside before it can set a fresh one down. No one has seen an animal set up a post. They have been seen trying to get a thread off a fallen one, walking the length of it and starting over at the other end.

On a morning after a high storm, some of the smudged pebbles will be gone. You can watch this happen if you get to the posts before the ground warms. A pebble stirs, rolls a little, and comes to rest. Presently it rolls back. With each turn more of the smudge is exposed to the light. The threads ease out one at a time and grow taut, not all in the same direction. For a while the pebble rocks between them. Then one side lifts far enough for a scattering of fine grains to run underneath, and the whole thing travels a few inches without turning. It settles and begins again. By noon you may find it a long way from the posts, lying among stones so like it that you have to put your face down close to see which one is holding itself just clear of the ground.
