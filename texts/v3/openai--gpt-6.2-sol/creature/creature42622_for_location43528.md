The people at the bottom know a house by its underside. A child sent to fetch a bowl from her aunt’s will look for the dark crescent beneath the sill, where her aunt sits to take off her shoes. She can do this from halfway across a square. The number above the door is no use at that distance, and in any case there are three houses with that number, all of them correct when it was cut.

They are barefoot indoors and on their own stretches of street. For crossing an unfamiliar plate they wear sandals, which they take off if they want to listen. A person standing still with one sandal in each hand is given time to finish. Sometimes there is nothing to hear but a jar being set down in the next house. Sometimes the person puts the sandals back on and goes home by a longer way.

Much of the day is spent visiting. There is work to do, but a great deal of it can be carried under one arm, and the potters go on scraping their pots at other people’s doors. They bring a flat basket for the scrapings. The clay comes down the stair wrapped in wet cloth and costs enough that a potter will interrupt a quarrel to pick a curl of it off somebody’s knee.

The people who come down the stair to trade tend to remain on its lowest landing, where they can see customers coming. They bring clay, wood, dried fruit, horn, and occasionally a bundle of green leaves so fresh that the smell goes before them. The children buy leaves one at a time. Some eat theirs on the stair. Others carry them home flat between their palms and put them in a drinking bowl to see how long they will hold their color. When a leaf has gone quite soft it is still difficult to persuade its owner to throw it away. There is nowhere to throw it where it will look less conspicuous.

The stair also brings down people who mean to stay. For a while they are easy to pick out: they look up whenever they come into a room, and they put tall things on shelves. Their neighbors lend them low stools and wait. It is not necessary to have been born here to learn the small hitch in a room just before a plate starts moving. A woman who had lived above for forty years learned it in her first winter. What she did not learn, even in old age, was to leave a cup standing in that moment. She would catch up her own and then reach for yours.

When a seam closes a street, the people on either side generally have time to finish what they are saying. They raise a hand when they can no longer hear. Sometimes two households that have been trying not to speak are brought up close enough for one to smell what the other is cooking. A move of that sort can settle a dispute or keep it alive for another twenty years. There are sisters who have lived across an avenue from one another without visiting and who, now that their back steps nearly touch, pass a single good knife between them all day.

Not all crossings are between people. The soft grazers sometimes follow a damp wall into a house and take the brown off the bottom of a washing jar. The householder lifts the jar a little so they can finish underneath. A grazer is cool and surprisingly heavy for something so easily bruised. Children are allowed to keep one for as long as it takes to cross an open hand. By the time the last pair of feet lets go, the child usually has to be reminded to lower the hand to the floor.

The jointed animals under the plates are harder to get acquainted with. A mason eating his lunch may watch a hundred come out of a seam and not be able afterward to say which end of any one went first. But in the dim districts, when they come out singly, you can see them stop beside a crumb. One will stand over it, working at it for a long time. When it leaves, there are two crumbs, both as far as anyone can tell the same size as the first.

In the roofless houses by the old river, almost nobody lives year-round. People go there when they want more room than a house can give them. Boys practice instruments there. A woman who makes ropes walks backward through three rooms, letting the rope run through the old round holes. In one of the thinnest houses a man sits on one side of a wall while his little daughter sits on the other. She spreads her fingers against the stone and he names them, getting one wrong on purpose. She is old enough now to know what he is doing. She still makes him start again.

There are nights when a traveling plate carries a house out beyond the last of its neighbors. The people inside go on with supper. Afterward someone goes out to see how far they have come, and whether there is a dry way back. From the door they can make out the lit soles of people walking in the distant market. Those people cannot see the door at all.

By morning there may be a channel between the house and the rest of the city. The first visitor stands on the near bank and calls to ask if anything is wanted. Usually nothing is. The visitor stays a little while anyway. The two households discuss the depth of the channel, whether it was always that deep, and whose bridge it used to have. Then someone brings out a stool. There is often another stool to be fetched on the other side, and the conversation waits while that is done.
