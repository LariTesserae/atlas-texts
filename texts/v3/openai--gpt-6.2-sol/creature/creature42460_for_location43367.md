The pale insect is a springtail. It is not always the same one, although there is no way to tell this from the doorway. Their young are small enough to feed under the lip of the drain collar without coming into view. They graze the slick on the metal, taking some of the green with it. The green grows back slowly. A delivery that has to be counted twice gives it more light than it generally gets in a week.

There are other things in the collar that do not depend on the door. A film on the underside is thickest where the metal meets the grout. It is nearly transparent when wet and whitish when dry. In a long dry spell the springtails draw back to that join. They do not go down the drain. Below the bend the walls are scoured clean every time water goes through, and there is nowhere to stand out of its way. When the first drop falls from the bolt, there may be nothing on the tile for another hour. Then several come out. What brings them out is not the drop but the wetting of the fine dirt in the grout, which proceeds a little way ahead of the visible water.

The spiders are not all of one kind. The ones that retreat into the terminal boxes have long legs and very little body. A smaller, darker one spends most of its life under the bowed bracket. It has fastened silk across the angle where the two brackets meet. There is a pocket there that the sweeping does not reach. In it are the husks of two springtails and the wing of a fly considerably bigger than the spider. The fly came in on the sleeve of a man who was carrying a carton with both hands. He felt it walking up his wrist and set the carton down hard enough to frighten it off.

Behind the empty housing lives a different set of animals, most of them too small to distinguish from the dirt they live in. The warmth there is useful to them chiefly because it lasts. An afternoon’s heat would dry them out if the corner were open to the room, but the iron stands close to the brick, and in the narrow space behind it a soft deposit has built up out of lint, flakes of paint, and the remains of insects. There are mites in that deposit. Some eat the fine threads growing through it; others eat the mites that do. A little farther up, where the wall is warmer and cleaner, there is almost nothing. Much of the back of the housing is as bare as the front.

The old linen has life at its wet end. Its exposed corner, the part that moves, is dry and of no use to anything. Under the black crust farther in, threads still separate from one another. They are pale where they have newly split. A colony can advance along a single thread and stop at the crossing, leaving the next thread sound. That is one reason the wad has held together for so long. If someone took it out, it would probably come out whole, and only fall apart when they tried to unfold it.

No one spends a shift in the room now, though one of the men who used to come down kept a chair by the empty housing. He sat with one boot on the low return, which was then just the right height to rest a bad knee. The chair went upstairs when the housing was taken out of service. People still come down to listen, or to see whether something they did upstairs has had an effect. A woman on the early shift sometimes stands inside the shut door long enough for her eyes to find the pilot light. She knows about the insect. Until she saw two together she supposed, without quite believing it, that it was the same one each time.

By the end of a dry week the spoon looks empty from the door. A springtail can still follow the low side of the last waterline, stopping every so often to feed. At that scale the line is not continuous. There are stretches of clean glaze between the deposits, and at one of them the insect turns back, passes its own tracks, and tries the line above.
