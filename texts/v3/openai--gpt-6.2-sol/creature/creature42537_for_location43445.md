There is a woman in the hollow who knows the wren by the way the fern springs back. A mouse lets the frond up by degrees. The wren lets it go all at once.

She sleeps at the far end, with her knees drawn up and one heel in a little depression she has worn in the grit. In the morning she fills the depression with the side of her foot. She does this even on days when nobody comes. Her blanket is the colour of the floor and has a hem made with three different kinds of thread. When she leaves, she rolls it tight and takes it with her. She has had things go mouldy here that she would have sworn were dry.

At low water she goes out to eat. There are mussels on the rocks below the bank, but she gets most of her food from the village, where she takes in mending and is thought to live with somebody else. She washes at an outside tap behind the old school. The woman who keeps the key to the school knows about this and leaves a nailbrush on the sill, bristles down. Neither of them has mentioned it.

The pale pebble is hers. It was in the pocket of a coat she was given to shorten, and by the time she found it she could no longer remember which coat. She keeps meaning to bring it back. Sometimes she wakes and can make it out before she can make out the entrance. On those mornings it takes her a little while to know whether her eyes are open.

She was small enough once to sit upright under the slab. Her father used to bring her here while he waited for the tide to uncover his pots. It was his lamp that hung on the peg. He used to put it out between the big waves so she could hear the sea better, which made no sense to her until she tried it herself. The first time she came back after he died, she brought a lamp of her own. It had a broad flat bottom and nowhere to put a string. She set it on the floor and spent the whole evening moving it out of her way.

She talks in here, but not often to him. Mostly she rehearses things she has to say in the village. She has learned to begin while the water is still drawing off the ledge. That gives her time for a short sentence. A longer one she must finish without hearing how it sounds.

Last winter the wren began bringing threads of moss in under the slab. She watched it try three places before choosing a pocket behind the fern, where the bank was dry on the underside. For several weeks she went in on her elbows so as not to brush the fronds. Then there was a morning when the bird stood on the middle kneeling stone with something green across its beak and would not move for her. She backed out onto the beach and waited. It was raining, and she had left her coat inside. She stood a little way down the bank with her hands in her armpits, looking out to sea so the bird could get on with what it was doing.
