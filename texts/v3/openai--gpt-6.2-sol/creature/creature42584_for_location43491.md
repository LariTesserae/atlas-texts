One animal lives in the middle of the crossing. It is about as long as a foot of one of the young, and for much of the day it wears a foot that a young animal has shed. The skin slips over its blunt end like a sleeve. When the current turns it round, the empty toes spread. From either wall it looks like somebody trying to get a purchase.

At first the sleeve is all you can see. Then a little dark line begins to travel inside it. This is the animal’s mouth. It goes up one toe and down the next, eating the skin from within. By the time it reaches the heel it has usually found something else to get into: a joint, the clear half of a face, sometimes a whole cast body. In a whole body it eats the feet last. Until then the feet are useful. They catch on the feet of the living and carry it a little way through the traffic, though never all the way to stone.

It has no feet of its own. It has two soft hooks, one at either end, and can draw either hook in so completely that there is no mark where it went. When it feeds, it keeps one hook out. The young are continually bringing it things this way. They drag a strip of skin behind them until it catches, pull without turning to look, and go on lighter. If the strip is still attached, the animal lets go. It is particular about this. Once it fastened on a loose flap that turned out to belong to a swimmer and was carried deep into the white commotion on the far side. It came back wearing nothing. For two joinings afterward it took only what brushed against its mouth.

The animal does not lie still when the water parts. That is when it leaves its skin and goes about bare. There is room to pass between the overturned young without touching them. In the dark it moves by putting both hooks out and drawing first one in, then the other. The hooks find purchase. On what, there is no seeing from either wall.

It keeps its eggs in an old jaw. The jaw is small, though the animal that left it must have been large for the crossing; when open, it could take the head of one of the young. Three teeth remain. The eggs fit around their roots, where even at the thinnest of the water they stay wet. The parent has worn a shallow notch in the jaw with its hook. It seldom misses the notch now. Earlier in the day, before it had worn it deep enough, it would go round twice, feeling among the teeth for a place it knew.

Sometimes a black head comes through while the parent is there. The parent draws in both hooks. There is very little to it then. The head can nose right against it and find only the old jaw. Once a head took the jaw in its mouth. It held it for a long time, trying first one angle and then another. One of the eggs came loose. The parent let it go past and did not put out a hook until the jaw was set down. There were two eggs left. It tucked them both behind the same tooth.

As the light withdraws from the blade, one of those eggs opens. What comes out is smaller than the tool with which the parent keeps it from drifting. It has a mouth already. The parent brings it the foot it has been wearing all afternoon, now thin enough to fold flat in the water. The little one gets in at the heel. It goes too far and lodges crosswise in a toe.

The parent waits, holding the heel open. The toe swells, straightens, and swells again. Then the little one backs out and tries the next toe. By the time the bridge parts, it has eaten a hole big enough to put its own hook through.
