The woman with the covered bowl is taking supper to her brother, who has put his back out lifting a door. He can walk if he has something in both hands, but if he has one hand free he puts it to his back and stops. His daughter has discovered this and sends him from room to room with a cup in each hand. He says she will make a good foreman. She would rather have the baker’s stool and be allowed to sit on it when the baker is there.

The baker is a small woman who cannot see well in the street at noon. In her own rooms she can tell whose loaf she is lifting by the cut in the crust. People bring her dough in the morning, with a cloth over it, and come back for it when they smell the first batch. She keeps a little flour on a shelf above the wood. Mice have reached that shelf, though no one has seen one get there. Her youngest takes a pinch from the spilled flour and lays a trail to the edge, in the hope of seeing the mouse come out. By morning there are prints, but they belong to his own fingers, which he rested there in the evening while he waited.

Several of the people cutting the town have lived all their lives within sight of the hill. Others came for the work and sleep in rooms they have been told they can have until the owners need them. They are the ones who know which of the new floors dries first. A man who cuts stairs has been carrying his rolled mat eastward for two years. He likes to be alone until breakfast. Each time a household moves in beside him, he stays long enough to help them set the door, then takes his mat farther along. He has a daughter at the western end who would give him a room if he asked. When he visits, he takes her an armful of chips for scouring pots, and she takes the best ones and puts the rest out after he leaves.

He is not the man who waits with his hand against the wedges. That man has a wife and two sons and likes to be within earshot of all three. The younger boy is learning to bring him tools. He can name every tool in the basket but has trouble giving one up now that he knows what it does. Sometimes his father holds out a hand without looking, and the boy, who has been permitted to keep the hammer until it is wanted, lays the handle in his palm so slowly that the man has time to turn his head and see what it costs him.

People who lived on the slope before the cutting began still come down to borrow things. One old woman brings eggs and stays to comb her granddaughter’s hair in the big court. She takes off her shoes before she goes into the girl’s house. Her granddaughter has told her she needn’t, but the old woman can remember what was growing directly over the room, and to her it is still a marvel that there is anywhere clean to put a bare foot. She will not sleep there. She has hens to shut in, she says, and this is true.

At the drain shared by three households, the middle household is a widower and his grown son. The son does the sweeping before he goes up to the fields; the father takes out the little stone afterward, so that if water comes it can pass. The neighbors think the son forgets to put it back. The son knows what happens and says nothing. Once, after two days of rain, the widower took out all three stones. There was a wad of onion skin and hair caught just below the last one, and when he worked it free, the water ran all the way to the street. He left the wad on the rim where everyone could see it. By evening someone had swept it away, and the three stones were back in place.

In the afternoons one of the plasterers brings her baby down to the settling basin. She sets him in a basket in the shade while she works. He has just learned to sit and can keep it up for a long time if nobody looks at him. The older children bring him things to hold. A flat chip is too sharp. A twist of rope goes straight into his mouth. What he likes best is a little wooden door peg, worn smooth in somebody else’s house before it came here. His mother feels for it in the bottom of the basket when she picks him up. More than once she has carried him halfway home and gone back, thinking she has left it, to find him holding it under his thigh.

At the eastern end a family comes after supper to look at its house. The smaller rooms are still joined to one another by stone. They can stand in the yard now, and the father shows the children where each doorway will be. One of the children has asked for a shelf low enough to reach from her bed. Her mother has drawn it for her, below the other shelves, and rubbed it out twice while deciding where the bed can go. Tonight the child brings a cup to see how it will fit. She holds it up to the chalk line and takes her hand away before she thinks. Her brother catches it against the wall. For a moment they both leave their hands there, his over hers, holding the cup in the place where it will stand.
