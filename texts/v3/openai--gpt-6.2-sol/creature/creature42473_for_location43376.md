There are mouths on the undersides of the warmest leaves. They are no bigger than the grains of mineral that rise through the forest, and as white. You can tell them from the grains when a leaf moves. The grains go on rising; the mouths hold fast by their lips. Each has a little gut, green at the end nearest the mouth and clear at the other. At the change of season the guts empty. The animals let go of the old leaves and hang in the cold water until the new leaves come up beneath them. An animal that lands on the upper side of a leaf will spend the better part of the season getting to the edge.

The jointed things eat them. This is hard to see even if you have one in front of you. Its mouth is deep between the roots of its forelegs, and it feeds by standing over a branch and drawing the leaves, one by one, against its breast. It takes so little from a grove that the same branch may serve it for years. There is usually a leaf it will not touch. Beneath that leaf another of its kind is growing, folded so neatly along the rib that the two halves of its body look like the two halves of the leaf. The adult begins leaving food there before the young has a working mouth. You can find the white grains ranged in the shelter of the rib, some of them still opening and closing.

A freshwater front is a bad time to have a young one. The adult can feel the water changing and works its way down among the roots. The young cannot unfold yet. If the hanging grove stays, there will be a young one in each grove, under the same leaf. For a while the adult goes from one to the other. It climbs down a trunk, walks across the silt and climbs up, then, where there is no more trunk, lets go and rows clumsily across the gap. It is a good walker and an indifferent swimmer. On the far side it sometimes catches the wrong branch and searches it, leaf by leaf, all the way to the tip.

Eventually one of the young unfolds first. This can happen in either grove. The adult follows it about for several days, steadying the branches while it learns to put its weight down. By the time the other one unfolds, it has to manage alone. There are more of these late young in the forest than any other kind. You can sometimes recognize one long afterward by the bend in a foreleg where it took its first fall. The bend does not keep it from feeding or from having young of its own. It makes the leaves click when it feeds.

The upright fans in the footprints are not food. For most of their lives they are not upright, either. They lie under the skin of the silt with their green surfaces pressed together. When a foot comes down, the warmth and pressure open them. The walker feels this through the sole: not the opening itself, but the loss of resistance just as it is lifting the foot. On ground where the fans grow thickly it takes very short steps. The bare stretches are mostly places where something stood still for a long time. In the middle of one there is an old animal with a grove growing through the joints of its back. It still feeds. Its branches come to it now.

The low walls belong to something smaller. At dusk, if you wait by the middle enclosure, you can see the hollow empty from the edges in. Near the end a little brown head appears at the center. It has a wide mouth and two dark eyes set so far apart that at first they seem to belong to different creatures. It swallows until the last of the water is gone, then comes out on six soft legs. Two more follow. They are the length of a finger, though the worn places in the walls are much broader than that.

They go to the outer enclosures and nose among the stones. At one loose corner they work side by side, their hind feet braced in the moss. One puts its head under the stone while the others push. They shift it scarcely the thickness of a leaf. Then they spend most of the night bringing grains of silt and pressing them into the gap. They do this even where the wall needs no mending. There is a short stretch on the inland side that they have taken down and put back so often that all its stones are round. In the morning they go back into the hollow. The smallest goes first and the largest backs in after it, drawing the silt smooth with its chin.

Farther inland, on the shoulder that is moving uphill, there are two more walls. They stand well apart, with enough room between them for a third. Their stones fit closely, but have sharp edges. Small brown animals work there too. For now they are taking the stones out one at a time and carrying them to the upstream end of the shoulder, where the rock is coming together. They set each stone down in the current and keep a foot on it until it holds. This can take all night. Once, with only the top of its head still free, one of them pulled its foot out and went back for a smaller stone.
