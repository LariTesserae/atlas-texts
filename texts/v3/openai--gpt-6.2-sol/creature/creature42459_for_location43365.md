A woman named Esta lives in the weighing hall. She has put her bed on the scale, not to keep it above the water—the platform is only an inch higher than the floor—but because the scale tells her when the cellar is filling. The iron underneath swells as it warms, and the beam lifts just enough to tap its stop. On hearing it she rolls over and takes her shoes off the floor.

There are eleven people in her district. There would be twelve if the man at the far fountain would consent to be counted there, but he sleeps on the other side of the reservoir and says that coming over twice a day for a drink is not the same as living somewhere. He brings Esta the newspaper when there is one. It is printed down below and arrives folded around the incline’s oil cans. She reads the columns in the order in which they dry.

The children fish at the submerged stairs with no hooks on their lines. A bare thread will sometimes come back with a little clear weight at the end of it, like a drop that has learned to hold on. In a saucer the weight opens into a fan of legs. Left alone it walks to the side nearest the reservoir, even in a room from which the reservoir cannot be seen. It cannot climb porcelain. The children have discovered that a saucer with a chip in it is therefore more interesting than a sound one: you can turn it a little at a time and see how far the animal will go in the wrong direction before it stops.

The large thing in the reservoir is not, as the children believe, their mother. The clear animals breed in the overflow channels, among the little ridges left by the masons’ thumbs. The large one lies all day against the reservoir wall with its mouth in a feeder pipe. When the black crust starts to loosen, it takes hold of the shelf from underneath and backs out with it. Most of what it eats is on the underside. Sometimes the shelf breaks before it has got a good grip. Then it goes on backing for a surprising distance, as though reluctant to admit, even to itself, that its mouth is empty.

On the far side of the water, a boy sleeps in the old bridge inspector’s house. The court outside his room holds the reflection of a roof that was taken down before he was born. You can see where a rope has worn a groove in one rafter. His grandmother used to hang washing from that rope. She told him this once, and he asked her to hang something there again. It took her a while to understand what he wanted. Afterward she brought out a shirt with a collar too stiff for anybody now living and hung it on the rail above the pool. The shirt showed in the water, hanging below the reflected rafter. He was satisfied. His grandmother stood looking at it long after he went in.

A good many rooms are kept dry for people who no longer live in them. This is less work than it sounds. Twice a year you carry a chair upstairs, open the glazed cupboard, and wipe the inside of the door. If you find a little animal in there you put it out on the landing. The difficult part is knowing when to stop. In the house next to the inspector’s, somebody kept one cupboard so faithfully that the wall went down around it. The cupboard floated as far as the corner stones, where it stuck fast with its glass doors uppermost. All the way down the avenue you could hear the cups knocking together inside.

At the foundry, Esta leaves a lamp on while she goes for water. The distance between the bed and the nearer fountain is enough that she loses sight of the lamp twice: once at the pits and once at the short turn into the tiled passage. In the rainy months she often meets somebody at that turn. People come in by the receiving yard without seeing her light, and she hears them stop at the fountain and set down whatever they have brought to fill. A jug makes one sound, a kettle another. There is a man who brings two drinking glasses, one inside the other. She used to think he meant to fill both. Then one evening he took them apart and gave her the dry one to hold while he drank. He lives a long way off, in the district where the landings have no lamps. He brings the second glass so he can set the first down in the dark without getting grit on its rim.
