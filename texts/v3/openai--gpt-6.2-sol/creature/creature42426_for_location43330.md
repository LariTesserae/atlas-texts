The little clack is usually the first part of one you notice. You can be looking straight at the shelf as it goes down and still turn at the sound. It comes from behind you, or from under the slab you are standing on. If you put a hand down in time, you can sometimes feel a second click too faint to hear. Then the warmth changes places under your palm.

Most of the movements in the valley are of no use to the things that live there. A wall can come up a hundred feet without disturbing one. What matters is a place where several pieces of stone, all cooling at different rates, bear on the same piece. Given a little play, one will shift and take the weight off another. The freed stone lifts, hardly at all. Somewhere beyond it a third stone takes the load. Ordinarily there’s an end to it: the weight comes to rest and the rock cools where it lies. But in a few places the shift comes back to where it started, and by then there is warmth enough to make the first stone move again.

That is a small one. It might go round twice in an evening. A grown one has several rounds going at once, not all of them in the same stone. You can lose half of it into a cut and find the rest still going about its business a year later. It will have changed its pace. There will be a long wait where it used to pass quickly, while the weight that once went straight across the missing shelf makes its way around the end.

They do not have to wait for the valley to provide them with new joints. They work at a tight seam by loading one end of it and then the other. For a long while there is nothing to see. Then a plate a few inches wide parts company with the floor. It fits so well that even when loose it looks like part of the floor, but it can now rock between two corners. A creature can spend a great deal of its warmth getting one such plate free. Sometimes the plate turns out to be useless. It lies there and is never touched again.

The groups of four stones are where the young are easiest to find. The uprights have been brought onto their edges a little at a time. The flat stone in the middle is not quite touching any of them. At first, when the parent shifts its weight below, all three uprights make the same small movement toward the middle and back. Later one goes in while the others go out. There comes a time when you can lay two fingers on the flat stone and feel it answer before anything underneath has moved.

If you lift it then, almost nothing happens. The stone is warm on one side, and there is no mark on its bed. But when you put it back, even exactly as it was, the three uprights no longer move about it. Much later the parent may loosen a different plate and begin again. It seldom uses the same place.

At the arrangement by the cut, the one remaining upright still leans inward. There is nothing now for it to lean toward. The middle stone went down with the other two. Yet on some evenings the upright draws itself back a little, to a position no mere settling would take it to. The young one was old enough to have found a route underneath. It is still small enough to spend part of what it has keeping that one stone in the right place.

When a new reach opens, the first warmth goes out of it too fast for anything to live there. Shelves rise, tip, and fall; the grains make their fans and slip away. Afterward, far in from the seam, there may be a clack. Another comes hours later, farther in still. The sound is easier to follow than the creature, and following it gives the wrong idea. The clack is the stone it has just finished with. Ahead of it, in the dark, a piece of the new floor that has never moved by itself is beginning to take weight.
