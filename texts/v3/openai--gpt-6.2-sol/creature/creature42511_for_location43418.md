Under the oldest house there is a bed of willow leaves. A fox has slept there, but the leaves were brought in by something smaller. They have been laid one at a time with the pale sides up, even in the back corner where there isn’t room to turn a hand over. If you lift one, there is usually a live beetle underneath. The beetles are cold and slow. Some of them have been there since before the house was built.

The animal that puts them there is about as long as a stoat and much wider. It has short gray fur that the water goes through without wetting the skin. On gravel it goes badly, with its belly swinging from side to side and its little forefeet feeling for an edge. On a fresh bank it can run straight into the silt. It parts the mud with its head and brings the two sides together behind it with a sweep of its hind feet. A heron will watch the line this makes until it runs under a root. Herons take the young ones, which have not yet learned to close the mud.

There are several beds under the house. Only the newest is up among the clay feet. The others are down in the bank, at the heights where there used to be dry ground. When a channel cuts through them you can see the leaves in section, a thin white seam with a little swelling at the end. Some of those seams are in silt that has not been dry in a hundred years. The leaves at the bottom are from trees that do not grow in the basin now.

In a hard rain the animal lies under the floor with its chin on a clay foot. There is a place on the foot that it has worn smooth. It stays there through the ordinary shaking of the house. When a load comes through, it gets up a little before the willows dip. Sometimes it goes down into one of its old beds. Sometimes it brings the beetles up. It carries them in its cheeks and sets them out again, each under a leaf of its own. A big beetle will push the leaf up and walk off. It does not go after those. The ones it wants are small enough to remain where they are put.

This morning it has three young in a pocket on the channel side of the house. As the channel opens, water reaches the pocket from below. The young come up through the leaves with their mouths open. The mother takes the first two uphill to the clay foot. By the time she comes back for the third, the whole pocket has gone. She works her way along the cut, putting her head in at the exposed roots, then goes into the water.

The new bank is still soft enough to take the full print of an otter. She climbs out in one of the prints and stands there with the third one in her mouth. The shortest way back to the house is already too wide for her to swim with the young one held clear. She goes instead to the upstream end of the bank, where a willow branch is taking the force of the current. There is a little heap of leaf litter against it. She gets the young one under the leaves and lies over it. By daylight there will be wagtails within a foot of her, picking through the litter that the water is still bringing in. If the branch holds, she’ll have a burrow by noon.
