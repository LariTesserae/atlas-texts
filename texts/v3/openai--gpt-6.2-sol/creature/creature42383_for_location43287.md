There are eels below the municipal stair. The bridge keeper has never seen one. He knows them by what they leave in the grease on the lower hinge: a print like the mark a wet bootlace makes, with a little fan at one end where the tail went smartly round. In summer he finds the same mark higher up. He puts less grease on in summer.

Where the water spends the winter is less certain. There is a laundress in the second settlement who sometimes draws a basinful that smells of bruised mint, though there is no mint in her district. She says it comes from under the bridge. The water men say it comes from her washer. They replaced the washer in March and for three weeks her water smelled of rubber. Then it smelled of mint again.

The laundress has a son who lives across the gap on Foundry Street. He works nights at the mill and sleeps in the front room, which is over one of the shuttered shops. The shop used to sell shoes. Its owner still comes up on the lift twice a year to check the stock for mildew. Nothing in the shop is the size it says on the box anymore, but there is a low stool with a sloping footrest that the mill worker borrows to sit on when he takes off his boots.

While he sleeps, the starlings collect on the bus shelter. It has a warmer roof than the neighboring roofs, although the light inside has been out since before he moved in. A few birds have learned the sound of the lift brake and go down to the loading bay ahead of the goats. They pick the little white grubs out of the goats’ heels. The goats appear to dislike this, and stamp and swing their heads, but they come down slowly enough to let the birds finish.

Farther up, in the cables left loose for streets that have not been made, there are mice with unusually long nest runs. A cable crew once opened a junction box in one district and found a nest lined with blue wool from the seats of a tram that ran in another. The two districts were four miles apart by rail that winter. This was not considered remarkable until a conductor found the mice coming out of a seat cushion while the tram was in motion. He could keep them out by sitting in that seat himself, but only until he got up to take a fare.

Some of the city’s oldest inhabitants are the rowans in the culvert woods. A given tree may be no thicker than a wrist, but where a culvert has been mended and a wood has died back, the same rowan will often come up at the next leak. A woman on the drainage crew recognizes one by the strip of green china it carries in a fork. She first saw it when she was an apprentice, in a tree growing beside the powerhouse. The tree is now uphill of the apartment block that gained its view. She takes her lunch there on fine days. From under the branches she can see straight into her old kitchen, where someone has painted over the cupboard doors without taking the handles off. She knows this because one handle used to be brass and now the thumb of whoever lives there has worn it brass again.

At the foot of the unfinished stair lives the bridge keeper, in a house that was built with a second door for the towpath. The door opens onto moss. As the children get older they venture a little farther along the bank, but he does not worry about them falling in. He worries about their leaving things on it. A lunch tin put down on the moss can be found dry and closed on the bridge a week later, with its sandwich gone and a little heap of gravel in the wax paper. Last year one of the children waited on the bridge for her tin and heard its loose handle knocking for some time before it arrived. She said there was a pause between knocks, as if somebody carrying it had stopped to change hands.

The keeper used to have a dog that would go down to the towpath door at a quarter to six every evening. There was nothing on the other side but the bank and the sound underneath it. The dog was not asking to go out; when he opened the door she stayed on the mat, wagging. She had belonged to the previous keeper, and for a long time he supposed that this was when the woman used to come home.

He has been alone in the house for nine years now. Last Thursday, while he was oiling the gates, someone below him whistled twice. He put his oilcan down and leaned over. There was no break in the moss, but beneath the sound of the water he could hear a dog coming up a flight of steps. It was in no hurry. Every so often it stopped, the way an old dog does, to get both its hind feet onto the same tread.
