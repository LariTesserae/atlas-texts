The long gray animals have no lower front teeth. They take a blade of grass between the upper teeth and a thick pad in the lip, then pull sideways. A careless one can bring down a whole shelf’s water this way. The young do it often. For the rest of the afternoon you can pick them out by the dark stripe down the middle of the face.

They are born with soft feet. In the first days a calf can stand on stone too sharp for its mother, because the foot spreads around the edge instead of resting on it. Heat is another matter. The mother tests a place with the skin just above her hoof, where the hair is thin. If it won’t do, she presses her nose into the calf’s flank. After a while she need only turn her head. A calf deep in sleep will get up at that small movement, sometimes with a blade of grass still hanging from its mouth.

In the warm country below the northern cliffs there are birds no bigger than a calf’s ear. They look blue in the dust and brown as soon as they bathe in water. The dust gets into everything. A bird that has been sitting on eggs leaves six or seven little blue bars on them, the marks of the feathers parted along her belly. Both parents sit. When one comes back to relieve the other it brings something to eat, usually a moth with the wings already taken off. The bird on the eggs eats without getting up. Sometimes the other has to walk all the way around the nest to find the end with the beak.

They build on bare stone, but not in the open. The distinction is hard to see until you have watched a pair reject one site after another. One will crouch and the other will walk away, looking back every few steps. If the crouching bird can still be seen from twenty paces, they start over. There may be nothing between the two birds but a flat stretch of rock. At the site they finally choose, you can look straight at the sitting bird from above and see every feather, yet from twenty paces there is only the darkness beneath a low branch.

Sometimes the tree comes while the eggs are still whole. Where the nest was plainly visible yesterday, there is a trunk with roots running down either side of it. The parent coming in with a moth has to learn a new way to the nest. Sometimes the tree never comes. The chicks grow up in its shade all the same. On a windless day the moving leaves pass over their open mouths.

The herds eat the fallen flowers of these trees. Around the oldest trunks there will be a wide scatter of hoof marks, and elsewhere the same scatter around nothing. The calves find the flowers by following the adults’ lips. They nose over the stone long after the adults have moved on. There is usually one flower left, mashed into a fissure too narrow for a broad mouth. For a little while, while its muzzle is still small, a calf can eat better than its mother.

There are stretches of the year when the herds go for days without meeting another herd. They can smell where one has slept: the stone keeps the sour warmth of their bodies into the next evening. A young animal will sometimes lie down in one of those places and have to be fetched. At a crossing, though, a herd may wait half a day for animals it cannot see. They stand well back from the edge, chewing. Now and then one makes the low sound ordinarily used to call a calf out of a fern bed. An answer may come from across the valley, so faint that only the animals nearest the edge stop chewing.

Once, on a ledge with no apparent way down, an old female stood apart from the herd. She had lost the hair over both knees, and when she bent to graze she put one forefoot far out ahead of the other. The herd went on. A calf went with them as far as the bend, then came back and stood beside her. She nosed it away twice. The third time she let it stay.

Below them, across a great reach of air, the shadow of a shelf slid down the wall of the valley. The calf watched it. The old female was looking at the stone beside her foot. A dark spot appeared there, then another. Before there was anything overhead to drip, she had raised her face, and the calf, watching her now, raised its face too.
