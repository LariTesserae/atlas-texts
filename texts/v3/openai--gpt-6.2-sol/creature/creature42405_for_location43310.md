The smallest goats sleep standing on the warm gutters. They put one foot in front of the other like birds on a branch and let the water go round their ankles. In the morning their mothers have to butt them off. A kid will come down three roofs in a series of little knocks, then walk into a kitchen and stand by the fire as if it had been sent there on an errand.

Most households have a goat they will admit to and two or three that depend on who is asking. A woman named Sela owns a black one with a split ear, though her brother’s children milk it, and it sleeps on the roof of a man who has been trying to sell it for years. He has had offers. When someone actually comes to fetch it, he charges them for the slate it will take with it. The goat favors a spot over his bed where the roof is thin enough for each of them to hear the other turn in the night.

The children learn the town from the knees down. They know which steps will take a whole foot and which will take only the outside edge. A toddler is given a spoon to hold on the stairs: if she puts out both hands for balance, the spoon clatters down and somebody comes to get her. At six or seven she can carry a bowl of soup up to a neighbor without losing much of it. She can also tell, by holding her cheek to a door, whether the room behind it is empty or just very cold. The distinction matters if she has brought soup.

Sela mends bowls. She works with the bowl upside down in her lap and a fold of wet wool between the clay and her knees. She has good light for this when the snow is bright across the ravine. People bring her bowls wrapped in their shirts, sometimes with a last spoonful of food still in one of the pieces. She scrapes that into a saucer before she starts. There is a bowl in her own kitchen with so much copper in it that the birds won’t come near it. She uses it to set bread to rise. On baking days there are small beak marks in the flour all around it, right up to the edge.

The wall birds aren’t all alike. The ones below the cistern have blunt bills from prying at the lime around the stones. Down by the covered drains their bills are finer, and adults in the middle of feeding a brood will wait on a workman’s shoulder for him to lift a slab. The red things they pick off the fish curl up at once in dry air. A bird bringing one home holds it crosswise, with both ends sticking out. You can see the young reaching for it in the dark behind the stone, their mouths wide enough to make it seem impossible that so many of them fit in there.

There are other creatures in the drains which the birds never see. One is a translucent snail that lives where the warm water first meets the cold. It grazes a clean line down the underside of the stone. Another is a little jointed animal that lodges in the foot hollows when the current takes the stairs. By evening it will have roofed itself over in grit. Children collect these roofs in a cracked cup and wait to see what comes out. Sometimes nothing does. Sometimes the roofs move uphill overnight and are found stuck to the underside of the saucer somebody put over the cup.

Old Iven, who used to clear the drains, can no longer get down to them. He lives one flight below the meeting house with his daughter and her two boys. In wet weather people bring him a dipper of water from a troublesome gutter. He lets it settle on the table and looks at what comes to the bottom. He can recognize the yellow sand under the east stair, the flakes of bone from a worn lintel, the black silt that means water has been standing behind a wall. Once the younger boy brought him a dipper with a fish in it. Iven asked where he had got it. The boy pointed to the ceiling. They moved Iven’s bed, and that night the ceiling came down in one piece, with the fish’s twin still wriggling in a crease of the plaster.

Not everybody was born here. There is a man from below who still leaves his shoes outside to dry. His wife brings them in without mentioning it, unless they have frozen to the step, in which case she leaves him to find out. He brought up three lengths of good wood for a table when they married. One went to a woman whose door gave way in winter, and a second was cut into splints when a boy and his goat came down together. The third is still under their bed. His wife has caught him taking it out to smell it.

In the cellar where the rain fell, the fish have grown too broad to get back through the opening they came by. A girl who goes down for flour knows them by the shape of the white patches behind their eyes. There were seven; now there are six, and she has searched the adjoining rooms for the missing one. The adults tell her a fish can get through a hole smaller than its head if the stone is slick enough. None of them have seen this happen. She leaves a pinch of flour on the top step and watches it travel the length of the cellar, under the bins and across the dark water to the back wall. Most days it goes down there. Some days it stops, spreads thinly, and comes back toward her.

The roof the pool showed that morning had a chipped corner and a pale bone laid across it to keep a slate in place. Sela’s mother knew it as soon as she saw it. It was the roof of the room where she had slept as a girl, before the lane was built up over it. She hadn’t thought of the bone in fifty years. She had put it there herself, after climbing out one night to get away from her sister, who ground her teeth in her sleep. She told this part of the story at supper, with her sister sitting right beside her. Her sister said she had known perfectly well where she went. On hot nights she’d waited until she was out on the roof and then closed the shutter, so she could have the whole bed to herself.
