The people of the upland sleep with something of their own hanging below them. A comb on a string will do, or a cup in a sling. In the night, if the floor begins to change its pitch, the cup knocks. A person can sleep through a great deal of weather but will wake to the sound of her own comb touching a wall it has never touched before.

They live mostly under the walks. On top there is too much to do for anybody to have a house there. The rushes have to be combed back from the treads; the water has to be let out of its little pockets before it gets heavy enough to make a pocket of its own. Underneath, the ribs of a walk spread apart, and there is room between them for a bed, a shelf, and sometimes a child who will not come out. You can tell which households have babies by the bottoms of their floors. They have a second, very close weave to catch what is dropped. After the child has learned to set a spoon down, the parents cut away the close work a little at a time and discover things they had stopped looking for.

There are animals that take advantage of this. One, no longer than a thumb, with a blunt white face and a tail it can tuck under its belly, goes from house to house along the underside. It takes only things with food on them. People blame it for clean spoons too, to spare one another’s feelings. In the rain it climbs into the walls, where it combs its wet coat with both hind feet. A family can go all day without seeing it and know by the dry ticking behind their heads that it has come home.

At one of the older crossings lives a woman named Cada, who keeps three steps from joining. Two are ordinary work. The third is a broad, well-made stair whose rails, for reasons nobody has found, put on a handspan of new growth whenever the terrace below begins to flower. The terrace in flower smells of bruised pear. Sometimes the smell comes up the stair in the coldest part of the rains. Cada’s father used to go down as far as the last tread and eat his supper there with his feet in the summer air. Coming back up, he would find the little hairs on his shins wet with cold.

Cada has a boy, Iren, who is good on a smooth floor and bad at jumping. He has been bad at it since he was small enough to be passed across. Other children look at the place where they mean to land. Iren looks down, to see what there would be to catch him. Over the third gap there is a fine view of the terrace’s outer ribs, which are much too far away to be of any use.

In the flowering season a kind of little brown bird comes to the stair. It has a soft bill and cannot bore a hole, so it finds two living tips and brings them together with wet bast. The tips swell around the binding. By the time there are eggs, the fork that holds the nest is all one piece. This is how the bird gets a cradle that will not come apart in a squall. It is also why people at the crossings knock on their rails before they take a knife to them. If a bird flies out, they have to look.

One morning Iren knocked and got no answer. He found the binding anyway, on the underside where he had to lie with his cheek against the tread to see it. The two tips had met. Where they touched, a little pale root had already flattened itself against the opposite bark.

His mother was away getting a tooth pulled. Iren fetched her shell. He knew enough not to cut through either live tip: a cut there would throw out a whisk of shoots by evening, and some of them would be in reach of the other side. He picked at the binding until it slackened. The root let go with a noise he felt in his teeth.

A bird came back then, carrying a strip of bast so long that the free end trailed below the stair. It sat on the lower rail and waited for him to move. Under its feet the terrace was wet and green. Iren could see the pale undersides of the flower buds; none had opened. He had smelled them all through breakfast.

He went home for a forked shoot from the rush bed and tied it sideways to the lower rail. To do this he had to cross. He put the shell down first, because he did not want to land holding it. Then he put it in his pocket, because he would need it on the other side. He spent so long doing one and then the other that the bird laid its strip of bast on the rail and flew away.

What he told Cada afterward was that he had jumped. What she saw when she went to inspect the work was that he had thrown himself across on his belly and pulled his feet after him; there were two long wet marks in the green dust. The new fork was a little askew, but he had set it where both tips could thicken without reaching anything on the stair. She left it that way. In the evening the bird came back and began to bind it.

When there were young in the nest, the family could hear them from their beds underneath the walk. They had the thin, complaining voices of very small birds everywhere. During a rain they went quiet, and Iren would put out a hand to his cup to learn whether the floor was moving or whether the sound he heard was only the stair, shedding water into another season.
