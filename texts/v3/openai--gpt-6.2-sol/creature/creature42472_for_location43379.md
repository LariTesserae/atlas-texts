The birds are wider across the back than they look in flight. Coming in, they fold one wing an instant before the other and fall the last little distance sideways. A bird that has been away all day does not have to find its own hollow. It puts a foot on the back of a neighbor, and the neighbor leans until the foot meets stone. In this way a row can receive a bird without any of the others getting up. The young try to come in headfirst. For a week or two after they learn to fly, there is always one hanging by its feet below the row, calling as though it has been put there.

Some of the birds spend the daylight hours down among the alders, turning leaves with their bills. Others go all the way to the sheep fields. The fur they bring back is mostly wool pulled off fences, though once in a while a bird comes in with a whole mouse’s nest in its bill and loses half of it on the way down. The wool catches on the walls. The soft animals at the bottom collect in the drip beneath it, where water keeps coming for a little while after it has stopped everywhere else. They are no bigger than a grain of barley and have dark guts that show through their sides. When two meet, the one with the wetter back generally goes on walking.

The pale runners under the birds are several different kinds. The ones that move the feathers are flat enough to pass between a quill and the stone it lies on. They eat the soft material left inside the quill. A feather going uphill is usually turning, very slowly, as one runner after another works its way in through the broken end. There are also long-legged ones that eat the flat ones. Each stands with three or four feet on different flakes, and if something moves below it, it lifts the foot nearest the movement. You can watch a whole animal cross a patch of floor without seeing anything but those feet go up and down.

The dark animals at the cup have ears so small that the fur grows over them. Their young have bare ears and look, for the first few days out from under the flakes, like a different sort of animal altogether. They follow the adults to water by putting their noses where the adults have put their hind feet. The place where each adult stops is slightly different. There are several paths across a span you could cover with your hand.

The broad chip is good for lifting ice that has frozen clear across. The pointed one is for getting between the ice and the side of the cup. An animal trying the wrong chip will push for quite a while before it lets go. Sometimes a young one follows too closely and puts both forefeet on the ice while an adult is working. The adult waits. It may wait so long that the young one lies down, and then it has to nose it off. In summer they leave the chips alone. The young lie on the smooth patch with their bellies against it, close enough to the cup that an adult coming to drink has to step over them.

The three-note cry belongs to the birds, but not all the sounds that answer it do. There is a little dry click made by one of the moths when it opens its wings. In the lower cracks it comes just where the last note would come, often enough that a young bird will sometimes answer it. The moth goes on opening and closing its wings. The bird, which cannot see what it is calling to, works its way farther along the row until an older bird puts a bill across the back of its neck.

Once the cold has gone far enough into the mountain, there are fewer birds, though it is hard to say which have left until the light comes down. The empty hollows are not empty for long. A moth gets into one, then another hangs from it; the runners come through to pick at the scales they shed. At the cup, an old animal that used to drink from the near edge begins coming around the far side, where the ice is thinner. It carries the pointed chip with it. For several mornings the young still search the dry patch for that chip, going over the place with their forefeet, while the old animal drinks a little beyond them in the dark.
