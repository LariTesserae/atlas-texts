On the back of the number, where a printer’s burr curls away from the plate, there is an animal with seven feet. There ought to be eight. In the socket of the missing one a new foot is growing, folded twice, already tipped with the two black hooks it will use to hold on.

She is a kind of shrimp, shorter than a midge’s wing and so flat that she can turn around under the burr without breaking the surface of the water. The brown chevrons down her back are her gut showing through. When she has fed on one of the green patches, the chevrons are green for most of a spill, beginning at the head. The very last one stays brown. Food seldom gets that far before she eats again.

She feels the pumps chiefly through her feet. On the plate she keeps them bent. On the ceramic she stands higher, with only the hooks touching, and there she does most of her feeding. Her mouth works sideways. She takes a strand of film by its loose end and walks backward as she eats it, rolling the strand into a little dark rope between her jaws. Now and then the rope proves to be somebody’s leg. The owner pulls at one end and she pulls at the other until one of them gives up.

There are nineteen of her kind in the ravine. Two live so far up the seam that their feet have never touched standing water. The smallest live among the printed stones, in the curved spaces left where three stones meet. The red larva can get its head into those spaces but cannot turn it. It has learned to lie with its chin on a stone and wait for a shrimp to come out backward, which is how a shrimp comes out when it has found something worth eating.

This one carries her eggs under her belly. Most are clear enough now to show a pair of eyes and, behind the eyes, a faint, persistent chewing. One egg has gone white. She spends part of every spill working it loose with her hindmost foot. She can reach it with the hook but cannot get a purchase without letting go of the burr. On the other side she could have used the foot that is growing back.

When the collector tests, she flattens herself against the plate. The sheet of water combs her feelers out straight. The white egg goes with it. So does a good egg, torn open on the burr, and for the length of the test the hatchling hangs by one hook from the outside of her egg pouch. Its legs have been ready for days. It puts them down in order, onto her belly, over the ridge of her back, and finally onto the plate. There it stops with its feet spread much too wide.

The water comes back up the ravine. Loose grit ticks against the plate, and a springtail comes down hard enough to go under, kicks free, and is gone. The hatchling walks in the wrong direction for shelter and reaches the shallow groove at the bottom of the number. A bead gathers there. Before it falls, the amber strip shines through it, and the little shrimp, which has been walking all its life inside an egg, goes on walking up the bright inside of the bead.
