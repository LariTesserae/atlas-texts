The gray seam animals have ears on their bellies. In the bright period they lie with all four feet tucked under them, listening to the plates from below. A sound plate gives back their breathing. One that has begun to part along a vein gives back a second breath, a little late. A mother with young will move them out of a plate like that even if there is good eating in it. She carries them one at a time, folded over her nose. They are nearly transparent at that age, except for the black pads of their feet.

Their nests are in the old thumb-worn corners. The depression that once took a thumb takes a litter of five. The mother lines it with hinge scrapings, which stay soft in the cold, and leaves the young there while she feeds. At the first turning of the shutters the young put their feet out of the nest. Not all of them put them out on the same side. A litter can divide at its first change of weather and live thereafter on avenues that never meet.

There is a white animal in the pipes, long enough to occupy several miles of verge. It is seldom seen whole. At a junction you may see a length of it go by, with small dark bodies lodged at intervals inside it. These are its young. Each has a hard rim that will eventually fit the bore of a pipe. For much of its life the animal goes with the pressure, feeding on whatever catches in its fringe. When it comes to a tear it travels against the pressure. The young nearest its mouth works free and seats itself in the damaged place. For a few days it can be seen through the wall, opening a hole in itself a little at a time. The breath comes through with a whistle until the hole is wide enough.

The factories admit these animals to their smaller pipes but keep them out of the main trunks. A trunk with a live patch in it never quite stops singing. This does no harm to hinges or blades. A runner grown to that sound, however, will turn toward it at a fork, even when the sound comes from the road it has just taken. In one district there are six runners of different ages going around the same low rise. The rosettes there have acquired deep notches from their passing teeth. A gray animal can feed very well on that circuit and never learn to cross a road.

Under the white arches live the broad-backed things that give off the smell of cut wood. They have no eyes on their upper surfaces. They hang beneath the leaves with their pale undersides toward the spent light, and where it falls evenly on them they grow a coat of thin brown scales. A scale takes years to loosen. When it does, the animal brings it to the edge of its back and holds it out until a passing leaf takes it. Most fall through. A few are pressed into the older courses and can be found much later as short stretches of brown grain running across the white veins.

These things move, though an arch may have to be unloaded before one can get its back past it. A small one makes the passage in the time it takes a shutter front to go over. An old one may spend an entire bright period with one side of its body on either side of a root. The pressure of the root keeps a part of its back bare. When it finally gets free, that strip is soft and sharp-smelling, and the seam animals come down to lick it. The old ones will wait for them. They have very little feeling left in the scaled parts of their bodies.

Something still walks upright in the oldest passageways. It is made of several growths that can live apart. At rest its feet spread into a low rosette, and the long parts of its legs lie down along the stair. When the light reaches a certain depth in the passage, the legs draw together and lift a narrow, folded weight. It takes a step. On some stairs the worn places are exactly right for it. On others it puts a foot down, raises it, and tries a little farther forward. The repeated attempt has polished the risers in places a foot would never ordinarily touch.

Nothing has been found inside the folded weight except a set of small leaves. They open on different sides as it climbs. On a stair that has kept its old exposure they open in order, from the bottom of the bundle to the top. Where a wall has moved, one may stay shut for the whole climb. At the landing the walker lowers the bundle to the paving and separates. Its feet take hold wherever they happen to have stopped. You can sometimes tell that a landing has been occupied for years by the two rosettes growing there, a step apart, with a little patch of shade between them that moves when neither of them does.
