The dark-backed grazers are called shelf deer, although an old one standing in the open is scarcely higher than the grass it feeds in. What looks like a slick coat from the ridge is two coats. The long outer hairs lie flat and come to points over the ribs. Beneath them is a short, smoke-colored wool that is dry even on an animal that has been out all night. When a doe gets up from her bed, the grass springs back wet around her and stays dry for a little while where her belly was.

They eat the grass from below. A doe puts her nose in at the base of a tussock and works it up into the pale new leaves, leaving the tall blades combed apart. The openings close over slowly. In the morning you can follow where she fed by the vertical streaks of drier green. There may be twenty of them in a patch no bigger than a blanket, and a little way beyond, a single round hole where she put a foot through the turf and had to pull it out.

The fawns have hair that stands out all over. On the first wet morning it collects so much water that an animal no bigger than a hare can leave a shining track through the grass. This matters to the hunting birds. A doe with a new fawn feeds in short stretches, nosing it ahead of her under the grass, until she finds a place where stone overhangs the turf. She pushes it in with the flat of her nose. Once it has learned to go in, it will do so at the touch of her nose even where there is no overhang, flattening itself into the grass with its hind legs stuck out behind it.

Under one of the tipped slabs there is room for a fawn only if it puts its head sideways. The roof is white with the growth that lives out of the light. On the lower side, beyond the fawn’s feet, a beetle has opened a ragged patch in the white and is feeding at the edge of it. The doe comes back twice while the stone is still warm. She stands outside and lets the fawn suck with its hindquarters under the slab. When she goes off again, it folds down in almost exactly the position she left it in. By the third visit the beetle has worked its way to the other side of the patch.

The deer from the orange marsh have stained feet. In a dry week they cross the low saddle into the next drainage, and for a day or two you can see the color of the marsh in their footprints. They find water there by pulling up the little mats of moss that plug the joints in the rock. They take hold at one end and draw back until the whole strip comes free. The deer that live on that side do not pull up moss. They go down to a pool beneath a tilted shelf, stand belly-deep, and drink. The visiting deer will walk right past them and spend half the afternoon opening cracks.

At the bank of ferns there is an old doe with a split ear whose fawn is nearly full grown. They often feed with one on either side of the bank. The movement of the fronds does not interrupt them. Late in the season, when the water has gone down and the chamber takes longer to fill, a small bird begins coming to pick over the insects on the undersides. It waits on a stem at the edge. Each time the air comes through, it flies in under the lifting fronds, takes what it can, and comes out low.

One evening the young deer walks through the bank instead of going around. The bird flies in at once, too early, and comes out almost between its feet. It stands looking down at the place where the bird went. Its mother has already gone into the cloud. Then the ferns lift of themselves, and the young deer puts its nose among them, far enough in that when they settle, two of the fronds lie across the back of its neck.
