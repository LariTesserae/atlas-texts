Just inside that opening lives an animal the shrimp will not stand in front of. They keep their row on either side of it, facing the empty water, and leave a space about as wide as a hand. There is nothing in the space to see. Put a finger down, though, and something takes hold of the nail, very gently, in three places. It lets go before you pull away.

The animal is called a hem. A young one could sit in the bowl of a spoon. An old one may be twelve feet long and is seldom thicker than your wrist. It lies in the crease where two roots have come together without quite joining. What takes hold of your finger is one of its feet. There are hundreds of them, in pairs, and they do most of the animal's tasting. Its mouth is at the end farthest inside. Food gets there by being passed from foot to foot. If a shrimp strays into the space in the row, for a while you can follow its progress by the little knocks its tail makes against the wood.

The hems eat very little. A more considerable part of their lives is spent moving out of the way of the trees. The wood closes on them so slowly that a foot can stay in one place for years and still have time to let go. An old hem may have its front half in a joint that opened before any of the present leaves grew, and its back half in a joint just opening now. Between them there is sometimes a place no thicker than a page. Along that part of the animal the feet have been drawn in. If the wood finally meets there, the two halves go on living. The half without a mouth grows one. The other half has to grow the smaller thing it has lost, a tuft at the tail that tells it which way the water is going.

At the rim the hems mostly remain where they settled. Farther in, there are kinds that travel. They do it when a fish goes through a gap and takes away the note. In that instant the feet on one side let go. By the time the note comes back they have found another hold. Where fish pass all day, a hem may move the length of itself in a week. In a passage the fish have abandoned, you can find them still stretched between roots that have ceased to be neighbors. Their middle hangs in the current. Little eels sleep against it, much as they would against the wood.

Once in its life a hem puts out a number of round young. They are hard, unlike the parent, and have no feet yet. Most are carried straight into the next gallery. A few go over the edge. There is no telling, from the edge, which of those have been lost. A young hem can shut itself so tightly that nothing goes in or out for months. It can come down on a terrace far below, roll until it finds a crack, and open there. This is why a newly broken span, with its pale ends and its galleries still full of torn leaves, sometimes has a kind of hem otherwise found only in the oldest wood of the upper country.

They have a habit down there that the upper ones do not. When a leaf lands on the floor, the hems come out for it. Each takes an edge and backs into its own seam. A broad leaf may take days to disappear. It is not the leaf they are after, but the small animals that have ridden it down. By the time there is nothing left but the ribs, the riders have had time to scatter. Some will live out their lives in the lower roots. Others, when they are grown, will let go of the wood and swim upwards. The very small ones take shelter under the bellies of fish. Now and then one arrives at the top in the mouth of a fish that has not yet noticed it, and gets out when the fish opens its mouth to take a shrimp.

There is one more animal in the passage under the rim, though people who know the place are apt to count it as part of the passage. It is a wide, flat thing, with a small head and little fans at the corners of its mouth. It lies over a slit in the wall. Water comes out of the slit under its belly and leaves on either side, passing through the fans. You can swim by it a hundred times without seeing more than two pale lines where the fans are working.

If it wants to move, it has to lift an edge. The slit then gives a thin high sound, quite different from the low note in the wood. The shrimp nearest it flatten themselves. The hems take a step. Usually the animal settles back before you have turned to look. But sometimes it lifts all the way off and swims out into the opening. It is surprisingly small away from the wall. What looked like its long back was only the mark it had left on the wood by lying there.

The slit goes on sounding after it has gone. Perhaps on another terrace somebody puts an ear to the floor and hears a note that has not been there in their lifetime. Here, at the opening, you can watch the animal go a little way into the empty water and turn on its side. It scarcely swims after that. There is a current outside the rim that runs up along the curve of the wood. It carries the animal over, onto the bare floor, where there is no slit for it to lie on. For a while it drifts there with the fans open. Then one corner of it dips. It has found a seam too fine to put a hand through, and the whole animal draws together over it, slowly, until the fans are the only parts moving.
