The woman with the paper bag is the one who paints the depth marks. Her name is Iva. At work she wears a skirt with a white line across one knee where she once steadied a ruler against her leg. In the bag are two pears and a pair of shoes for her son. She has bought the shoes a little big. By the time she gets them home she is no longer sure she has bought them big enough.

Her son is at the kitchen table with his feet in a basin. This is not for any ailment. He has found that if he sits very still, the little fish in the basin will come out from between his toes. Iva puts the bag down and asks him where he got the fish. He tells her he got the water from the tap.

They take the basin out together. The boy wants to pour it into the seam where the heron stood, but Iva makes him carry it to the far gutter, where the ferns hang down into the water. He pours too quickly. For a second a fish lies on a fern with its whole narrow side showing. Then the fern bends.

Iva knows the height of everybody’s floor. There are only three houses, but no two rooms in any of them are quite level, and the front room of the middle house has been getting lower since the woman who lived there died. She used to keep a treadle sewing machine by the window. After her death her nieces took the machine away, and the floor, relieved of the weight, rose almost to the sill. Then something began to grow in the space underneath. Now when Iva goes in to renew the marks she can feel it give a little beneath her heel. From outside there is nothing to see but a fine white fringe, like the roots on an onion left too long in a cup.

The two listeners at the water office are brothers. The elder is deaf in one ear and sits with his good ear toward the floor; the younger sits facing the street. They disagree about the knocks. The elder says there are six, sometimes seven. The younger says the last two come from a cupboard in their own office. In twenty years neither has got up at the right moment to put a hand on the cupboard.

At three they stop work. The younger brother eats an egg. The elder goes out to see what the heron gets. Once it got a fish so long it had to set it down and take hold of it farther back. He went in to call his brother, and when they came out the fish was gone. His brother asked how long it had been. He held his hands apart, then moved them a little closer. It troubled him that he could remember the effort the bird had made much better than he could remember the fish.

There are people in the streets beyond the square, though from the middle you mostly see their washing. They come to Iva’s door when a line has gone green or disappeared into the grain. A woman from the east street has a line across the lowest drawer of her dresser. She brings the drawer in each year because the dresser won’t go through the door. Iva paints it on the kitchen table, with a sheet of newspaper underneath. The boy likes to read the news through the handles while the paint dries.

Late in the day he tries on his shoes. He walks the rim of the square in them, taking the corners wide. At the brass pin he stops and puts his thumb under the drop. The water is colder than he expects. He goes home holding his hand out in front of him, but by the time he finds his mother there is only the little whorl of his thumb to show her.

After dark the crabs come farther out. They are pale underneath and can raise themselves surprisingly high when they need to get over a seam. One climbs onto the lowest step of the water office and spends much of the night there, picking something invisible from the bark. Inside, the brothers’ two chairs stand at their different angles. In Iva’s kitchen the new shoes are side by side under the table. The old ones are by the door, with their toes turned up, and sometime before morning a frog gets into one.
