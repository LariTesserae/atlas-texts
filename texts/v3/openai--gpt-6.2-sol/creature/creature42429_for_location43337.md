The woman who keeps the bathhouse is named Senn. She can tell who has come in by the way they cross the dry tiles. A person who meant to bathe stops at the bench to take off their shoes. A person who has come in to wait out the water walks straight to the far door, tries it, and comes back. Senn puts out a towel during that second crossing. She has learned not to ask people whether they want one while they are still counting the things they will be late for.

For a while a boy lived with her who had never seen a freestanding tree. There was a picture of one on a tin of soap, and he thought the green part was an animal that had settled on a post. What he wanted to know was how it got down. Senn took him to see an elm in a pot at the surveyors’ office. It was winter, and he looked at it for a long time before asking whether they would let him come back when the animal was there.

He works on a freight deck now. He keeps a pair of dry socks in a tin screwed to the underside of a cart, which is how Senn knows he has stopped being new at it. New porters keep their socks in their lockers.

The quiet pools have inhabitants besides the jointed animals. In the bathhouse pool there is a flat creature about the size of Senn’s hand. It spends most of the day on the underside of the bottom step and comes out after closing to eat the thin skin the bathers leave on the water. Senn knows it has come out when all the little islands of powder begin moving toward the step. Once, during repairs, a mason lifted it off with a wet tile and discovered a smaller one underneath. He called Senn over to see. By the time she got there the large one had spread itself over the small one again, so that there was nothing to look at but a wet tile and the mason holding it very level.

Up Above the Seventh, the rooms nearest the stair are rented cheaply to people who sleep in the daytime. At night the drain talks too distinctly. In one room it says something like *put it there*, with a pause in which another person might answer. A woman who mends freight gloves has lived in that room for twenty-three years. She has set her table in the pause: the drain makes its sound, she draws the thread through, it makes its sound again. When the drain is shut for cleaning she takes her work to the bathhouse. Senn lets her sit by the hot tank, where there is a loose washer that does nearly as well.

The glove mender has a grandson who collects the little cups from under the eaves. He likes the ones that come away in stacks. He used to think each cup had been made by the next animal to occupy the same spot, until he watched a group return to a pipe that had been cold all summer. They went up the wall on their narrow back legs. One stopped at a cup, put its arms in, took them out, and went on. Another passed over six empty cups to get to one with a chip in it. By evening some of them had settled and some were still walking. He had brought a jar to take one home and found he could not decide which one to interrupt.

On the day the rain from the shell yards arrives, the boy on the freight deck is let off within two streets of the bathhouse. He comes in carrying his socks in his hand. The cart has gone on without him, and he is pleased that for once he thought to unscrew the tin before stepping ashore. Senn gives him the end of a bench. Neither of them says anything for a while. Through the open door they can see the rain darkening the old names on the parapet, one cut at a time.

Then he asks if she remembers the tree. He has been past the surveyors’ office that morning. The pot is gone, and he wants to know if she thinks someone took it home.

Senn says they can go and ask when the water goes down. She has been meaning to find out herself.
