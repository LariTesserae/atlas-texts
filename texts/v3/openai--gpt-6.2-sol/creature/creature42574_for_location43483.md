The octopus in the bore this spring is smaller than last year’s. For the first week she can get all the way to the bottom without folding her arms over her head. She brings the gravel out a piece at a time and sets it down on the uphill side, where the next hard tide takes it. The flat stones she keeps. These she fits into the mouth of the bore until the opening is no wider than the space between her eyes.

She hunts the mussel beds after dark. By midsummer there will be a scatter of paired shells below the bore, most of them still joined at the hinge. A little fish sleeps in one of last year’s shells. When the octopus passes over it, the fish backs in until only its mouth shows, opening and closing in the narrow end.

At the chutes, the seals have different ways of waiting. The young ones tread water close to the mouths and turn after any fish that shows itself. A scarred female lies well downstream, belly up, where the hard water begins to spread. Fish that make it past the young seals come out of the chute fast and too close together. She takes the one that breaks low. Often she seems to have moved no more than her head.

When the old cable starts to sing, a few of the bigger fish leave the crown. They don’t go far. They drop into the gullies and hold close to the floor as the first of the flood goes over them. A ling as long as a man spends this part of the tide under one of the concrete feet. Only the end of her tail fits beneath it now. The rest of her lies along the rock, with the current lifting and laying down the thin feeler under her chin. At slack water she goes through the kelp after smaller fish. She has a pale patch on her back where she still tries to pass through a gap she fitted through when she was young.

Most of the animals on the ridge would be hard to see even if the kelp were gone. In a gully too clean for a mussel to get started, a limpet has worn a shallow oval in the stone. It goes out to graze when the water eases and comes back to the same oval, turning until its shell seats snugly in the rock. Farther down, where the silt begins, worms build tubes out of grains so fine they scarcely look like grains at all. When a crab goes over them, their pale feeding fans vanish in succession. By the time it has gone a yard, the first fans are out again.

In the south bowl there are skates. Their tracks are easier to find than they are: a wide shallow print where one has lain all afternoon, two little scoops where it pushed off. Along the rim of the bowl, where there is still stone for things to catch on, their egg cases lodge among the broken stalks. A fresh one is brown and glossy. Older ones have gone black and acquired a fur of silt. Some are empty. In others, if the case turns edgewise to the daylight, there’s a tail beating almost without pause. It keeps water moving through the case long after the yolk has dwindled to a small yellow knot.

A storm rolls one of the cases down into the bowl. It rests for a while against a mound of torn kelp, then a crab climbs the mound and dislodges it. One horn catches in the curl of a blade. The kelp shifts a little on each tide, but the case stays there through the winter.

By the time the skate comes out, the blade has softened enough for a snail to eat through. The little skate clears the split end of the case and swims a short way into the current. It is scarcely wider than the open shell the fish sleeps in up on the crown. It settles, lifts again at once, and settles a second time. A pair of small hollows form behind its eyes as it draws water in. In the fine gray silt, those are the only parts of it that go on moving.
