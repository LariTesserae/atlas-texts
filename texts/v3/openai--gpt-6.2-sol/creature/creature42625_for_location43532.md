At the blue door live two sisters who take in washing. They have a pulley over the stairwell and can send a wet sheet up three floors without it touching a step. The elder sister has lived here long enough to sleep through the works. When a belt breaks at night she wakes and puts on her slippers, thinking someone has called her.

Above them lives a boy who is allowed to go as far as the settling tank. He has been told not to put his hand in. Nobody has told him not to lower things on a string, so he lowers things. A brass button comes back warm. A crust comes back with the soft part gone and the string threaded neatly through a hole in the hard part. A little tin horse does not come back. Three weeks later he finds it on the footbridge, standing on the rail in a place he can't reach without help.

There are rats in the works, where it is dry above the belts. There are snails in the ferns, small flat ones that spend the day pressed together under the lip of a step. No one knows why there are no flies. The younger sister remembers them. You had to shake out a shirt, she says, before you folded it. She still does this, once, with a sharp motion of both wrists.

The thing in the tank is easiest to see after the bridge lamps have come on. Something pale goes across under them. It takes about as long as a person takes to cross the real bridge, though it stops sometimes, and though the tank is only eleven feet wide. On good evenings there are two of them. The smaller one goes ahead and comes back, ahead and back, until the larger one has finished crossing.

The man who cleans the tank has seen them without the water. He told the sisters this once, while they were taking up the hem of his work trousers. He had expected, he said, to find something lying in the silt. Instead, as the last of the water went down the drain, he saw two places where it wouldn't go down. Not puddles. He could see the brick through them. They went very slowly up the side of the tank and over the rim. He waited a while before climbing out himself.

They know the neighborhood farther than the boy does. In the long rain, when the cellars join below the dividing walls, people upstairs hear a careful passage among the coal bins. A woman in the end tenement keeps her potatoes on a shelf down there. One morning she found the smallest potatoes arranged along the edge, with their white shoots hanging down into the water. She took the big ones for supper. By the time she went down again the little ones were gone, shoots and all.

When the river falls, whatever has been visiting the cellars goes back to the court. The boy can tell by the gap in the third rib of silt. Sometimes it is wide enough for two fingers and sometimes barely wide enough for one. He used to think it was a doorway. Now he thinks it is where the smaller one lies, because late one evening he saw the larger one come halfway back across the bridge and wait there, and in the red silt below it a narrow place began to clear.

The elder sister calls him in before the last light goes. On his way home he passes beneath the pulley, where the sheets are still dripping. He has a new tin horse in his pocket. The old one stood on the rail for nine days, and on the tenth there was a second one beside it. His is the second one. It has legs of four different lengths. It can only stand in the shallow dish of a paving stone, with all four feet in water.
