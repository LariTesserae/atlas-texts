The thing that takes the cricket is smaller than the cricket. Only the part that takes it, anyway.

It is a clear lip, usually folded into the lower edge of a mortar joint. When the wall is wet the lip is hard to distinguish from the little surplus of water the joint will hold before it spills. The cricket puts a foot on it. The lip turns, taking the foot with it, and lies flat again. There is no struggle to speak of until the cricket tries to move on. By the time the traffic lets up, there is a cricket standing on five legs with its head toward the dark, and in the morning there is a cricket standing in exactly the same attitude with nothing inside it.

The rest of the animal is at some distance from the lip. There are blunt white sacs behind a patch of gritty mortar, and a great many threads, thickest where an old repair has pulled away from the original stone. If you took a block out you could follow some of the threads across its bed. At each place where the stone bears hard on the stone below, they go around. In a few places they go a very long way around, and in one they end on either side of a point of contact without meeting. It is possible that what lives on one side of that point has not fed what lives on the other for a hundred years.

Its common food is much smaller than crickets. At night a grey flour comes down in the condensation: yeast from the houses, the powder off moth wings, whatever has got into the air shafts and stayed there through a dry afternoon. The animal puts out lips all along the sweating wall. They face up while the water is gathering and down while it runs. The traffic does most of the work of turning them. A long wagon will turn a whole row, not at once but in order, with time between the first and the last for a bead of water to pass from one to the next. The little that remains in the folds is sufficient. A cricket is an accident of plenty. After one, the nearest sacs go yellow, and for several nights nothing opens within a handspan of the husk.

There are also animals at the bench. They are not connected to the ones below. They live high up in the seam that takes the weight of the middle street and are nearly dry, and their lips, when they put them out, are no bigger than the cuticle of a nail. People sit on the bench with their feet well out in the way of other people. Now and then someone draws both feet in to let a pram by, or gets up and sits down again to make room. In the wall this is a little lifting and resettling of the stone. It is enough. The animals there can go unfed for weeks, which they do when it is too cold for anybody to sit.

The young are made in the bowls where the drops collect. For most of their first season they are free of the wall. You could dip a finger in a bowl and take out a dozen without seeing anything on your skin, although after your finger dried there would still be a feeling of wetness in one place. They are soft all the way through. The rain washes many of them onto the paving. Some go down the slot; some are left behind as the last of the water passes, and dry there. The ones that survive are the ones that happen to get caught at the entry to a seam.

Getting caught is not enough. The seam has to open. The young animal will wait with part of itself against a joint too fine to enter, sometimes until the water around it is gone. A particular load comes over, or two loads arrive together from streets that ordinarily take their turns. For a moment the joint gives it room. Once inside, it may find that the same load, passing a little faster or on the other side of the street, will close the way behind it. There are entire broods in the north wall that have got no farther than this. They live on what comes through a joint they cannot themselves pass.

Just before dawn, the bowl farthest from the lamp holds a single young one. The water it hatched in has gathered into a drop heavy enough to pull clear of the mortar. It hangs there a long time. Nothing below it is wet now.

The first tram puts weight on the wall and then, for the length of the space between its wheels, takes some of the weight away. A seam opens behind the bowl. There is scarcely any motion to see: the drop grows short and broad, and is gone. A moment later the stone has settled. The young one is still in water, though there will not be room for it to turn over until the next tram.
