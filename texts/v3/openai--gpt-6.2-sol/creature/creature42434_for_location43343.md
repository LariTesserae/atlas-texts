In the clean room there is a gray line along the foot of the back wall. It looks like the mortar the builders used to bed the first course, except that it goes over the mortar in places, and over a chip knocked out of one of the stones. At the doorway the line turns up the jamb. It is thickest in the little pocket where the door once swung on its pin.

Put a lamp there and wait. The gray does not withdraw from the light. But when a flake of silt comes over the sill, you can see how long it takes to reach the floor. It catches two fingers’ breadths above the paving, turns a little, and goes on turning without going down. There are threads stretched across the room, too fine to see except where something has caught on them. At their ends the gray line swells into a row of soft mouths. Each closes so slowly that you are likely to look away before it opens again.

The mouths belong to one animal, though it would be hard to say where the animal ends. There is more of it under the back wall. A thin part goes down between two bricks and comes up on the far side, in a room whose floor has fallen in. There it has made nothing like the net in the first room. It lies in the rubble in flat loops, with the water passing between them. If a brick shifts and pinches a loop in two, both ends seal. The part cut off may last a hundred years. Given enough to eat, it will put out mouths of its own.

It lives on what the gardens lose. A length of white growth gets too heavy and breaks; the current rubs it down to flakes before it has gone a street. Most of these settle in hollows. A few fetch up on a thread. The mouths are not particular about what they take in, and every so often a little twist of clay comes out of one and is passed along the wall. In the pocket for the doorpin the twists are packed into a hard gray plug. The finest paving in the room is under the net. Outside the doorway, where a net would fill up in a day, the silt lies undisturbed.

There are other rooms like this, but not many. A good room has two ways for the water to get in, neither much larger than a cord hole, and a low place where the waste can be put. In a row of houses built to one plan, the animal may occupy the same room in each. The walls between them need not be breached. It can pass from house to house below the sills, in the spaces left by timbers that have been gone longer than the sea has been cold.

Once in a long while, a knock comes through the water and a whole run of mouths closes. Farther down the street the mouths stay open: the sound alone is not enough. Where the stone has moved, even a little, the threads pull tight, some of them break, and the animal takes in what it can of the slack. It may be years before there is another net. In the meantime the floor takes silt.

In one room a slab has fallen across the doorway, leaving a gap at the top no wider than the shallow mark of a hammer. The current through it is just enough. Behind the slab the gray line has climbed the wall and spread along the underside of the lintel. The old sill is buried now, but directly above it, on the upper edge of the fallen stone, there is a band as clean as the place where a hand would rest.
