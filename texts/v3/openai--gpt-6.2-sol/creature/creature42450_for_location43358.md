The copper fish does not spend the night where it spends the day. When the meadow begins to go down, it leaves the light between the crowns and swims to a split in the rock. There it turns on its side and slides in until only the lower edge of its tail is showing. The split runs clear through the fin. On the far side, in a crack no wider than the first, six small fish hang head downward. They are nearly transparent. When the adult settles, they come through one at a time and pick at the pale underside of its jaw. By morning they have gone back. From the lighted side of the ridge it looks as though there is only the one fish.

The chalk mouths on the wall belong to something that has been growing there longer than any of the trees. If a wedge comes away, there are white threads on both faces of the break. For a little while the mouths on the fallen piece still open when the cool water reaches them. Then they close, and the stone slowly takes on the color of the other loose stone in the gully. On the standing wall, a break in the threads is covered over in a few seasons. New mouths appear first along its lower edge. They are no bigger than the hollows left by the points of needles.

Something grazes those mouths. It does not come every evening, and its arrival is most easily judged by the hanging fronds. One frond lifts, then another some distance away. The animal is as long as a forearm and almost as flat as a crown. It has a gray back with a loose margin that fits down over the little irregularities of the wall. Under that margin are six soft feet. It can stand on five of them for a surprisingly long time while the sixth feels about for a place to settle.

The flesh around its mouth is dark red. It presses this against the chalk until the opening widens, then takes whatever comes out. There is no hole afterward, only a clean patch, whiter than its surroundings. An old grazer leaves a row of such patches a little crooked, missing a mouth here and there where the stone is awkward. The young ones follow these rows. For their first year they feed only on mouths that have been opened before.

They hatch in the saddle meadow. The eggs are fastened low on the blades, below the clear tips, and seldom get enough light to cast shadows of their own. As the grass rises, the eggs come out of the still water close to the roots. As it sinks, they go back into it. This is all the tending they get. Farther down the scarp there are places where loose eggs have lodged and developed until the little folded feet can be seen through the skin. They do not hatch there. At the meadow edge, where the blades rise only a little, they hatch late and the young come out walking. In the tall grass they come out loose-limbed and have to catch hold of something.

There is one grazer on the highest crest that has stopped making the evening trip to the wall. A pale line across its back marks where the stone once caught it. Two of its feet on that side have grown back shorter than the others, and it does better among the roots, where there is always something close to hold. The new shoots have grown up around it. Where their tips bend into the water, it feeds on a faint white growth in the folds of the leaves.

It has tried the leaf in the air. Near the base there is a shallow bite with a red rim, taken while the leaf was still opening and its lower edge still touched the water. Now even that edge is dry. In the afternoon the grazer climbs until its foremost feet are just below the surface. From there the leaf is sometimes in reach and sometimes not. A little movement of the water brings its shadow down over the animal’s back, then takes it off again. By dusk the grazer has usually turned to another shoot. The dry leaf stays where it is. On the side away from the sun, close against the stem, a second leaf is beginning to unfold.
