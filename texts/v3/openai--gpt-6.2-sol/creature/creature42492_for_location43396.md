The animals that come up in the rain are brown on top and white underneath. They climb through the holes in the drain cover one at a time, turning sideways to get the broad part of the head through. The young come up with their mouths fastened to the backs of the larger ones. Once in the square, each animal puts a foot on the animal ahead of it. In a long rain the line reaches the corner and turns. They make room for the little pale drinkers, though this means taking a foot off a back and feeling about for it again afterward.

In the cellar they live on the undersides of shelves. There is nothing on the shelves. The animals have eaten whatever was there, including some of the wood, and the young hang in the shallow hollows left by the eating. They are fed on drops of water. An adult holds a drop against the shelf with its mouth until a young one has taken it. Sometimes the drop escapes through a joint. You can hear the young one sucking for a while after the water is gone.

The animal at the pumping station is a different sort. It has six feet, but uses the front two mostly for turning things over. At the end of each pump stroke it puts one of them flat against the hatch. If there is a new sucker underneath, the hatch trembles a little longer on that side. The animal gets a tooth under the sucker and walks backward, drawing out a white length of plant that can be several times as long as its body. It eats the soft end and carries the rest away.

It has been carrying these lengths into one of the tall houses. Inside, they are laid crosswise over a hole meant for a pipe. The draft keeps the middle of each length cold and wet, and here the wood has begun to grow again. There are eggs where the lengths cross. The animal turns them at intervals, using the sides of its feet. When it goes back to the station it leaves one foot in the hole for as long as it can reach.

Some nights the eggs can be heard from the street. There is a fine scraping, quite high up, although the hole is not much above the paving. The gutter hunter has moved to that house. It lies under the sill of the fourth-story window, where the leaf has grown close enough around its back that only the heels show.

Under the bark of the new root there are chambers with walls thin enough to see the water traveling in the wood. The gray animals there are bigger than the ones in town. They still sleep in slits, but they go out to feed, and the empty slits close behind them. At the end of a night’s feeding each animal has to cut a new place to sleep. The ones with eggs cut two slits side by side and leave a little wood between. You can tell how far along the eggs are by how little wood is left.

There has already been one rain on that side. The first sign in the square was a brown animal coming up through the drain with a yellow crust on its head. It got all the way out and stood waiting, but nothing came up behind it. After a time it went back down. It did this twice more before the cellar animals began to rise. The first of them put a foot on its back, and it lowered itself a little, as they all do under the weight, though there was hardly any weight yet.
