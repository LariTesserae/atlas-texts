The ledge hens build on things that are about to fall. A sound branch is too busy for them: the herds come along it, and the little animals that eat eggs can run up the bark as easily as down. A dead leaf rib, hanging by a few wet strings, will do for a hen and two eggs. She puts the nest out near the tip. When her mate comes with food he lands close to the stem and waits for her to walk in to him. If both birds go out to the nest the rib dips, and it sometimes does not come back.

The young learn to hang before they learn to fly. They spend much of their first week underneath the rib, with their toes showing above it like pairs of brown staples. When a rib goes, the hen follows it down. If it catches somewhere she goes on feeding the young there. A traveler once found three nearly grown ones under a shelter floor, on a rib that had fallen across the lashings in the night. The hen had been bringing them the white insects that collect in the seams of a sleeping hood. Nobody in the shelter had slept so well in weeks.

There are mice in the dry reaches, though a sound made by a mouse may come out of the stem twenty feet from the mouse itself. They live in the space left when a long splinter springs away from the growing wood. At the open end they keep a plug of chewed leaf, which they can haul in after them by a tuft of fiber. The plug is seldom the color of the wood around it. A mouse will pass several good splinters to get to one with a patch of old black bark at its mouth, against which a plug can be seen in very little light. Coming home with its teeth full, it wants to find the door on the first pass.

In the wet bands there is an animal about as long as a hand that carries its house slung beneath it. The house is a loose purse of hairs combed from the roots, with a fold at the bottom for water. The animal sleeps with its hind legs in the fold and its head out, breathing against the bark. In a cold snap the water freezes. You can find the purses then by tapping lightly on the stem: the animal turns in its sleep and the ice knocks. Once the thaw starts it goes from purse to purse licking the melt from the neighbors’ seams. They do not appear to mind, but a mother with young sleeps with the opening drawn up under her chin.

People who stay through the winter keep a lower shelter as well as an upper one. Often the two are not far apart in a straight line. Getting from one to the other can take most of a day, and there are families who eat their noon meal within calling distance of the pot they left behind at breakfast. Their children learn the routes in the order in which they are allowed to carry things along them: an empty bag first, then a bag with something breakable in it, then a cup full to the brim. The point of the cup is not to bring the water home. It is to find out which steps make the branch move *after* you have taken your weight off it.

At the end of the brief light season the cup animals lay their eggs above the waterline. For a while the eggs look like the small clear blisters that come up on new bark. They hatch when the cup next spills, and the young go over with the water. Most are caught almost at once on a leaf or a crossing root. They flatten themselves there, scarcely thicker than the wet that brought them, and wait for another rain. It may take a whole season to reach a cup. This is why a cup that has been empty for years can have animals on its rim the first night it holds water. They were not in the mud. They were on the long slope above it, coming down a little at a time.
