There is an animal in the hollow that the attendant has seen whole only once. He was replacing the gutter. He had it down on the path, with the far end propped on his lunch tin, when something came out of the spoon in the root and climbed the brick pier to where the drip had been.

It was about as long as his hand and so thin that the corners of the bricks showed through it. It climbed by setting down one edge and drawing the rest of itself after, the way a man might pull a blanket up without waking the person under it. The part he took for the head passed the old drip mark and went on up under the siding. Then the other end lifted off the brick, felt about for a while, and went up after it.

He put the gutter back a little crooked. For the rest of that summer there were two spoons, the old dry one and a new one a finger’s width nearer the house. It was the animal that made the second. You could come in the morning and see where it had spent the night with its edges wrapped around a little water. The wood inside that outline went soft first. Slivers came away in the water, and the animal took them in. It left the harder grain standing until that softened too. In September, when the rain came back in earnest, it moved into the new spoon and the old one began to grow a skin.

In most of the hollow it would be no thicker than the wet on your palm. It eats what comes loose: the pale outer fuzz of a root tip, the backs of leaves after the green has gone out of them. A snail can go over it without stopping. Where a root presses against another root, it gathers into a white seam you could take for new wood. Those seams are the oldest parts. There are pieces of it under the road that have not been in daylight since the road was gravel.

The pump makes it let go. Not altogether. There are places where it can hold as firmly as ever while the motor runs, and those are the places it retreats to when the current comes on. Through the day, thin portions venture out from those holds and get shaken loose and have to venture out again. Around the pump house it has learned to lie under the little round leaves. They are heavy enough to keep it down. When the motor stops, it works its way out from beneath them, lifting each leaf by the stem. The row at the door lifts first because there is a good hold in the crack under the threshold.

The attendant used to think the sound at the entrance was two roots moving against one another. Then one year, when he cut back to the chalk, the noise went on in the piece he had cut off. He had set it on the road with the cut face up. A pale strip ran through it, and a bead of water moved along that strip against the slope, stopped at a saw tooth’s nick, and hung there. He turned the piece over into the ditch. By quitting time the strip was empty. A little farther down, under a stone that the county trucks had pushed into the mud, there was a white edge no wider than a clipping from a fingernail.

The ditch dries out in August. He has looked under the stone then and found nothing. In wet years it gets as far as the other side of the road. The easiest place for it to cross is inside the discharge pipe, not along the bottom where the water runs, but on the crown, where drops hang too far apart to touch. It goes from one to the next. At the far end it has made a shallow cup in the wood of an old fence stake. The stake is nearly gone now. There is a live alder an inch away, and a dark spot on the alder where, when the ditch fills, the two touch.
