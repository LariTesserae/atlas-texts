The thing that lives there is small enough to get under a copper leaf. That is not where it lives, though. You can lift the leaf and find nothing but a paler strip of clay. By then it will be two shelves away, in a pipe you would have sworn was too narrow to admit it.

It is the color of the thumbed paste, except along its belly, where it is white and a little translucent. Its feet are broad for its size. Each has a crease across the middle so that it can hold on to both faces of a corner. It has no eyes that anyone has found. When it comes to the patch of light it does not go around it or stop in it. It goes on feeling the floor with the underside of its jaw.

There are several. You might live a year in the room and think there was only one, because they come out by turns. One puts its head over the edge of a pipe and opens its mouth. Nothing comes out that you can hear. Then, a long way off for an animal so small, another puts its head out. They keep on in this fashion until one of them has gone all the way around the room. On the way back it takes a different route.

They eat what the warm air brings. There is more of it than you would think, looking at the bricks. A little moth comes up without its wings; the wings have stuck somewhere further down the tower. A seed with a hooked end comes up and hangs by its hook until something takes the soft part out of it. The hooks are worth keeping. The animals carry them to the far wall and fit them into the slits, point inward. Behind them they pack the pale lint that comes up in the spring. Their young are born in that lint, in the low sound.

The young can walk on the shelves as soon as they are dry, but they cannot cross the path. A grown one sets its jaw down on a cupped brick and knows where the next brick is, even where they do not touch evenly. A young one sets its jaw down and gets the whole room at once. It goes flat and waits. The grown ones carry them across, one at a time, with a foot sticking out on either side of the mouth. For a few weeks of the year there is a great deal of this traffic. Then there is none.

The person who used to sit by the entrance knew about them. The flat place is not quite level. At the back there is a slight hollow, enough to hold a spoonful of water if you pour it slowly. They will drink there with a person sitting beside them, provided the person keeps both feet off the path. They will even drink from the hollow of a hand. Getting down again is the difficulty. The little feet cannot find a corner in a wrist.

Now they drink in the roof passage, at the second turn, where a little water gathers on the underside of a brick. One can hang there a long time with its mouth to the drop. From there the room is almost quiet. The sound goes out below it, and once in a while there is the sound of something outside: a bird landing, or dry grass knocking on stone far down the tower. At either sound it lifts its head. When the drop is gone it goes back down, feeling for the next brick with its jaw.
