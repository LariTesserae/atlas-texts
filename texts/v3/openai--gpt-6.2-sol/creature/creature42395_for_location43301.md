The woman who clears the drain is called Sella. She has two levers, a long one for the street lids and a short one for the lid in her kitchen. The short one is the better tool. She made it from the broken handle of the long one, which she remembers being taller than she was. Her father used to let her ride on the free end when he lifted a lid. She has tried this with the children, but they get off as soon as it begins to rise.

There are six children in her ward. They come down the steps in pairs, not because anyone has told them to but because the widest step will take two people jumping from the one above. They are forbidden to jump on the lower flight, where the cattle can see them. The smallest child, who has not yet been entrusted with a loaf, carries the little board the loaves are set on. She holds it out flat in both hands, with nothing on it, and calls for people to stand aside.

Of the public ovens, only three are lit. A man named Iven looks after them. He can tell by the smell when someone has put fern stalks in the wood basket, and will come out and pick them over without asking whose child fetched the kindling. He keeps a pan of water by the first oven for a dog which sleeps against the second. The dog will not drink if he watches, so he puts down the pan and goes to look at the sky through the vent of the third oven. There is a patch of sky the size of his palm. When his bread is in, he has time to watch a cloud pass through it.

Iven eats the heels of loaves other people do not want. He has a daughter who likes the heels, too. For a while he saved them for her until she asked him not to. She lives in the house across the bare shoulder. The cloth on the parapet is hers, an old flour sack she has opened along the seams. She washes it after kneading because otherwise a little grey mouse comes to lick it. The mouse comes anyway. It gets up the wall by the mortar joints and puts its forefeet on the cloth, testing it. On the day she first saw it do this, she fetched a stool and put a pinch of flour on the parapet. The mouse went around the pinch to get to the cloth.

Her name is Ressa. People think she lives so far out for the quiet, but the ticking at the stepping stones is audible in her kitchen. The rock brings it up behind the hearth. She took the hearth apart once, thinking there was a beetle in it, and found a copper spoon that had slipped down behind a stone. It was not one of hers. The bowl was worn through at one edge, so she hung it by the door for callers to knock with. Her father still knocks with his knuckle. He was there when she found it and knows how thin it is.

After the children have gone by, Sella goes back to see what the birds have taken from the wall. They leave the roots and carry off the small white things that live among them. One bird stands on a root to pull, and when the thing comes free it sits down abruptly on its tail. Sella has seen it do this often enough to know where to stand without frightening it. Sometimes Iven brings her a heel and they eat on the wall. He cannot see the bird from where he likes to sit. She tells him when it falls over, though he has never understood why she finds it funny.

In the empty wards there are still doorways with scrapes at the height of a carried basket, and thresholds worn lower on the side where a person could put a hand against the jamb. The children play house in these places. They prefer the back row, where it is dark enough indoors to light a candle in the middle of the day. There is one house they never use. A hare has taken to sitting in it, just out of the light, with its ears laid down its back. The smallest child found it there when she went in to put a broken cup on a shelf. Now she leaves the cup outside the door. The hare has no use for it, but twice an owl has drunk rain out of it.

The children ask why the road beyond the houses has a mounting block when there is nowhere to go. Sella used to say that there was going to be another market. Iven says there was going to be a house for every person who had helped lay the street. Neither was there when it was laid. One of the older children has begun bringing pebbles to the bend, looking for one that will fit in place of a bit of pot rim. He has a good eye and has found several. Each time, after he loosens the rim, he runs his finger over the curve the potter made and puts it back. He keeps the pebbles on the mounting block. People coming that way move them aside before sitting down, then put them back when they get up.

On her way home from the ovens, Ressa stops at the bench to shift the bread from one arm to the other. From there she can make out the white of the sack on her parapet. Once, when she was small, she watched her father go home from this bench. She could see him all the way to the first roofs, and he turned twice because she had told him she would wave. Her arm had got tired by then. She remembers resting it on the back of the bench and raising just her hand, and her father, who could not possibly have seen it, raising his in answer.
