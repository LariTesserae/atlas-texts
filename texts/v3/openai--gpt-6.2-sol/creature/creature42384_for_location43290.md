The animal from the boot is no bigger than a grain of barley. In a palm it has a blunt end and a pointed end, and no part of it seems especially suited to getting through leather. It lies on its side until the water around it cools. Then it puts out a clear foot and walks to the lowest part of the palm. If you tilt your hand, the animal goes with the water, but the foot stays where it was for a little while. You can see the fine bones of your hand through it.

There are thousands of these feet on the undersides of the shelves. At low water they look like the marks left by drops that have fallen. The animals themselves collect down in the wells, several deep, with their pointed ends outward. A well that looks white at noon can be black by evening, though none of the animals in it has changed color. They have all turned over. Their undersides are dark and soft, and on each there is a little hard mouth. They eat the grit that settles in the wells. What they pass is clear enough to see through, and comes out in one long thread, no matter how coarse the grit was. In the oldest wells the threads have wound together into loose balls. When the water lifts one of these out, the combing animals go after it. For a few yards they can run on the ball itself, picking out the threads as it unravels beneath them.

The combers are pale only from above. Underneath, between the roots of the legs, each carries a patch of color. There are yellow ones and ones the blue of a bruise, and a few with a red so dark it is easy to mistake for a hole. The patches are broods. When two animals contend for a place in the current, they rear up and work at each other with their combs. Some of the young come loose. Those that catch in the other animal’s hairs creep down to the underside and settle among its brood. A large comber may have six or seven colors on it by the end of a flood. It bends its hind legs carefully over them when it folds.

The broad-backed animals eat the combers, though not as many as their size would suggest. They have a pair of thin lips well back from the nostrils, almost at the shoulders. You can watch a comber work its way past one of these mouths, stopping to clean each foreleg with the other. Nothing happens until it reaches the far corner. Then the lip draws in by the width of a hair. The pale shell drops away a moment later with all the legs still on it. The brood is gone.

Most of what the big animals take in is water. They take it slowly and let it out all at once through slits along the back. In a deep channel this does little more than move them forward. On the shelves the discharge clears a patch down to bare stone. The little animals that have been swept aside right themselves and hurry back, sometimes getting there before the first silt has settled. A good place on a shelf is worth being swept off it several times.

There is one kind of animal found only in the patches the big ones have cleared. It has no shell or bones, but is stiff enough when empty to keep its shape. It stands about a finger high, with a small opening at the top. Water poured into it comes out warm. On a falling tide the combers climb up and stand over this opening, their legs crooked around the rim. You can find a dozen crowded onto a single one, with their broods hanging into the water inside. By morning the rim is worn in notches where their legs have been. The next flood takes the animal away. Nothing seems to eat it. It floats upright for as long as you can follow it, taking water in over one side and putting it out over the other.

The wet ridge on the shelf does not follow the broad-backed animal when it leaves. For the first hour it keeps the animal’s slow rate of breathing. Then it begins to breathe faster. Small stones slide down either side, and what looked like a single ridge separates into a row of low humps. These are not joined. A comber can pick its way between them without getting wet.

At the next rise of water the humps fill from below. Each has a pair of nostrils, too far apart at first for the width of its head. They make a surprising amount of noise. The sound is like water being sucked from a boot, and once one of them starts the others do it too. They cannot yet take the current lengthwise. They roll, put a flank up, and roll again. The combers go over them in little parties. Every so often one stops at a place where a lip will be and combs there for a long time.

An adult comes back toward morning. It does not go onto the shelf. It lies in the channel with its nostrils closed and lets water out through the slits on the side nearest the young. They turn in that weaker current, one at a time, until they are all lying the same way. From a distance there appears to be just the one animal, with a broken line of reflections along its back. Then the wind changes. The adult slides away, and the smallest of the young goes with it. The others are still there at noon, under a long shadow that has begun to fill with combers.
