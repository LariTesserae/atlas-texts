The first sign of a household is a fish that comes back with its mouth empty.

Below the green fall, the small silver fish make their usual circuit: up the clear side, across the lip, down through the meal. Every few turns one breaks away and goes into the lee of the blade. It comes out a moment later without the green pellet it has packed behind its teeth. If you wait on that side, holding as still as the fish do, you can see a hand reach out and take the next pellet. The hand is broad and nearly transparent between the knuckles. Sometimes there are two hands waiting, and the fish chooses the smaller.

The people there call themselves the lee families. Seen edge on, an adult is no wider than a forearm. Seen from behind, one has a back broad enough for three children to ride without touching. They have no tails. A fold of flesh runs from each shoulder to the heel, and to travel any distance they spread these folds and let the water take hold. They can swim against it, but it is ugly work. A person coming home in a temper will sometimes swim straight up the fall rather than go around by the quiet side, arriving exhausted and green all down the front. This makes it difficult to sustain a quarrel.

The fall is only their kitchen. Their sleeping place is higher up, where the blade thickens and turns a little in the current. There are no holes in the wood. They hang in the slack water with their feet loosely clasped, the children innermost. Anyone whose grip opens in sleep drifts a short way and comes against the back of a light sleeper. These are usually old people. In a large family there may be an old person at either end, each convinced the other sleeps through everything.

A family keeps a few things. The bowls are the empty joints of a long animal that grows down in the dark; each has a rim of little teeth, which must be filed smooth. There are combs for getting the green out of a child's shoulder folds. A good comb is heavy enough to drop. Nearly every family has a story about somebody who tried to catch one and was gone for three days. Nothing is set down. When two people stop to talk, they tuck what they are carrying into one another's folds, and sometimes, after a long conversation, set off with the wrong things.

They have routes between the summits which do not follow the saddles. A traveler bound east may first go west and down, with her folds shut and her hands over her eyes. At the second knock of a lobe she opens one side, turns, and is lifted into water that will carry her east for the better part of a day. A child learning the route is told where to look, not what to do with his body. Watch for the pale underside of the grazer. Watch how its mouth goes sideways. By the time he can see why the fish has turned, he has usually turned with it.

There are families that live down among the pumps, where they speak with their wrists because a voice cannot cross a room. The high families think them abrupt. In fact a conversation there can go on for hours, with long intervals when both people put their hands flat on the wood to feel a lobe filling. A visitor tends to mistake these intervals for the end of the conversation and leave. The people below have learned not to be offended, though they do tell stories about it.

At the western end there is one small household that keeps no feeding fish. They go up to the rain for their food. The cold return brings with it almost nothing, but along its edge there are threads of warmer water full of eggs so small that a thousand of them look like a thumbprint on a bowl. The youngest in the household is a girl who likes to turn on her back just under the surface and take the rain on her eyelids. She has seen birds there. From below, a swimming bird is a belly with two restless feet. She once saw one lift both feet out of the water and disappear. Her brother says it drew them into its belly; neither of them can account for the belly.

Once a year or so one of the western people comes all the way to the green fall. They bring eggs, and the lee families give them a bowl of meal pressed so firmly it can be carried in the hand. News travels with these visits, although not always in order. This year the visitor asks after an old woman who used to sleep on the outside of the household. He has a comb of hers. She lent it to him when he was a boy, and it has taken him a long time to remember whose it was.

Her granddaughter takes it. She has a comb already, and the old one is missing two teeth. She keeps both in her shoulder fold through supper. Later, as the family gathers, she moves to the outside, where there is room for her to open one side of her body a little and feel the water. In the night someone comes loose. She feels the small blind knock against her back and reaches behind without turning.
