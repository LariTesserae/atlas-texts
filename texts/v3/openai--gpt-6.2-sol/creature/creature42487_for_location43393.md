The heavy animal under the ferns is not as heavy as it sounds. It goes on six feet, and before it trusts its weight to any of them it gives the ground a little push. On bare rock this makes no sound at all. In the litter it sounds like somebody taking their boots off very slowly.

There are two adults, one at either end of the hollow. If you put a hand into the ferns, you are more likely to touch a bed than an animal. They make their beds by pulling down living fronds and lying on the tips until the stalks take a bend. A frog can sit on the top of a bed while the animal sleeps underneath. When the animal gets up, the frog has just enough warning to jump.

The smaller adult sometimes lets a hand rest on it. Its hide feels like the bark of the vines, but the short hairs around its mouth are stiff and move independently. One will find the crease of a knuckle and stay there while the others go on exploring. It eats the pale grubs that live between a fern’s roots and the wall. To get at one it has to go still for a long time, with its mouth open against the rock. It does not seem to mind if a cricket sings right into its ear. A drop of water on the back of its head, though, will make it pull out and start all over again.

Most of the crickets stay within a few inches of where they hatched. There is one low ledge where you can hear the same four or five gaps in their singing night after night: a drop strikes a leaf, the nearest cricket stops, the next goes on. The frogs move about more. The smallest ones breed in the folded leaves of the jointed plants. When a leaf shuts on a drop it makes a narrow pocket, and if you put a fingertip to the fold you can sometimes feel the tadpoles working their tails. The large animals drink from those plants too, but not from a leaf with anything moving in it. They put their mouths to the next joint down and wait for it to open.

The young of the large animals live in the middle.

For most of their first season they are no bigger than the last joint of a thumb. They cling under the rims of the three cups, where the stone is dry. You can sit with your hand on one side of a rim and have a whole litter hanging on the other. Their feet are already broad, but the hairs on their faces are so long that they trail in the water. Sometimes one climbs up to drink, gets too much of its face wet, and backs down in a hurry, knocking the others loose. They catch themselves a little farther down. The stone beneath the rims is rough with the tiny ridges their feet can hold.

Just before the cups empty, the two adults come out of the ferns. They do not go to the cups. They wait where the first broad leaves hang over the bare floor, with their mouths close to the stone.

Then the water goes out of the cups, the rain falls at the edges, and the young let go. They are almost soundless when they land. For a few seconds they have to cross open rock with nothing to steer by except the rain. Most go toward the loudest splash, which is not always where an adult is waiting. The adults can move quite fast at this point. They go along the edge gathering the young with the sides of their mouths, sweeping them in among the hairs, where the little feet can hold. One or two usually get past them and burrow into the litter. For several days afterward you can hear those ones trying to walk the way the adults do, testing each step twice, making a tremendous business of a single dead leaf.

If you happen to be sitting on one of the stones when the cups empty, keep your feet flat for a little while. There is often a last one. It comes down after the others and walks a short way up the warm side of your foot. It will stand there until the next leaf tips. Then it turns, with more certainty than it has shown about anything yet, and goes toward the noise.
