The tracks behind the stones belong to a gecko. In the dust each foot opens into a little hand, with the outermost finger set well apart. Going in, she puts her feet down in much the same places every time. Coming out, she sometimes stops with her head under the middle stone and her body still on the dry side. You can tell where she does this by the two deeper prints her forefeet have made. The tip of her tail leaves a mark only at the turn.

On the wall she is harder to make out than her tracks. Her back has the color of limestone where it has been wet and dried again, and a few darker flecks that could be pits in the stone. She can wait with one foot lifted so long that when she sets it down you notice the movement without quite finding the animal. What gives her away is her eye. Seen from below, it catches a little of the light that has already left the lower roots.

She hunts the wall above the leaf litter. Most of what falls there keeps going, but a cricket will sometimes catch itself on a ledge and wait, and the gecko knows the ledges. Once she went down after a cricket that had fallen as far as the deep cup. The nearer frog took it while she was still working her way around the rim. She stayed there afterward and drank, passing her tongue over the stone just above the waterline, where the splash had left a row of beads.

There are two eggs in the dry triangle, farther back than the dust, fastened to the underside of a small shelf. You would have to put your cheek against the floor to see them. Beside them is half a shell from an earlier year, with dirt lodged in the break. The middle stone must have stood lower when that one hatched. Whatever came out of it could have passed through the old gap without touching either side.

The gecko doesn't stay with the eggs. On warm nights she goes up the steps and hunts among the dry grasses, where there are moths she never sees below. She usually comes back before the light reaches the bush. Once, after rain, she brought a grass seed in on her foot. It lay in the dust for days, shifting a little each time she passed. A beetle finally got hold of it and dragged it under the middle stone. The husk is in the leaf litter now, soft at one end.

Late in the morning a thread of water works down the root that has lifted the stone. It hangs for a while almost directly over the entrance. The gecko comes as far as her two deep footprints and waits. When the drop falls, it lands just outside, scattering dark specks across the dust. She steps over them. By the time she comes in again, the specks will be gone, and her feet will find the same places.
