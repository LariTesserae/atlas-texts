A shrimp lives in one of the shells the ice cut open. Not in the shell itself, which would scarcely take its head, but in the small recess behind it where the clay has softened and fallen away. Its feelers lie out across the white edge. When a snail comes that way the feelers withdraw, then come out again on either side of the snail.

The shrimp is carrying eggs. You would have to see it from below to see them properly, and there is no getting below it. From the mouth, in the last of a clear ebb, you can make out a brown fullness under its body and the little movements that pass through it. The shrimp works its legs even when it has found a purchase and has no need to swim. A flake of peat catches on its back; it reaches up and takes the flake apart.

There are things living in the roof that never enter the water. A springtail walks the underside of a root, stops at a drop too big to go round, and goes back the way it came. Farther in, the roots are fine and white. A worm draws one of the dead ones into a hole by degrees. Each time it lets go, the root straightens a little. Over several nights the loose end grows shorter, though you could sit through a whole turning of the tide and not be sure you had seen it move.

The sticklebacks are visitors, except for a small one that got left behind. It spent an afternoon in the spoonful of water on the creek side of the pebble. Its back broke the surface whenever it turned. The heron was still outside then, and twice its shadow went over the fish without the bird finding a way to it. When the tide came back, the fish did not at once leave the hollow. Water a finger deep went over it before it rose and took its place under the bank. By the following ebb there was no telling it from the others.

The shrimp leaves on a night when the creek rises high enough to put the roots under. For a while it feeds out on the clay, picking at the silt lifted by the incoming water. Then it swims past the cress. Nothing comes out of the recess after it. The next day a snail grazes across the lip of the shell, and the white shows a little brighter.

Weeks later there are shrimp in the pocket so small that what you see first is not the body but the eyes. At low water several gather where a root keeps a wet line down the wall. One comes as far as the cut shell and hangs there, head outward, fanning the water. The shell has loosened by then. On the next tide it tips from its hollow and falls white side down, and the shrimp moves up the bank to another place.
