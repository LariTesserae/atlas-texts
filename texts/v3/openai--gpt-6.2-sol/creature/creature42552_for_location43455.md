The smallest animals in the room are the ones with the longest legs. They hatch inside the hollow legs of their parents, and for a while there are two sets of joints to a leg, one moving inside the other. When the young come out they can stand across a mortar seam without wetting their mouths in it. This matters. The first thing they eat is one another, and a seam gives the shorter ones somewhere to go.

Those that survive to grow heavy come to the floor. Their legs thicken and fold along their sides. Seen by the light of a neighbor, an old one is hard to distinguish from a little heap of husks. It can lift a brick at one corner, though it takes most of a year to do it. Under the corner it lays a single egg. The warmth that leaks past the egg draws the flat animals, which settle around it and in time hold the brick up themselves. The old one goes off without waiting. There are bricks in the room that have not rested on clay in a hundred generations.

The flat animals have mouths all along their undersides. When they move they leave rows of little clean ovals. They get something to eat from the warm water as it passes through the mortar, but there is more of it in the mortar itself. A good seam can support three or four of them abreast. At the door the blunt-mouthed kind eat across the seam instead of along it. They swallow the soft part and hold the grit in their cheeks until there is enough to be worth carrying. A small white animal with its front legs over the edge of a step may stay like that for days. Then its cheeks work and a little clay sifts out beneath it. It backs up carefully, feeling for the edge after it has already left it.

The hanging bodies are not asleep. A current too slight to stir the grit will put a crease in one of them. The crease travels from end to end and the animal makes a slow turn, bringing the crease uppermost. On some nights, if night is a word for anything here, nearly all of them turn. The legs on the ceiling draw in to let them pass. A body that brushes a pillar leaves a patch of clear jelly in the thumb groove. The jointed animals come down to eat it. They will pass a fresh patch to get to one that has been there a while.

Each of the hanging animals has, a little below its middle, a band of skin that does not stretch. The band is very narrow on those born in the room. One of the largest has a band wide enough to see even when the rest of it disappears in the dark. There is a grain of red clay caught in it, much coarser than anything the animals bring out of the mortar. The grain came from a broken basket corner far down the ridge. The animal picked it up when it was small enough to fit into the impression left by a strand of wicker.

It used to go out often. Now it makes the short descent past the sill only when the water coming through the floor is warmer than usual. Outside, it lets its lower end hang until the band pulls taut. It can remain there long enough for the animals on the steps to empty their cheeks and go back in. Then it turns. In the room its turns are narrow and begin at the lower end; out there it rolls over all at once, and for a moment the clay grain is at the top.

The blue lights belong to the jointed ones. Most flashes happen when a foot finds a place already occupied. The animal underneath lights first. The other waits with its foot raised, sometimes for so long that it puts the foot down in exactly the same place without anything further happening. But there are also flashes from inside the bricks, where the young are coming out. Those are very faint. An animal upside down on the ceiling will answer one that nothing on the floor appears to have seen.

Sometimes a little one makes it all the way to the sill before its legs have hardened. It stands lengthwise in the empty lane, every foot on the warm brick, and seems for a while to be the only animal there. If a hanging body passes over it, it reaches up. Usually there is too much water between them. Once in a while a foot catches in the soft skin, and the little animal is lifted off the sill with its other feet still making the motions of walking. It may be carried as far as the first step. There it lets go, drops among the white animals on the edge, and has to find its way up by the warmth.
