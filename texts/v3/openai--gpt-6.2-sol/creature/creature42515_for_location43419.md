There are ewes on the underside of the basin. In the morning, when the pools are low, you can sometimes hear them knocking their horns against the rock.

They are small, even for mountain sheep, and have a fleece the color of a spoon worn thin. They graze the beads at the ends of the hanging water. There is something in those beads that does not occur in the pools above: a skin, almost too fine to see, which wrinkles when a ewe puts her mouth to it. She takes the skin and leaves the water. A good ewe can go along a whole thread without breaking it. The lambs come away with their ears wet and nothing to eat.

The walls are for the lambs. A newborn can get up and walk on either face of a shelf, but it has not yet learned the edge. Its forefeet go round, its hind feet stay where they are, and for a moment it is standing on both sides of the mountain. Then it falls toward whichever side its mother is on. Down below, where she generally is, there may be no rock for a long way. The low walls give the little ones something to follow until they come to a broad place. The crosswise stones at the ends are rubbed smooth by their turning.

Three people tend the flock. They have a house under the shelf with the dry patch, though house is generous for two rooms with a wool curtain between them. The ceiling is low by the door and high at the back, where the rock bells out. They keep their milk there in shallow pans. When somebody walks across the dry patch overhead, the cream trembles in the pans, and the youngest of the three puts a spoon down and listens. She likes to know whether it is a sheep or a visitor. A visitor usually stands still for a while, which a sheep will not do on dry stone.

In the late afternoon one of the three comes up the stair. Coming from their side, you put your thumb in the cup before you can see where to put your foot. The oldest has a swollen thumb and uses a knuckle now. She brings a sack for the lambs that have got themselves stranded above. You can hear them in it as she goes back down, each making its small offended noise. She brings up things, too: a cracked pan, sweepings, a stool with one leg gone. The broken things are set out on a shelf in the sun, in case anyone thinks of a use for them. Nothing dries there, but she keeps hoping.

Not every thread will bear a sheep. The shepherds know by the way a bead moves which ones go all the way down into the weather. On those they sometimes lose a ewe for a week. She comes back fat, with seeds caught in the wool beneath her jaw, and the others follow her about smelling them. The youngest once teased the seeds out and planted them in the grit behind a wall. Two came up. One was grazed before she got a good look at it. She put a broken pan over the other every evening, and it grew pale and tall and never opened its leaves.

There is a bell in the house that nobody wears. It belonged to a ewe who used to bring the flock in without anybody going out for them. All evening you would hear the scatter of the other bells, the little hurried sounds from wherever the grazing was good. Then hers would come from under the doorstep, one knock as she lowered her head. The oldest still leaves the curtain pegged back until the last of the light. It costs her nothing in summer. In winter the snow comes up from below and lies in a narrow drift across the floor, and in the morning she sweeps it out.
