The people of the plain have a way of carrying a sleeping child that leaves the child’s feet bare. At the first clucking of the little holes, they set the child down. Even a very young one will wake when water touches both soles at once. It is better to learn that feeling in the arms of somebody who has time to pick you up again.

There are stretches where nobody has a shelter of their own. A family uses whichever one the water leaves within reach, and if the horn cup is full when they arrive, they drink and put it back under the drip. Things too large to carry are wedged under the roof slabs. A stranger feeling about overhead might find a dry wick, a fish spine long enough to use as a needle, or a child’s shoe stiff with salt. A good slab has a little room above the fingers. You can tell which shelters have been built by people who have spent a night in one and which were built by people who wanted to get the work over with.

The flat animals in the pools are called knees. A live knee, turned over, has more legs than there seems room for and a mouth that keeps opening after the legs have stopped. The small ones are almost clear. When the mineral grains come in, you can watch a meal go through one as a pale cord. The old ones leave their shells wherever they die, and other knees sometimes settle under those shells, two or three to a roof. What looks like an exceptionally large animal, one that does not stir when your shadow falls across it, may be a dead one with a great deal of life underneath.

The long-legged hunters eat knees as well as fish. They turn them with the tip of the bill and wait for the legs to unfasten. They have trouble with a knee that has got hold of another knee. At a good pool you may see a hunter lift a whole struggling chain, then set it down rather carefully and try again.

No one has found a nest. At the very lowest water, the hunters walk into holes that have broad sloping mouths, folding their legs so far that from behind they appear to be sitting down at every step. They come out ahead of the returning air. Sometimes a young one comes with them, small enough to walk upright where the others crouch. For its first few tides it keeps knocking the end of its bill on the stone. On open water it waits for an older bird to lift its head and lifts its own, usually a little late.

There is something else that comes out of the holes, though it takes a long time to see one whole. At low water you find lengths of it hanging from the walls: a pale, puckered strap with one edge fixed to the stone. The straps belong to animals that spend the draining tide folded into cracks. When the water rises over them they ease themselves out and hang across the current in loose loops. The grain that catches on them goes slowly along the puckered edge to a dark place at the root. Most never reach the surface. In a few shallow holes they grow so close to the lip that a spout brings the ends up into daylight. They twist there for an instant, pink and surprisingly broad, and the falling water takes them back.

People cut loose the ones that have been torn from their cracks and wash them in the last of the ebb. They are tough except at the root. In a shelter you may be offered the tender piece before you have made out what anyone is eating. The rest is put aside for mending baskets. After a season in use, a basket develops a pale band just above the bottom, where the old bindings have given way one at a time.

There are people who seldom go farther than the next three shelters. They know every trap in that reach and can tell by putting a wrist into the water whether a gap has opened in a wall. Others make long crossings to see a sister or fetch a slab, and sometimes stay away through so many tides that somebody goes looking. Looking consists mostly in sitting where two routes diverge and waiting for the water to go down. Calling is of little use. Even in the quiet interval a voice travels strangely over the stone; you can hear a word quite distinctly and turn to the wrong person.

When the holes are blowing, two people working a trap keep track of each other by touch. One holds out a hand without looking up. The other puts a stone in it, or takes the hand and lays it on a place where water is getting through. At the shelters they speak against one another’s ears, but it is tiring to spend an evening that way. Often they sit with their knees touching and do not speak. A child who has been sent out of the rain will go from knee to knee in the dark until someone makes a space.

On the incoming tide there is a little fish that does not go with the rest. It works landward along the shallowest channels and sometimes gets into a shelter through the entrance. By then the people have taken their feet out of the water. The fish goes around beneath them, nosing the submerged stones, and finds the trickle from the horn cup where it runs over the floor. It will hold there for much of the flood, its head in the falling fresh water and its nearly invisible tail in the tide. Someone reaching for the cup feels it brush a knuckle. They tip the cup back a little so the trickle does not stop.
