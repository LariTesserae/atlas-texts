The people in the second dish know one another by sounds that would not carry in a wider town. A woman who works the washing floor knows when the boy across the lane has got his arithmetic wrong by the way he puts down his pencil. She has never been in his house. At the end of the school year he sets a piece of cake on her sill, having first tested the reach with an empty plate. By the time she gets home the icing has taken on enough water to run between the crumbs.

There are families with a person in each of the three factories, and families that have made only one thing for as long as anybody knows. The latter are not always the most knowledgeable about what they make. The best judge of felt in town is a retired papermaker who spent thirty years listening to what happened to a belt when the felt beneath its bearings grew thin. She is nearly deaf now. When they ask her to come in, she takes off one shoe and stands on the floor in her stocking.

The children know where the trees are. They can go a long way without touching a paving stone, following the buried trunks by the give of the moss. In the gardens they are allowed to do this so long as they leave the branches alone. A branch turned aside to make room for a tank may spend the rest of somebody's childhood finding its way back to the surface. There is one in a kitchen off the station road; it comes up between the flags behind the stove. The people there used to cut it back. Now they hang the dishcloth on it. Its leaves are small and very dark, and it has never put out a leaf on the side toward the stove.

The two black birds are not a pair in the sense people mean when they say so. One is the other's grown young, and the older bird has a bad foot. When the freight sheds were still busy, it learned to open the paper wrappings the men left on the loading ledge. The younger bird has never seen a man eat there. It still goes through the motions with the strips of paper that blow down from the mill, standing on a corner and pulling until the wet paper parts. Once there was a crust inside one. Nobody knows who put it there.

Most of what lives on the plateau is too small to be seen from the height at which people go about their work. There are little grazing things in the water that hangs beneath the rails. A drop can shrink all morning without falling, and in its last hour the grazers crowd into the curve at its bottom. There are pale worms in the moss of the roofs, and in the black prints left by the sedges there are eggs that will wait through a dry summer. The roof moths spend much more of their lives crawling than flying. Their caterpillars feed among the dead leaves under the tanks, where a hand laid flat on the soil can feel the warmth of a day's water long after dark.

Something lives in the pools that stand out of level. The first person to bring any of it indoors was a girl who thought she had found frog spawn. What she carried home in a saucer looked like the clear part of an egg with a little soot in it. By morning the saucer was dry in the middle. The water had gathered at one edge, where it stood just high enough to wet the underside of a spoon laid across the rim. The girl put her finger in it and the whole spoon rang against the china. She took the saucer back because it belonged to her grandmother and she did not want to explain how it had cracked.

There are a number of such stories, nearly all about things that had to be taken back or mended. What can be seen in the pools is a faint lacing just under the surface, easiest to make out when a dark coat is held over the water. It runs from stem to stem. It takes a long time to draw in, and where it draws in the water rises with it. Along the edges of the larger pools it is possible to watch one patch go slack while another tightens. On a day when a wagon goes over the open peat, the threads all loosen at once. The pollen does not move until the wagon is out of sight.

The town has had time to build around this without much agreement about what it is building around. The drains on both sides of the old streets are useful. That is enough to keep them clear. In the newer lanes, where there is only one drain and the foundations are deeper, people sometimes come downstairs to find water standing against the inside of a door. They put on their boots and go out the back. A little later, at work, they can tell when the water has gone, because the chain at the bottom of the lane starts striking the wall again.

At closing time there is still somebody on the washing floor. He has to wait for the last of the water to come down from the header tank, and he spends the interval washing his hands in it. Through the tall window he can see one of the black birds on the freight bed, picking its way between the ties. Farther out, the pools have begun to empty into one another. He knows the order in which they do it, though he could not draw their shapes. One of them stays bright after the rest have gone dark. In summer there is often a moth reflected in it, crossing and recrossing a little patch of sky, and nothing to be seen above the water unless he goes outside and stands quite still.
