Something lives under the warm square of pavement. You can see it best on nights when the lower street is dry. The gutter runs then, too, but only for the length of one shopfront, and what comes out of it is warm enough to take the chill off the shopkeeper’s bare feet when she pulls down her shutter. She has asked the water office about it twice. The second time they sent her a drawing of the room. She recognized the pavement. Her father used to sell pears from a stall with one bad wheel.

The thing under the pavement has no shape you could lift out. At one time of day it has a skin against the underside of the stone. At another, that skin is gone and there are six or seven taut little surfaces spanning the fault, some no wider than a hair. They come apart without tearing. When the first one lets go, the note sounds at the handrail. The note from the floor, much later, is the last one taking hold.

It feeds in the evening. As the walls come together, it moves the little surfaces down into the narrowing crack. They are sticky on one side. The bright dust from the two faces of the fault adheres to them, and when they draw back under the pavement you can see them go cloudy with it. This is what darkens the fresh violet. The creature takes very little at a time. In the old stone, where it has been at work longest, it has eaten a passage that follows the bad wheel’s trough from underneath. There is nearly a finger’s depth of stone left above it. It could eat through, but when it gets that close to the air it turns aside.

It is not alone. Once, when a crew lifted the conduit strip, they found three small warm squares on its underside. They laid the strip down on its edge while they worked. Over the course of the morning the squares moved to the edge that touched the floor. One made it across. One cooled. The third stayed warm until they put the strip back, and by the next visit there was no telling it from the rest of the metal. The woman who maintained that stretch of conduit began bringing a pad to lay the strip on, so that whichever face was down would stay warm. She never saw the squares again. She kept the pad in the delivery recess until the stone pinched it fast.

The night vapor is when the young can travel. They cannot make the passage themselves. They lie along the old curb until the room widens, and the first wetness carries them, flat and almost weightless, into whichever street is taking water. Most go down the drain. One spent nine years under a public drinking fountain, making a warm place just large enough for a person to set a heel. The paving around it was lifted last spring and brought up the mountain. It is in a wall now, five streets higher. On cold mornings people lean against it while they wait for the bakery to open.

The one in the lowest room has been there since the pavement was laid flat in the rain. It had a great deal more room then. These days, when the stone approaches the red marks, there is scarcely space left for it to draw itself out of the fault. For a few seconds it is all under the bench, pressed up against the place where the pear seller used to stand. Then the crack opens, and it puts out the first of its thin surfaces. The warm square cools from the edges inward. Down on the lower street, the shopkeeper hears her gutter run and lifts the shoes she has left out to dry.
