The pika has a summer door and a winter door, though both open under the same stone. The summer door is the one you see it use, a slit on the dry side of the sill. The other leads down among blocks too large for the frost to shift. By late summer it has tucked a little hay into several of the spaces there. It does not take the sedge nearest the water if it can help it. It goes farther up the sill for a few stems that have dried standing, cuts them, and brings them back crosswise. Sometimes a stem catches at the entrance and the pika backs out and turns its head. There is no other way to get it in.

The geese have no such arrangements. They know the valley as a place where, for a few days, they can get at the pale bases of the sedges without having to reach far through water. A family that came down together is still together in the flock, although the young are now about the size of their parents. One of them keeps trying the black crescents behind the stones. It draws up a mouthful of sand, shakes its head, and tries the next crescent. By the time they leave, the shore is stippled with the holes their bills have made. Water comes up in the holes before the last goose has lifted off.

There are residents in places that don't look like places at all. On the underside of a flat stone in the seep is a brown film so thin that it scarcely changes the colour of the rock. It is thickest at one corner, where there is enough current to keep the silt from settling but not enough to scour the stone clean. In that corner, between grains you could not pick up separately, tiny animals graze. A change in the sheet of water can leave them dry by evening. A stone a hand's breadth away, bare yesterday, may have water over it for the next ten years.

The midge larvae have a little more room to move. They feed where fine sediment lies soft over the coarser bed, and some come up to the ice while there is still water beneath it. In a clear patch you can see one fold itself nearly double, straighten, and fold again. In spring the adults will rise out of the pools on days when the air is barely warm enough for them to fly. Most will stay low. A few will get caught in a current going up the face of the sill, and the pika, sitting out with a stem in its mouth, will have them about its ears.

A wader can find a larva in water that looks opaque even from the height of its own head. It works the mud with the tip of its bill, taking two or three steps between probes. When it stops, it is often not watching the water. It is watching another wader. There may be a mile of shore by evening and only a few yards worth feeding on, and a bird that finds those yards will spend some of its time driving its neighbour off them. After dark the wind can move the whole feeding place. The birds return in the morning to the old spot first.

Below the most established patch of moss, the rock is younger than the moss makes it look. Lift the edge of the mat and there is very little earth, just a close packing of dark stems and grit. The living tips are green even after a night of frost. They hold a little water back when the seep falls, and when it rises, loose grains lodge among them. A sedge has got a root into one such patch. Its lowest blade is worn nearly through where it rubs on a stone, but the newest blade comes up inside it, unmarked.

By the time the pass closes, the waders have gone to stand on the lee side of the sill, each with one foot drawn up into its feathers. Under their feet, deep in the loose rock, the pika moves a few inches through a dry space. The last open seep is on the other side. Along the underside of its new ledge a thread of water keeps running all night, passing over the brown corner of the stone and out again into the marsh.
