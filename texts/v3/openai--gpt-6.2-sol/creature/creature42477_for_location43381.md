Something sleeps under the fern bed with one end of itself in the grit and the other in a crack in the rock. It is white where it lies in the rock and the colour of old tea where the water has been getting at it. It has been there longer than the room. When the builders cut back the ledge, they took off a piece of it about the size of a thumbnail. One of them picked the piece out of the slurry and put it on the bench, thinking it was the soft part of a fossil. By the end of his shift it had drawn itself up into a knot. He went home with it stuck to the underside of his lunch tin. The rest of the animal spent three nights feeling over the bench.

Most evenings it comes out as far as the first seam. In the wet it is hard to see. It spreads so thin that the light goes through it and shows up the grains of grit it has brought along from the bed. When the floor opens it gathers those grains in, and for a moment there is a little ridge across the slab, like the line of dirt left by a door that hasn’t been opened all winter. Then the seam shuts. On the warm ceramic the ridge lifts itself and sets off on six or seven clear feet. There are never quite the same number. A foot that finds itself in a drop stays there until the rest of the animal is far enough away to pull it free.

It feeds at the mouths of the gutters. Little Orchard brings it the soft skin from fallen fruit, worn nearly to nothing in the pipes. The Parade brings a good deal of soap, which it backs away from no matter how hungry it is. At Lime Street it puts up a narrow lip against the stamped letters and waits. Wet flour catches in the hollows of the M. The animal works at those two hollows separately. When it has finished, the lime in *Lime Street* is still white enough to read from the bench, if you know where to look.

There is a smaller one now. For some weeks it lived entirely on the underside of the longest frond. It wouldn’t eat the leaf. It lay along the rib and drank from each drop as it formed, moving down a little at a time. When it got too heavy for the tip, it fell. The larger one was halfway to Lime Street. It stopped and put down all its feet, including two so small they hadn’t touched the floor before. The little one went on sliding. It was the floor that caught it: the seams closed and the water was gone, and there it was on the warm slab with nowhere to slide to.

Since then the two have made the trip most evenings. The small one takes longer over the gutters, and sometimes the big one goes home without it. A film of water comes back across the slabs. The little animal, which is no bigger than the piece the builder carried away, has learned to climb out of the film onto the low end of a fern stem. It waits there with the front of its body raised. Now and then a tram goes round the curve and the whole stem trembles under it. It puts out a foot, finds nothing, and draws the foot in again. By morning it will have crossed the last inch. The fern will be standing straight.
