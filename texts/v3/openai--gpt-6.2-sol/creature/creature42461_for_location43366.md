Something lives in the space between the last stair and the mountain. It’s no thicker than the gap, but it’s longer than the stair is wide. At either end it folds back on itself. If you put a finger in the gap it folds a little further. It doesn’t touch you. The water that comes out around your finger is warmer than the water in the square.

For a long time I thought the animals in the conduit were pieces of it. They have the same color, and when they come out of their shells they’re flatter than you’d expect. But I’ve seen one go up to the last stair and wait there, its shell tipped back like a person looking up at a house. A fold came out and passed over the shell, and the animal went away down the stair. It took the shallow treads one at a time. It fell the whole depth of the first deep one and had to right itself.

There are things the animals will not cross. One is the strip of light in the shelter. Another is a place in the smallest house where the kitchen door used to open. The door is gone, but they go around the whole sweep of it, even if they have to climb the wall. At the turn of the stair they gather under the shelf. The smallest can fit into the place where the extra shaving was taken off. You might mistake their shells for the heads of nails, except that there are more of them every year.

The thing at the top of the stair comes down sometimes. I know because the gap is cold then, and because the three stops that were elsewhere become quite difficult to walk past. At one of them you have to lift your foot much higher than you meant to. At another you put it down gently, without thinking, as you would on a landing where somebody was asleep. The thing goes around the loop several times. Each time it passes the shelter, the light goes out from one end to the other. The last name takes longer to go dark than it takes to read.

Once I was at the rail when it came by. It laid a fold along the top bar, where the metal is flat, and stayed there. Up close it wasn’t pale all over. There were little brown marks in it, as in the skin on the inside of a wrist. Farther along, where it hung over the drop, I could see through it. Nothing rose from below. After a while it took its weight off the bar, a little at a time, from one end to the other.

Going back through the square, it passed beneath a bubble I’d left there. The bubble moved sideways the width of a window. I waited for it to rise or come back, but it stayed where it was.

By then the thing was going up the public stair. It could have gone straight to the gap; there was nothing above the stair to stop it. Instead it laid a fold on each tread and drew the rest of itself up after it. On the steep part the folds were very short. I could have kept pace with it there, even tired.
