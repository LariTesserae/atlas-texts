At the oldest feeding place, three of the roots belong to the leaf. The fourth belongs to a woman who lives across the avenue.

You could not tell her root from the others with the pad of a finger. With a nail you might catch it drawing back. It runs under the paving and up the back wall of her house, where it divides into a number of little cords. Most of these end in the wall. Two go on into her. She can move from one side of the room to the other without pulling on them. If she wants to come to the door, she has to turn sideways.

She does this after the report, while the houses are still answering. She puts her cheek against the jamb. One of the last notes to die is from a room two streets down that has no opening left. She lived in that room as a child. There is a shallow place in its floor where she and her brother used to lie heel to heel, taking turns pushing each other out of it. She can tell when the floor puts on a layer. The note shortens, though not by enough that anyone who hadn't slept there would hear it.

Her brother lives in the square. There is very little of him above the paving. If you went down on your knees you could find the seam where the skin of his back goes into the floor, and farther along you would find a hand. The hand is loose and can be lifted. He likes it laid down with the palm upward. His sister used to come and do this for him, before her cords grew too short. Now the report turns it over every few winters. It is a good winter when it happens to turn the right way.

There are younger inhabitants. They fit into houses that the old people remember being able to stand in. They spend a great deal of time with the crowns of their heads against the lintels, not because they are shut in but because that is where they can feel a person coming. When two of them visit, the guest lies in the street and puts a foot over the sill. They talk by tightening the long muscles of the leg. You can walk past such a visit and think there is nobody there. You can step over one and hear, in the scuff you make against the paving, how abruptly they both go still.

They don't eat the loose meal. A grain is too large for the passages in their mouths. They take what the roots have already taken in, and it reaches them slowly enough that a good feeding at one corner may not be felt in the next street until the cold is over. The woman at the avenue could take more than she does. Her root is the only one there that can lift its tip. She lifts it when she feels a grain coming, and sometimes the grain lodges underneath. For years she did this because her brother had liked to roll the grains down the slope. He could get one nearly to the square by blowing after it as he went. There was never enough air in one breath. He had to catch up to it and put his face down again.

There is a house the young ones use that has no lintel and hardly any wall. It is not a house any of the old people remembers. One of the young ones found the turn in the grain and lay down with her foot in it. Another came and put a foot beside hers. The floor there is wearing a little hollow for their heels. At the far end, where neither can reach, a rib has begun to rise. It is no thicker than a thread. When the ceiling gives, it flicks against the paving, and the two of them lift their feet to let it finish.
