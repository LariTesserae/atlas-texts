The brown insect has not come to drink. It goes around the rim of the cup taking off the dry edge in little square bites. When it has nearly finished the circuit it turns and feels its way back to a place it has missed. The ticking is its jaw striking the stiff part of the leaf. There is enough of that edge to feed it for most of a day, and by the time it comes round again the first bites have filled in.

Its young live in the drop overhead. You can see them if you get your eye below the point and look up: three curved threads, each with a bead at one end. The drop does not fall, but it turns a little as they move. When one is ready to come out, it climbs the inside of the drop to the leaf. For the first part of its life it walks upside down. It is pale then, and broad enough across the back to be mistaken for a bit of leaf that has not yet taken on its color.

There are larger animals on the slopes. You can stand within reach of one without seeing it. It lies with its belly well down in the stems and its back level with their tips. The stems bend toward it on either side, so there is no bare outline. What gives it away is a small patch of growth all leaning at once. Then another patch leans a foot farther on.

At the root lane it has to lift itself clear. It is about as long as a person lying down, with six short legs and a blunt, almost neckless head. The underside is soft and white. It crosses with the stiff, careful gait of an animal accustomed to having something under its belly. On the other side it pushes its head into the brush until it finds the tender end of a stem. It takes that end between its lips and draws the whole stem gently through them. After it passes, the stem stands up again, stripped of the little animals that were living on it.

You might suppose there were few of these grazers until you spend some time at a fork. The ones going uphill take the smaller root onto the roof. They fit their feet over its sides, and where it turns under, they turn with it. A long way above the floor, one will stop to feed and let two legs hang. From below those legs look like the ends of a split stalk. Nothing falls from it. Even the thin skin it sheds from its feet is taken off in strips by insects before it comes loose.

The clay walls belong to people. They are low because a person sitting on the ground can reach over them. On the side away from the path, where you would expect to find a sheltered place to sit, there are shallow heel marks in the clay. The people sat facing the lane with their feet up on the wall. It was a place to wait and listen for somebody coming. The recess held a drinking bowl; the lining kept the unglazed foot of the bowl from drawing water into the clay.

A woman comes along the lane with the bowl tucked against her side. Behind her is a boy carrying a wet strip of stalk folded over both hands. He has to take two steps for each of hers, but he does not hurry. Coming to the first wall, she steps off the root and says something to him that you cannot hear. He says something back. Then she puts the bowl in its recess and sits, and he begins to ease the strip of stalk open on the warm clay. Inside it are four of the young insects, past the age of living in a drop. They walk along the wet fold and will not step out of it. The boy turns the stalk a little so that the light falls differently. He has brought them to show her that one of them has brown feet.

The woman looks as long as he needs her to. Then she takes the bowl to the next cup and dips just the lip of it in. She leaves most of the water. On her way back she rests a hand on the last wall, but not where the thumbprint is. There is a little polished place farther along, at the height her hand comes down as she passes. The boy puts his hand there too, though he has to raise his elbow.

They live beyond the unfinished ridge, where the stems are tall enough to part and let a person through at ground level. The passage closes at shoulder height behind them. There are sleeping mats laid on the warm stone, and folded leaves hung edgewise between them to keep the light out of people's eyes. Close by, a grazer feeds on the far side of the stems. You cannot hear it from the mats. You can see, low down through the parting, the place where it has put one white foot, and then the stems slowly straighten.
