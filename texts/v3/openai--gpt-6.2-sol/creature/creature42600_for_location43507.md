In the hollow at the foot of the new slope, the minnows face a gap no wider than an alder leaf. Water comes through it fast enough to roll grains of sand. The fish hold just to one side of the moving grains, taking what comes past: a midge larva, a shred of something too soft to keep its shape. Every so often one drops back into the pond. From above, the hollow looks too shallow to have anything in it.

The beaver that packed the gap in the dam is an old female with a notch in one ear. She sleeps through the middle of the day in a chamber scarcely higher than the pond. The floor is dry, though the sticks beneath it are wet, and the chips she has brought in for bedding have gone soft and dark where she lies. Two kits sleep with their noses in her flank. They are old enough to go out by themselves but still come back smelling of the particular things they have tried to eat. One has spent much of the morning stripping the leaves off a fern. Its sibling has learned to bring a twig inside and turn it crosswise in the entrance, where it has to be pulled out again by somebody bigger.

There are beavers here that have never climbed above the first belt of ledges. They know the basin chiefly at the height of a floating leaf. A place that looks open from the wall may be impassable to them at the end of summer; a place that looks solid may have a good deep passage under the roots. In their oldest pond a willow has grown out over the water and rooted again at the tip. The young beavers go under it without slowing. When the water is low the old female goes around. The underside is rough with roots, and once, coming through with a branch, she caught her ear there.

A water shrew lives in the lowest of the dry lodges. Dry is relative: he can reach water from his nest without going outside, down a passage between two sticks that were laid there before he was born. When he dives, the air in his fur makes him look silver, though on land he is almost black. He works along the submerged wood with his nose down, finding caddis larvae in their little cases. What he cannot eat he leaves on a ledge inside the lodge. A vole sometimes sits on the ledge to wash its face. The two have met there and both have backed away, the vole into the sticks and the shrew into the water.

The trout below the broadest dam spends much of the day under a shelf of bank that ought to have fallen in. The rush roots hold it. In the cool he can stay a long while with scarcely a movement, but when a wren knocks a spider out of the root plate he comes up so quickly that the spider seems to vanish before it touches the water. The wren has five young in the root plate. They have begun to put their heads out, and the smallest one opens its mouth at the trout’s rise. It opens its mouth at the drip from the root above it, too. For several days more almost anything that moves will be worth a try.

There are animals that come only as far down as the ledges. A fox has a resting place on one of the old ones, where a projecting lip keeps off both rains. She comes down after the swallows have gone and watches the dams from there. Once she walked out along a bank and stood a long time with one forefoot raised. The bank was sound, but the grass beyond it lay over water, and she could hear a vole eating on the other side. At last she went back the way she had come. The vole had been sitting under the bank all along, cutting a sedge stem into lengths it could carry home.

In winter the ponds wear ice along their margins, and much of the town goes about its business underneath. The beavers have sticks laid by in water deep enough to stay open below the ice. The trout draws back from the shallows. The wrens are gone, and the wrens’ nests, which looked so well hidden in summer, can be seen from halfway across the basin. In one of them a spider will pass the winter. It is a small nest for five birds and a great deal of shelter for a spider.

Tonight the old female will come out by the approach nearest the fall of stone. She will find that the new run has taken some mud with it and left a root bare. She will put her nose against the root, then go upstream for a cutting. She will eat the bark from the thinner end before bringing it back. By the time she presses it into place, the minnows will have moved out of the hollow and into the narrow slack water behind her hind feet. One will stay there until she pushes off. Then it will go back to the gap, where there is still enough current to bring it something.
