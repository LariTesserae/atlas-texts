The people who live on the saddle put their beds against the inside walls. The outside walls are wet, sometimes on only one side of a joint, and a child can lie awake watching a dark patch come through the stone in the shape of a hand or a head. By morning it will have reached the floor and lost its likeness. The children give these patches names anyway. In one house a leak above the flour chest is called Grandmother, though Grandmother herself sleeps dry in the next room and objects to it.

There are fewer people than the stairs would suggest. At supper you can count the occupied houses by the steam at the doors. Still, a person carrying a hot pot from one house to another will take the square turns of the streets rather than cut across the open ground. The shortcuts are for going empty-handed. This distinction is not taught so much as acquired, usually on the day a child is first trusted with something hot.

In the mornings three or four people go down to lift silt. One holds the basket while the others work the scoops. The silt comes up black and glossy, with white root ends in it and sometimes a little transparent creature that folds itself in two. They pick these out with a stalk and put them back. Nobody agrees on what they become. There are also small, blunt fish under the lowest step. If you sit there with your heels in the water, they come and taste the salt on your ankles, one after another, making the same mistake.

The big courts have uses that don’t leave much behind. Reeds are sorted in one, roofs’ worth at a time; in another, two sisters have been teaching their father to walk again. Each day they set his stool a paving stone farther from the stair. He knows what they’re doing and moves it back when they go for water. There is a court where the children roll clay balls into a joint in the paving. They used to be able to get them out with a bent reed, but the joint has opened just enough to take the balls beyond reach. They keep rolling them in. A remarkably good ball will draw children from the farthest houses to watch it go.

The woman who dresses the new stair blocks works under a lean-to above the cistern. Most of the stone she needs has fallen lately enough that she can match one broken face to the next. She chalks a number on each piece before she has it dragged away. Once, fitting a tread, she turned up a hollow with a fern growing in it, pale and curled from the dark. She left the block on its side by the lean-to. The fern has gone green, and someone has put a cup beside it for watering, though it’s hard to imagine the fern needs the help.

At dusk, if there’s still warmth in the steps, people sit out after they’ve washed their bowls. Conversation travels farther then than anyone intends. A man at the cistern says he’s thinking of cutting his hair, and a voice from two courts up says it’s about time. Later the sisters’ father comes down alone. He rests at each landing, looking out over the valley as if the view were what had stopped him. Nobody offers him a hand until he reaches the last step, where the stone is wet. Then several people do, and he takes the smallest hand among them.
