There is an animal on the water side of the leaf that has opened against the far wall. You can pass a finger over it and feel only the leaf. If you keep your finger there, the animal will begin to clean it.

It works along the edge of the nail first. Its mouth is wide enough to take in one of the little shreds of skin that lift when a hand has been in water a long time. It pulls steadily, without the small backward jerks of the shrimp. When it comes to skin that is still alive it lets go. You may feel this as a succession of cool points, although the water is the same temperature throughout.

The animal has two dark parts. One is the mouth, which shuts to a line. The other is a little knot farther back, where what it eats collects. Between them it is so clear that the edge of the leaf continues through it without a break. Its underside is covered with short feet. They do not all let go at once, even to cross the width of a hair. If the leaf were turned over you would find the animal on the same side of it, still crossing.

It spends much of its time attending to a root that has not yet reached the wall. Where the root will arrive there is a patch of clay that the animal keeps bare. The old skin of the root sloughs away as it grows, and sometimes this is what the animal eats. Sometimes the skin arrives with nothing inside it, already worn through in places, and the animal draws it down over the edge of the leaf and leaves it there. There are several layers of this stuff under the leaf now. The oldest has taken the impression of the grit.

In the clear season the animal lays its eggs. It puts them where a root divides, one in each fork, and closes the fork over them with its feet. No two are laid on the same root. The eggs near the entrance hatch while the clear tips are still on the leaves. Those farther in take longer. One has been there long enough for the two branches that hold it to die and be grown through by another root. The young inside is alive. Now and then it puts its mouth to the shell, opens it very wide, and shuts it again.

A newly hatched one can be carried out. There is sometimes one on the shrimp when it comes to feed, walking along the clear part of a joint. The shrimp does not seem to feel it. It draws the stalk through its mouthparts, and the young animal walks in the other direction. When the shrimp backs into its cleft, the young one steps off onto a leaf. For a while it goes wherever a leaf touches another leaf. Then it finds a root and follows it inward. It feeds on the way. The knot behind its mouth grows dark.

Once, while your finger is against the far wall, you may hear the drop. The animal stops working. For the length of time it takes the sound to die, all its feet stand free of the leaf. Its body lifts a little. Then the feet come down again, one after another, beginning at the mouth. The last two come down on the tip of the root.

After that, if you put your nail to the wall, there is a soft point among the grains. The animal goes around it when it cleans. In time the root thickens enough to raise the leaf a little, and the animal can get underneath, where there has never been room for it before. There is a great deal to eat there. It takes the old skins first. It leaves the living surface of the leaf.
