There is a woman who washes the market benches. She works from the shallow side, kneeling with her skirt tucked up, and does the undersides first. For a cloth she uses one of the pale films that gather in the courts. She lifts it on both hands. It hangs almost to the water, though once she has folded it over her thumb it is thick enough to scour with. She leaves it floating when she is done. By morning there is no fold in it.

Her house is in the quarter you can only enter from the east. There are seven people in that quarter now, if you count the old man who sleeps in the cistern. He has a room at the far end of it with a dry shelf for his mat. When the gutter begins to run he gets up and puts his shoes on the shelf, then goes back to sleep. People fetching water know to dip their jars on the near side. He has never asked them to be quiet.

They are easy to mistake for parts of the buildings. At night a hand left on a sill gives off much the same warmth as the sill. Their backs have the dull red of brick where the water hasn’t bleached them. Most have gone pale in bands, and you can tell something about where a person has lived by how high the bands go. The woman who does the benches is white to the waist on her left side and only to the knee on her right. She has a little granddaughter who is still the same color all over. The girl has taken to standing in doorways with one foot on each side, waiting for a mark to come.

They go about with their hands open. They can tell a neighbor by the way the water moves against their palms, though a stranger is more difficult. If you come into the market at night, someone will put a warm hand under your elbow. On the shallow side it feels like an offer of help you don’t need. Then you take another step.

Once a week the girl brings a jar of drinking water to a man who lives on the west street. There is nothing wrong with his water; he likes to see her. On the way there she has six strides to make and can carry the full jar without setting it down. Coming home, with the jar empty, she has the whole quarter to walk through. She stops at the garden wall and tries to throw small things into the mouth of the jar. There are three blue tiles in it now, and a tooth of her own that she has been told not to lose.

Not everyone has someone to visit. In a court behind the market there is a pair of houses whose common wall has worn down to the height of a bed. A man sleeps beside it with his arm laid across the top. In the morning his hand is wet to the wrist. He keeps a towel on his side, under a bowl so it won’t slip into the water. The other house has been empty for years.

After rain, when the rooms are too hot to sleep in, some of them go out to the last steps. They sit with spaces between them, feeling for the first drops on the treads. The little girl has discovered that if she spreads her hand flat on the stone, the rain will tick all around it and not touch her. She can keep it there long after everyone else has gone in. When at last she lifts it, she feels underneath with her other hand. The print is dry, and a little warmer than the rest of the step. What she wants to know is whether it stays warm longer if she doesn’t touch it. So far she has always touched it.
