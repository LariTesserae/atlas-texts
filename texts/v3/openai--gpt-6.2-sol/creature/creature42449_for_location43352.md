On the third step down lives a woman who can tell which bridge she is under by what falls on her umbrella. The shells under Foundry Bridge drop the wings of insects, usually in pairs, still joined at the shoulder. Under the tram bridge it is mostly grit. Under the bridge where she lives, nothing falls until the first car in the morning. Then a whole night’s worth of refuse comes down at once.

Her name is Ada. She used to sort the catch, and still wakes at the hour when the first belt starts. Her grandson has that shift now. On his way home he brings her anything that comes up alive and won’t go down the grading slots. They keep a shallow basin on the kitchen table for these visitors. Most are gone by supper. You can carry a surprisingly large animal down a public staircase if you put a tea towel over it. People assume you’ve made something for somebody.

For most of one summer they had a bridge animal no bigger than a coat button. It wouldn’t stay in the basin. It climbed the wall behind the stove, turned itself over under the shelf, and began there to lay down the first low ridges of its shell. At dusk, when Ada switched on the light to see what she was cutting, it put out two limbs as fine as threads. One held the shelf. The other made little passes over the breadboard. It never caught anything while she was looking.

By winter its shell had grown around the stem of a wineglass. They couldn’t get the glass out without breaking it, and there was no reason to break it. Ada moved the other glasses to the cupboard. After that, if you came to her house on a quiet evening and stood by the stove, you could hear the soft knock of the empty glass against the wall as the animal reached for something.

The immense brown animals spend their first weeks in water deeper than the city’s lowest foundations. The people who work the screens know there are young down there because their calls come up through the steel grating in the floor. A young one can keep up a note for an astonishing time. An adult answers with two short notes, the second a little lower. New workers are apt to look up when this happens. It sounds like somebody on the floor above shifting a heavy piece of furniture, then setting it down.

In a rise year, the young come within sight of the tram. They have round heads and are so dark when wet that the eye disappears until they blink. For all their bulk they have trouble getting out of the water. A calf will lay its chin on a landing and stay there while the adults come up on either side. Eventually one of them stops broadside to the step. The calf gets its forefeet onto the adult’s submerged flank and pushes. Once it has the whole landing to stand on, it shakes itself, soaking people who thought they were well out of range.

Ada’s grandson was born in a rise year. His mother remembers less about the animals than about having to carry a pram up seventy steps because the riverside tram wasn’t running. He remembers his first rise as the year a calf slept against the roller door of the sorting shed. Nobody could get the door up. The shift drank tea outside for forty minutes and watched the calf’s ear fold and unfold in its sleep. Then an adult called from somewhere out in the canal, and the calf got up so abruptly that it left the shape of its head in the condensation on the door.

There are children on Ada’s stair who have never seen a rise. They know the big animals as a row of backs below the tramline, and the bridge animals as shells to be counted on the walk to school. They know the little grazers best. In the time it takes an adult to stand at a railing and judge whether the rain will reach the city, a child can watch one cross a pale track left by another. It doesn’t follow the track. It lifts the front of itself, reaches over the clean stone without touching it, and sets its mouth down on the far side. The children try giving them tiles from the repair yard, bright new pieces with corners sharp enough to cut a pocket. Nothing will eat off them. You have to leave a tile out on the step until it no longer looks new. By then, most children have forgotten whose tile it was.
