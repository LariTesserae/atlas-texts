There is an animal in the stair. It has been there long enough that the edge of the middle tread has grown around it, a little at a time, with each new deposit of lime. When the water is low you can put your finger into a shallow groove and touch its back. It feels like the stair except that it gives a little. If you take your finger away and put it back in the same place, the groove is empty.

The animal is not as small as that suggests. A piece of it lives under each tread, and another piece comes out under the platform some distance away. Between these parts it is scarcely thicker than wet paper. Where there is room it folds itself into blunt lobes, cream at the tips and rust-colored nearer the seam. In a broken stair farther out on the plain you can see the empty mold of one of these lobes. It has the shape of a hand held shut around something, though there is no provision for fingers.

On the long stroke the animal opens along its underside. Water goes through it and leaves behind the soft gray matter that otherwise collects in the blind ends of pipes. A lobe that has fed well will stay open into the interval when you can hear the rain. Then it shuts from one end to the other, with a series of little creases. Nothing of this is visible in the puddles. The mouths are all on the far side of the stone.

There are others. You can count six on a stretch of pavement by laying a hand on the slabs when the machines pause. They let go of the underside one after another. What comes through to the palm is very faint, and by the sixth you may be supplying the sensation yourself. They are not evenly spaced. Two may occupy opposite sides of a seam for years, close enough that a flake of scale passing between them is cleaned on both faces. Then there will be half a mile of sound joints with nothing in them.

Their young live higher up. Look under the lip of a loading platform, above the reach of the ordinary water, and there may be three or four white buttons in the wet. They are soft enough to come away on a sleeve. Up there they feed on the thin film that gathers on the concrete. As they grow they get too heavy for the roof of their little space. Some fall out on the open pavement, where they dry the first time the pumps leave it bare. The ones that live are nearest the wall. They sag until another part of them touches it, and then let go of the lip.

The stair is a good place for them. Even on the lowest cycle there is water in the angle behind the bottom tread. A young one can live in that angle for a long time before it is broad enough to reach a seam. For its first few years it has a round back. You could see it easily, if you knew to lie down on the platform and look back underneath yourself.

As the water reaches your boot, it also reaches a white button on the underside of the lowest projecting edge. The button has been out in the cold all through the long stroke. It loosens, hangs by a clear corner, and comes down. There is no splash. A moment later, in the brief quiet, you hear a tick from the stair. It is a grain of grit coming free where the old animal has drawn back from the edge. The young one settles in the space it left.
