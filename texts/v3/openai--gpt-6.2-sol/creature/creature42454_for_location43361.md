The smallest frogs live in the hoofprints of the grazers. A print made in the morning can have eggs in it by dark. The eggs lie in a clear sheet with the mud showing through, and when the next rain joins one print to another, the tadpoles wriggle across the submerged ridges. The frogs themselves are the colour of a sedge stem where it goes into the water. A calling male swells until his throat rests on the mud. If you put a finger down beside him, you can feel the call before he lets it out.

The ones under the roots at the rim are larger. They have pale toes and a fold of skin over each eye that sheds the drip from the ferns. They seldom go into the open water. Their young live in the wet earth under the roots, in pockets no deeper than the bowl of a spoon, and come out with legs and a little tail that lasts them a day or two. At night the old ones sit facing out over the drop. Moths come up the cliff in the moving air, and now and then one gets just far enough into the warmth to open its wings.

The grazers have four spreading toes on each foot. On firm ground they place the little back ones so delicately that you might take them for spurs, but on the flats all four go down, and the foot comes out with a sound like a stopper pulled from a bottle. They eat the young sedge below the joint, taking the white part and letting the rest fall. In places where they have fed for a long time, the discarded tops lie all the same way, combed by the slow movement of the water.

A calf is born with a dark streak along its spine. The yellow birds pay no attention to it until the streak fades, though it has the same flies as its mother and more trouble shaking them off. When a bird finally lands on it, the calf tries to look at both of its own shoulders at once. For a while that is enough to dislodge the bird. By the end of the rains the calf will stand asleep with one foot folded under and a bird working its way down the crease behind its ear.

The birds that break the snails are brown, with bare white skin about the bill. Each keeps to a few stones. The young can spend much of the morning carrying snails to a stone too soft to break them on, or striking them on the side that will not give. An old bird brings a snail in with its foot already drawn back into the shell, knocks the lip off with two blows, and eats. The stones are easy to find from the heaps of shells. In some of the oldest heaps the shells at the bottom have softened to a white paste in which the sedges grow particularly well.

There are fish in the returning water, though you generally see only their shadows. They are flat along the belly and have fins that lie against the rock like wet cloth. At the head of the fall they spread out to feed on the film that grows there. When one loses its hold, it folds its fins. You can sometimes pick it out in the falling water, dark and straight among the tumbling leaves. At the shelf upstream it opens them again. The young fish collect there, on the underside of a ledge where the returning water runs smooth for the width of a hand. In the dry months the ledge is crowded. Some of the fish have grown too large to fit there by the time the rain comes, and can be seen holding on in daylight with their tails out in the current.

Something lives beneath the floor of the building in the shallows. The mud daubers go in and out of the upper room without disturbing it, and the grazers will feed right up to the walls. It is a soft grey animal about the length of a forearm, with small front feet and a broad, naked tail. There are several of them. They sleep pressed side by side against the underside of the floor, with their noses in the narrow space above the water. At dusk they swim out to turn over bits of bark for snails too small for the birds. A mother carries her young one at a time to a ledge inside the wall, where they wait while she feeds. They can swim before they can climb. If the water rises to the ledge, she has to keep putting them back.

Along the rim the trees put down fine roots into the line of stranded seed. Soil builds there a little at a time: a husk, a dead moth, a pellet left by a frog. The roots bind it through the rains. Occasionally a whole strip pulls free. Grass, soil and roots hang out over the cold air until their weight takes them. The frogs leave before that happens. They work back into the ferns as the earth begins to open, and for a few nights there is no calling at that spot.

Then one calls from the cut face of the bank, where the new ends of the roots are still white. Another answers from under the old root farther on. Between them is a gap just wide enough that, walking the rim in the dark, you can hear the answer better than the call.
