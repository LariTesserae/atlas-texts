Most of the people of the ridge can tell one another by the way they stop. A mason comes down feet first and sets both heels on the stone. A gardener puts out a hand even where there is nothing to take hold of. The woman who sells cord in the market has done without her left foot since childhood and stops by turning sideways. Her daughters do it too. They have all their feet.

They are narrow through the chest and broad at the hips. When they are working hard the folds behind their jaws open far enough to show red. In the market a person with something private to say will often wait for the other person to finish carrying a load. It is difficult to listen while the folds are wide. Babies have to be held with the chin up until they learn to do it for themselves. A common sight at a stall is a parent choosing supper with one finger crooked under a sleeping baby's chin.

In the houses, people sleep close to the floor. A bed is a shallow stone tray with a rolled mat at either end, one for the head and one to put the feet under. A child who has just been given a tray will sometimes turn up at breakfast against the roof weed, still asleep, with a heel through the leaves. There is no great hurry to bring them down. The room is warmest up there.

People who travel any distance from the ridge take a pebble to strike against the rock. A blow made with the hand is too soft to be heard through much stone. The succession of knocks used to say *I am here* is the same for everyone, but there is usually no difficulty telling who is making them. Once, when the bell was rung for a boy who had followed the white road out of sight, his grandmother put her cheek against the gatepost and said he was on his way back. The others heard nothing for some while. What she had heard, she said, was the second knock coming too soon after the first. He had done everything too fast that year.

The woman who keeps the measures for the north bench used to cut roof ribs. She still goes to see the ribs put up when a new house is finished. She takes no part in the work, but if the masons have left her a place to sit, and they generally have, she will tell them which way the ribs ought to face. A rib cut from the seam has a side that was toward the open water and a side that was toward the mountain. Set it in wrong and it may spend twenty years turning a little every winter. By then a whole roof of weed has taken hold, and the only practical thing to do is widen the doorway.

It was her shoulder that left the print on the bench. She had been sitting there with her granddaughter, eating the white ends of shoots and laying the tough green ends beside her. When she went back for the scraps, they had sunk to the thickness of paint. The shoulder was still there the following year. The girl put her own shoulder in it and found that she almost fit. By then she was old enough to know not to stay there long.

The bench keeper has a shelf of things brought up from the gardens: a length of cord with stone grown between the strands, half a shell wedge, a paving chip dark on both sides. There is also a thumb-sized piece of the soft white seam in a bowl. The girl took it home one winter, thinking it would harden if she left it alone. Instead it got smaller, and the water in the bowl grew cold. Her grandmother put it back on the shelf. It has not got any smaller since, though neither of them can remember whether the bowl was always so heavy.

On days when nobody needs her, the girl sits at the new well. She is saving for a stone tray of her own and has been told she can have the first stall on the square if she helps keep the weed off it. So far there is no one to sell anything to. She scrapes the rock within reach of the well and lets the rest go. Every few days a white grain comes up while she is there. If she holds a hand over the opening, the grain goes round it, on one side or the other. She has tried this often enough to know there is no favored side.

At the end of the day her grandmother knocks on the bench with the butt of the measuring rod. The girl puts away the scraper and knocks back. She is a little too far out now for the old woman to hear her through the stone. They have both noticed this. Neither has moved her place. Each year, when they shift the rope, they try again.
