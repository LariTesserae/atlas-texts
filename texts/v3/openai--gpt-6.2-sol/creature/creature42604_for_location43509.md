There is a plant in the deep cut that has been the width of a thumb for longer than the cut has been there. If you put your thumb into the rounded socket beside the roll of stock, you can feel it on the upper surface, where the roll never quite touches. It is soft in the middle and hard at either end. At the hard ends it passes into the wall. You would have to take the wall apart for some distance to find out how much of it to call the plant.

The lip that holds the roll is its youngest part. Before there was a shop here, it held the lower end of a rain pipe. There is still a little rib on the inside of the lip, exactly the width of the seam in the pipe. Each roll gets a shallow mark from it. The marks are of no use to the shops that take the rolls, but one of them has been taking them for so long that its feeder has thickened on either side of the mark. An unmarked roll goes through a little crooked.

When the shop stops drawing oil, the plant in the socket gets warm. The warmth comes slowly, through enough old wood that it arrives well after the last roll has been taken away. A series of flat buds swells along the lip. In the morning, if the shop starts again, most of them go down. One or two will have got far enough to remain as knobs. You can tell with a finger which stoppages lasted a long time. The biggest knob is on the back, where the lip has grown around it and nearly met itself. Whatever stopped the shop that time has left no other mark in the cut.

On the avenue there are plants that live for less time than it takes one of those buds to go down. They come up between the slats of the delivery belts when a piece of stock begins to slip. The first catches are blunt and grow in a row. If the stock keeps sliding, finer ones come up in front of it, so close together that they feel like the nap on a leaf. Eventually the weight is held by many more catches than were needed to stop it. When it moves on, the catches dry. Some are swept off by the next load, but those directly under the weight are pressed into the belt. Their dry ends can be felt on its underside as it comes back empty.

When the factory lifted tonight, one strip of catches held a stem by its thin end. The thick end went over the side. For several minutes the stem hung there, with two belts brushing past it, and the catches kept coming up under the bit they could reach. By the time the stem broke, they had grown into its grain. The short piece will go all the way around with them. On each circuit it will pass through the warm place under the shaft. It has a bud on it that hasn't opened yet.

Below the rim there are inhabitants that get nothing from the belts. The flakes settle against the undersides of the ledges with their stalks pointing down. Only a few settle close enough to the wall to make a root reach it before the heat dries them. Once a root gets in, the leaf can thicken until it is too heavy even for the rising air to stir. Then it puts out ledges of its own. They are broad at the wall and hardly thicker than paper at the outer edge. On the underside of each is a thin slick of oil, drawn through the root from a depth at which some forgotten conduit is still warm.

You can find the older ones by putting your hand over the edge. The heat divides around them. To either side of your wrist there will be a steady rise, and between the two currents a strip of almost cool air. A flake coming up in that strip will turn over and over without gaining any height. Sometimes it touches the same ledge a dozen times before it sticks.

The curve in the avenue is lived in all the way across. The plants on its inside have never had to carry a load. Those on the outside are worn flat, down through layers that are still soft when first exposed. If you kneel there after a belt has pulled away, you can find very small leaves coming out of the smooth wood. They lie with their tips pointed into the curve. On the inner side the leaves are broad enough to overlap, and they point any way there is room.

Tonight, while the new belt lay across the old turn, a runner came out from the inside and fastened to its edge. It reached far enough to take oil. Then the pavement began to cool and the belt drew away. The runner stretched until you could have passed a finger beneath it. It didn't break. For a while, as the belt moved into place, the runner went on making a leaf.

The leaf was still folded when the first stems came round. The lowest stem caught it and laid it flat against the paving. By the time the next load came, the leaf had put both its edges down. The middle stayed raised, no higher than the seam in the old rain pipe. Each stem that passed over it pressed it a little deeper into the wood. By morning there was a narrow rib across the turn, and under the rib the runner was warm.
