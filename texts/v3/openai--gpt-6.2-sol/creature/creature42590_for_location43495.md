Under the bridge at the washhouse door lives an animal the color of the skin that forms on boiled milk. It has four feet and a tail, though the feet are so small that when it walks it seems to be drawing itself along by its chin. The tail is broad and lies flat on the stone. In a strong current the animal can lift its head to feed without being carried away.

The woman who lunches between the basins has known this one for eleven years. It was as long as her little finger when she first saw it. It is now as long as her little finger and considerably wider. She says it has got fat on soap, which it does not eat. What it eats are the small things that come out of the mountain in the clear water. You can leave a glass of that water on the sill all afternoon and see nothing in it. Leave the glass overnight, with a thread hanging over the rim, and in the morning the submerged part of the thread will be furred with life.

These animals are called soles. A child who is told there is one under a bridge will put a hand into the water and feel the underside of the wrong stone. The soles live on the bed of the channel, in the cover the bridge provides. They do not fasten themselves to anything. When a bridge is lifted, they stay where they are, even in full sun, making themselves flatter and flatter. The proper thing is to finish the work and put the bridge back. An apprentice once took one to the tea shop in a saucer to show how flat it could get. By lunchtime it had walked out of the saucer and into the fold of his wet cuff. The account of this incident usually leaves out the hour he spent on his knees looking for it.

There are streets where every third doorway has a sole, and streets with good water and old bridges where there are none. The difference is not apparent at street level. At the washhouse, if the basins are empty and you put your cheek against the second one, you can feel a fine tremor in the porcelain. At the third there is a coarser tremor, with a little hitch in it. In the strip between them there is nothing to feel. Under the floor, in the same strip, there is a shallow run of water scarcely longer than the lunch stool is high. In late summer it contains the young.

They come out of their eggs standing up. For a day or two they are narrower than the eggs were, and stand on their tails with their feet held in close, opening and closing their mouths. In the ordinary trembling water they would be knocked down and rolled together. Here each has a small space around it. After a while, one tips forward and gets a foot on the stone. It stays like that for some time, as if it has met a surface it was not expecting. By the end of the week they are all lying flat. The next time the washhouse is closed and the basins are drawn down, they go out under the door.

Most go downhill. They lodge beneath the scales in the old market or in the damp seams of the retaining wall, where a person looking over the parapet could have one six inches below their hands. Some reach the ponds. In the morning, the newly exposed mineral band is flecked with their footprints, which are no bigger than the marks a sewing needle makes in paper. They turn back from the dry part and leave a second line of tracks over the first. By noon both lines are gone.

The woman at the washhouse is leaving at the end of the month. She has shown her replacement which tap needs to be turned past shut and then brought back, and how to stand the stool so it doesn’t rock. She has not yet shown him the sole. She tried yesterday, but a delivery came while they were at the door and he went to help the driver with the sheets. She stayed where she was, a foot on either side of the channel. After a little while the animal put its head out from under the bridge. She had meant to tell him that this was how you saw it: not by lifting anything, just by standing where you were going to step and waiting a bit longer than seemed necessary.
