The woman who answered the whistle is called Sella. Her daughter has been gone since the first thaw, working at an inn on the other side of the ridge. There is no trouble between them. A man who came over on Tuesday said the girl had learned to carry six plates at once. Sella wanted to know whether she carried the sixth on her arm or in her hand, but the man hadn’t noticed. She still puts out two cups in the morning. She drinks from one while the other warms, then pours what’s left into the warm cup and takes it to her stall.

She sells needles, lampwicks, and the little clay feet that keep a chest clear of a wet floor. The feet go in sets of four, though most people buy one at a time. There is a man who comes every few weeks for a foot taller than the last. Sella has offered to look at his floor. He’d rather she didn’t. His wife’s chair stands there, and if he builds up the short leg little by little she won’t have to get out of it while he does the work.

The two boys with the ducks are not brothers. They look alike from above because they’ve both had their hair cut by the same person, who sets a bowl on the head and tilts it to get at the ears. The smaller boy can tell every duck apart. The taller one can go into anybody’s yard. This is a real accomplishment. There are yards you enter by way of a kitchen, and kitchens you enter by putting your foot through a window and feeling for a stool. He knows which people will steady the stool for him and which will make him go round.

It’s the smaller boy who finds the duck on the roof. She isn’t stuck. She has got into the gutter where it widens behind the chimney, and when he puts his hand in she flattens herself and makes a sound he’s never heard from her before. He lies with his cheek against the shingles until he can see the pale edge of an egg. Then he climbs down. He tells the other boy they’ll have to count to eleven for a while and just pretend that’s all of them.

Under the market live several hundred larvae that nobody has occasion to count. They wear cases made of grit, each stone set beside the next with silk. Where the current sorts the sand fine, the cases are fine. In the seam below the dumpling stall they are coarse and include little red chips from a broken pot. After the branch goes through, some of the larvae are left in a run of water no wider than a finger. They keep feeding there. One has built with a grain so large that its case rolls a little every time it reaches out.

The potter’s husband is the one who frees the branch. People think he knows the water especially well, but for years the person who told him when to get up was the potter. She could feel a change in the wheel through the soles of her feet. Since she’s taken to working upstairs, he has had to learn the sound for himself. At night he sometimes goes down with the pole and finds nothing at the sluice at all. He stands there a while anyway. From the bottom step he can see into the room of the old woman across the channel, who leaves her shutter open while she combs her hair. She sees him too. Neither of them has ever waved.

The wren in the stair has five young. When someone steps on the new tread, it bows just enough to darken the slit above their heads. In the first days they opened their mouths at that. Now they wait for the scratch of their mother’s toes on the old wood. She comes in sideways with a fly, braces one foot against a nail, and feeds the chick that can reach her. Then she goes out the other end, into a part of the town that nobody has built a way to.
