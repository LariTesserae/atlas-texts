The youngest person in the town is six days old. Her name is Ida, and she sleeps in the bottom drawer of a gauge cabinet that her father brought up from the bearing works. The drawer above her still says REJECTS. Her father means to take the label off, but it is a good enamel label, with a screw at either end, and he can think of several places where it might come in useful.

Ida’s grandmother, Mara, lives across the gap. She passes things through the window: a clean cloth, a heel of meal bread, the little hammer her son can never find. To go from her door to his takes two flights of stairs and a bridge that has recently begun to knock. She has been over once since the birth. She held the baby for half an hour and then, on her way home, stopped to mend the bridge. Someone had put a strip of felt under the sliding end to quiet it, and the felt had bunched up. Mara pulled it out. The bridge knocks again, which is how she knows it is free to move.

She was a fitter for thirty-seven years. Now she is paid to show apprentices how to make a joint that will come apart in the cold, what to do with a nut that has run out of thread, and how to tell when a stopped machine belongs to somebody who has gone to fetch a tool. She gives this last lesson more often than any of the others. Her hearing has gone unevenly. She can hear the lift being called from her kitchen, but if you ask her a question while she’s washing a cup she will sometimes answer the question you asked yesterday.

There are eighty-three people, counting Ida and counting Fer, who left in the autumn. Fer has a room down in the valley and a job looking after the compressors at a dairy. He writes that the grass comes right up to the compressor shed and has to be cut. His sister reads his letters aloud on the landing before the evening changeover. She read the bit about the grass twice. No one had asked her to.

His bunk has been taken by a boy who arrived with a tool roll and no indoor shoes. For his first week he kept setting things down on the stairs, out of the way as he supposed, and finding them in other people’s rooms. He has learned to put a belt through the handle of anything he intends to see again. The birds unbuckle nothing, but they can get a pin out of a hinge if it stands proud. One of the older birds has a neat bald patch on its neck from reaching through the same wire square for years. It likes the grease in the hinge on the meal-bin lid. Somebody widened the square for it. Somebody else narrowed it again. This has been going on longer than the boy has been alive.

The skinks have their own routes through the town. A fat one with a forked tail can get from the switchboard in the upper press room to the warm back of Mara’s cupboard without showing more than an inch of herself. In summer she lays eggs behind the cupboard in a crack that won’t admit a fingernail. Mara knows the eggs have hatched when she finds the little ones in her folded sleeves. She shakes them out before she dresses. They are nearly transparent at first, and furious.

Down at the original cleft there is a moth that never goes near the meal. It is the color of the fresh rock, down to the slight shine along the fold of its wings. The boy found one on his cuff after leaning in to look at the valley. He carried it upstairs and showed it around in the cup of his hands. By the time he got to Mara it had stopped moving. She looked at it for a while and told him to take it back where he found it. He said he thought it was dead. She said that made no difference to where he’d found it.

He took it back. Coming up again, he stopped on the bridge because he could hear Ida crying through two open windows. At the dairy, Fer’s sister had read, there was a room where they washed the bottles, and the water ran down a channel in the middle of the floor. The boy had been trying all day to imagine a floor you could do that to. Now he held the rail and listened. He had never heard a baby this small. She would get almost to the end of a breath, and he would think that was all she had in her, and then there would be more.
