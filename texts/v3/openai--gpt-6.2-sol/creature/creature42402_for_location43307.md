There are three of the brown animals, though it takes most of an afternoon to count them. They come out from under the same fern and cross to the same crack, and their coats are all the color of the dry stone beneath the fern. The smallest has a notch in one ear. The largest is missing a toe. You can hear the difference after it has gone by: there is a space in the ticking where you expect a tick.

In the shade they are less brown than they look out on the slab. Their bellies are the dull white of roots that have never been underground. Their forefeet are wide and soft, and when one sits up to wash its face it has to peel each foot off the stone with a little backward tug. They spend the hot part of the day lying flat among the roots. Now and then one reaches up without lifting its head and takes a root between its lips. It holds it there for a long time and lets it go unmarked.

They have been carrying the empty skins of insects to the bench. You can see the pile if you put your cheek down on the hot slab and look up through the break: a tiny horn, a pair of forelegs still folded together, half a skin with the eye covers in it. The animals don't eat these. They push them into the gap above the hanging fragment, where the rising drops wet them and press them against the underside of the bench. The little animal with the notched ear is forever bringing in things that won't fit. It brought a whole fern leaflet yesterday and worked at it from both ends until the leaflet tore. Both pieces are still lying where they fell.

There is a fourth animal in the pile. Its coat is so sparse that you can see the dark of its last meal through the skin of its belly. It lies with its back against the underside of the bench. When it wakes it reaches down, catches the edge of the hanging fragment, and pulls it close. The fragment stops turning. From above there is only a broken bench that looks, for a little while, as if it might be whole.

Toward evening the one with the missing toe goes into the deepest seam. It gets there well before the water begins to lift. There is room for its hind feet on a ledge, but none for its forefeet, and it waits with them tucked up under its chin. As the pane rises it puts its muzzle in sideways and eats what comes by. It can take a live insect out of its husk without bringing the husk out of the water. It eats the first few itself. Then it climbs to the bench with its cheeks moving and disappears beneath it. If you are sitting there, you can feel the young one scrabble against the underside between your knees.

Once the water breaks, all three come out to feed in the rush. They are clumsy in running water. Their feet won't hold, and they go sideways on the hot stone, nosing after soft things that the pane carried up too fast for them to catch. Last of all the young one comes down. It is able to make the dry places with its feet, but it hasn't yet learned to make them one at a time. It plants all four feet, bunches itself, and springs. Long after the rush has gone over the lip, you can hear where it has been: four ticks so close together they sound like something small being dropped, then a long silence while it gets ready to go again.
