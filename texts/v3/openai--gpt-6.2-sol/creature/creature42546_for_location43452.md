The shelf has a soft side. It is underneath, where the current doubles back, and is no wider than the white margin on a mussel shell. A grain of quartz passing within reach will stop there for several days. When it moves again it leaves a little cup, which closes slowly from the rim. The grain may end up miles away, in a seam that will not open in the lifetime of the shelf.

There are hundreds of these soft margins along the ridge. Some have been buried under slabs; some face one another across water so narrow that a green ribbon could bridge it. When a margin is uncovered by a fall, it takes the impression of the first stone that knocks against it. After a night the impression is gone. If the same stone comes back, it does not have to touch.

That is how the thing on the ridge comes to know what is loose.

It has no particular sense of what is large. The slab at the foot of the scarp is a long hesitation in a sound that comes through the ground. The grain in the seam is a sharp ending to another sound. It can move either one, but not until the water turns. All night, in weather that leaves the summit in darkness, it feels the weight and position of things it cannot so much as stir. Then the turn comes and for a little while even a mountain is easy to handle.

A ridge is not one of these creatures. There may be two on the same ridge, working either side of a fault, or one stretched between summits that cannot be seen from each other. Where two adjoin, the stone is often exceptionally fine. One lifts what the other has let go; neither will put it quite where it was before. Over years they make a joint that admits water but hardly a grain. On still days the joint ticks as the water warms.

The green ribbons are the oldest things on the low shelf, though they have never occupied the same crack for more than a season. When the shelf goes over, the ribbons generally go with it. One went down last winter on a piece of stone scarcely wider than its roots. It lay in the dark for thirty-one mornings. On the thirty-second the turn took the stone up, and the ribbon came into daylight with its tip gone transparent and two small new leaves folded against its stem. It had grown those leaves in the dark. It had no means of knowing how much farther down it could have fallen.

The sea was made to raise the bottom. It still makes the turn where there is nothing loose to lift, and where there is no soft margin for a hundred miles. A creature can live without catching it: there are a few in the hollows that have done so for a very long time. They take up minerals through the skin and grow little collars around grains that happen to lodge against them. The collars are the only hard parts they make for themselves. When one of those creatures dies, its collars remain on the rock, each with a different stone inside.

Near the top, the creature beneath the ribbons has been extending its margin toward the light. It has made a poor job of the last few inches. Twice it put down a lip that the evening current could get under. This morning it used a flat stone with a nick in one corner, setting the nick downstream. The water came through in a little jet and cut a pocket in the soft tissue. For most of the afternoon the pocket widened. Then a ribbon bent into it and stayed there, taking the force of the jet along the length of its leaf.

By dusk the pocket had begun to close. There is no root in it yet. The root is still feeling its way through yesterday’s stone, following a seam that will bring it out, if the lip holds, on the other side.
