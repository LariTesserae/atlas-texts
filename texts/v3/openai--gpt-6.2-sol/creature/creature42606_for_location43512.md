The first thing to cross the new leaf comes out of the bead. It is no longer than an eyelash and so nearly the color of water that on the green you see only its two dark ends. It goes to the rim, leans over, and draws back. Then it does the same thing at another point. The leaf has not yet made a seam with anything. By the time its shadow reaches the layer below, the little animal has gone back into the bead.

In an established seam there are thousands of them. They lie lengthwise in the dark between the stitches, with one end fixed to the wood and the other free. In a shower they let go in order from the high end of the seam, so that a run of clear water turns faintly smoky as it goes downhill. Most fasten again within a yard. A few go over the falls. They have been found a long way out on the undersides of the outer terraces, living in pendant drops that take all afternoon to fall. In a dry week a seam looks empty. You can slip a fingernail into it and lift a gray skin no thicker than breath on glass. Put it back before the rain and by morning the two dark ends will be moving again.

The pools have a different tenant. The red leaves are no bigger than thumbnail parings, and each has a crease down the middle. Turn one over and there may be a white animal tucked into the crease, its legs folded so exactly that it looks like part of the rib. It eats the film that gathers on the underside of the leaf. Its mouth is wide and soft, and when it feeds it leaves a clean arc you can see through from above. As the water goes down, the leaf folds around it. Sometimes the pool has been gone for days and the gray leaves are loose enough to blow about, yet a leaf opened by hand is cool inside. The animal takes a surprisingly long time to unfold. It puts down its feet one by one, even when there is nothing under them.

There are larger creatures, but they live where the green stops.

At the foot of a fresh break, look for a patch of pale wood rubbed brown. It may be two feet wide and recur at intervals up the face, though the wall between the patches is quite smooth. These are the resting places of the cleft animals. Their bodies are broad and low, with a rim of flesh all the way around. In motion the rim lifts in sections. A section reaches, takes hold, and goes flat; then another lifts. They climb a sheer face no faster than a shadow moves across a terrace at noon. A full-grown one can cover the mouth of a gutter. When this happens the pool behind it rises until it spills by some other route. The animal will still be there the next day, with a little fringe of red leaves washed against its back.

They feed on the brown substance beneath the roots. Where an old fall has exposed it, they rasp out hollows and sleep in them, fitted so well that even close up the only sign of an occupant is a narrow slit at the lowest point. Water goes into this slit. It comes out a little warmer. In a crowded cleft you can put your hand against the wall at night and feel that warmth from hollow to hollow.

Their young are pale and thin enough to get between layers that have only just begun to separate. For a season or more they live with growing wood above them and growing wood below, eating what dies in the dark as the two surfaces press together. Some are enclosed. Others reach an edge and emerge broad side first, milky and creased. For a little while after they come out, the creases show the grain of the wood they grew against.

When the western rim fell, a number of the old ones went down with it. Two stayed on the cut face. They could be seen toward evening, well below the line of new leaves, moving apart. One went into a socket left by a torn root. The other made its way down to where water was coming out of the wall in a fan. It stopped with half its body in the water and half on the dry wood. During the night the wood swelled against it. By the next noon there was a green lip over its upper edge, thin enough to show the brown of its back beneath.

Far above it the first little leaf has acquired a neighbor. They do not touch. In the afternoon a shower fills both, and the nearer one bends until a drop falls from it onto the farther one. For the length of that fall there is a way across.
