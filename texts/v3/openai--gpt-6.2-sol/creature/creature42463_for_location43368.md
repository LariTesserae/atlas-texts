The animals that make the streets spend their whole lives looking out of them. A tube dweller has a soft body, a collar that lays down shell, and a fan divided into fingers too fine to see except when they cross. In a good current the fingers keep apart. In a poor one they comb the same bit of water over and over. A neighbor need not touch a fan to disturb it. The water displaced by its opening is enough, which is why along some streets the fans open in pairs, and along others only one side feeds at a time.

Farther down each tube there is an animal of much the same shape as the tube. It can turn a little at the collar, but it cannot turn around. When it dies, the fan goes first. For a while the opening looks unusually wide. Then a grazer comes and puts the front of its body into the vacancy, and comes out with a dusting of shell on its back. By the time the tube has a new occupant, the mouth has often been worn too low for a fan. These are the holes the young use when the hunters come through.

A grazer is clear except for its meal. You can follow one in the last of the green light by the dark curl going slowly through it. Its mouth is underneath, and as it feeds it rocks a little from side to side. The crescents it leaves on the wall are the width of that rocking. Two grazers coming toward one another on a ledge do not turn aside. The smaller one goes on feeding across the back of the larger, and for a short distance leaves no mark on the city at all.

The hunters are more difficult to see when they move. At rest, each has two little grains of color in its head. In motion the grains are gone before the rest of the animal has lifted from the pavement. They lie where a passage widens, on the far side of the turn, so the water brings them young that have already begun to spread out. Even there they miss most of what passes. A brood contains individuals small enough to go through the space between a hunter’s jaws without being caught. Some of these are still passing when the hunter settles again.

Under the grain in the square live three animals whose fans grow sideways. They are not the animals that raised the pillars. Those tubes have filled in, and the living tubes are laid against them, so close that the junction is hard to find. The three get the first of whatever comes along the floor from the seam. Once, a scrap of red growth came that far and lodged under the grain. The fans could not move it, and for several tides one of them opened with a red strand caught between its fingers. The strand grew pale from the free end inward. When it parted, the fan went on opening with a gap where it had been.

There are animals no bigger than the gap in that fan. Some live on the ceiling lights and are visible only while their hosts are dark: when the green comes on, their bodies vanish against it and their little shadows show on the stone. Others live in the soft shell itself. A grazing crescent sometimes cuts across one of their chambers. The occupant draws back as the roof opens, then puts out two blunt feelers and begins feeding in a place that was, until then, the inside of a wall.

At the rim, the young on the caught thread have not all settled. Those that have are white at one end, where the first shell has gathered around them. The others still work their way among these fixed bodies, pausing in the water that each one slows. From one side of the thread the current carries them toward the old courts. From the other it carries them under the hanging district, where there is nothing yet to stop them. One goes all the way to the stone and holds there through the turn of the tide. When its fan opens, it opens upward.
