Six people winter on the shelf. In summer there can be twenty, though not all at once and seldom for a whole week. People bring up blankets in the same sacks they take down to be washed. They say they are opening the house, even if all they do is sit in the doorway and eat something they brought with them.

Nella keeps the goat. She has tried three different pieces of wood on the gate where its hoof strikes, not to quiet it but because the boards split there. The goat stands quite still while she works and puts its foot up as soon as she has finished. In the morning Nella carries its milk across the square in a blue jug. Some goes to her brother, who no longer gets up before the sun reaches his step. He can hear her coming but waits to call out until she has set the jug down; once, when he called too soon, she turned her head and spilled half of it.

The youngest person is a boy of nine who goes down to school with his father. He knows the streets by where it is possible to run in them. He can start at the cistern, disappear beneath the stairs, and come out close enough behind you to touch your coat before you have drawn a bucket. He is not allowed to do this when anyone is drawing water. The rope has taken skin off two of his fingers, and he understands the rule now in a way he didn't when it was explained to him.

His father has a van at the bottom of the path. He brings up what they need in several trips, leaving the heavier things in the notch until he has got his breath. Once a month he brings up a bale of hay. No one has found a way to carry it through without leaving hay on both sides of the stone. In the evening the boy pulls the longest stalks free and pushes them through the goat's gate, one at a time. The goat reaches for each as though it were the first.

Ada, who lives above the spring, is usually the one who cleans the drain. The spoon came from her kitchen, where it had been worn too thin at the lip to stir anything stiff. She likes to do the work while the paving is still wet. Once the grit dries it takes two hands, one to hold the spoon and one to keep it from jumping out of the hollow. She puts what she scrapes up beneath the currant bush. There is a little fan of pale sand there, with the blue end of a match and several seeds that have so far come to nothing.

The other winter resident works nights at a care home across the valley. On her way back she sometimes sees the choughs from below, coming out from the cliff with their wings spread and their red feet tucked up. By the time she reaches the square they have gone on. She washes at the basin, eats bread standing at her kitchen window, and hangs her wool cardigan in the doorway before she goes to bed. The boy knows which mornings not to touch her door. In summer her daughter sleeps there too, and is often up and dressed before her mother comes home. They have breakfast together then, though for one of them it is the last meal of the day.

A small pale spider lives under the cistern lid. When the lid is raised it runs the wrong way, toward the widening light, then doubles back along the grain. Nella waits for it before putting the lid down. She doesn't wait long; it knows the distance better than she does. In the lower walls there are snails sealed into crevices, some well above a person's head, in what used to be an upstairs room. The boy found a spoon there once, iron and almost eaten through. He asked Ada if she wanted it for the drain. She said no, but took it inside and washed it.

The bench gets used most in the weeks when the sun is low enough to reach a person's knees. Nella's brother comes down after breakfast, bringing a square of folded sacking because the wall is damp. He used to live in that lane. His house was not where the bench is but a little farther on, and he still sometimes says “past our door” when giving directions. There isn't much reason to give directions in the lower lanes. He gives them to the boy, who is old enough now to be sent looking for people and young enough to enjoy being told a longer way round.

If someone is coming up from the road, you lose sight of them well before they reach the notch. Nella's brother has time to fold the sacking smaller and set it beside him on the bench. Often nobody comes. When somebody does, they are usually too warm from the climb to want to sit, but they stand awhile in front of him with their coat open. From there they can watch the patch of pasture they have just walked through come out of shadow. Behind them, out of sight around the turn, Ada lowers the short-handled bucket into the spring. The handle knocks once against the stone, and then she has it.
