There are snails at the mouth, and farther in there is something that wears their empty shells without carrying them. It has set six of them along the underside of a ledge, mouths outward. From above they look like the blunt teeth on all the other ledges. From below, when the light gets that far, you can see that each has been wedged in place with a little gravel.

For most of the day the only part of the animal in sight is a foot. It is planted on the wall between two cups, just below the waterline, and has four white toes spread wide on the stone. A grain of sand rolling down the wall will lodge against one of them. It may stay there an hour before the toe lifts, lets it through, and settles again.

When the shadow of the sill reaches the back wall, the foot comes away. A small, flat body follows it out from under the ledge. The animal is about as long as the last joint of a thumb, with a head scarcely wider than its neck. It can fold all four feet along its sides and pass through a gap that looks too narrow for its head. In open water it swims badly. Its hind end swings out at each stroke. It keeps to the walls, taking hold again as soon as there is anything to take hold of.

It visits the shells in order. At each it puts its head into the opening and stays there a while. Sometimes it backs out with a bit of white skin in its mouth and lets it go in the water. In the last shell there are young. Their feet show first, resting on the rim in a row. When the larger animal moves away, they pull themselves out and hang under the ledge, where the water just reaches their backs. One lets go too soon. It drops to the gravel and has to climb the wall while the others go across on the ceiling.

The cups have different uses. In one, where the seepage enters, the little animals pick over whatever has lodged against the grit. In another they do nothing that can be seen except open and close their mouths. They seldom cross the tops of the ribs. At a place where two ledges nearly meet, they go up into the hanging thread of water and down the other side. The thread fattens around each body and draws thin again behind it. Viewed from the entrance, the only sign of the crossing is a brief sag in the thread.

At the sill there is a seventh shell, unwedged, lying in the groove somebody cut. Most evenings the large one goes out to it. It turns the shell a little, puts its head inside, then leaves it where it was. One night the tide comes far enough up the flat to turn the thin water on the sill back toward the basin. The shell rocks in the groove and comes free. By morning there is a fan of dark mud inside the entrance, and the shell is under the ledge with the others. It is too big for the space left in the row. The animal has wedged it in sideways. For several days a white foot sticks out of it at an angle, feeling along the wall for a place to rest.
