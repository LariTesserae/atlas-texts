The smallest frog in the hollow lives in a footprint. It is last winter’s footprint, made when somebody came down the bank and found out how little the moss would bear. The heel has filled in, but the toe still holds a teaspoon of water. The frog sits there in the afternoon with its chin on the rim. At night it goes up into the sedge, where it is much harder to find. You might put your hand down six inches from it and hear it call on the other side of the hollow. There is a frog over there too. They are both very small.

Under the fallen alder lives a wren that has never seen the alder. She goes in through a slit in the old sedge and comes out on the other side of the rise, often with something pale in her beak. In spring she takes moss from the uphill edge of the footprint. She pulls backward with both feet braced, and sometimes the piece she wants comes away with a great deal more attached. She stands on the loose end and picks it apart. By midsummer the little bald spot has grown green again.

There are animals in the moss that spend their whole lives below the level of the frog’s chin. A white springtail walks along a root hair, stopping to clean one antenna and then the other. In a dry month it goes down with the moisture. The distance it goes is no more than the thickness of a fingernail, but down there the passage it took yesterday may be shut today. Roots swell; grains settle; the walls of a little room come together. When the springtail comes up again it may be on the far side of a stem it has been living beside all summer.

The thrush comes from a garden beyond the pumping station. You can tell where it has fed by the old blades turned pale side up. It turns them with a quick sideways movement of the bill and then stands very still, with one foot sometimes still lifted. The pump goes on under both feet, or under the one foot, and the bird waits for something that sounds nothing like it.

Once or twice a year the pump stops. Usually nobody is in the hollow when this happens. The frogs do not all fall quiet. A beetle halfway up a stem keeps climbing, though the stem is no longer shaking against its feet. Water gathers at the bent tip above it. The beetle walks around the water to the underside of the blade and stays there through the interval, and through the starting of the pump, until a wren comes along the bank and takes it.
