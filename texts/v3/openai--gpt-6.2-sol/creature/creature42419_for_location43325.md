The people of the upland are easiest to see when they sit down. Standing, a woman may be quite lost among the broad leaves, though her bare feet are in plain sight. When she squats to put down her basket you see her face, and perhaps the faces of two children who have been waiting beside her all along. People greet one another at this height. They can keep up a conversation across a stretch of growth through which neither could throw a stick.

Their houses are under the lifted roots of trees. A good fall near the top of a swell is worth going some distance to look at. The first thing to do is lie down in the opening and see where the water goes. There is often one place, no bigger than a hand, where a drop forms and falls all afternoon. A pot goes there. The beds go far enough back that a sleeper can reach up and put a finger into the roots overhead. Near the entrance the light is good enough to mend a basket. A visitor will sit there until someone moves over.

There are households that have lived under three different trees within earshot of one another. There are others that have gone a long way in the course of a marriage, following falls along the side of a river they have never seen whole. What they leave behind is mostly useful to something else. The sleeping shelves are taken first by the little birds. These carry no bedding into a house that has been slept in recently; they pick at the edges of the mats until the fibers stand up, then back into them. In an old house a person may find six or seven birds asleep in the impression left by a hip.

The low walls are older than anyone’s account of them. People add a stone now and then, most often when a child has found one and brought it home. It is an awkward present. A stone the size of a fist will wear a hole in a carrying net, and a wet one stains whatever it rests on. At a wall the child can spend an afternoon trying it in different places. If it rocks, a bit of fern stem will hold it steady until the roots take hold.

The flat stones across the uprights are where people go to be ill. The stone stays cool well into the afternoon. There is room for somebody to sit beside it and pass a cup up or keep a hand on the sick person’s ankle. Since the stones are on the old paths, a person lying there hears news without having to ask for it. A woman who had been feverish for six days once learned, from two people who stopped to talk beside her, that her youngest daughter could cross the river alone. When the girl came that evening, the woman asked her to show how she did it. The girl walked the length of the stone, slowly at first, then with the peculiar hurry of someone who has felt the ground move and knows the next step will be good.

At low water people make visits. They bring soft things that travel badly in the wet: cakes that crumble, flowers with long loose stamens, dry tinder wrapped in leaves. A girl carrying eggs may meet an old man on the path and find he is carrying eggs to the house she has just left. They set the baskets down and talk, and sometimes exchange eggs rather than carry them any farther. The paths are busiest toward evening, when the crowns have begun to disappear and word has got around about who is home.

One of the paths runs along the back of a tree that fell in the lifetime of a woman still living near it. She remembers the opening as high enough for her father to stand in. She took her first child there to sleep through the hot part of the day. By the time the child could walk, there was only room to lie down. Now the woman’s grandchildren can cross the ridge in six breaths. In the wet season they fish off it, lying on their bellies with their arms through the leaves.

Early one morning the woman goes out with them and finds a place where the ridge has begun to give. It sags under the smallest child, who steps off and watches the hollow slowly fill. The older children want to fetch branches. The woman puts her hand down into the water. Below the sag, where the trunk has gone soft, there is a current cold enough to hurt her fingers. She has the children bring their lines. They lie there until the first drops come out of the crowns, letting their hooks down through what was once the roof of her house.
