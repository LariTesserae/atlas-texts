The pale animal at the weighhouse is a mother, though the young do not look much like her. They are about as long as a thumb and have hard, jointed backs. For the first few weeks they drink off her ears. She dips one ear into the pool, then lays it flat against the stone, and they come out from under the door to take the water. Sometimes one gets hold of the ear itself. She waits for it to let go. Her other ear is ragged from having belonged to somebody less patient.

The young are easy to mistake for the little curb animals, but the curb animals have seven feet. The seventh is a white pad that comes down from the middle of the belly when they are about to be tipped over. On a quiet street you can turn one gently with a finger and see it put that foot out against the air. Afterward it will walk off in much the same direction it was going. They have very little interest in a person who is not carrying anything.

A person carrying a length of pipe, however, will have company. The curb animals come along in twos and threes, raising the front of the body to see where the pipe is going. They can tell a stopped pipe from an open one at a distance. Set an open one down and the first animal to arrive will go in without slowing; lay your hand over the far end and it will back all the way out. There was one with a bent shell that did this six times while a fitter ate his lunch, each time attending to the opening as though the trouble might have been put right in its absence. When the fitter took his hand away, the animal went through and did not come back. The fitter had got rather fond of it by then.

The two under the canopy are brothers. One has a bare place high on his shoulder where his brother rests his jaw. They move the length of the canopy over the course of a day, not quite keeping pace with its shade. They eat the hard cream-colored rind that forms on the underside of the loading plates. To get at it they lie on their backs and work with their lower teeth. It sounds like somebody drawing a nail out of a board, stopping now and then to get a better grip. In the afternoon the smaller brother carries a piece of rind to the weighhouse. He never goes in. He puts it where the door, if it opened all the way, would just touch it.

The thing on the stool comes out for these pieces. On the floor it is an awkward animal, soft as a tongue below and stiff above, and it has to get over the threshold a little at a time. None of the young have the patience to watch it do this. Once it is across, it lays its underside over the rind. When it lifts itself there is nothing beneath it but a damp spot with the shape of the piece still in it.

It has another place to sleep, in the hollow behind the scale beam, where it fits so closely that you can only see it breathe by watching the beam rise. It slept there when the scale man was alive. He knew to put a finger on the beam before reading it, and to wait for the little push back. On days when he was alone he sometimes said, “All right,” at that point. Since he went away, the animal has taken to the stool, but gets down from it at the hour he used to come in. For a while it lies behind the beam as before. Then it comes out and climbs back up. It is much quicker going up the stool than coming over the threshold. The wood is full of little holds made by its feet.

There are animals nobody has seen entire. At the grate on the west street a gray flank passes once or twice a day, near enough to touch. Its hair is parted in a straight line by years of passing under the same iron bar. Well after the flank has gone by, a small black nose appears between the bars and makes a careful inspection of the street. Whether these are two animals is a matter on which the people who work at the hall differ. The night fitter says he once heard the nose sneeze and felt the flank go by under both his boots at the same instant. He considers that proof either way, depending on whom he is talking to.

The large wet animal by the curb is new to daylight. Its eyes are good, but it has never had to look farther than the next bend in a passage. For a long time it keeps lifting its chin and setting it down again. It can see the canopy from where it lies, and the pale mother taking a drink, and beyond her the lip of shale. A truck passes along the rim without coming down. The animal follows it with its whole head until the truck is gone. Then it keeps looking at the place where the truck went out of sight.

Toward evening one of the pale mother’s young goes down into the space under the curb. There is a little warmth left there from the bodies that moved out. It goes in far enough to vanish, then comes back, dragging something that catches twice on the edge. It is a piece of the shell it had when it was born. The mother has already eaten the rest of it. The young one carries this piece over to the big animal and spends some time trying to tuck it into the crease behind its jaw. The big animal raises its head. The piece slips out. The young one tries again.

At last the big animal rolls a little onto its side. There is room now under its jaw for the shell and for the young one too. The young one crawls in and, after all that work, pushes the shell out with a hind foot. It lies on the shale until the mother comes by. She eats it without stopping, on her way to see where her child has got to.
