Under the footbridge live animals the fitters call hems. A full-grown one is about the length of a hand, though it is hard to tell where the body stops. The fur lies flat along the back and continues out to either side in a thin fringe. When a hem settles against the underside of a plate, the fringe covers the feet and the edge of the body. You can look straight at one and see only a place where a coat of paint was put on too thickly.

They feed on the little white moths that come out of the engine-house felt. A hem will follow a moth right up a wall, slowly enough that the moth goes on opening and closing its wings. The last inch is very fast. In the morning there are wings stuck to the oil on the stair treads. The fitters used to blame the finches for this, until somebody found wings on the ceiling of a locked cupboard.

The young have no fringe. They are pink at first, with dark feet and a surprisingly broad, rather solemn head. They ride under the mother, three or four abreast, each with its front feet in the fur of her chest and its hind feet braced against a brother or sister. On a warm evening she will take them down to an exhaust grille. They get off one at a time. Afterward, when she comes to fetch them, they try to board whatever passes low enough: a hanging rag, the toe of a boot. There is a fitter who has learned to stand still while a young hem climbs the whole leg of his overalls. At his knee it usually realizes its mistake. It will sit there for some time before deciding to go down.

The first winter the bridge widened, a woman from the engine houses found a nest under the middle weld. She could see it from the lower walk, a wad of felt with cream-colored seeds caught in it. She sent her apprentice around to the other end with a lamp so she could see whether the felt had fouled a sliding joint. The apprentice went out onto the bridge. She heard his boots go over the weld, and then, for a little while, she heard them going directly over the nest. There were eleven steps. From where she stood, the nest was no bigger than her fist, and when she took it down the following spring, after the hems had left it, there was nothing unusual about its size or its weight. At the back of it she found a whole moth laid on its side. One wing had opened in the felt.

There are hems now under most of the older crossings. They do not seem to like a new span, though a new one has better paint and fewer cold seams. When the country is lifted, they leave the bridges and come into the town. You see them on the enamel tubs after sunset, all flattened against the side that has had the sun. There is very little warmth in it by then. Still, if there are young, the adults take the bottom places and let the young climb over them to the warmest part.

In the house nearest the far rise, a woman leaves the passage light on during a lifting. Moths gather on the glass above her door. Now and then, from the kitchen, she hears the small sound of a hem dropping onto the mat. The first time it happened she went out with a broom, thinking it had fallen and might be hurt. It ran up the door and dropped again. The mat was the one thing in the passage it could land on without making the moths rise. She has since moved it a little nearer the door. In the morning she takes it out by two corners and shakes the wings into the felt grass.
