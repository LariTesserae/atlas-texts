The white animals have eyes, but not on the side that faces out. The eyes are on short stalks at the back, in the narrow space between the animal and the rock. If you slide a mirror under a ledge you can see the stalks turn toward it, one after another. What they ordinarily look at is a strip of basalt no wider than a finger. Each animal keeps its strip clean. Some have been there long enough to wear a shallow hollow in it.

The mouths are less particular. They open into the water and take what comes. Under magnification the catch is mostly transparent: little jointed things with no opaque parts except the contents of their stomachs. The stomachs are black. On a good feeding day the mouths fill with what looks like filings. A colony starts at the uphill end and works down, though nothing in the water seems to be moving that way. If an animal is missing, its neighbours open on either side of the empty place at the proper interval. They will do this over a vacancy older than any of them.

There is another animal that lives among the white ones. You can find it by holding the mirror still after the eyes have lost interest. It has six soft feet and a back the colour of the stone it has most recently been sitting on. It goes from hollow to hollow after the mouths close, picking at the corners. A white animal will lift one edge to let it underneath. Sometimes the visitor stays there for several days. When it comes out again the white animal has a ragged edge, and the visitor is too full to fit into the next hollow. It rests on the lip of the ledge in plain sight. Almost everything that might eat it is looking up.

The long dark animals come closer to the ridge than they appear to. The upper surface of one may pass ten metres below a ledge while its lower surface is lost in the blue. Most of that depth is taken up by a loose skirt of skin. Little dark heads push through slits in the skirt and withdraw again. They are the young. At first they have round mouths and can only hold on; later, when their jaws have lengthened, they begin to reach out and take the white animals off the rock. They are clumsy about it. They leave chalky halves hanging under the ledges, and for several days afterward the six-footed animals scarcely have to walk.

Farther down the slope there are whole cliffs with nothing under the lips. There are hollows, though. Some are deep enough to put a hand in. In one of them a surveyor found an eye stalk still moving, with no other part of the animal left. She went back the next year and found it pointed the other way. It may simply have been bent by the passage of something too large to get into the hollow. There is no useful way to watch a place for a year.

Something lives at the third governor. The crew used to say *someone*, because of the print in the grease, but the print is a poor piece of evidence. It has four fine scars through the whorl, and it has never been overlaid by another. The evidence for a resident is under the deck, where the three legs come together. There is a shelf there across two diagonal braces, made of small flat stones laid with their thick ends inward. Among the stones are the brittle jaw tips of young dark animals, two porcelain chips, and the red handle of a survey screwdriver. All are wedged in. Nothing has been allowed to fall.

The shelf is not a nest. A camera left below it for eleven weeks showed that the resident slept on the handrail, folded over the top bar with its underside pressed to the steel. It was about the size of a person’s forearm. It had no head that the crew could agree on. When it woke, it went around the deck touching each upright in turn with one of its ends. On three occasions it carried a stone out along the ridge to the narrow crack. It tried the stone at several points, and if the stone did not fit it brought it home. The stones it left in the crack were not plugs. They were thin enough to stand on edge with water on either side.

The camera did not show what the resident ate. It did show a dark animal coming up beside the deck and waiting there with its skirt hanging open. The young had gone. Along the inside of the skin were pale oval patches where they had held on. The resident unfolded from the rail and went down over the animal, stopping at each patch. It took most of a day to reach the other end. When it came back it was carrying something white, hardly bigger than a grain of rice. It put the white thing against a porcelain insulator and stayed beside it until it stuck.

There are eleven such marks on the third governor. On the machine across the drop there are too many to count from the ridge. They cover the side of the handrail that faces inward at the bent corner. With the lens braced on a rock you can see a few of them lift at one end, hold there, and settle flat again. After a while another lifts. Nothing on the deck itself seems to move. Then, from underneath, a stone comes up over the edge. It is turned twice in the water before being set down.
