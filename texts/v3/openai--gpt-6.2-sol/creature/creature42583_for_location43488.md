The woman who washes pots in the lane is called Sella. The pot is hers, but she seldom washes only her own. People set things down beside her while she is working: a bowl with a rim too narrow for a man’s hand, a lamp with a black crust in the spout. She leaves them upside down against the door of whoever brought them. She has been known to leave a dirty bowl upside down as well, if there was still enough good food in it to make throwing it out a matter for its owner.

Her brother put in the groove that wets her heel. He cut it a little too deep where it joins the main gutter, then went away to live on dry ground before anyone noticed. When he visits he asks if she wants him to put it right. She says she will tell him when she does. He is the only person who comes into town and wipes his feet before going out again.

Three of the people who remember the bank breaking live within a few doors of Sella. One remembers the water coming first, another the sound, and the third remembers being told to hold an egg. She was four. She held it while her father lifted her onto a roof, and for some time afterward, because no one had told her she could put it down. Her granddaughter likes to ask what became of it. The answer used to be that somebody ate it. Lately the old woman has begun to think she may have dropped it, and can recall, almost well enough to settle the question, the feel of both hands emptying at once.

The children know which bits of the bank are in which houses. A block under the bench has the socket for a hinge in it; two houses away there is another socket upside down at the height of a child’s knee. They argue that the two once held the same gate. The mason says the holes are the wrong size, but he has never brought his rule over to spoil it for them. This is the same mason who can hear the stone under the bench shift when somebody sits down hard. He has a wedge drying on a shelf for it. The bench is the only place his mother will sit outdoors, and she likes the little knock it makes when she gets up.

In the space behind one of the oak wedges lives a long-legged spider. Its web is a loose thing, with a few strands out in the lane to catch the small flies that come up out of the gutter. When the water rises, the spider goes higher into the wall. There are old skins in there, and the dry husks of flies, and a twist of blue wool pushed into a crack by a girl who was trying to find out how far the crack went. The patch over it was done by her father. Anyone could tell his work by the strokes of his fingers, except here, where he smoothed the clay flat because she asked him to leave no mark over her hiding place.

At the outlet slab a boy keeps watch for an eel. He saw one there once at dusk, on the town side of the hole, with its head in the wrong direction for getting out. He is old enough to know it will not be the same eel if he sees another, but not so old that this makes a difference. There is a woman who would pay him to bring her one. She fries them crisp enough at the tail that her husband, who has very few teeth, can eat that end. The boy has not decided whether he would tell her if he saw it.

There are quarrels about the gutters. A man can clear his own length beautifully and leave the scrapings just beyond it, where the next woman must stoop to lift them. A young couple once choked the gutter outside their room with rush, to stop the sound at night, and slept so well that they did not hear their neighbor knocking. In the morning she brought them a bowl of water from beneath her bed. It had a thread from her blanket floating in it. They took out the rush, and the young man cut a shallower drop beneath the threshold. Now the water runs almost without a sound. The neighbor has asked him to do the same for her, but not until after her sister’s visit. Her sister sleeps badly in a room where she cannot hear the water.

Before rain, the little reversal is not quite the same at every door. At Sella’s it comes against her heel in a thin, cold line. In the lane below, it gathers under one edge of a paving stone and brings out a flake of onion skin that has been there since yesterday. Farther down, the boy sees a bubble leave the outlet hole and come toward him. He gets on his knees. He would do that for a bubble in any weather, because of the eel.

Sella comes in with the pot and finds her brother sitting in the good light by the door, turning a bowl in his hands. It is one she put out for him to take home last time and he forgot. She sets the pot down, goes back for the lamp, and by the time she comes in again he has moved his stool so the rain will not spot his knees. His feet are still in the doorway. She puts hers beside them while she works a thread through the lamp spout. Neither of them has to move until someone wants to pass.
