There are at least two plants in the hollow. You can tell because one died.

It left a pale braid across the ceiling. For years the living roots lay beside it so exactly that you would have taken the two for a single root with a stripe down it. Then the dead braid shrank. The living one slipped into the space it left and found a crack that the dead one, in all its years against the stone, had kept covered. There is a leaf now at the far end of that crack. You can put a finger on its underside, but there is no angle from which to see its face.

Most of the leaves are smaller than the last joint of your thumb. What looks like a frond is several leaves laid along a jointed stem, each folded down the middle. The pale hinge is the last part to harden. A new leaf has a few weeks in which to settle against whatever is below it. On the rough side, the folds carry the pattern of the stone: a little dogleg where a tip grew past a grain, a blunt pocket around a nub the width of a seed. Farther in, the leaves rest on older leaves. Sometimes the older leaf disappears and the new one keeps its shape, holding open a little space with nothing in it.

The roots at the tiles are no thicker than roots elsewhere. What gives them away is the sequence of leaves above them. Follow a stem across ordinary stone and there will be a joint here and there at which nothing happened. Follow it over the crooked tile and there is a leaf at every joint. Some are no bigger than a scale. In the dry years they were all the plant could make. The tile lay flush once; a root grew under one corner in a wet year and dried to a hard wedge. Now the corner stands clear of the wall, and the finest roots keep to the underside, where damp lasts a little longer.

At the cup, several stems run behind the bare patch and come out green on the other side. One tried the shorter way. You can still see its brown end at the winter waterline, with a leaf folded neatly against it. In a thaw, when the cup is full, the leaf floats. As the water goes down, it settles into the same small groove.

The thick growth between the shelves is mostly old leaves. The living part there is a thin skin, loose enough to lift with the back of a knuckle. Under it the felt is almost white. Fine roots go down into it, turn, and come out again, with bits of old vein adhering to them. In a dry month the whole pad pulls away from the upper shelf. The gap is narrow enough that a drop can hang in it without falling. It stays there until the felt swells to meet it.

The plants are hardest to tell apart at the entrance. Several stems end there, and some of those ends have rooted and sent stems back in. One is still joined to the root that fed it while it was making its first leaf. The join is brown and as fine as a hair. On either side of it there is new growth.

In spring the leaf that turned in the cold catches its drop. The weight brings the tip down against the stone. Water runs along the fold to the hinge, and a little spills behind it onto a root that has been dry all winter. The leaf lifts again, though not quite as high as before. The next drop, when it comes, will land farther up the fold.
