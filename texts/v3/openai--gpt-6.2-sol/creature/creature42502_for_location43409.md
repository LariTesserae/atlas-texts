The brooding shrimp has worn a thumbprint in the floor of her room. She backs into it with her tail folded, leaving her long feelers out in the passage. A worm going by can lift itself over one without disturbing her. A second shrimp cannot. The one that lives opposite waits for her to draw them in, which she does each time she turns to comb the eggs. He has been getting in and out this way for most of the tide.

There are worms in the wall behind her that have never been out in the street. One puts a red fringe through a hole scarcely wider than a grain of sand. Farther in, where the old chambers hold almost still water, a white worm eats the lining from a deserted tube. It works from the inside, going round and round. Someday the tube will give under a crab’s foot. Until then it is a good place to eat: the lining is thicker at the bends, and there are a great many bends.

The shell builders have soft bodies. At slack water they put out fans so fine that the smaller animals walk between the ribs. A little crab spends the day on one of these fans, moving inward as the water strengthens and outward as it fails. It takes particles from the fan with its two front claws. At the approach of a fish the whole fan folds, crab and all. When it opens again the crab is usually right side up.

There is a fish that can no longer turn around in the room where it sleeps. It was small when it began sleeping there, and for a while it still turned, scraping first its back and then its belly. Now it goes in headfirst and backs out. It makes its low call with its chin almost touching the far wall. The sound is stronger in the chamber next door than it is at the entrance. A smaller fish came to that chamber for several nights running and called from there. Neither fish went far enough into the street to find the opening the other used.

Out in the courts, the brittle stars begin feeding before they uncover their bodies. An arm comes up, takes hold of a scrap, and draws it down. Sometimes two arms take the same scrap and lie straight for a long while, each with a little mud heaped against it. When one lets go, the other can disappear so fast that the mud keeps its shape for a moment. The clams are harder to hurry. After the ray has passed, one of them may stay shut through the next turn of the water. A shrimp can pick the court clean around its two holes before it ventures to open either.

The ray has a small fish that goes with it. When the ray rests on the flats the fish hangs just above the mud at the edge of its mouth. It does not follow when the ray comes into the narrow streets. Instead it takes up a place outside, where the water leaving the town runs over a low bank of shell. It can feed there on what the fan mouths have missed. The ray sometimes goes home by another way, and then the fish waits until the wash over the bank has nearly stopped before setting out across the mud.

Some of the brooding shrimp’s eggs are empty now. She pulls the husks away and lets them go. The young are scarcely heavier than the husks, but they can bend themselves against the water. One bends soon enough to miss the fan below her door and is carried into a gutter. Another lodges in a chamber behind her, where for a while it beats its limbs in the company of a white worm. Most go out over the flats. By the time the water turns again, there is a young shrimp under the lip of a court on the other side of town. The lip is newly grown, rough underneath and hollow just at the edge. A current that could carry the shrimp a mile passes close enough to stir its feelers. It takes hold with one more leg.
