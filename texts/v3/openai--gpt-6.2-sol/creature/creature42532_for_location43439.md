The white ones are only white when they are hungry. After feeding they go gray from the mouth backward, and for a little while you can see which way each one is facing. Their backs are hinged. They flatten themselves against the nickel and pass food from mouth to mouth, not willingly: a piece too large for one to swallow projects far enough for the next to get hold of it. This is what makes the white animal seem to fold. Twenty or thirty backs go down in succession after a morsel no bigger than the point of a pin.

The clear one has hooks where you might expect feet. It sets them in two scratches in the glass and lets the water bow it out. Its young are born with hooks too, but none of the scratches near it are vacant. For their first few minutes they hang from the parent, and then from one another. The last in the string swings almost as far as the far bank. Now and then it obtains a hold there and the whole string snaps taut. By the time it breaks there are mouths feeding at both ends.

The mouths under the overhang belong to animals that cannot turn around. They grow into their places with their hind ends toward the water, and all their lives the current brings them food from behind. What you see in the dry space is not feeding but breathing. When one dies, the newcomer backs down past it, pressing it out into the current. The white ones can tell this has happened before there is anything to see. The whole underside of the bank lifts a little.

The tuft that turns is an animal carrying moss. It has gathered three or four loose threads and caught them under the plates of its back. It moves so slowly that the threads have put out new growth. At the wet end of the bright scratch it stops and lowers itself until the moss drinks. This leaves the animal’s head in the current, where the clear ones can catch it. They have done so often enough that the older moss carriers are easy to recognize: their tufts are broad and handsome, and where the head should be there is a blunt plate with a mouth low down on it. They go on bringing the moss to water.

There is also something living on the lip. It accounts for very little of the white you see from the walk. Most of that is ice. Even from inside, with the lamp shining through it, the living part is hard to distinguish from a flaw in the freezing. It is a row of soft bodies standing side by side with their mouths turned inward. They feed on what washes up to the edge and divide when there is room. There is seldom room. On the outer side of the lip, where the air is dry, each body ends in a little hinge of dead skin. These hinges overlap. When the pressure rises, the row leans out, and the hinges draw flat against one another.

Once in a long while a drop gets through. It hangs beneath the rivet head, clear at first, then white. It is larger than the whole shore looks when you peer in from the walk. Nothing in it lives long enough to make a sound.

Inside, the pressure falls. The lip draws itself upright, one body a little later than the others, and in the space beside it a new mouth opens toward the water.
