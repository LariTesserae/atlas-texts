The first thing to cross the new street is a gray animal with a drop of water under its tongue.

It is about as long as your hand, though when it comes to the far crease it gets considerably longer. It puts its head into the crease and keeps walking until all but the soles of its hind feet have gone in. The feet remain there for some time. You can see the toes spread and take hold whenever a sheath tears. Then a tongue comes out of a slit a yard farther down and lays the drop on the green floor. The animal draws the rest of itself after it, in a series of folds.

There are others under the floor. In the sun their backs show through like the pads of fingers held against paper. They come up around the drop, denting the leaf before they find a way through it. None of them drinks. One puts its lower lip to the water, pulls back a little, and holds still. The others do the same, until the drop is suspended between their mouths. In the middle of it there is something white that might be a fleck of pith. It turns over and opens a red mouth.

You can find the adults by the little patches of nap they leave on the underside of a leaf. They have no hair elsewhere. This grows on the belly, against the grain, and when a leaf tears away with an animal still clinging to it you can hear the nap pull out in rows. The bare patches are blue and take a long time to grow over. The oldest animals are nearly all blue below. They travel high up, where there is less to squeeze through and where, if they lose their hold, there is usually another leaf coming up to meet them.

The young spend their first days in the drops. They have feet quite early, but the feet come off if they dry. A brood going any distance must travel by mouth. At the end of a dry afternoon you may see half a dozen adults waiting with their chins raised beneath a rib that has not yet begun to sweat. Their mouths are so full they cannot close them. When at last water comes down the rib, they take it in without lowering their heads. The young turn slowly in the newly rounded mouths, putting out their feet.

Once, in a court on the north side, a leaf came down while they were waiting. The adults got clear except for one. Its head and shoulders were in the shade, its hindquarters in a strip of sun not much wider than a stem. It made no effort to pull itself out. Another animal came from under the fallen leaf, walked around the edge of the court, and approached the trapped animal from behind. The two put their mouths together. Then the second went back the way it had come and did it again.

From the other side of town, in the shadow of that same leaf, the rib was still overhead. The whole brood was there, pale feet working in the water while the adults stood about with their chins in the air. One animal kept leaving the others and coming back with an empty mouth. It nosed in among them, took a single young one, and left again. A small one that had been jostled out of the drop lay kicking on the leaf. On its next trip the animal picked that one up first.

After the evening blades spread, the adults come out into the open. There is food then. They scrape the tender skin from new shoots with a hard edge inside the lower lip. Often the shoot grows fast enough to carry one of them up while it eats. Seen from across a square, an animal will hang at the same height for a long time, chewing steadily downward as the tip rises past its head.

The little ones do not know how to do this. On their first night out they tow scraps of chewed skin into the creases and eat lying on their sides. They are still almost white. When two meet in a narrow place they test each other with their mouths, and sometimes one climbs into the other’s mouth and has to be pushed out. They sleep where the leaves are close enough to touch them on both sides. In the morning a leaf may rise with a whole row still sleeping on it, their feet working gently at the air where the other leaf was.
