The smallest children know the town by its undersides. They know which stair tastes of salt, which overhang has a moth under it even in winter, and where to put a hand into the leaves to bring out a black-foot asleep. They are not supposed to bring the animals out. A black-foot disturbed in its sleep will spread its ribs and lose, in a few breaths, the heat it has spent half a day collecting. A child who has done this must carry it inside their shirt until its feet begin to stir.

At night the black-feet choose their own beds. They like the hollow behind a bent knee, or the place between two people who have turned their backs on one another. The thin plates lift and settle as they breathe. By morning an animal is no warmer than the stone, and a person getting up has to remember it is there. The very old sometimes forget. Their grandchildren come early to their rooms and feel through the bedding before helping them stand.

The faces in the walls belong to animals that can go almost a year without showing the rest of themselves. In the season when the lowest leaves turn brown, you may hear one coming out: a small sound like a coarse thread being drawn through cloth. Its shoulders are narrow, its belly white and long. It goes along the wall on the thick ends of the stones, picking moths out from under the stairs. It eats only one of a pair. By daylight the other has shifted into the middle of the stair lip, and if you did not know where to look yesterday you would never guess there had been room for two.

People living beside an occupied wall leave the stones alone, even when a room needs widening. There is a woman in the third of the seven houses who has cooked for forty years with one foot on the stair because the wall behind her stove is full of sleepers. Once, when her daughter came home with a baby, they moved the stove instead. It stands on the sill now. At supper the daughter passes the bowls out through the leaves to anyone coming down from the upper lane. You can eat there with your feet on the stair and your head in the roof garden. The baby, who cannot yet stand, has learned to hold a bowl level with both hands.

There is water in the town, though none in the places a stranger would look. Before sunrise a little collects on the blue sides of the oldest leaves. The people hold a cup below the tip and touch the blade with one finger. In a good week there is enough to soften meal without unsealing one of the jars brought up from the plain. The six-legged grazers drink sooner. They stand with all six feet around a bead and lower their heads in turn. Nobody knows whether they are the same grazers from one morning to the next. They have no marks anyone can see except when they have just shed, and then every one of them is white.

Across the widest fissure lives a man who used to cut thresholds. For years people brought him a bowl and asked him to set it on a new doorstep. If it stayed where he put it, they paid him. He no longer takes the work, but at midday he sits with his feet on the two halves of the step outside his house. His left foot is in the lane; his right is a little lower, on the shelf with the seven houses. He used to be able to sit that way with his knees together.

His granddaughter brings him his meal. Lately she has begun to come by the roofs, where a long leaf hides the gap and two old roots cross under it. She gets down on her hands and knees, not because she is afraid of the height but because the bowl is hot and she can set it ahead of her on the stone. Under her, a black-foot making the same crossing pauses where the roots part. One of its hind feet opens, showing a pale pad. It lays that foot on the other root and brings its weight after it. Above, the girl takes her hands off the bowl a moment to let them cool.
