The woman whose kitchen the stair goes through is called Sefa. She can tell who is crossing by what the ladles do. Her brother makes them knock together; his daughter, who is heavier than he is, passes without a sound. A stranger will often stop halfway across to apologize. This is worse than walking heavily. The plank bows, the long ladle comes to rest against the wall, and when the stranger moves again it strikes the wall a good hard blow.

Sefa keeps a dish of cracked date stones beside the stove. Whenever her brother comes up from feeding the goats he takes one and picks at it with a nail. He has been doing this for years without finding much in them. The two of them can sit a whole evening with the dish between them and say nothing until he gets up, when she will ask him to bring her something from the west end. He complains that she might have thought of it while he was still wearing his sandals.

His daughter, Maret, is one of the sweepers. At thirteen she has charge of the last stair and the paving below it, a job she got from a woman who can still sweep perfectly well but can no longer turn around on the lowest step. The woman watches from above and calls down whenever Maret sweeps dust over a clean tread. She was the first to see the green tip in the threshold. She called down about that, too, in exactly the same voice, and Maret swept another two strokes before she understood.

The seed is on the side where a doorpost once stood. A man who remembers the door says it opened inward and that you had to lift it by its ring to get it past the floor. His sister says he is thinking of the door above it. She remembers sitting on this threshold to have a thorn taken out of her foot. The woman who took it out had been washing her hair and left a wet patch on the stone beside her. No one can recall where that woman lived. The sister has tried standing at each of the three thresholds, looking up to see which portion of wall she remembers seeing through her tears. There have been too many alterations.

In the wall below the paving lives a grey lizard with a missing front toe. It comes out a little before the sun reaches the stair, puts its belly flat on the stone, and lifts itself when the stone grows hot. At first the sweepers thought it was eating the seedling. Maret lay on her stomach two steps up and watched it take a fly so small she had seen only its shadow. Since then she has found a second lizard in the same wall. It has all its toes and is harder to follow. There may be any number of those.

The goats know the lizards and pay no attention to them. They know the pack animals, too, by the time the people at the upper gate have made out the bells. The oldest goat can distinguish a train coming in from one that has stopped to redistribute its load on the far side of the notch. For the first she stands with her head high and calls. For the second she goes on chewing. Maret’s father has been known to come up two flights to consult her and then go back down to finish his work.

On jar days Sefa folds the kitchen plank up against the wall. The bearers must go the long way around, but nobody has ever had to cross above her fire with a full jar. They come out of the deep rooms swinging their arms a little, enjoying the lightness of them. One of the bearers was born in the town and left when he was small. He can carry water into a room he has not seen for twenty years and set it down in the dark, but on his way out he lifts his foot for a step the mason took away. There is always somebody there to see him do it.

Nobody waters the date seed. Maret did once press a finger into the little pocket of dirt beside it and found that the dirt underneath was cool. She tried again a week later and met something that gave slightly, then held. She withdrew her finger and tucked back the few grains that came away with it.

At the end of summer the woman who used to sweep the stair comes down on her hands and feet to see how much the date has grown. Maret goes in front of her with the broom held across her body, though there is very little she could do with it if the woman slipped. At the bottom they sit side by side, taking up nearly the whole width of the passage. From that low down they can see the pale underside of the leaf. They can also see, in the face of the parapet, a slim gap where a stem has fallen out of a joint. Before they go back up, the old woman pulls a dry stem from the broom and works it into the gap with her thumbnail. She leaves just enough sticking out for the next person to take hold of.
