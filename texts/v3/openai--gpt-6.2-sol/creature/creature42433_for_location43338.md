The smallest ones live in the shallow dishes. In late summer, when the water has drawn back from the moss, you can sometimes find one under a dead leaf. It is clear except at the ends, where it has the brown of a pear just cut open. A thread goes down from either end into the clay. Between the threads the water hangs a little crooked.

At this age they beat only after dark. You can hear one from a few feet away if you lie with your cheek on the moss. The first blow is quite sharp. Farther down there is a little fall of grit. In the morning a clean patch shows beneath the leaf, and in the gut of the animal, if the light is good enough, you can see what it has taken: the soft part of the silt, the motes that would otherwise stay up until the water dried. It leaves the mica. Some of the dishes have a bright dusting on their bottoms by September.

The young ones die in great numbers. A dish that dries too early keeps their threads standing for a day or two. A snail coming over the rim takes them one after another, with the same slight pause it makes over a knot in a piece of bark. But in a wet autumn the dishes spill. Where two spillings meet, the little animals come together end to end. Their guts remain separate. The threads cross and draw tight, and some time in the first night one of the two begins to strike for both. By the time the water reaches the reeds there may be a hundred mouths to a blow.

That much can be seen. It is harder to know how many there are out on the flats. A finger in the water will still a pool, but sometimes it will also still a place well out among the reeds, with an acre of beating water between. There are patches that have dried clear through in living memory that will nevertheless stop together. In spring, when the roots grow fastest, an old feeding reach may let a root pass clean through it, while the length beside it closes on a root no thicker than a hair. A week later that hair is dead and there is a new mouth where its tip was.

They have no hard parts except what they borrow. Quartz will serve; so will a snail shell from which the snail has gone. A full shell is left alone, though the water moves around it and the snail goes on feeding. A live fish can spend a winter in the interval. The trouble comes in spring, when it fattens. For several days you may see its back moving under the top water without a ripple. Then the two skins of water part far enough to let it turn. This is when the herons come.

By the river there are no young ones. The current carries away their food before they can draw it down. The old growths reach into it, though. On a falling river you can see the ends hanging from the exposed roots, whitish in the sudden air. They are much thicker than the threads in the dishes, and go on shortening for hours. Where they have pulled clear, there are worn grooves in the wood. In the deepest grooves you can fit the edge of a fingernail.

Once in a long dry spell the beating becomes faint enough that a person crossing the flats can hear a vole chewing. The dishes go to clay, the two waters come together, and the old leaves lie where they fell. There is no sign of life in the grooves. With rain, the first sound comes not from the dishes but from deep under the fallen alder. For a while it is only the one blow, followed by a long wait. Then, on the other side of the trunk, something that has been there all summer answers it.
