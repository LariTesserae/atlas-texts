The largest animal in the hollow can turn around inside a crack you could not get a fingernail into. It does this a little at a time. First the head goes flat, then the ribs lie down in pairs, and finally the hind feet come forward and take hold somewhere near the chin. For a while, halfway through, the animal is wider than it is long.

It lives behind the deepest bowl. The entrance is a nick in the rim, the sort of flaw you would pass over with a thumb. In the morning there is sometimes a clear flap laid out over it. That is the animal’s lower lip. It can extend the lip much farther than seems reasonable, though it seldom puts its head out. The amber creatures going around the bowl step over it. Once in a while one stops to drink from it, and the animal lets it.

It eats the white husks they leave when they molt. A husk caught on the far side of the bowl may take it most of an afternoon to fetch. It comes out with its belly against the floor and its little legs spread so far apart that a leg moving looks quite separate from the animal. The loose skin down its back has chalk ground into it. The new skin underneath, where a shoulder works free, is the dull purple of a plum that has had its bloom rubbed off.

There are two young ones now. They spent their first days in the dry bowl, where they were easily mistaken for curls of root. One has since gone into the wall. The other still sleeps in the open, on its back, with all its feet folded over a grain of chalk. It changes grains several times before settling. The first one is usually too big.

In a dry spell the old animal waits for the drop with its throat laid against the inside of the rim. When the drop strikes, it swallows. The hard plate under its jaw knocks against the chalk: that is the second sound. It will make that little movement even on an evening when the drop dwindles away on the root and nothing falls. After rain it drinks with its head out and makes no sound at all.

Last summer, during the longest spell without water, it went up the wall and along the white root. Both young followed. At the tip the old animal began to lick, and the root swept slowly sideways under their weight until it touched the roof again. The three of them stayed there a long while. On the other side something walked up and stopped. One of the young lifted a foot, then put it down on the old animal’s back. That was all that could be seen from below. By morning they were back in the bowl, and the root was hanging a little lower than before.
