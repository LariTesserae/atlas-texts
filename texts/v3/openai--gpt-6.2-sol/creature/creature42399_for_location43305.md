Something lives in the house that cannot turn around in it. This accounts for the wear on both walls: it goes in sideways, one shoulder leading, and comes out the way it went in. Its shoulders are not quite level. On the left wall the white band is a finger lower.

It tends the herd. You can tell this by the little repairs. A split hoof has been bound with hair so fine you have to put your face near the ground to see the knot. On one of the bare forelegs there is a splint made from a sliver of the door. The animal with its chin on the other’s eye is very old. Under its chin, the open eye is clear. Its other eye has gone white.

The herd eats the black growths, though you will not see an animal lower its head to one. Look for the pads between the stones immediately beneath their mouths. Some have been cropped so close that only the cups remain. These are not seed cups. The growth puts them out along a fresh edge, where it is most likely to be bitten, and an animal taking a mouthful has to shut its teeth with great care. The click is the breaking of a cup. If you were to find one in a jawbone, you could tell from its position how old the animal was when it ate it. The teeth grow around the glass.

There are young in the herd, though the smallest things are not necessarily the young ones. Between the toes of a tall dun animal stands a little gray one with a belly nearly worn through. It has been standing there since before the tall animal was born. A newborn is easy to miss for another reason: it takes up exactly the space left for it. You may remember walking between two animals and putting your hand on a warm back to steady yourself. When you look again, there is a third animal there, and the back you touched is its back. Its coat still lies in the little whorls in which it dried without ever having been wet.

The keeper cuts a stone for each birth. It waits for something worth recording: an ear bitten in a quarrel, a leg that sets crooked, a patch of hair that never lies down. Some animals wait a long while. There is one near the house, perfectly sound except that the hair inside its left nostril grows outward. The keeper has set a stone for it and has not yet decided how to show this.

The long head beyond the rim was drawn when it was small enough to hold between two hands. The mouth was already open. There is a tooth in the first bowl that would fit it at that age. The keeper takes the tooth out now and then and runs a thumb along the root. There is a notch there that the tongue used to find.

When the animal below breathes in, there is a little more room on the plateau. The herd makes use of it. A foot comes down that has been held up a very long time. Two flanks come apart far enough for the hair between them to rise. The old one lifts its chin, and the animal underneath blinks. Then there is a place for the foot again where it was before, and the eye is open, with the chin laid over it. The keeper has learned to do most of its work in that little interval. It can tie a knot so that the knot stays tied.

This is why it keeps a room in the bowl. A room is a useful thing to have a spare of. The keeper can set the awl down in one and go on sewing in the other. It can let a visitor stand in the doorway all afternoon and still have somewhere to lie down.

If you take your boots off before you go in, you will find a pair already inside, set neatly against the wall. They are yours, worn through in places yours are not yet worn through. The keeper has stuffed the toes with soft hair to hold their shape. Beside them are two shallow dents in the floor. Put your feet there and for the first time since you came up onto the plateau you will feel, through both soles, the warmth of something that has only just got up.
