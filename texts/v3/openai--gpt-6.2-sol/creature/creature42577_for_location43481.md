The heavy body in the ferns is a sow. She knows the stair well enough to come down it without looking, but one of her young has got into a jar and can’t turn around. She waits, snuffing at the ferns, while he backs out a little at a time. The lip catches him under the belly. When he drops, she puts her nose to the place where he landed, then goes on. Three more young come down after her, taking the high steps sideways.

They drink at the low dishes. The smallest puts a foot in his water and has to wait for the mud to settle. Over his head, in the arch, a frog is sitting so close to the underside of the clay that the drops gather on its back. Now and then it raises itself on its front legs and lets the water run down between them.

There are eels in the ridge, though not many and not high up. As long as a seam is wet they can get through it. Sometimes, when the water falls away, one spends the summer in the bottom curve of a buried pot. The roots drink from the same little store. A tree on the lower terrace came down in a dry autumn and brought up an eel with it, loosely knotted among the roots. It lay still in the light until a child laid a wet skirt over it. Then it began to move, and she had to carry it skirt and all to the foot of the ridge.

Her family lives in the pots that look out over the east stair. They don’t sleep in the big jars in summer. The clay is still too warm at dawn, and the bats are coming home by then. They put their mats on the stair landings and fold them when the sun comes round. Their cooking pot has a flat bottom. Every few years someone brings home a round one from the market because it’s cheaper, and it rolls off the hearth the first time anybody tries to stir it.

The girl who carried the eel is old now. She can still show you the tree, or where it was. What she looks for is not the hole but a pot with a thumb hollow at the base of its handle. When she was small she could fit her whole thumb in it. Her grandson has to lie on his stomach to reach it now; the fern bed has grown out over the handle, and there is a nest in the hollow. He says there are five eggs. His grandmother tells him not to go back and count.

On the far face of the ridge there is a dry run of jars where a cat has her kittens. Rain never gets in there, but the room is changing all the same. A root comes through the neck of the jar behind her and bends down to the floor. The kittens climb it as soon as they can walk. For a few days she takes them down by the loose skin of their necks. Then one gets past her and she has to follow him, up the root and through a mouth that opens under a thicket a terrace higher. From then on she brings her mice in that way. Below, at the old entrance, a little heap of mouse ribs is all that’s left to show she ever used it.

Late at night the people on the landings draw their feet in to let the sow go by. She doesn’t always come with young. In some years she stops at each landing, even the empty ones, and sniffs along the clay where the mats have been. The old woman leaves the wash bowl out when there’s water to spare. In the morning there is usually a little left, with the fine dirt from the sow’s nose lying in two dark commas at the bottom.
