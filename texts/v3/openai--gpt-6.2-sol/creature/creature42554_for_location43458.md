Under the first tread of the stair there is a little row of worn places where the roots come out. Each is about as wide as the nail of your smallest finger. If you lift the tread, the things making them draw their feet in, and for a moment all you see is a row of dark beads with the roots passing through them. Then one puts a foot out again. It has six, with long toes that lie along the root rather than curling around it. The toes are white on the side that takes the weight.

These are the young. They sleep where the stair is thick, in the warmth of the pith. When they wake they go down to the ends of the roots to drink. A drop hanging there is large enough for two of them. They drink from opposite sides, and you can see their mouths through the water, much larger than they are. When the drop falls they stay a little while with their faces together.

The grown ones are longer than your hand. In the afternoon they hang flat against the underside of the valley, with their bellies in the thin layer of water that clings there. They are gray on the side you would see if you were looking up from below. Along the back, where the leaf shines through them, they are green. They eat the loose skin that comes away at a growing edge. One will take hold of a strip and walk backward with it, gathering it in folds against its mouth. The old ones sometimes get all the way to the end of a root before the strip gives. Then they hang by their last pair of feet and go on chewing.

The loud place is where they have their young. The floor there is doubled over a chamber so broad that its lower leaf sags. Water comes into it through the wall of a basin and falls the length of the chamber onto this leaf. There is a narrow dry ledge halfway down. The mothers lie along it, their feet on the ledge and their backs in the falling water. The young come out one at a time from a crease low on the mother's side. She catches each between two toes and holds it there until it takes hold of her. A mother with six or seven young moves slowly along the ledge, stopping wherever the water strikes hardest. Seen from above, there is only the basin and, some distance out in it, a leaf with a dull side and a bright side.

The young are carried to the stair before their feet are strong enough to leave it. For a few days they reach for anything the water brings within reach. They take hold of one another, or of the narrow wings of an insect that has come down to drink. There is one under the second tread with a bit of its eggshell still stuck to its back. The snail sometimes goes over it, slowly enough for the little animal to put all six feet on the shell and be carried the width of the tread. It gets off on the same root it started from. For the rest of the afternoon it keeps reaching into the place where the snail was.

By evening the grown ones have gone out to the pale lips at the end of the valley. You can see them there without lifting anything. They lean out over the gap, holding on with their hind feet, and take the loose green from the rims of the leaves. They leave the clear tips alone. At one of the tips an old one has stopped eating. It reaches into the gap, draws its foot back, and reaches again. Its toes spread at the full length of the leg. Across from it the nearest rib is still a long way off.

There is a young one on the old one's back. It comes forward a little every time the old one reaches, until its head is out beyond the edge. A drop swells at the leaf tip. The young one puts a foot into it just before it falls. Then it draws the foot in under its belly and waits for the next drop.
