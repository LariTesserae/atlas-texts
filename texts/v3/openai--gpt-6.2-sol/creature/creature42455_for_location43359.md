The woman at the end of the eastern road lives with her brother and his daughter. Her brother has a bad heel. On fine days he sits out by the basin and draws copper wire through a hole in a stone. The girl takes the finished coils into the city and brings back salt, lamp wicks, and pots that have cracked but are still worth carrying. In winter, when the road is dry, she can be gone four days. In summer there are mornings when she can go and come back before her uncle has drawn a yard of wire. She does not like those mornings. There is a place on the road where she can hear him at his work long after the house is out of sight, and another where she can hear him stop.

The people of the city are good at leaving off. A woman roofing a house will come down with three slabs yet to lay if a trickle starts over the top rung of her ladder. A man mending a public drain will leave his tools in the opening and walk home with his sleeves wet. The tools are generally there in the morning. A few come back with new handles. The wood is pale and stringy and shrinks so much in a dry room that the heads fall off. No one knows where it grows. The man who has had a tool returned in this way usually leaves the old handle by the drain, even if he could use it for something else.

There are households along the paved streets that have not had a neighbor within calling distance for six or seven generations. They still lend things. A basin under a roof is a good place to leave a borrowed pot: the owner can collect it whether the borrower is home or not. At one basin on the southern road there is a row of little stone lids, each with a different knob. Some fit pots in houses miles apart. One fits nothing anybody has been able to find. Its knob has been rubbed smooth on two sides, as though it were taken up often by someone with a very small hand.

Nobody has seen an animal on the plateau. The children know what they are from people who have gone down the pass. They draw dogs with mouths at both ends because a dog can make a noise while it runs away from you, a thing they find difficult to account for. The little prints around a jar are not thought to be animal prints. There are five toes to each foot. Sometimes there is a crescent where a heel has slipped.

A pot mender in the lower city once heard a child crying in a jar he had been given to repair. He took the lid off. The crying became fainter, and there was nothing inside but a little water and a bright patch on the bottom. He put the lid back and sat with both hands on it till the crying stopped. A day later he heard a woman laugh in the same jar. By then he had found the crack. It ran all the way through the foot and would have been a tiresome thing to mend. He brought the jar back whole the next morning. It had been stitched from the inside, with the sharp ends of the wire turned out where they would catch on his apron. He clipped them off.

There are other things in the wet that no one would mistake for people. In the northern gardens, at the height of a flood, the leaves sometimes close in a line across a bank. It happens a leaf at a time, not fast. If there is a gap in the growth, the closing stops on one side and begins again on the other after just the interval it would take a slow walker to cross. A gardener who watched this for a whole afternoon saw the line turn aside at a heap of cut stems. She had put the stems there herself, on rock that had been bare in the morning. The next day she moved them to see what would happen. Nothing came for seventeen years. Then her grandson, cutting stems in the same garden, found a clean dry passage through the heap he was working on, wide enough to put his arm into. He could feel leaves opening at the far end, though the heap was made entirely of dead stalks.

The growth itself is less patient than it looks. Under the big banks there are always young leaves pushing up into the places where old ones have lain. A gardener can part a mat and show you a stem that has been bent flat for so long it has put out leaves at every joint; if the weight is taken off, the stem rises over the course of a morning and the leaves hang down like washing. People transplant those stems under their windows. They keep the rooms cool. After a death, when a house stands empty and nobody comes to thin the bank, it will grow across the sill. Years later someone lifting a roof slab may find a cushion of leaves on the inside of it, every leaf turned toward a nail hole.

The girl on the eastern road was born in a dry year. When she was small, her aunt took her out to clear the drain and let her pull the leaves loose with both hands. One afternoon the leaves came away and the girl saw fingers doing the same work a little farther in. She called her aunt to look. By the time the woman knelt down there was only the rush of water, but the girl had seen that the other person was trying to get a long stem out crosswise. She lay flat and pushed one end of it through. Something took the weight off it. She remembered that better than the fingers.

Now she is nearly grown and is beginning to be asked to stay overnight in the city, where there is always work for someone who can carry a pot without knocking it against her knee. Her uncle would like her to go. Her aunt has been showing her how to pack a deep jar for the road: lid on, cloth around the neck, the strap low enough that it cannot ride over the shoulder. They have not used their own deep jar since the bad year, when there was so little in the basin that a cup brought up grit. The girl asks if she can have it. Her aunt says of course, if she can get it out from under the bench.

It is heavier than she expects. She thinks at first that someone has put a few stones in it to keep it steady. She gets her shoulder under the strap and feels the weight move, all at once, to the other side. From the yard her aunt calls to her to set it down gently. There is plenty of time. The road is dry all the way to the city, and someone has just swept the paving on the far side of the door.
