In the house nearest the gate lives a woman named Ada who can tell how long a thing has stood in the ward by the underside of it. The milk crate outside her door has been there eleven years. Its bottom is gone, but you can lift it by either handle and the square of earth inside comes up with it. She grows parsley in it. When she wants to move it into the sun she rocks it gently first, the way you would wake somebody you didn’t want to startle.

Her grandson lives at the other end, in one of the houses with two steps. He works at the mat works and comes home with his sleeves wet to the elbows. On Thursdays he eats at Ada’s. They watch the weather on television with the sound off because Ada can hear the presenter better that way, she says, though what she actually watches is his hand moving over the places where her sisters live.

Most of the people in the six houses have worked at the rollers at one time or another. The man who does the washing in the sunny gap used to straighten the wet bands with the heel of his hand. He still takes a crease out of a pillowcase with that same short push. Nobody now living in the ward remembers tying a boat to the rings. When his daughter was small he used to tell her they were handles, and that in bad weather the people on the other side used them to hold the wall down. For years afterward she worried about those people.

There is also a small white animal under the paving. Ada found one when the pipe was repaired. She’d put the loosened brick on the upturned lid of her dustbin, and while the men were in the house trying the taps, something began to fold itself out of the pad of roots. It was no longer than a grain of cooked rice. At first she thought it was a grub, but it had three little feet along one side and none she could see along the other. It laid each foot down twice before trusting its weight to it. On reaching the edge of the lid it turned and went back into the roots. By the time the men came out she had put the brick back herself, upside down. You can still see the maker’s name.

There must be a great many of the animals. When the grandson was eight he left a tin boat in a rut and found it the next morning clear of the water, resting crookedly on a wedge of roots. He put it back and lay down to watch. For most of an hour nothing happened. Then, beneath the stern, a little fan of white feet came out. They did not push the boat. They took hold of the loose ends of the roots and drew them together. The stern rose just enough for the water inside to run out over the bow. He tried for a week to make them do it again, but they had no interest in the boat once its paint had warmed in the sun.

The people don’t have a name for them that everyone uses. They turn up in places that have to bear something: under the foot of a rain barrel, under a loose doorstep, in the wet felt beneath a paving brick that rocks when you tread on it. The pump man found them once in the sole of a boot he’d left standing by the door. Only the left boot. The right one had fallen on its side.

Last winter Ada began taking a chair out under the vines in the afternoon. One leg was short, and her grandson offered to cut her a shim at work. By the time he brought it round the chair had stopped rocking. He tipped it back to see what she’d done. Under the short leg was a little black cushion, densely woven, with a square depression in the top. He touched it with his thumb and a drop of water came out.

He set the chair down an inch to one side. Ada, coming out with her tea, moved it back without looking.
