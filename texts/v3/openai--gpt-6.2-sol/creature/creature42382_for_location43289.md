The old seal has a white scar across one nostril. When she sleeps, the scar is the last part of her to go under. She hangs head up over a joint, sinks a little, comes back without opening her eyes, and breathes. Her pup sleeps flat on the surface. He can do this in water that would carry him eight miles before he woke, and sometimes she has to go and get him.

There is a gate she can hear opening from her sleeping place. The pup hears it too and dives at once. He spends most of his breath getting down and has to come up before the fish arrive. She waits for the loose blades of kelp to lift off the grate. Then she goes down the lee slope, keeping close enough to the concrete that her whiskers brush it when she turns. The herring come over the crest all facing one way. The first few pass her. She takes one from farther back, where there are enough fish on either side of it that it cannot turn.

At the grate a crab has outgrown the square it lives in. It can still drop through if it goes sideways and folds one claw under the other. In the dark below are three of its old shells. The smallest is almost whole. A shrimp goes in and out of it, picking at a little meat left in the hinge. The crab will have to leave the grate before it sheds again. For several nights it has been going as far as the first bolt after dark, feeling around the head of the bolt, and coming back.

There are things on the bare pitches, though you cannot see them from the water above. Between two ridges left by a board in the concrete, a thin brown film is growing. A snail no bigger than a grain of rice has eaten a wandering white track through it. Where the snail came to a raised letter in the maker’s stamp, it went all the way around the letter and then doubled back to eat the middle. It has taken most of the summer to get that far.

Under the lamps, the small fish find enough to eat after sundown. They hold at the edge of the light, where little drifting animals flash into sight and are gone. A larger fish can come right up under them in the dark. Every so often a lamp has a crowd of young fish above it and then none. A few minutes later they are back, though not all the same ones.

The pup has begun to catch his own fish. What he catches most often is a kind that lies along the joints and is mottled like old stone. On the new floor he can see it from a long way off. He brings it to the surface and lets it go, then dives after it. If he does this over a valley, the fish can get down to a joint before he catches it again. Over the high ground it reaches the concrete and lies still. He goes past it once or twice, looking for something that moves.

Late in the evening the old seal surfaces beside him above the white line. The last gull has gone inland. From there they could swim north to the old shore, and she used to do that every night when he was small enough to leave on the stones. Now she turns down the basin. He follows her for a while, then stops to nose at a patch of smooth water where the warm flow is coming up. She is almost out of sight when he lifts his head. He gives a short call, more like a cough than anything that ought to carry. She stops. With her nose just clear of the water, she waits for him to do it again.
