The first one you see is coming home. It fills the doorway from side to side but not quite to the lintel, and at first you take it for a mat somebody has hung up to drip. Then it puts a fold of itself over the sill and draws the rest after it. On the underside of the fold are small white pads, set far enough apart that you can see the dark skin tighten between them as they take hold. It is neither quick nor particularly careful. The last pad slips, and the whole animal settles back against the sill with a wet slap. From inside the room another one puts out an edge for it to lean on.

Given a dry floor they would not get far. In the streets they go along the gutters with their backs out of the water, passing one another by flattening against the sides. The biggest are the width of a bed. Their backs have the dull red of the bark on the rim, though the older ones go black in patches and carry little gardens at the edges. You can sometimes tell where one has been by a fern frond left standing shiny side down. There is very little else to show for their passage. They feed on the soft coats that grow on submerged stone; a well-used gutter is clean all the way to the bottom.

The steps are for the young. Inside each of those rooms is a shallow bath with a place at the rear where a thread of water comes through the wall. The sill sets the depth. A newly settled one lies in hardly more water than would wet a plate, working its white pads against the floor without going anywhere. In the next room you can see the sides of a young one lift and fall. It has water enough there to turn itself over. The family moves it along as it grows. Nobody seems in a hurry to see it out in the street. In one of the deeper rooms an animal already big enough to block the door is still being fed: an old one brings it a round stone, turns the green side up, and waits while it eats.

They have no eyes that you can find. To get a look at you, one lays a narrow edge in a runnel and waits for you to shift your feet. You can fool it by standing absolutely still, but only until you lift your foot again. The little taps at the corners are how they keep track of one another. Under each sounding lintel there is a thin flap in the wet wood. A drop hits it, and the jar of that drop travels down the root to rooms a long way off. Sometimes a resident puts a pad up and stops the flap, leaving the drop to fall on its soft back. A little later, from a corner you cannot see, a second tap stops. Then the first one lets go. You can spend a whole wet morning in a doorway listening to two neighbors do this, with intervals long enough for you to forget you were listening.

They go to the square to find those who have left a room. At the pool they hang over the wall and lower an edge into the water. If you watch from the steps you may see a dozen of them doing this, not touching, their broad backs turned to one another. The still water brings them the knocks of far-off doors, the wash of bodies passing in narrow streets, the interruptions where something has laid itself across a fall. Now and then one pulls out all at once and goes off with such purpose that you want to follow it. Usually it takes a gutter too narrow for your feet.

There are very old ones in the square whom you could mistake for part of the wall. The roots have divided around their pads and thickened over them. They can no longer go after what they hear. The others bring them stones to eat from and, sometimes, a youngster newly able to travel. It is set down beside the old body with one edge in the pool. For a while there is nothing to see. Then the youngster begins to lift its pads, one after another, making the motions of going down a street.

This is how it learns the way to a house it has never been to. Later, when you meet it coming down toward you on the street you have been descending all afternoon, it will go by on the wall side without a pause. It is on its way to the crossing with the white root. There is a room just beyond it where the water is deep enough now, and someone has been keeping the door.
