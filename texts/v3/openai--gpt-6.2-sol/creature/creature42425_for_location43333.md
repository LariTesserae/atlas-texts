Something sleeps with its weight on the four wires. You can tell when it rolls over. Three of the wires go slack and the fourth takes a little bend, not much more than a hair will take when you lay it across a finger. The leaves don’t turn then. The click comes later, when it has got comfortable and the mountain can draw all four wires out again.

At noon you can sometimes see a piece of its underside. It passes through the second glint, the distant one, and for as long as the light is cut off there is a patch of gray belly where there ought to be miles of air. The skin is thin. A white rib shows through it, and another rib moves up against the first as the thing breathes. Along the belly are four shallow grooves. The wire has been lying in them a long time.

It comes up to the old leaf after dark. There is hardly room between the stone and the leaf for what it puts through: a flat, jointed toe, with a soft pad on either side of the joint. It sets one pad on the buried part and the other on the part still opening. A grain comes uphill between them. The toe lifts to let it pass. On some nights it does this until morning, without shifting its weight on the wires. By then the pads have left two small clean places on the leaf. The grains go on past them and disappear under the stone.

The shadow on the mountainside is where its young one lies. At first there is only enough shade for the head. The second lobe makes room for a shoulder. The rest of it is folded small enough that, when it stirs, the whole shadow gives a twitch like a leaf about to turn over in a wind. In the basin the corresponding leaf is still rolled tight against its stem.

Just before the sun goes off the face, the little one stretches a foot out of the shade. It touches the bright rock and draws back. Far down, the gray belly tightens. One wire goes taut, and the rolled leaf opens by the width of a nail. It will take most of the night to open the rest of the way. By noon there will be room for the foot.
