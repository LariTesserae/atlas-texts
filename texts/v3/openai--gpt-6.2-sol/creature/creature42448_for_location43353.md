Under the bench lives an animal the color of the bench. Its back continues the shallow dip of the seat. It has six feet, four on the underside and two around the end, just below the bead of water. The two you can see are small and square, with a nail at each corner. There is a dark line in the ceramic between them. What looks like dirt in the line is a row of its young, each with its feet set out in the same order as the parent's. The ones nearest the bead have clear bellies. In the last one you can see a little white coil.

It would be easy to take the whole family for an accident of manufacture. Elsewhere there are animals that look like the parts a fitter has set down and will come back for: a length of split tubing with eyelids along the split, a washer with a muscular rim that has pulled itself oval around a bolt. The resemblance gets poorer the farther you go into the wet. The swimmers have broad, unencumbered faces. Their eyes are set far enough apart that one can look into the face of a neighbor while the other looks out along the basin. They rest their lower jaws on each other's backs. Where one has lost a corner of its jaw, there is a fold of loose skin on the back that receives it.

The little animals in the larger animal's mouth are not all of one kind. Two have their tails in the water and their heads firmly wedged between teeth. Another is standing on the tongue. It has raised one foot, either to take a step or to keep the foot dry. Between its toes is something so fine that at first you take it for the shadow of a hair. There are several strands of it. They run out past the teeth and down the wall, crossing the boundaries between one broad animal and the next without regard for which feet belong to which back. Where they pass through the water they carry small clear beads. The beads nearest the mouth are round; those farther down have begun to draw out into threads themselves.

On the dry hump, a creature more than halfway out of its shell has a strand at the corner of its eye. It runs back into the shell. You can see it through a chipped place low in the white wall, stretched taut across the space the creature has lately occupied. There is almost nothing else left inside. In an older shell nearby, the strand has gone slack and fallen into loops. A small blind animal is asleep in the loops with its blunt head dropped over one of them.

Not everything is resting. At the weir the little animal with the scar is in trouble. From above you see the raised front feet, which make it look as though it has sprung clear. From below you can see that the water has caught and turned a hind foot. There is an animal on the lower face of the weir, a thin one the color of water running over steel. It has got most of its body out from under the sheet. Its head is pointed down. It has taken the little animal's heel in its mouth, not between its teeth but in the loose skin at the side, and the whole weight of the water has come onto that corner of its mouth. A second thin animal lies across its tail. You can follow the line of them back into the shadow under the lip, where the last one has a foot braced against a bolt.

There is a place farther along where the rain has missed a strip no wider than a hand. Small animals stand there with their heads projecting into the rain. The water on their heads has made them top-heavy. Some have leaned against the bodies of others; some have sat back with their forefeet held in the air. A few are old enough that their translucent skins have gone cloudy at the joints. The oldest has a youngster under its chin, on the dry side. Its throat is stretched, and the youngster has fastened the flat of one ear to it.

Sit long enough on the bench and the electrical note disappears without your going behind the pier. The bench carries a different sound. It comes up through the warm seat at intervals wide enough that the first two might be the same sound remembered. At the next, you can put a finger to the end of the bench and feel the ceramic answer. The square feet do not shift. The coil in the last of the clear bellies tightens a little, then lets go.

The bead at the end of the seat is still there when the light fails on the wet side of the road. On the dry side the sun has a long way yet to go. The reflection in the bead is too dim now to make out the wings. Against the bright paving you can see the mouth of the animal under the bench, a shallow opening between the square feet. Its lower lip has reached up to the bead. Along the lip, in a row too fine to see until the water touches it, are the mouths of the young.
