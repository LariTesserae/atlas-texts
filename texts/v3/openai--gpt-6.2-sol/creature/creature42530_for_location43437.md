The woman who measures the pools lives above the bakery. She keeps her good boots under the stove and her work boots on the landing, where the water off them can run into a dent in the sill. Her daughter, who is eight, knows the pools by their numbers and the grazers by their faces. There is one with a black patch on its lower lip that she has been seeing since she was small enough to be carried. This year it has a calf. The calf puts its feet down in the water wherever its mother puts hers, even when there is perfectly good footing to either side.

The baker came up from below with enough fruit trees to plant the yard behind the ovens. He has one left, a pear in a wooden tub. For years he brought it in at night, but it was the heat indoors that was doing it harm. Now it stays out by the wall, and he wraps the tub in old flour sacks before the first hard freeze. It opens a few flowers most summers. There are no other pears within reach of whatever visits them.

There is a man at the hospital whose work is to fit people with the little bottles of oxygen they need while they get used to the height. He has lived here thirty-one years and still needs one himself after a chest cold. He takes the tram home past the flooded shelter. In very cold weather an oval of open water remains under the bench. One of the gray birds goes in there to sleep. He knows because he once got off at the next stop and walked back to see whether the bird was hurt. It came out over his shoulder, close enough for him to feel the air from its wings.

The gray birds have been here longer than the trams. Their nests are low in the bridgework, out of the wind, and in a bad thaw some of them drown on the nest. The fox takes the ones the water brings out. She has a dry place under a slab a little uphill from the new bench, with three cubs in it. They have begun to come out while she is away. One has discovered that if it steps on the loose end of a stone the other end comes up. It does this over and over, biting at the edge each time, until the stone slips into the gap and lies flat.

Most of the herd has never been inside a district with houses, but in late winter a few of the older animals come to the bakery wall after dark and lick the mortar. The baker used to drive them off. Now he leaves the yard gate unlatched so they won’t lean on it. In the morning their breath is frozen on the bottom pane of the back door. In the lee of the wall their hooves have worked hair and grit and the chaff swept out of the bakery into a dark strip deep enough for a willow seedling. The baker pulls the seedlings out of the drain, but he leaves that one. When he puts the pear out in spring, the willow is already in leaf.

Under the vacant districts there are stonefly young in cracks the surveyor cannot get a gauge into. Some of them will spend three winters there. They feed on small things washed down from the occupied streets, and on smaller things that grow on the washed-down things. This is why, when a pool falls too fast, she looks at the stones as well as the waterline. More than once she has found a fringe of living bodies above the water, each holding to the part of a stone that was still wet.

Her daughter goes out with her sometimes after supper. At the third light the girl squats to see what the thaw has brought. A few of the grains are blue, and one is clear enough to go dark when she puts a wet finger on it. She starts to pick it up, but there is something tucked against its downstream side, so small she can only see it by its movement. She calls her mother over. By the time her mother kneels beside her, the water has risen enough to cover the grain. They stay there a while anyway, with the surveyor’s book shut on her knee and the light on above them.
