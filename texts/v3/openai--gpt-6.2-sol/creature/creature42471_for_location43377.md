The oldest resident has one root in the threshold and another beneath the bench. Between them there is nothing you could point to as a body. The water under the bench runs too fast for even a root hair to hold, so the root comes up on the far side, through a hole bored for a peg. In the house it puts out small, nearly transparent leaves. Outside, where the current can get at it, its leaves are black and long enough to lie across the roof.

The cups belong to it in much the way the roof does. It did not make them and could get on without them, but the cord passes through a thickening in its stem, and the knock of the cups makes that stem contract. Three knocks close the little leaves indoors. Usually the cold arrives just afterward. If a loose stone strikes the cups in otherwise still water, the leaves close all the same, and open again, one at a time, over the next several days.

Farther down the valley, where there are no cups, a grove has to wait for the cold to touch it. The long leaves flatten first. This uncovers a second set of leaves, short and cupped, which sift the water for what the dense current has brought off the high shelves. Afterwards their hollows are full of white grit. In the quiet that follows, the leaves take up what little they can use and tip the grit onto the slate. The pale crescents below the groves are made this way, a pinch at a time. They last until the next hard flow.

A falling boulder sometimes takes half a grove with it and leaves the other half alive. For a while both halves light at the same change of pressure. As the lower one settles into the silt, its leaves begin to open at different times. Their light comes later, and in a year when the cold does not reach that depth, they do not light at all. New growth comes only on the upper face of the boulder. Below it the old roots soften in the silt, still fitting every hollow in the stone long after they have ceased to feed anything.

At the saddle the plants are small. The broad leaves against the dust are mostly old growth; the living tips are hard white points tucked into the joints of the paving. Now and then one pushes out into the road. There is nothing there to stop it but the water, which bends it back and forth in the same place until it breaks. Along the road’s edges the little upright stones give just enough shelter for a tip to make the next joint. The plants have advanced farther along those two lines than anywhere else on the saddle. They are not quite joined from end to end.

Nothing in the valley goes into a house for shelter. Even the plant under the bench began outside, and it is only by following its roots through the doorway that you can tell how much of it lives in the room. At the next cold flow the cups will lift. The stem has grown around so much of the cord that the cups can scarcely reach one another now. One strikes the lintel instead. Inside, the leaf nearest the door folds at that single knock. The others stay open until the cold gets to them.
