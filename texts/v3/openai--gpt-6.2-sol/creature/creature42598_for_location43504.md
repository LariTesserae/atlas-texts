The wall grazers are longer than they look from above. Most of the length is neck and belly; the legs are short, with two toes that can spread far enough to take hold on either side of a root. An old female working her way down a wall keeps one hind foot in place until the other three have found somewhere to go. She can stand like that for an hour, cropping the little white shoots that grow from the lower faces of the leaf mat. There is usually a strip of brown turf above her where she has already fed. By the next rains the leaves there will have turned themselves green side up again.

The young are born on the tops. For the first few weeks they follow anything that moves ahead of them at the right speed, including a person. At the rim their mothers step out of sight, then call from just below it. A young one may spend a whole morning walking up and down the edge before it puts a foot over. There are paths with a shallow trench running beside them, worn by generations of young grazers looking for an easier place to begin.

Sailwings spend the night with their wings folded along the rock. What looks like gray foliage is mostly the soft outer edge of the folded wing, which hangs down over the animal's feet. When the colony leaves, the wall behind it is yellow with droppings and bare of everything but a wiry fern. The fern grows in little shelves. The ledge birds nest in them after the sailwings have gone to feed, and learn to sit very still when they come back. A bird that flies at the wrong moment can be carried halfway out over the valley in the rush of wings.

A sailwing feeds with its mouth open and its head tucked down, taking the small things the warm air brings off the walls. In a bad spell it will work the same stretch of rising air until dusk and come home with nothing in its crop but seed floss. The old ones have white patches where the floss catches in the corners of their mouths. There are years when almost every sailwing you see has a white mouth.

The people who tend the willow huts are few enough that a hut with its door open is worth walking over to. They keep to the tops while the grazers are bearing and follow them onto the side paths once the young can manage the steps. Much of what they carry is cord: short lengths for the feet of milk animals, long lengths for lowering a basket past a torn place in the path, and very fine lengths, boiled with willow bark, for sewing. A child learns to tell a good root from a bad one before being trusted to carry a full cup. The live roots are cool inside. A dead one can feel perfectly sound until it parts.

They milk only a few of the grazers. These are animals that have taken to sleeping on a path or at the upper end of a stair, where a person can get a knee behind them. The rest belong to whoever can find them, in much the sense that the rain does. A family may know the voice of an old female on the next mesa without anyone in the family ever having been there. When she dies there is no way to go and look. For a while they still notice the hour at which she used to call.

The stone cups at the thresholds are for washing grit off the feet. Children sometimes put fold beetles in them. The beetles run round the waterline and never stop long enough to eat; a cup, even with a leaf laid over it, has nothing for them. In the folded leaves they feed on the pale growth that comes after rain. Their young are nearly transparent, and when the water gets low you can see them gathered at the deep end, each with its head against the leaf. An empty fold will fill again in the afternoon. If the leaf has been opened flat, it may take a week to curl back, and by then something else will have laid eggs in it.

Below the root mat, the burrowing animals leave long chambers roofed with the roots they cannot chew. Their hunters are thin enough to pass through the spaces between those roots. They do not always follow a burrow from the entrance. One will lie across the roof with its chin down, feeling for the scrape of teeth below, and enter where the roof is weakest. Sometimes the first sign of one is a little spill of dry earth out of an otherwise green ceiling. The ledge birds watch for that. They catch the hunters as readily as the burrowers, and carry either one to the nest folded in the middle.

There is a hut on one of the old mesas that has grown shut around a pool. The pool was no bigger than a washbasin when the willow was planted. Its drowned leaves raised the bottom, and the willow roots spread into the shallows until the water went out under the wall and opened on the far side. Nobody remembers using the hut, but people still come there with their cups. In the heat of the day the grazers stand in the open water, letting the little fish pick at the skin between their toes. At the first drop of rain they lift their heads. From inside the willow there is an answering movement: the young ones have found a gap low in the stems and have been sleeping on the dry floor. They come out one at a time, on their knees at first, with the bent leaves dragging along their backs.
