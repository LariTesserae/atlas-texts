The woman who reads the meters carries a teaspoon. Before she opens a cabinet she runs the bowl of the spoon along the bottom of the door, where the little shell animals wedge themselves in the gap. If she forgets, she hears their legs go against the metal when she turns the key. She tips them out onto the gallery and reads the meter while they find their feet. They usually go back into the cabinet before she’s finished.

Her name is Iven. She lives above the old laundry, in a flat with two doors, one a flight of stairs higher than the other. The upper door was once a window. When her father lived there he kept a chair in front of it so nobody would forget and step out. Now there’s a gallery, and the chair is on it. Iven eats there in the evening with her heels on the lower rail. She can see which of the western pans will last the night by the places where the shell animals stop walking.

They don’t all want the same depth. The young ones will make do with water thin enough to run up a leg by itself. The old ones need enough to get their shells clear of the bottom. An old one stranded on a divide walks high and rather fast, pausing every few steps to lower itself until the tip of its shell touches stone. Iven used to think this was how it tested for water. Once, on a dry gallery, she watched one do it repeatedly over the same spot. A smaller animal came out from beneath its shell, went a little way on its own, and climbed back under. After that she started turning the old ones over with the spoon less often, even when they’d got somewhere inconvenient.

Under the return bridge there are animals that never come up into town. Their backs are soft when they hatch and harden on the side away from the pipes. A full-grown one taken off the underside of the bridge can’t stand properly on level ground: all its feet turn in like fingers around a cup. In the cooler part of the year there are empty skins among them, so exact that you can see the slender tubes that lined the joints. The apprentice at the works mistook one for a live animal and spent half a morning trying to persuade it off a valve handle. Nobody told him until he’d fetched a dish of water.

The apprentice is Iven’s nephew. He sleeps through the changeover at two now, though for his first month he woke a little before it and lay waiting for the tremor to stop. His room is on the works side of the building. In the other room his mother takes in mending, mostly cuffs, because people here put a hand down to steady themselves on wet stone and wear through a sleeve long before the rest of a shirt. She does the work by the upper door. On days when the water takes the streets, she keeps a second chair out for whoever has come the long way round.

Children fish in the empty blocks as soon as the current slackens. What they’re after is a small translucent animal that comes up out of the joints and swims on its side. You can see the meal in its gut, a cream-colored line no longer than an eyelash. Nobody eats it. The children hold a kitchen sieve flat against the paving and lift it quick, then carry what they catch to the shallow landings where the four-footed fishers wait. For a while the fishers won’t touch anything put there by hand. The children have learned to leave the sieve where it is and go upstairs. From the gallery they watch the fine dimples at the ends of the animal’s feet close up one by one as it moves.

In the evening Iven sometimes has company on the chair beside her. More often she has a shirt somebody’s brought back for a second repair, or a bowl with another bowl turned over it to keep supper warm. Below her are the little sounds that don’t carry as far as the bridge: the lid of a meter box settling, a spoon dropped in the laundry sink, the dry tick of a shell against a pier. On a night when many of the western animals come through, the ticking goes on for an hour. They cross the paving under the houses, where the water that rose in the afternoon is still coming up, and by morning the only sign of them is a set of clean tracks through the film on the lowest stair.
