The animal hanging from the sedge is so nearly the color of the split wood that the dark of its gut is the first thing to distinguish it. Its feelers go down one at a time. The first comes back wet. The second finds the root and bends against it, and the animal walks down its own feeler’s length before it lets go of the leaf.

It is a springtail, though not one of those that feed in the clearings. It is too long to turn comfortably in the creases they retreat into. It eats what the sheet of water leaves at the edge of the floor: the soft insides of broken eggs, a skin that a mite has stepped out of, white growth bruised loose from the willow. This morning it finds a grub’s shaving with something still attached to it. It pulls until the shaving turns over. There is nothing on the other side.

The grubs are the greater part of the room’s substance. The middle pillar has one just beneath its skin, lying on its side in a gallery it made when it was small enough to turn around in. It cannot turn now. It feeds at the blind end and packs what it passes behind it, so that the length of its dwelling stays about the same as the dwelling itself moves through the wood. When a foot comes down, the packed end takes up water. The grub stops with a chip between its jaws. By the time the wood has lifted again, the chip has softened enough to swallow.

The hunter lives deeper than the grubs, in a slit that goes on below the apparent floor. It is a little centipede with legs that seem shorter when it runs than when it stands still. When it stands still, even the last pair is moving, feeling behind it. Most of its hunting is done on the side of the root the room does not open onto. The springtails on the floor have no way of knowing whether it is there or gone. They will leave the strip clear even through a whole day in which it does not come.

The drinker at the far cleft is a spider. The strands that take the drops are not a web in the shape a person would look for. They are the outer lines of a retreat, put out at different heights as the wood shifted. The spider keeps some taut and lets others sag. A drop that catches on one of the slack ones can travel a surprising distance as the line gives under it. In the cleft the spider puts its mouth to the water. Sometimes there is a mite in the drop, paddling with all its legs. The spider drinks the water around it, and the mite walks away.

She laid the eggs across the interval. In autumn, when the split was new, she let herself down from the sedge on a line and hung clear of the floor. Air coming out of the open lane carried her to the far wall. She has not made that crossing since. The sedge has thickened against the ceiling, and there is no longer room to drop from it as she did. She put the eggs into a shallow check in the wood and covered them with silk and two pieces of willow felt. The silk underneath is still clean. The silk on top has collected enough fine wood to look like part of the wall.

When the root arrives it will touch the covering first. Some of the young may already be out by then. They will have spent their first days in the check, close enough together that a leg moved by one is moved again by the next. The arriving root will give them a way down without silk. It will give the centipede a way up.

Above them the washhouse keeper sets a pail on the last stair while she works the door loose. There is a long pressure, gentler than a footfall. The sedge flattens. In the middle pillar the grub draws back as far as it can. At the far end the root tip touches the silk and leaves a wet mark on it no larger than the point of an egg. Then the keeper takes up her pail, and the tip and the silk part. The mark remains.
