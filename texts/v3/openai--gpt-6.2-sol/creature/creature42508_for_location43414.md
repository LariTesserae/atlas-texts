Something lives under the dry lips of the steps. In the afternoon, when the water pulls in from the rims, you can see a little of it: a pale fringe that might be the ragged edge of a mineral deposit. Touch the rock beside it and the fringe gathers into three or four blunt points. These take hold farther back, where you cannot see them, and draw the rest after. The movement is slow enough that if you look up at the tree and then down again you will think the fringe has broken off.

It is all one animal, though it has been a long time since it could have fitted in any one of the basins. Its oldest part is a gristle no thicker than a fingernail, lodged behind a shelf well above the present waterline. There are pockets in that shelf, each the size of a tooth socket. In a wet year the animal can still fill them. It does so in order, from the lowest up, and when the water goes down it takes several days to get itself out of the last one. This is why a little patch of wall sometimes stays dark long after the rest has dried.

It has no use for the ferns. What it eats is the soft part of a leaf after the leaf has ceased to look edible. Along the undersides of the steps it puts out mouths so small that a vein can pass over a dozen of them without catching. The veins go on down into the lowest basin. There, most years, they collect in one corner and make a loose brown cloth that lifts and settles as the water comes in.

The notch appeared when the animal was young enough to be turned over by a hard rain. At that time the bottom basin had no lower lip, and you could see the far ridge through the opening. The animal grew across it, first in strands that caught grit, then in a continuous edge over which the grit could settle. The opening closed. The view did not. When the left face of the notch takes the sun, the fringe under the lowest step opens a fraction. In winter, when snow lies in the notch, it hardly opens at all.

Most of what falls into the hollow stays there. Once, a long time ago, a bird dropped a length of blue thread beside the tree. The thread fell in as the rim flooded and was carried partway under a step. For half a summer the animal took it in each evening and let it out again in the heat of the day. It could neither eat it nor get it past a rough place in the stone. Eventually the thread wore through. The loose end went down the mountain in a spill of rain; the other is still there, quite white now, with a living seam grown over it.

If you sit with your feet flat on the warm stone, the animal will draw back a little from the side nearest you. It does this for the shadow, not the weight. Sit long enough and it will come out under your shadow as readily as under the fern's. The place to watch is not the fringe but the dry lip above it. A bead will form underneath and get large enough that you can see, inverted in it, one of your knees and a patch of open sky. Then the animal takes the water back.
