There are twenty-three people in the town. At dusk, when the courts look deserted, most of them are in the long room under the lowest courtyard, eating supper. You can hear the spoons from the steps if you put your ear to the wood. The people who live above the room know when supper is over by the scrape of a particular bench. It has one short leg, and the woman who sits on it will not let anybody mend it. She hooks her heel under the low end to get up.

They are not especially small, though visitors who have seen the town from across the valley tend to expect it. There is a tall man called Edrin who has to turn his head sideways to get into his own bedroom. The entrance was high enough when he was a boy. Now he has two daughters, both taller than he was at their age, and every spring they ask why he doesn’t move to a better room. He says he can reach the shelf from bed. It is a very good shelf, grown out of a knot, with a dish in the middle that keeps a cup from sliding off.

Most things they eat come up from below the snow line. In autumn there are sacks in the courts and an argument about which floor will take their weight. The town grows a few things of its own: a sour green leaf that is good with potatoes, and soft white knobs on the roots beside the seep, which taste of almost nothing but are ready in the middle of winter. The children eat them on the way to fetch water. They are supposed to leave the little ones, and have developed a wonderfully fine sense of what constitutes a little one.

Each household keeps its part of the town open. This is less work than it sounds for a well-used door. The trouble is with the turning at the bottom of the stair, where everybody puts a foot in the same place and the root grows up hard on either side. Once a week someone goes round with a bucket of hot water and a wooden wedge. There is also a passage the width of a hand which nobody has tried to widen for years. Two sisters live on opposite sides of it. They used to walk through to borrow things from one another; now they pass things through on a saucer. When one of them is ill, the other has to go all the way round, and arrives cross and out of breath, carrying more than she meant to bring.

The children know more ways through the town than the grown people do, but they lose them in the course of growing. A boy named Tavi got his head through a fork in the roots one morning and could not get it back. His older sister lay down on the other side and talked to him about what they would have for supper until he stopped pulling and she could show him which way to turn his chin. Afterward, when he asked why she hadn’t just told him to turn it in the first place, she said she had. He remembers the potatoes very clearly, but not that.

The new rock at the top of the town fell in summer. Before the fall there was a narrow room beyond the landing, where a woman named Mara used to sit to sew because the light was good. Nobody was in it when it went. For weeks afterward people kept bringing her things to mend and leaving them on the landing, although she had put her stool downstairs and was working there in much worse light. In the end she went up herself and came back with an armful of shirts damp at the shoulders.

It was Mara who laid the stem across the nearest block. She goes up every few days now, not to tend it but to see whether it has taken hold. The tallest of Edrin’s daughters goes with her sometimes. She wants to know whether there will be room for two stools, and whether they could have a window low enough to see out of while sitting down. Mara says they can decide about the window when they have a wall.

The last time they went up, the girl put one finger under the stem and found she could no longer lift it. Mara had already started down the stair. The girl called her back, and each of them tried it once more, very gently, so as to be sure.
