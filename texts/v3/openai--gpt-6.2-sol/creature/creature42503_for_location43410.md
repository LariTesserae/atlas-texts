The folded blades are the oldest things on the plateau, except for the trees. It is possible to tell this by the way a blade fits its crack. The smallest has a blunt little hook at its tip, and opposite the hook, in the stone, there is a hollow of precisely the same shape. The blade did not thrust its way out. The crack opened around it.

A tree is alive in all the places you would expect, though not quite in the expected order. A branch cut from the crown will hold its leaves as it fell, each turned the same way to the light, for years. In the first hour after the cut, however, the collar at the foot of the tree draws a little away from the spindle. Its inner surface is dull and finely ridged. If the fallen branch is laid across a root, the collar closes again. It does this whether the branch belongs to that tree or not.

Most of what lives here is too thin to see from above. In the narrow spaces between matching faces of rock there are pale sheets, no thicker than the skin inside an eggshell. They hang from knobs on one face and trail into sockets on the other. Where a fissure widens, a sheet has long straight holes in it, all parallel. Held to the light, the edge of each hole shows the same pale grain as the inside of a broken leaf. There is no such tissue on the outer faces of the rim. Whatever the sheets do, they need a surface to reach toward.

The ivory cups have a use at dusk. As the sunlight leaves one crown and reaches under the next, a faint tremor crosses the cups, starting with those nearest the trunk. A loose salt crystal laid in one will walk a little way up the side. On some evenings it falls back. On others it goes over the lip and is gone. Below those flowers there is no scattering of salt. The crystals turn up much later in the fissures, caught at the margins of the pale sheets, where the tissue thickens around them. The oldest sheets have so many that they feel like the rough side of a matchbox.

Nothing goes from tree to tree above ground. Underfoot it is different. A foot set on a bare spindle makes a change in the next grove: the shadow of an ivory cup on a leaf goes ragged for a moment. Farther off, a folded blade eases just enough to show the fine white line along its inner edge. The movement does not always go in the same direction. Sometimes, by the time you take your foot away, it has come back to the spindle beneath you, and the tremor in your teeth has a second beat.

At the last tree the root across the gap is broad and flat. On its shaded underside are little transverse scars, some so old that their edges have gone the color of the stone. The scars are close together at the inner end and farther apart toward the wedge. Just beyond the newest one there is a slight swelling. It has the shape of a folded blade, but it points down.

After dark the wedge cools sooner than the plateau. You can lie at the rim and keep a finger under the root. Nothing closes on it. The bare rock draws away by an amount too small to see, and the root, which was slack enough to lift, takes the weight. A little farther in, where the stone is still warm, the collar of the last tree lets go of one ridge on the spindle and settles against the next. By morning there is room for your hand again. The swelling is still there, green all the way to its tip, feeling for a face of rock that has not yet come into reach.
