Under the stool lives a small, jointed animal. At rest it looks like a repair to the ceramic. It lies along the leg that enters the wall, with its belly over the join and its feet tucked into the cracks in the glaze. The exposed part of its back is grey. The part that touches the stool is the brown of a tooth that has never been used.

It comes down to feed in the hollows. For several years after a tick there is good feeding: the animal can put its mouth to the lip of a hollow and draw out a grain that has been worn away on one side and is still sharp on the other. It turns each grain in its mouth. It swallows the worn part. The sharp part falls through, clicks against the stone, and is there in the hollow again. That click is too small to hear from the height of the stool. With an ear to the floor you might hear a short bout of them, then a long interval in which the animal cleans its mouth with a foot.

In the lean years it goes farther out. It likes the heel of a handprint. There is more to eat where a person leaned hard before getting up. It has learned to start at the rim and work inward so the print does not cave in on it.

Once in its life it carries an egg out from under the stool. The egg has a soft shell and will dent if set on a flat surface. The animal walks about with it for a long time, trying the shallow places. A hollow under a stool leg would do, except that some of those hollows will be gone before the egg opens. The animal tests them by putting one of its hind feet into each and waiting. In the wrong hollow the foot gets steadily warmer. This can take most of a year.

The oldest egg in the chamber lies in the crease of a thumbprint, out beyond the patch of light. It was laid when the print was deep enough to hold it. Since then the seam has come through and the dust has drawn almost level. The animal that put it there is still alive. Every so often it goes out and takes the egg in its mouth while it digs the crease a little deeper. In that posture its underside shows. It has a row of very small scars where the young of a previous clutch held on.

When the egg opens, there will be a period in which the young animal can stand on the floor but cannot cling to the stool. Its feet have to harden first. The old one will stay down in the dust with it. If the tick comes in that time, both will shorten their legs and go flat, and the young one, which has never heard a sound so large, will try to get underneath the old one. There will be room. The old animal has been keeping itself just that far off the floor.
