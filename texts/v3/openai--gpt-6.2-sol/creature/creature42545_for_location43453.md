There are animals in the two plots that keep their water. In the deeper one you can see them without kneeling, provided the wind has dropped: little dark commas that jerk up from the bottom and go still. In the shallower one you have to lie down and put your cheek nearly on the grass. The things there are pale. What you see first is their shadows, going very fast over the gray stone.

For most of the year the shallower plot is dry. Its inhabitants spend that part of the year in the grit. Some are eggs, and some are animals that have drawn themselves in until there is hardly more to them than there is to an egg. Not every shower wakes them. There is one kind that comes out in the cold, while the water is still clear enough to count the grains of sand through it. By the time the pool is warm and brown and full of legs, that kind is gone. If you take away a spoonful of dry mud in August and leave it in a saucer in the rain, you may have a little of the town living on your windowsill before you have anything you can see.

A vole lives in the long bank on the uphill side. There is probably more than one, but you can follow the work of this one. It cuts the sedges low and eats them under a branch of willow. The discarded ends lie in a neat pile with the cut faces all one way. In fine weather it goes as far as the first turning. It doesn’t cross by the shortest route. It goes up the bank a little and comes down through a place where the grass has folded over the lane. There, for the length of its body, it has a roof.

Once the pipit lands on the bank above it. The bird has a white grub crosswise in its bill. It stays only long enough to get the grub turned round. The vole doesn’t come out for some time afterward, though the bird could hardly have done it any harm. When it does come out it takes a cut stem from the pile, eats the soft end, and puts the rest down crooked.

Under the square, away from the place where the water strikes, there are shelves of stone that never dry. Small white creatures live on those shelves. A dead leaf is a considerable event to them. Most of the leaves that come down the crack go straight through, but occasionally one lodges by its stalk and hangs against the rock. It goes soft first in the spaces between the veins. Weeks later the veins are still there, with water running down them and the white creatures moving in and out of the holes. None of them has ever been in the square. The willow that fed them might be growing directly overhead.

The willows are harder to count than the voles. On the middle downhill bank, what looks like a row of separate bushes is one willow that has rooted wherever its branches touched earth. If you feel beneath the leaves you can find the old wood running through it, quite thick in places, cold and smooth as a stone in a stream. Then there is a gap where the bank moved. On one side of the gap the broken end is already dry. On the other, the branch has six or seven sets of roots of its own. Its leaves are as green as any on the bank.

That branch will go on growing. The vole may take the way under it next winter, if snow fills the turning it uses now. The old way will still be there under the snow, with its little roof and the stems laid more or less in order. When the thaw comes, water will find it. For a few days it will carry water straight across the lane. Then the sod will settle, or a mouthful of earth will lodge there, and that will be the end of it.

You could come every summer and know the place well without noticing any of this. You might notice that you no longer have to step over the wet place at the first turning, or that the pool below the square is a little wider. Meanwhile, in that pool, as ice takes hold along both ends, the pale animals go down into the seams in the rock. The rain in the middle is still moving. Now and then it lifts a bent blade of sedge clear of the surface, and the blade lies down again with a small dark grain caught in the fork of it. In the morning the grain will be frozen there. It may stay there all winter, half an inch above the place it needs to fall.
