Under the fallen tree live two kinds of termite. The ones on the clay side bring their earth up in little covered roads and eat the wood without showing themselves. On the wet side the wood is soft enough for a thumb to go into it, and the termites there are pale, with heads the color of old honey. Where the two workings come close there is a rib of sound wood no wider than a hand. A gecko spends the day in a split in that rib. At dusk she comes out to the end of the trunk, where the last of the warmth lasts a little longer than it does in the air.

There are frogs in the figs. The smallest sit in the leaf axils with their legs folded so neatly that there seems to be nothing to them but a blunt green head. Higher up, in a fork where the tree has grown around a broken branch, there is a frog the color of the water it sits in. It sometimes goes weeks without coming down. Beetles fall into its fork, and ants come to the rim to drink. When a bat lands above it, the water takes the shake of the branch and puts it out again in rings so small they stop at the frog’s chin.

The toads are elsewhere. A spadefoot let the last flood pass over it in a chamber below the clay. There is a polished place on the chamber wall where its back rests. When enough rain gets down, it will dig out backward, leaving a plug of clay that looks, in the morning, like something pushed up by a growing root. It may find no water by then. A shower that brings the termites out by the thousand can leave nothing fit to lay eggs in. The toad will feed and go back under. On the nights when it does find water, its call is surprisingly hard to place. Standing at one pool, you can hear the toads in the next and miss the one at your feet until it takes a breath.

A small mongoose with a torn ear hunts the banks in daylight. She puts her forefeet on an exposed root and peers underneath before lowering the rest of herself. Her young have begun to follow her, though they still stop to nose places she has already searched. One has learned to turn over dry dung with both paws. Another keeps trying to turn over pieces of clay that are part of the bank. When a monitor goes by, they all get into a root hole, and the mother comes out again before the monitor’s tail has passed. She has brought them there in a dry year. The hole would be underwater in a good one.

Out on the flats the broad bands of grass are not all the same height. The herd crops the sweet new growth in the old channels, leaving the wiry stems on the rises to seed. By the end of the dry months a calf can vanish standing up in those stems. Its mother will go on grazing, taking a mouthful and then standing with her jaw still, listening to the calf pull at a leaf it hasn’t yet learned to bite through. Later it will follow her down a bank and put a foot in the hole she drinks from. She will shift her muzzle over and drink around it.

There is a heron that comes to these holes before daybreak. It spends most of its time standing in places where there cannot be fish. It takes beetles, a small snake once, and, after rain, the frogs that have come down farther than they meant to. When the herd approaches, the heron steps up onto a root. On some nights the animals leave it a little open water; on others they tread the edges in, and it has to wait for the water to come back through the mud. It has time. The first weavers are already squabbling in the thorn trees when it lowers one foot, opens its toes, and steps into the print the calf has left.
