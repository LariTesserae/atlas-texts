The porter keeps a second chair at the far end of the awning. It is a good chair, with a curved back and both its stretchers intact. Nobody sits in it. On the first night of every month he moves it a little farther from the wall.

There is a foot behind it. In the lamplight it looks like the bottom of an iron downpipe, except that it has three blunt toes, each with a small rim of white dust around it. The foot was there when the porter took the job. In twenty-three years it has come far enough forward that he can put the chair behind it instead of in front.

The rest of the animal is difficult to see from the awning. If you go to the far gate and look back on a night when the lamps are out, you can sometimes make out the underside against the sky. It has the shallow sag of a loaded wagon sheet. One leg stands in the freight square, one in a gap between the foundry and its pattern shop. Two more are out among the sidings. There may be another. A man looking for it once went down a cut and found, at eye level, a patch of skin with the marks of old harness on it. The marks were no wider than a finger. Whatever wore them into the skin must have been put on when the animal was small.

Small ones can be found in the warm workings below the factories. They are about the size of a hand and much more active than the old animals. A fitter going down to oil a bearing will sometimes find one straddling the housing, with its feet planted well apart and its belly clear of the metal. When the teeth meet it lowers itself until the skin of its belly just touches. After a few turns it gets up and goes to another machine. The fitters say there are teeth it likes and teeth it won’t bother with. They have also noticed that a young one will keep visiting a stopped machine for several nights after it has learned that the machine is stopped.

They do not seem to eat the grease. A little black yard animal can feed undisturbed on the same housing, even underneath one. The young have a fold along either side of the belly which closes over any dust that touches it. In the morning there will be two clean lines on the casing. What they get from the dust, if anything, is not known. They leave the filings.

An old animal moves one foot at a time. Before it lifts the foot, it spends a long while putting its weight somewhere else. This can be heard if you happen to be in the right place. One winter the people in Number Four heard a noise in their front room like a man trying the handle of a locked door. It came every night for eleven nights. On the twelfth it came once, quite loudly, from inside the iron sill. In the morning there was a print in the pavement across the street that had not been there before. The youngest child could stand in it with her arms out without touching either side. By the time she left home, the dust had filled it to the level of the street.

Out on the plain there are stretches of smooth stone which the surveyors used to join up as roads. The error is easy to make on paper. On foot you find that one stretch may be a quarter mile from the next, and that between them the rock is sharp enough to cut a boot. At the old turn below the yards there are six such stretches, each at a different distance from the road, all going around the hill.

Once, while the porter was sitting under the awning, something set a foot down at the far gate. He heard the grit shift. Before he got up he heard a second foot, much smaller, set down in exactly the same place. Then both were beyond the reach of the awning. He went out with his lamp, but the square showed him nothing. Early the next morning, at the turn in the road, a driver coming up from below stopped for a little animal. It was standing by itself in the middle of the road, waiting with one foot raised. The driver sounded his horn. After a while it set the foot down, not forward onto the paving, but sideways onto the bare, level place where the hill had been.
