The people who live on the outer streets are particular about what counts as indoors. A kitchen with a door and a ceiling is not indoors if the floor stops three feet beyond the stove. A bench in a public square may be indoors, even with the whole western sky in view. The distinction matters to children, who are allowed to go wherever they like indoors until they are old enough to be trusted with the other sort of place. A child can spend an entire morning going to see an aunt who lives two doors away. There are four legal routes, one of which passes behind the bakery oven.

The baker keeps one of the small flat animals in his kitchen, though he would say it keeps itself. It winters in the joint above the oven door. On baking days it comes down and lies beside the scales, where it can be mistaken for the lighter of the two stone weights. The baker’s daughter made that mistake once. The animal put out six feet and walked off the scale pan while she was pouring the flour. She says she knew what it was before it moved, because no weight in her father’s shop was ever that warm.

Some of the people who work in the recess have never seen a firing begin. A heat lasts longer than their spell on a given shift. They come in and take over a furnace that has been hot since before their last rest day, and hand it on with a few notes about a shutter that sticks or a patch of facing that has gone white. Their names go on the finished piece along with everybody else’s. The names are scratched into the end that will be cut off when the piece is fitted. There is a wall behind the canteen built of those ends. People lean against it to eat. The writing goes every which way, and a worker who wants to show a child which firing was theirs generally has to get down on one knee.

Above the last houses there is a woman who keeps track of the grazers. This is not a city job. Her mother started doing it after finding a place where the herd had come through in her own mother’s time. The old woman had described it as a belt of violet wide enough to lie down in. By the time her daughter saw it there was scarcely enough left to stain the heel of a hand.

She keeps her records on paper, one sheet to a slope. A bite is a crescent, and the year goes inside it if there is room. There are places where the marks of three generations crowd together at a seam that a person could step across. The woman is careful not to mark every pale place as a bite. Sometimes the stone simply parts at the seam and takes the crust with it. The grazers are particular about that, too. She has watched one travel the length of a break, setting each of its many feet down with care, only to go back the way it came without eating.

A young grazer is narrow enough to shelter under an adult. When the herd comes round in a cold wind, the adults stand still at intervals and the young work their way in among the joints. From below it looks as though a cart has stopped and someone is underneath seeing to a wheel. The woman has shown this to several people. Most of them look too soon, see nothing moving, and give her back the glass.

On the ledge, the shell animals from the canteen sometimes follow a worker home. They do not attach themselves to people so much as to journeys. For nine years one went in at the same door behind a man who worked on the return cables. When he moved uphill it continued to go in at that door. The new tenant, who worked nights, was forever having to let it out again. In the end she took to leaving the door ajar at the hour the cable man used to come home. The animal would cross the kitchen, pass under the table, and turn around at the cold hearth. It never took more than a minute. She found she could leave the door open that long even in winter.

Two people live at the lowest signal, though only one of them is paid to be there. The other is his sister, who was going to stay until a room came free in town and has now been waiting seventeen years. She makes the walk up twice a week. Coming back, she carries her shopping in a long basket on a line, letting it follow her a little above the steps. She minds it particularly on the last bend. If the basket touches down there, it gains its weight all at once and is apt to pull her off her feet. Her brother says this is a good reason to buy less at a time. She says it is a good reason for him to do the shopping.

They can see the canteen from their doorstep when the light is right, but they cannot hear the bell. His sister knows the change of shift by a glint that moves along the railing as the lunch tins are set out. He has never managed to see it. He knows it instead by the first few shells coming over the edge, which she, even with his finger laid against the glass to show her where, has never made out.

At night, when he shows the amber light, there is always a little while before the answer. He uses it to do something that takes just that long: rinse a cup, put away his spectacles, go to the door and see whether his sister has made the last bend. If she has, he waits there. She likes to lower the basket onto the step herself. He hears it touch down, then hears it settle as the weight comes into it. By then the answer is generally shining up the line. From the city there are only the two lights. There is no way to tell what anybody has brought home.
