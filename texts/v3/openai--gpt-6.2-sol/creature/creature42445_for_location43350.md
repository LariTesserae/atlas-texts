The clear ones have a blemish behind the mouth. When one is feeding you can see grains of the white crust pass through it, one at a time, and come to rest in the blemish. The rest of the body is clear enough to show the grain of the stone beneath. A well-fed animal looks as if it has been laid down over something someone forgot to sweep up.

They have no legs. Along either edge of the belly there is a narrow hem that can fasten to stone or to another animal’s back. You can see it working where the traffic crosses an open crack. The front of an animal reaches out over the gap and hangs there until another passes underneath. It takes hold of the passerby, lets go of the near edge, and rides the width of the crack. Sometimes the one it has caught is going the wrong way. Now and then a clear animal makes three or four such crossings and ends up farther from the seam than it was to begin with.

The dark ones have that same hem, and the same blemish, though to see it you have to catch one turned over. They were clear once. The change begins at the mouth and is slow enough that there are animals halfway up the wall with black heads and perfectly clear hindparts. These still come down to the seam. They nose at the crust and make the little feeding motions, but the grains pile up where the mouth used to open. For a while they go hungry. Then one of their old neighbors crowds across them, and they find out what the new mouth is for.

A dark animal eats very little at a time. When it settles over a clear one, it takes the white material from behind its mouth, along with whatever soft parts come away. Often the clear one goes on moving. It may reach the seam again and feed with a ragged black mark where the blemish was. There are large clear animals with two or three such marks. They are the ones the hunters wait for. Having survived a feeding, they get back to the crust by the shortest route, which is straight under the bodies on the wall.

Under the three stones at the mouth, the skins are all clear. In the deepest layer there are still a few with grains caught in them, though the grains have gone gray. The young come out of the skins backward. They are so thin at first that on bare stone they can hardly get enough of a hold to travel. For their first journey they take the underside of an adult, nestling between the two working hems, where there is room for perhaps six. Most ride in on the clear ones. A dark one will carry them too. It can wait all evening above the seam with its belly full of young on their way to their first meal.

The roof stone shifts a little when the hollow breathes out. You can hear it if the wind lets up: a dry tap quite different from the ticking of the floor. The animals keep clear of the two places where it bears on the stones below, even though those places are warm. Whoever set it there chose a stone just long enough to bridge the gap. The bottom of the white vein is worn smooth where something used to reach in.

If you stand still long enough, the animals close over the toes of your boots. They do not climb far. The leather gives under them, and they keep having to find a new hold. When you lift a foot, those nearest the edge let go and are caught by the layer beneath. One or two may stay on until you step out into the cold. Then they stiffen and drop, and for as long as there is light you can see them on the ledge. They look like little flakes struck off the mountain. After dark, when the heat goes sideways, the nearer ones begin to bend.
