The oldest sparrow in the room has a white toenail. It catches on the frayed edge of her nesting hole, so she comes out backward, a little at a time, and drops before she turns. In the morning she is usually the first to bathe. She gets one wing into the pan and then the other, but the pan is too shallow for her breast unless she lies right down. When she does, the water goes over the broken rim and wets the potsherd underneath. A mouse comes out later and drinks from the potsherd. It won’t drink from the pan.

There are mice that were born behind the baskets and mice that come down the stairs from the bakehouse. The ones from upstairs are easy to tell by their feet. They leave prints in pairs on the dark part of the middle terrace, four or five pairs before the flour wears off. They are also more willing to cross the open floor. A young one will do it at a walk with a piece of crust sticking out on either side of its mouth, stopping now and again to get a better hold. It will walk right past the barley jar without looking up. The mice born down here go to the jar even after they’ve cut everything in it. They stand on their hind feet and feel over the lip with their whiskers.

Under the jar is a place where the floor never quite dries. The little gray woodlice there have eaten a hole in the paper that once told what was in the jar. There’s only a scrap of it left, held down by the weight. When the room is cold they pack into the space under that scrap so tightly that if you lift an edge you see what looks like a single ridged back. In the warm they come out and go about separately. Some get as far as the stairs. They turn at the first tread, where the swept brick offers them nothing.

The snake spends less time behind the baskets than you might think. A great deal of the afternoon it is in the wall, in a gap just wide enough to admit the flat of a hand, though there is no getting a hand around the bend. The baskets are where it comes out to warm itself. It puts its head under the bottom of one and leaves the middle of its body exposed on the brick. Once, when a basket was taken upstairs, it stayed like that with its head uncovered for so long that a sparrow landed beside it. Neither moved. Then the sparrow cocked its head for a better look and the snake drew back, slowly enough that the sparrow went on looking at the place where the head had been.

The crickets don’t all call from the same depth. On a cold morning you can hear one very plainly under the stair, and when the oven heats there’s another almost directly overhead. The one under the stair has a short, uneven call. It lost a hind leg coming out of a joint in the brick, though that isn’t what makes the call uneven. It did that before. There’s a spider in the joint now. At the end of summer the spider takes down almost all of her web each morning, leaving two threads across the opening. Chaff collects on them until you would think she had abandoned the place. At night there she is in the middle again, with the chaff off to one side in a bundle.

Rain brings down things that don’t live in the room. A green caterpillar once came out of the bottom pot still holding its leaf. The leaf stuck against a pan, and the caterpillar climbed up onto the dry part of it and began to eat. By the time the water went down it had eaten the part it was standing on. There are other things that seem to have come with the rain but have only been waiting for it. Tiny pale creatures rise out of the mortar at the edge of the flow and jump so quickly that the water seems to be giving off sparks. When the floor dries you can put your face as close as you like to the same place and see nothing but the little pits left by grains of sand.

The person who sweeps the stairs is the baker’s mother. She lived over the bakehouse when the kneading trough still had four sound legs. She comes down to the third tread, sweeps what she can reach, and takes the dust back up in a shallow tin. At the third tread she always waits a little. She can see the barley jar from there but not the pans, and sometimes, hearing water, she calls down to see if somebody has left a tap running. There has never been a tap in the room. There was one in the yard of the house where she grew up, and it made just that sound when a bird got into the dish beneath it.

After she goes, the sparrows wait for the door at the top to settle. It gives a small second knock a little after the first. Then the old hen drops from her hole. In the evening she doesn’t try to bathe. She walks down the damp line, stopping at each hollow, and at the last one she has to turn sideways and put one foot on a ridge of mortar to reach the water. The others can get at it from the low side. She could too, but the high side is the one she comes to first, and there is still enough light for her to see what she is doing.
