# Preparing for the Nursery Fold

## Understanding What You're Entering

This is not a location you can plan to visit—it finds you, or you stumble upon it through the particular grace of wrong turns. Your preparation must therefore be general, carried with you through maintenance corridors and service passages, ready for the moment a wall yields that shouldn't.

But once you know it exists, once you've heard others speak of warmth where there should be cold, of seams that flex—you can prepare for the possibility.

## Physical Preparation

**Thermal regulation**: The space runs at fever temperature. Your body will struggle to shed heat in air already thick with moisture. Wear clothing that breathes, that wicks—but bring nothing you can't afford to leave behind damp with condensation that may not be entirely water. Some travelers report that synthetic fabrics develop an odd texture after prolonged exposure, as if something in the humidity begins to colonize the weave. Natural fibers seem safer, though "safe" is a relative term here.

**Hydration**: You will sweat. The blood-warm air will pull moisture from you even as it saturates your skin. Carry water, but drink before you enter—you don't want to be fumbling with containers in a space where the geometry refuses to stay consistent. Dehydration compounds disorientation, and you'll need your mind clear.

**Minimize your chemical signature**: The glass seahorses responded to *something* about your presence. We don't know what attracted them—heat, carbon dioxide, trace minerals in your sweat, pheromones beneath your conscious perception. Avoid strong soaps, perfumes, anything that might register as signal in an environment designed to detect subtle gradients. You don't know what else might be listening to your chemistry.

**Physical compactness**: The space is intimate. You will be pressing against surfaces that pulse, that yield, that may or may not be walls in any meaningful sense. Practice moving in tight spaces without panic. Know the difference between constriction and embrace. Your shoulders will brush the not-quite-flesh. Your back may press against something that trembles with metabolism. This is not an attack. This is simply where you are.

## Sensory and Psychological Preparation

**Train your eyes for darkness**: Spend time in true dark before you visit. Your bathroom, closet, a room with all light sources blocked. Sit until your eyes ache with the effort of finding anything. The bioluminescent threads provide almost nothing—your rods will do all the work here, and they need practice. Learn to look *around* things rather than at them. Your peripheral vision will serve you better than direct focus.

**Practice stillness**: The subsonic thrum only emerges when you stop. When you hold your breath. When you let the silence become a texture rather than an absence. Most travelers miss it entirely, moving too much, breathing too loud. What you hear in that stillness may be important. What it tells you about the scale of what's growing here—you need to know. Practice meditation, breath control, the particular discipline of becoming quiet enough to listen to what's almost too low to perceive.

**Accept geometric impossibility**: The folds hurt to follow. Your brain will insist that a space this small cannot contain this much. It will try to rationalize, to fit what you're seeing into models that preserve the rules you trust. Let it fail. Don't force understanding. Move through the space by feel and intuition rather than by attempting to map it. Travelers who try to count the folds, to diagram the alcoves, report headaches that persist for days, dreams that feel like being turned inside out. Accept that the geometry is self-consistent even if your perception cannot hold it.

**Prepare for intimacy without contact**: This space is close. It breathes against you. The pupae dream in proximity that feels almost improper, almost tender. You will feel observed even though nothing has eyes that you can see. You will feel held even though nothing touches you except the air itself. This is not threat. This is the character of a nursery—closeness, warmth, the particular density of a space designed for becoming. Let it be what it is.

## Equipment

**Nothing electronic**: We don't know how the bioluminescent regulation systems respond to outside electromagnetic fields. We don't know what frequencies the organisms use to communicate, if they communicate. Several travelers report devices that simply stopped working after exposure—not damaged, just... paused, as if something had asked them politely to be quiet. Leave your tools outside.

**A cloth mask, unworn but available**: The air is breathable but thick. The taste of metal and sweetness may become overwhelming, particularly if you stay long. A mask can filter the heaviest particulates, give you a layer between your lungs and whatever spores or chemical signals saturate that space. Don't wear it immediately—you want to know what you're breathing, at least at first.

**Nothing sharp**: This seems obvious but bears stating. The walls yield. The membranes bead with moisture. One careless gesture with a blade or even a rough edge and you've opened something that took years to grow, released pressure from a system in balance you don't understand. The nursery has never been documented as hostile. Don't give it reason.

**A small container, sealable**: If the glass seahorses approach you, if condensation beads on your skin from walls that pulse, you may carry something out without intending to. A sample container lets you make that choice consciously rather than discovering later that something has colonized the weave of your jacket. Bring it empty. Use it if you must.

## Behavioral Guidelines

**Move slowly and announce yourself**: Not verbally—we have no evidence anything here processes human language. But telegraph your movements. Pause before entering a new fold. Let the organisms adjust to your heat signature, your chemical presence. You are large and strange in a space designed for small things becoming. Give them time to understand you pose no threat.

**Do not touch the pupae**: This is the only hard rule that approaches universal consensus. The casings sweat. The shadows shift. Whatever is becoming inside those translucent prisons is not yours to disturb. Travelers who have touched them report nothing hostile—no defenses, no alarms. Just a sense of having interrupted something private. A sense of shame that persisted for weeks. The pupae are dreaming. Let them dream.

**The seahorses may approach you**: Let them. They bump, they make their crystal sounds, they drift away. They seem to be tasting you, mapping you, adding you to whatever model they maintain of their environment. The cold of them against your skin is startling in that fever-warmth, but it passes. If several approach at once, stay still. Let them investigate. They've never harmed anyone. They're simply curious, or following gradients, or doing something we have no name for that requires brief contact with warm foreign bodies.

**Breathe through your mouth if the smell overwhelms you**: The sweetness underneath the metal is organic. It's the smell of growth, of metabolism, of things ripening toward forms we can't predict. Some travelers find it pleasant, almost narcotic. Others need to leave after minutes. Know yourself well enough to recognize which you'll be, and don't push past your limit. The nursery will still be there when you've recovered.

## Ethical Considerations

You didn't make this place. You don't know who did, or why, or what purpose these organisms serve. The engineering predates your understanding, operates at scales your perception slides off.

But you're here now. You're breathing air that feeds the pupae. You're radiating heat that the systems must compensate for. You're introducing bacteria, skin cells, chemical signals into an environment balanced more precisely than you can measure.

Ask yourself why you're here. Curiosity is valid. Wonder is valid. Even scientific interest, though you'll take nothing from this place that means anything without the context you can't provide. But exploitation—attempting to harvest, to document for those who would harvest, to disrupt whatever patient process unfolds here—that's another matter.

The nursery has survived by being hidden. By folding into gaps that official schematics don't acknowledge. Your presence may already be more intrusion than it can absorb.

Visit briefly. Witness what you can. Leave without attempting to carry more than memory.

## What You Cannot Prepare For

The sense that something here is waiting. Not for you—you're incidental, a warm body passing through, briefly novel and then forgotten. But waiting nonetheless. Growing toward a state it hasn't reached yet, a threshold that no one alive has seen crossed.

You'll leave the nursery fold and return to corridors that make sense, to light that comes from sources you can name, to air that tastes of nothing but recycled station atmosphere. And you'll carry with you the knowledge that somewhere in the walls, in the gaps between what's officially real, thousands of shadows are shifting in their casings. Dreaming of what they'll be.

That knowledge doesn't fade. Prepare for it to stay.
