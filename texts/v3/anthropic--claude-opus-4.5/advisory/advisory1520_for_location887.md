# Preparing for the Terraced City of Unmoving Mist

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, you need to sit with the nature of this place. This is not a location where things happen to you—it is a location where things *stop* happening, where the very concept of happening grows soft and uncertain. The danger here is not violence or hostility. The danger is that you might find the stillness comfortable. That you might set down your pack one afternoon and simply never pick it up again, because there is no afternoon, not really, and your pack will wait for you with infinite patience, and so will the terrace, and so will the mist, and so will you.

Read the description again. Notice how it lulls. Notice the rhythm of the prose itself, the way it circles back, the way it soothes. This is not an accident. This is the place teaching you what it does. It will do this to your thoughts. It will do this to your intentions.

Write down, before you go, exactly why you are visiting and exactly when you plan to leave. Write it in language that is sharp and specific. Not "a few days" but "seventy-two hours from the moment I set foot on the first terrace." Not "to see what it's like" but "to observe the water-circulation systems and speak with three inhabitants about their experience of time." Carry this written intention against your skin. You will need to touch it, to read it, to remember that you once had edges.

## Physical Preparations

### Clothing

The cold here is not deadly, but it is permanent. Wool is your friend—not modern synthetic wicking fabrics designed to move moisture away from your body, because moisture here does not move. It simply is. Wool insulates even when wet, and you will always be wet. Multiple layers of wool: base layer against skin, middle layer for warmth, outer layer to break what passes for wind but is really just the slow movement of mist around your body.

Your outer layer should be densely woven but not waterproof in the sealed, synthetic sense. Rubber and plastic will trap moisture against you rather than letting it exist in equilibrium with the air. You want fabric that accepts the damp, that becomes part of the damp, that does not fight a battle it cannot win. Waxed cotton or tightly woven wool felt.

Pay attention to your feet. The stone is wet and smooth and cold. Boots with good grip, yes, but also boots that you can remove easily—the inhabitants likely go barefoot or in soft-soled shoes indoors, and you do not want to track the peculiar cleanliness of the terraces into their homes. Bring a second pair of thick wool socks. Rotate them, though "drying" is perhaps optimistic. Accept that your feet will be damp. Watch for the softening of skin that comes with prolonged moisture exposure.

Your head and hands will lose heat. Bring a wool cap that covers your ears. Bring fingerless gloves with a mitten flap, so you can manipulate objects while keeping your hands warm. The gloves will become sodden. This is fine. This is how things are here.

### Food and Water

The water here is safe—more than safe, it seems to be the very essence of this place, mineral-rich and clear and patient. Drink it. But bring your own food, and bring food that does not require fire to prepare. The text tells you fire is difficult here, and you should believe it. Assume you will not be able to light a flame at all. Assume even chemical heat sources will behave strangely, reluctantly.

Bring dense nutrition: hard cheeses wrapped in wax, dried meats, nuts, dried fruits that will not mind the damp because they have already surrendered their moisture. Bring flatbreads or hardtack. Bring foods with strong flavors—salt, spice, acid—because the muted quality of this place will extend to your senses, and you need flavors that insist on being tasted. A small bottle of vinegar. A twist of paper with coarse salt. Dried chilies if you can tolerate them.

Do not rely on foraging from the planting beds. The broad-leafed vegetables that taste of mineral water may sustain the inhabitants, but their bodies have adapted to this place in ways yours has not. The fruit from the silver-barked trees—this concerns me most. "Always exactly ripe, never falling, waiting to be picked but patient about it." This is not natural fruit. This is fruit that has made a bargain with stillness. Eating it may bind you to that bargain in ways you do not intend. If you must taste it—and I understand the curiosity, I do—taste only a single bite, and then wait. Wait to see what it does to your sense of time, to your desire to leave, to your edges.

## Tools and Equipment

### What to Bring

A mechanical watch. Not digital, not quartz—a wind-up mechanical watch that requires your daily attention to keep running. The act of winding it will anchor you to the passage of time when nothing else here marks that passage. Set alarms if your watch has them. When the alarm sounds, stop whatever you are doing and check your written intention. Read it aloud. Remember that you are a visitor.

A journal and several pencils. Pens may not work well in the damp. Write in your journal daily—no, more often than daily. Write every time you notice something. Write every time you fail to notice something. Write when you catch yourself thinking that you have always been here, because you will catch yourself thinking this, and you need to write it down so you can see the thought from outside.

A small mirror. Look at your face. You will not age here, but you need to remember what your face looks like when it is yours, when it is the face of someone who changes and grows and has a future that differs from their past. The inhabitants have been here so long they may have forgotten what that looks like. You must not forget.

A length of bright cord—red or orange, something that violates the gray-blue-green palette of this place. Tie it around your wrist. Let it offend the aesthetic. Let it remind you that you come from a world of colors that shout.

No maps. Maps will not help you here—the geometry does not cooperate with cartography. Instead, bring a system of markers you can place: small colored flags, chalk that will show against gray stone (though the damp may defeat it), anything that lets you mark "I have been here" in a way you can recognize. But understand that the markers may not behave as you expect. A marker placed at a junction may appear at a different junction. A chalk mark may seem to have always been there. Navigate by attention, by presence, by the sheer force of your intention to remain a visitor rather than becoming a resident.

### What Not to Bring

Do not bring clocks that run on their own. Do not bring technology that tells time without your participation. If these devices work at all, they may tell you things you don't want to know about how time passes here, which is to say: they may tell you that time does not pass here at all, and this knowledge might break something in you.

Do not bring seeds. If you plant something from outside in this soil, it will either fail utterly or succeed in ways that disturb you. Neither outcome is desirable.

Do not bring anything you cannot bear to lose to the softness, because you may set something down and forget you ever owned it, and it will wait for you with the same infinite patience as everything else here, but you will not remember to look for it.

## Navigating the Social Landscape

The inhabitants are not hostile. Nothing here is hostile. But they have been here so long that they may not fully comprehend what it means to be a visitor, to be someone who intends to leave. When they speak to you—softly, always softly, because the mist absorbs louder speech—listen carefully for assumptions embedded in their words. They may speak of "when you next harvest the broad-leaves" or "the terrace where you will live." Correct these assumptions gently but firmly. Repeat that you are visiting. Repeat that you will leave.

They will not try to keep you. They will not need to try. The place itself keeps people, and the inhabitants are simply part of the place. They may find your urgency puzzling, your insistence on departure slightly sad, the way one might find sad a child who refuses to accept a gift. Accept their hospitality but maintain your boundaries. Sleep in their homes if invited, but do not sleep more than one night in the same bed, because beds remember bodies here, and you do not want a bed to start remembering yours.

Ask them about the mist. Ask them about the geometry. Watch their faces when you ask—not for deception, because they are not capable of deception, but for the particular blankness that comes when a question does not register as a question. This blankness will tell you what they cannot see anymore. It will tell you what you must continue to see if you want to leave.

Attend their marketplaces, their distributions, their circulations of goods. But do not accept anything you cannot consume or return before you leave. Do not let objects from this place attach themselves to you. Every object here is part of the stillness, and taking part of the stillness with you when you leave is—I am not sure what it is, exactly. But I suspect it would make leaving harder. I suspect it would make you wonder, in quiet moments years later, whether you really left at all, or whether a part of you is still there, waiting with infinite patience for the rest of you to return.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

This is the hardest part to prepare for, because the challenge here is not fear or disgust or discomfort. The challenge is peace. The challenge is the seductive promise of a life without anxiety, without uncertainty, without the exhausting necessity of change. You will feel, after a day or two in the mist, a loosening of the tension you carry in your shoulders. You will feel your breath slow. You will feel, perhaps for the first time in years, that everything is going to be okay.

This feeling is not a lie, exactly. Everything here is okay. Everything here is always okay, has always been okay, will always be okay. But "okay" is not the same as "alive," and you need to hold onto that distinction with everything you have.

Before you go, make a list of things you are looking forward to. Specific things: a meal you want to cook, a person you want to see, a book you want to read, a morning you want to watch the sun rise. Make the list long. Make it vivid. Make it ache with anticipation. Carry this list with your statement of intention, and read it when the stillness starts to feel like enough. It will feel like enough. You must remember that enough is not the same as alive.

Consider also: what do you fear? Not the fears that exhaust you, but the fears that animate you—the fear of not finishing something, the fear of missing an opportunity, the fear of wasting your brief time. These fears are painful, yes. But they are also evidence that you believe your time is brief, that you believe in finishing and missing and wasting. Hold onto these fears. They are the gift of impermanence, and impermanence is what makes things matter.

## Contingencies and Exit Strategies

Plan your exit before you need it. Walk the path from the highest terrace you intend to visit down to the place where the terraces end and the ordinary mountain begins. Walk it more than once. Walk it until your feet know it, until you could walk it with your eyes closed, until the walking becomes a kind of prayer: *I am leaving, I am leaving, I am leaving.*

Mark the transition point where the geometry starts to make sense again, where shadows return, where the mist thins. Sit at this point and look back at the city. Let yourself feel the pull of it. Then turn around and walk away, even if only for a few steps, even if you then return to continue your visit. Practice leaving. Practice the turning around. Your body needs to know that it can be done.

If you find that you cannot leave—if you descend the terraces and find yourself back where you started, if the exit point seems to recede as you approach it, if you realize that you have been walking for what feels like hours and the mist is no thinner than before—stop. Sit down. Take out your written intention and read it aloud. Say your name. Say where you came from. Say something that was different yesterday than it is today, even if you have to make it up, even if you have to remember a difference that you're no longer sure was real. The act of asserting change is itself a kind of change, and change is the only path out.

In extremity, you may need to damage something. Not someone—never someone—but something. Tear a page from your journal and let it blow away. Drop a possession and do not pick it up. Commit an act of loss, of waste, of irreversibility. These acts are violence against the stillness, and the stillness may recoil from them just enough to let you through.

## A Final Word

I have described preparations as if this place is dangerous, and I need to be precise about what I mean. It is not dangerous in the way a cliff is dangerous or a predator is dangerous. You will not be harmed here. You will be *preserved*. You will be held in the soft gray light with the moisture beading on your skin and the mist absorbing your sharper thoughts and the stone steps worn smooth by feet that walk in patterns that never change.

You will be safe. You will be okay. You will be, in some sense, forever.

This is the danger. This is what you are preparing to resist: the kindest trap in any universe, the one that does not want to hurt you, only to keep you, only to hold you still until stillness is all you remember, until the very idea of leaving seems as strange and sad as a fruit choosing to fall from a tree that will hold it forever.

Go, if you must. See the silver-barked trees and the pale ungulates and the water that flows in directions that change depending on where you're standing. Listen to the bell-sounds of the beetles and the water-drop calls of the birds. Let the moss cushion your steps and the mist bead on your eyelashes and the soft light show you a world where nothing is ever wrong.

Then leave. Leave while you still remember what leaving means. Leave while you can still feel the ache of everything you haven't done yet, everyone you haven't loved yet, every morning you haven't watched break into color and change.

Leave, and carry the memory of stillness with you like a stone in your pocket—something you can touch in moments of chaos, something that reminds you that peace exists somewhere, that there is a place where everything is held.

But do not stay. Do not let the mist close around you. Do not let your edges go soft.

You have places to be. You have changes to make. You have a life that is brief and uncertain and therefore precious in a way that endless patience can never be.

The mist will wait for you. It will always wait.

You do not have to wait with it.
