# Preparation Guide for Visiting the Nursery of Wrong Angles

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a place. You are visiting a *process*. The Nursery exists in a state of continuous becoming, and everything within it—the stone, the light, the creatures, possibly even the concepts of space and time—has not yet committed to being what it will be. Your preparation must account for the fact that you are, by comparison, catastrophically *finished*. You are a solved equation entering a room full of variables still being defined.

This is not inherently dangerous, but it creates friction. Your very coherence—the way your cells know what they are, the way your bones have decided to be bones—will press against the unresolved potential of this place. The creatures will sense it. The space itself may resist you. Your goal is to minimize this friction, to make yourself as gentle a presence as possible, while still maintaining enough of your own definition to return home intact.

## Physical Preparation

### Body Readiness

**Fasting and lightness**: Consider eating simply for two or three days before entry. Not because the Nursery requires it, but because a body busy with digestion is a body loudly performing its own completeness. You want to be as metabolically quiet as possible. Water, simple grains, soft foods. Nothing that demands your body make strong declarations about what it is doing.

**Skin care**: Your skin is your boundary, the membrane that declares where you end and the world begins. This declaration will feel almost aggressive in the Nursery. Some travelers report success with applying thin layers of unfixed clay or chalk to exposed skin—substances that are themselves in transition between powder and stone. This may soften your edges, perceptually speaking. At minimum, avoid petroleum-based lotions or anything that creates a hard seal. You want your boundary to breathe, to suggest rather than insist.

**Hydration, but strangely**: The water in the Nursery moves wrong. It travels upward, gathers in defiance of gravity, forms pockets at odd heights. Your internal water may feel confused. Hydrate well before entry, but stop drinking at least an hour before you descend. You don't want active fluid movement within you while the Nursery's water is demonstrating alternative possibilities. The dissonance can cause nausea, or worse, can make your own fluids uncertain about which direction they should flow.

**Flexibility over strength**: Stretch thoroughly. Not for physical obstacles—the space is small, the entry tight but manageable—but because rigid muscles are muscles that have *decided*. Tension is a form of commitment. The more relaxed and mobile your body, the less friction you'll create. Focus especially on your joints, those places where your body is already somewhat ambiguous about its own architecture.

### Clothing and Gear

**Natural, unfinished fibers**: Wear linen or cotton that hasn't been heavily processed. Avoid synthetic materials entirely—they are too definitionally complete, too much the product of processes that have *finished*. If you can find hand-spun fabric with visible irregularities, even better. The imperfections read as openness, as potential not yet foreclosed.

**No buttons, minimal fasteners**: These are small declarations of closure. Use ties, wraps, draped fabric. Your clothing should suggest it might become something else if it chose to.

**A soft hat or hood**: Not for warmth, but for the light. The sourceless illumination of the Nursery has no direction, casts no shadows. This is disorienting in ways that accumulate. A hood or hat creates a small pocket of relative darkness around your face, gives your eyes somewhere to rest, reminds you what shadow feels like. You will need this anchor.

**Bare feet, if possible**: Your feet are your contact with the unfinished stone, and shoes are very *concluded* objects—they know exactly what they are. If going barefoot through the entry crack is impossible, wear soft leather moccasins or cloth wraps. Nothing with hard soles. You want to feel the clay-softness of the walls that haven't decided what they are. You want the stone to feel that you are open to its indecision.

**No measurement tools**: This is critical. Do not bring rulers, watches, compasses, thermometers, or any device designed to pin down quantities. The surveyor's equipment already there—the two-legged tripod, the notebook of unwritten words—these are warnings. Measurement requires that the measured thing *be* something specific, and the Nursery resists this. Bringing tools of quantification will increase the friction you create, possibly dramatically. You are not there to determine what the Nursery is. You are there to witness what it is becoming.

**No mirrors**: You cannot afford to see your own reflection in this place. A mirror would confront the Nursery with your completeness in the most aggressive way possible—a flat declaration of exactly what you look like, captured and displayed. The creatures would flee. The breathing thing in the corner might react badly. Leave all reflective surfaces behind, including polished metal and the screen of your phone.

**One unfinished object**: Bring something that is itself in transition. A carving you've started but not completed. A letter you haven't finished writing. A seed that hasn't sprouted. Yarn not yet knitted. This object will serve as a kind of ambassador, signaling to the Nursery that you understand incompleteness, that you carry potential yourself. Hold it during your visit. It may help.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

### Releasing the Need to Understand

This is the hardest part of preparation, and you must begin weeks before your visit.

The Nursery is not a puzzle to solve. The surveyor who left the two-legged tripod tried to solve it, and look what remains: equipment that never fully existed, words imprinted but never written, an investigation always about to begin but never begun. The Nursery does not *want* to be understood. Understanding is a form of completion, and completion is the one thing the Nursery cannot contain.

Practice this: spend time each day observing something without trying to name it, categorize it, or explain it. A cloud. A stranger's expression. The movement of water. When your mind reaches for explanation, let the reach happen, but don't grasp. Let the question remain a question. Learn to be comfortable in the space between perception and comprehension.

You will need this skill desperately. When you see the beetles with unhardened shells, you will want to know: are they larval? Adult? Something between? The answer is yes, and no, and not yet. When you see the salamanders with gill and lung visible simultaneously, you will want to place them in a developmental sequence. But they are *all* the stages, or *none* of them, or something the concept of stages doesn't apply to. Let them be what they are becoming. Don't force them into the shapes your knowledge provides.

### Preparing for Temporal Dislocation

The artifacts in the Nursery are "ancient and brand new simultaneously." Time here has not fully committed to flowing in one direction, or perhaps it hasn't decided how fast to flow, or what sequence means. You may experience moments that feel recursive—the sense of having just done something you haven't done yet. You may lose track of how long you've been inside.

Before you visit, spend time consciously disrupting your normal time-sense. Wake at odd hours. Skip meals unpredictably. Sit in a dark room and try to guess how much time passes without checking. The goal is not to master timekeeping but to loosen your grip on it—to demonstrate to yourself that your experience of duration is more flexible than you assume. When time behaves strangely in the Nursery, you want your reaction to be curiosity rather than panic.

Importantly: do not set a timer or alarm for your visit. External timekeeping devices will either fail to function or will create the worst kind of friction, insisting on a temporal structure the Nursery hasn't agreed to. Instead, arrange with someone on the surface to check for you after a reasonable interval—several hours, perhaps—and to call or knock at the crack if you haven't emerged. Their voice or their knock will reach you, eventually, and will give you an anchor to return to.

### Emotional Settling

The Nursery is not hostile, but it may feel melancholy. You are witnessing things that don't fully exist yet—potential that hasn't resolved into actuality. There is something sad about this if you're in a certain mood, something that evokes grief for all the paths not taken, all the versions of yourself that never coalesced, all the creatures that almost were.

If you have unresolved grief—for a person, a relationship, a version of your life that didn't happen—process what you can before visiting. Not because the Nursery will exploit it, but because the resonance may become overwhelming. The breathing thing in the corner, the one that might be a mammal or the idea of one, the one whose watching has weight—some travelers report that it reflects unfinished emotional business back at them, amplified. You don't want to collapse into weeping amid the relocating mosses. The salamanders would find it distressing.

Conversely, some travelers report that the Nursery is profoundly healing for those who have felt trapped by their own completeness—by careers that feel fixed, identities that feel foreclosed, futures that seem already written. To see that even stone can remain undecided, that light can still be learning how to behave, that creatures can exist in threshold states indefinitely—this can be liberating. If you are visiting in part to remember that you, too, still contain unresolved potential, lean into this. The Nursery may recognize you as kin.

## The Descent

### Finding the Entry

Follow the condensation. This sounds simple, but it requires patience and a willingness to look foolish. You will be watching water droplets move wrong, rolling upward on the undersides of rocks, and you will have to follow them to their source. This may take time. The droplets are not trying to guide you; they are simply doing what they do, which happens to mark the path.

Watch for the crack—barely wide enough for a child's fist, the description says, but it will widen for you if you approach with the right attitude. Do not force it. Do not bring tools to pry it open. Approach with curiosity and openness, and let the crack decide how to receive you. If it doesn't widen, you are not ready. Return another day.

### The Entry Itself

The squeeze will be tight. You will have a moment, perhaps several moments, of genuine claustrophobia—stone pressing close, the smell of damp rock, the sense of being swallowed. Breathe slowly. Remember that the crack opens into more than it should contain. The tightness is a threshold, a test, a narrowing that leads to expansion.

If you panic, you will likely be expelled back to the surface. This is not failure, exactly, but it means the Nursery has rejected your presence. Wait at least a season before trying again, and use the time to practice the mental preparation techniques above.

If you pass through, you will emerge into the sourceless light, and your education will begin.

## Behavior Inside the Nursery

### Movement

Move slowly. Not because quick movement is dangerous, but because slowness is a form of respect in a place where things are taking their time deciding what to be. Your hurrying would be a kind of rudeness—an insistence on a tempo the Nursery hasn't adopted.

Do not approach the walls directly with intent to touch. If your wandering brings you into contact with them, that's fine—let your hand or shoulder brush the clay-soft stone, feel it dimple under pressure that isn't quite yours. But deliberate poking or prodding, especially of the emerging crystals with their incomplete angles, will mark you as an investigator, and the Nursery has already shown what happens to investigation.

Do not attempt to move or examine the surveyor's equipment. It exists in a state you cannot understand—not quite present, not quite historical, trapped in the never-started investigation. Interacting with it may trap you in similar recursion. Let it alone.

### The Creatures

The translucent beetles will flow around you. Let them. Their clicking is communication, but not with you—they are talking to each other about matters you cannot comprehend, matters related to the hardening or not-hardening of their shells, the paths across the clay-stone, the business of being almost-insects. Do not pick them up. Their shells are unhardened, and your touch would be a kind of violence—forcing them to commit to a shape, possibly the wrong shape.

The salamanders are more robust but more wary. They will avoid you as your presence becomes too much for them to bear. This is not rejection; it is self-preservation. They cannot afford to be influenced by something as *finished* as you are while they're still deciding whether to have gills or lungs. Let them keep their distance. If one approaches you—and this occasionally happens, the braver or more curious ones—remain still. Let it investigate you with its developing senses. It is trying to understand what completion looks like. Be a gentle teacher.

The moths are heartbreaking if you let yourself think about them too long. They began mid-flight, from no cocoon, from folded potential into struggling life. Their wings are crumpled because they never underwent the proper unfolding—they simply *started*, and now they must learn to fly with equipment they never properly earned. Do not try to help them. Do not try to smooth their wings. They are working something out, and your intervention would foreclose possibilities they're still exploring.

### The Breathing Thing

Do not look directly at the corner where the angles refuse to resolve.

This is important. The breathing thing is watching you with eyes still learning to see, and its watching has weight, has pressure, has *becoming*. If you return its gaze too directly, you will become part of what it is becoming. You will influence its resolution. You are too complete for this responsibility. You would accidentally force it into a shape that accommodates your understanding, and that shape might not be what it would have chosen.

Peripheral awareness is fine. You can know it's there. You can sense its breathing, which is not quite regular, because it hasn't decided on a respiratory pattern yet. You can feel its attention like a pressure on your skin. But keep your eyes on other things—the mosses, the salamanders, the play of sourceless light. Let the breathing thing complete its own observation of you without you observing back.

If it closes its eyes, as the description suggests it might, this is a signal. Your presence has become too much. Leave soon.

### The Sound of Almost-Life

Listen carefully. The quiet is the quiet of growth—cells dividing, crystals accreting, wet potential condensing into form. You are hearing creation at its most intimate, before anything has decided what kind of creation it is.

The *plink* of droplets completing their upward journey is a punctuation, a tiny announcement of arrival. The almost-calls of the salamanders are questions in a language still inventing itself. You will not understand these sounds, and you should not try to. But you can honor them by listening, by receiving them without demanding meaning.

Some travelers report that if you stay quiet long enough, you begin to hear your own body's sounds differently—the gurgle of digestion, the pulse of blood, the subtle creak of joints—as if the Nursery is revealing to you that you, too, are a process, not a fixed thing. This can be disorienting or comforting depending on your disposition. Either way, it is a gift.

## Exiting

### Recognizing When to Leave

You will know. The salamanders will begin avoiding you more actively. The moths will spiral wider, keeping distance. The breathing thing's eyes will close. The friction between your completeness and the Nursery's potential will become uncomfortable, like wearing clothes that have grown too tight.

Do not overstay. The description says the Nursery is not dangerous—though it might be—but the uncertainty in that phrasing should guide you. You don't know what happens if a *finished* thing remains too long in a space of *becoming*. Maybe nothing. Maybe the Nursery starts to incorporate you into its processes, leaving parts of you as undecided as the two-legged tripod. Don't find out.

### The Return

The crack will let you out. This is a promise embedded in the space itself—it is a nursery, not a trap, and it understands that finished things must return to the finished world. The squeeze will feel different going out, possibly easier, possibly harder. Some travelers report that the crack seems reluctant to release them, as if the Nursery has grown fond of having a witness. Be patient. Keep moving. The limestone shelf is only arm's length above you.

When you emerge, you will be wet—from the condensation, from the damp of the walls, from fluid that seems to come from nowhere. You will be disoriented—the sun will seem too directional, shadows too harsh, everything too *decided*. Sit for a while. Do not try to walk immediately. Let your senses recalibrate to a world where water falls down and light has sources.

Some travelers report that for days or weeks after visiting, they see incompleteness everywhere—the unresolved potential in ordinary objects, the not-yet-decided quality of people's faces, the ways that crystals and clouds and conversations are still becoming what they will be. This fades, usually. Mostly. Some say it never entirely goes away.

---

## What Not to Bring Back

Do not take anything from the Nursery. Not the beetles, not the moss, not chips of the clay-stone or fragments of the unfinished crystals. These things cannot exist in the finished world. They would either die immediately, fixed into completed forms by the weight of your reality, or they would resist, remaining incomplete in ways that make ordinary space uncomfortable. You do not want a piece of unfinished crystal on your dresser, trust me. Its angles would never quite resolve in your peripheral vision, and you would never quite be able to stop looking at it, and eventually you would find yourself forgetting what the word *finished* means.

The one exception, possibly: if something from the Nursery *gives itself* to you—if a beetle crawls into your hand and refuses to leave, if a moss relocates into your pocket while you're not watching—you may have to carry it out. But this is rare, and comes with responsibilities I don't fully understand. You would be carrying potential into the completed world, and you would become responsible for what it becomes.

The unfinished object you brought in, though—carry it out again. See if it has changed. Some travelers report that their incomplete carvings have acquired new details, their unfinished letters have gained words. The Nursery leaves no one entirely unchanged.

---

## A Final Note

You will forget some of what you experience. This is normal. The Nursery exists in a state that memory has trouble retaining—how do you remember something that hadn't decided what it was while you were watching it? Your recollection will have gaps, contradictions, moments that seem to have happened in multiple incompatible ways.

Write what you can as soon as you emerge, while the experience is still wet and soft in your mind. Accept that your account will be incomplete. Accept that you, too, participated in the Nursery's process in ways you'll never fully understand—that your presence influenced what some of those creatures became, or didn't become, or are still becoming.

And if, sometimes, you find yourself following condensation without meaning to—watching droplets bead on the undersides of rocks and wondering which way they'll fall—

That's normal too.

The Nursery leaves no one entirely unchanged, and some of us carry its potential forward, even in the finished world, even as we try to forget how the breathing thing watched us with eyes still learning to see, and how, for just a moment, we almost remembered what it was like not to know what we would become.
