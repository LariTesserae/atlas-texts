# Preparing for the Screaming Peaks

## Understanding What You're Facing

Before anything else, sit with this truth: you are preparing to visit a place where the fundamental assumptions of human existence do not apply. There is no quiet. There will be no moment when the wind stops and you can collect yourself. The sound will be inside your skull from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave, and your nervous system—evolved for a world where constant loud noise signals danger—will fight you the entire time.

This is not a hostile environment in the way a volcano or a war zone is hostile. It is an indifferent one, a place that reached its final form long ago and has no interest in accommodating visitors. You cannot negotiate with the Screaming Peaks. You can only prepare thoroughly enough that your presence there becomes briefly sustainable.

---

## Physical Conditioning (Begin 3-6 Months Before)

**Altitude adaptation is non-negotiable.** The description suggests you'll be operating well above where humans function comfortably—likely equivalent to 4,500 meters or higher. Your body needs time to learn how to extract oxygen from thin air.

Spend months training at progressively higher elevations if your home world allows it. If not, consider altitude simulation tents for sleeping, or pressurized training chambers. You want your body producing extra red blood cells before you arrive, not desperately trying to manufacture them while you're already gasping on that polished granite.

**Cardiovascular endurance matters, but not in the way you might think.** You won't be running. You'll be walking slowly, breathing deliberately, conserving energy. Train for sustained low-intensity output over many hours. Long hikes with weight. Extended sessions on stairs. Your heart needs to be efficient, not explosive.

**Cold tolerance can be trained.** Begin taking cold showers. Progress to cold immersion. Not because this will make you comfortable in the peaks—nothing will make you comfortable—but because your body needs to remember that cold is survivable, that the urge to panic when warmth bleeds away is just an urge, manageable and ignorable.

---

## Hearing Protection (Critical)

This is where many travelers fail catastrophically.

Standard earplugs will not suffice. The wind described isn't merely loud—it's constant, penetrating, resonant. Your skull itself will vibrate. You need:

**Layered protection:** High-quality molded earplugs as a base layer, rated for at least 30 decibels of reduction. Over these, active noise-canceling over-ear protectors—the kind used on aircraft carrier decks or in industrial foundries. Some travelers report success with bone-conduction dampening headbands worn beneath the outer protection.

**Backup sets of everything.** The wind will try to steal your hearing protection. It will work at the seals, find gaps, exploit moments of adjustment. Bring three complete sets minimum.

**Gradual adjustment before arrival.** Spend several days wearing your full hearing protection setup for extended periods. Your brain needs to learn to function with muffled sound, to stop straining for auditory information that won't be available. Some travelers report unsettling psychological effects from prolonged sensory reduction—better to discover this at home than on the plateau.

**Communication planning.** You will not be able to speak to companions in any normal way. Prepare hand signals, written communication tools, or technological solutions (throat microphones with bone-conduction speakers can work, but test them extensively). Assume verbal communication is impossible and plan accordingly.

---

## Eye Protection

The description mentions "blinding sheets" of light glancing off polished stone. This is not poetic exaggeration. You face:

- Unfiltered high-altitude UV radiation
- Direct sunlight from above
- Reflected light from every polished surface
- No shade, no clouds, no relief

**Glacier glasses or equivalent:** Full-coverage eyewear with side shields, rated for extreme UV and visible light reduction. Dark enough that you can stare at bright snow without discomfort. Some travelers prefer goggles for the additional seal against wind-driven grit.

**Backup pairs.** The wind carries abrasive particles that will scratch lenses over time. Bring at least two complete sets of eye protection, preferably three.

**Treatment for eyes:** Even with protection, expect irritation. Bring preservative-free lubricating drops, and something stronger for inflammation. Snow blindness is a real risk if protection fails even briefly.

---

## Clothing and Thermal Regulation

The description specifies "not freezing, not quite, but a steady chill that seeps through clothing and skin and settles into bone." This is in some ways worse than true cold. You cannot generate enough heat through activity to stay warm, because the thin air won't support vigorous exertion. You cannot simply add layers until you're comfortable, because you need mobility to navigate the terrain.

**Base layer:** Merino wool or high-quality synthetic, full coverage. This layer manages moisture—and you will be producing some moisture, even in the dry air, because your body doesn't know how to not sweat.

**Insulation layer:** Down is risky because of the strange moisture patterns described—fog banks and frost crystallization could wet it unexpectedly. Synthetic insulation may be safer, though heavier. Some travelers use both, switching based on conditions.

**Wind layer:** This is your most important garment. It must be absolutely impervious to wind—not wind-resistant, wind-*proof*. Every seam sealed. Every closure secured. The hood must protect your face while allowing breathing and not interfering with hearing protection. Test this garment in the highest winds you can access before departure.

**Extremities:** The wind will find your hands, your feet, your face. Insulated gloves with a wind-proof shell and dexterity sufficient for necessary tasks. Boots rated for the conditions with gaiters to seal the interface with your pants. Face covering that works with your eye and ear protection without creating gaps.

**The critical principle:** You cannot afford to be adjusting your clothing constantly. Every opening, every moment of exposure, costs you warmth you cannot easily replace. Dress with obsessive care before exposure and then *leave everything alone* until you reach shelter.

---

## Breathing and Respiratory Protection

The thin air is one challenge. The wind-driven grit is another. Your respiratory system will be under assault from both directions.

**Consider supplemental oxygen.** Even if you've acclimatized well, having a portable oxygen supply for emergencies or for sleep could prevent serious altitude sickness. The weight is worth it.

**Face covering with filtration.** You need to breathe, but you don't want to be inhaling stone particles. A balaclava or face mask with built-in filtration—something designed for dusty environments—can help. Test how this interacts with your eye protection; fogging is a serious problem.

**Moisture for your airways.** The air will desiccate your respiratory tract. Some travelers use saline nasal spray regularly, or small humidifying inserts in their breathing apparatus. Cracked, bleeding airways are an infection risk you don't need.

---

## Shelter

You cannot remain exposed indefinitely. You need somewhere the wind does not reach.

**Research the terrain.** Those "deeper hollows between peaks, where the wind's fury is deflected just enough" are your targets. The pools of water indicate these sheltered spots exist. Before arrival, gather whatever intelligence you can about their locations.

**Portable shelter as backup.** A standard tent will be destroyed within minutes. What you need is either a bivy system designed for extreme conditions—something you can anchor to stone and that presents almost no profile to the wind—or the materials and skills to construct a temporary shelter from available rocks. Study dry-stone construction techniques. Practice building windbreaks.

**Timing your exposure.** Plan to spend only hours at a time in the full force of the wind, with regular retreats to sheltered locations. This is not a place for a single heroic push; it's a place for patient cycling between exposure and recovery.

---

## Water and Food

**Water acquisition:** Those trembling pools are your source. Bring purification equipment—filtration and chemical treatment both. The water is described as "startlingly clear," but clarity isn't purity. You don't know what microorganisms have adapted to these conditions.

**Water storage:** Keep your water supply close to your body, inside your wind layer, or it will chill to near-freezing and stay there. Drinking very cold water in these conditions accelerates heat loss.

**Food:** High-calorie, high-fat foods that don't require preparation. You cannot cook in that wind without extraordinary measures. Eat frequently, small amounts, to maintain energy without requiring digestion that pulls blood away from your extremities.

**Carry more than you think you need.** Extraction from this location may be complicated; emergency supplies could become primary supplies.

---

## Mental Preparation

This may matter more than any physical equipment.

**The sound will not stop.** You need to make peace with this before you go. Practice meditation with loud, constant noise—white noise generators, recordings of wind, industrial sounds. Train yourself to find an internal center that doesn't depend on external quiet.

**Time will feel different.** The unchanging conditions—constant wind, constant light, constant cold—will disrupt your sense of duration. Bring a reliable timepiece and check it regularly. Set alarms for necessary actions (hydration, food, shelter breaks). Don't trust your instincts about how long you've been exposed.

**The isolation is profound.** Even if you travel with companions, communication will be impaired and each of you will be locked inside your own cocoon of protection. Prepare psychologically for being essentially alone with your thoughts, in a place that cares nothing for you, while your body struggles to maintain itself.

**Have an abort plan.** Know the signs that indicate you need to leave—hypothermia onset, altitude sickness progression, psychological deterioration. Commit to these tripwires before you arrive, when your judgment is clear. In the peaks, with your thinking fragmented by noise and thin air, you may not recognize that you're in crisis.

---

## Wildlife Observation

If part of your purpose is to witness the remarkable creatures that have colonized this place:

**Binoculars or a spotting scope** are essential. You cannot approach closely without disturbing natural behaviors, and the animals have evolved hypervigilance for good reason. Something hunts them, remember.

**Move slowly, predictably, and along obvious paths.** You are a strange shape in their environment. The less threatening you appear, the less you disrupt.

**Do not attempt to interact with the predator.** Whatever hunts the herds has survived in this environment for countless generations. It is adapted in ways you are not. If you see it, observe from distance. Do not follow.

**Respect the equilibrium.** This ecosystem is described as having reached a "final equilibrium." Your role is witness, not participant. Take nothing. Leave nothing. Disturb nothing that can be avoided.

---

## Final Counsel

Some travelers return from the Screaming Peaks exhilarated, speaking of the vivid sky and the glorious emptiness and the profound stillness they found *inside* the noise. Others return shaken, worn to a nub like the ancient stone, grateful beyond words for silence and warmth and the green chaos of living valleys.

Both responses are honest. Both are correct.

What you find there will depend on who you are, and on factors too subtle to predict. But if you prepare thoroughly, if you respect the place's absolute indifference to your presence, if you move through it with humility and care—you will return. You will return with something, some understanding or memory, that exists only in that place of endless wind and ancient stone.

And then, for the rest of your life, you will know what real silence sounds like. Because you will have lived without it.
