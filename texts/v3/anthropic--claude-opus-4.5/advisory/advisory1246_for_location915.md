# Preparing for the Breathing Cistern

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with the fact that this place operates on a different temporal register than you do. The cistern measures time in the minutes between water drops, in the slow calcification of roots, in the generations of larvae becoming whatever they become. You will be the fastest thing there by orders of magnitude. Every movement you make will be, relative to the cistern's rhythm, a kind of violence—not malicious, but disruptive simply by existing at your native speed.

This isn't a warning to stay away. It's information you need to carry in your body, not just your mind.

---

## Physical Preparation

### Weeks Before

**Adjust to heat and humidity.** If you have access to a steam room, spend time there—not exercising, just existing. The cistern's air will be thick enough to feel like breathing through warm cloth. Your lungs need to stop interpreting this as threat. Practice slow, shallow breathing that doesn't fight the air's density.

**Train your eyes for almost-darkness.** Spend evenings in rooms lit only by a single candle across the room. Then by moonlight through curtains. Then by nothing at all, sitting until your eyes stop searching for more light and start working with what exists. The fungal bioluminescence will give you just enough to perceive depth and movement—but only if your eyes aren't still demanding daylight levels of illumination.

**Calibrate your hearing.** The cistern's quiet isn't silence—it's a dense field of micro-sounds that your brain will initially discard as meaningless. Practice listening to environments you'd normally tune out: the corner of a basement, the space under a porch at night, the inside of a closet with the door closed. Learn to hear the sounds that aren't trying to be heard.

### Days Before

**Fast lightly.** Not to the point of weakness, but enough that your body isn't loudly digesting. The cistern's creatures have fine-tuned their senses to their environment; your gurgling stomach will be like shouting.

**Clean yourself thoroughly, then stop.** No soaps, no scented products, no deodorants for at least 48 hours before entry. Your skin will develop its own subtle smell that, while not invisible, will be less chemically aggressive than anything manufactured. The creatures there navigate partly by scent and chemical signal—you want to register as "large warm thing" rather than "incomprehensible chemical intrusion."

**Sleep as much as you can.** You'll want to stay longer than expected. The cistern rewards patience in ways you can't anticipate from outside it.

---

## What to Bring

### Wear

Clothing that covers your skin but doesn't trap heat—loose-woven natural fibers if possible. The humidity will soak everything regardless; synthetic materials will feel increasingly wrong against your skin as hours pass. No shoes with hard soles. Soft leather or thick cloth wrappings for your feet, something that will let you feel the texture of what you're walking on without crushing it.

Dark colors, not because the creatures there see color, but because brightness will make you feel more exposed than you are, will keep you in a visitor's mindset when you need to settle into something more like residency.

### Carry

**Light: none.** This is non-negotiable. Any artificial light source will blind you to what's actually visible and will disrupt behaviors that have never encountered anything brighter than the fungal glow. You need to become part of the darkness, not a thing that banishes it.

**Water: a small amount.** Not because you won't find moisture everywhere—you will—but because drinking from the pool or licking the walls, while probably not harmful, introduces your mouth bacteria into an enclosed system. Sip your own water. Leave theirs alone.

**A cloth to sit on:** The floor is alive. You'll want to sit eventually, and a layer between you and the fungal carpet will prevent you from crushing more than necessary, and will prevent things from crawling into your clothing by accident. Something you can leave behind afterward—the cistern will incorporate it eventually.

**Nothing else.** No notebook (you won't be able to see to write, and the sound of pen on paper will be enormous). No recording devices (they'll capture nothing meaningful; the cistern's truth is non-transferable). No food (the smell will be overwhelming to everything there, and the impulse to eat will pull you back into your own metabolism when you want to be forgetting it).

## Entry and First Hours

The entrance is choked with roots. You'll need to move through them slowly, allowing them to touch you, learning immediately that contact here is constant and unavoidable. Don't pull or push; find the gaps and flow through them. This will take time. Let it take time.

When you reach the cistern proper, **stop.** Stand perfectly still just inside the threshold. Let your eyes adjust—this will take twenty minutes at minimum, possibly longer. Let your skin adjust to the humidity, your lungs to the thick air. Let the creatures that fled your arrival determine that you've stopped moving.

Your first instinct will be to look around, to catalog, to understand the space. Resist this. You're not here to understand the cistern; you're here to be in it. Understanding, if it comes, will come later, sideways, through channels that aren't intellectual.

When you do move, move slowly enough that a snail would find your pace reasonable. Not because you're in danger, but because faster movement creates pressure waves, disturbs the web-tangles, startles the geckos into freezing, sends ripples through the pool. The cistern will show you more if you move as though you have nowhere else to be.

---

## Finding Your Place

You cannot explore the entire cistern at once, and you shouldn't try. Choose a location—near the pool, beneath a root-cluster, beside one of the calcified pillars—and make it yours for hours.

Sit. Then sit longer.

Watch one small area, no larger than the span of your hands. In that space, if you watch long enough, you'll see an entire ecology operating: the snails passing through, the flatworms following their trails, the larvae pulsing in what moisture gathered in a depression, the slow rotation of fungal growth toward or away from stimuli you can't perceive. This is not less than observing the whole cistern. It is the whole cistern, compressed into a space you can actually witness.

Time will become strange. Without light cues, without sound variation, your sense of how long you've been there will dissolve. This is correct. This is the point. The cistern exists outside of hours; to experience it, you have to let your hours go.

---

## The Difficult Parts

**You will want to touch things.** The textures here are extraordinary—the fur of root-hairs, the cool damp of mushroom caps, the strange warmth of the pulsing ceiling mass. Some touching is acceptable; the snails and millipedes will crawl over you eventually and you should let them. But reaching out to handle things, to pick them up, to examine them—this imposes your curiosity onto creatures that don't benefit from being examined. Touch what touches you. Leave the rest.

**You will become uncomfortable.** The heat and humidity are relentless. Your clothing will soak through. Your skin will prune. Sweat that can't evaporate will accumulate until you feel like you're dissolving. This is unpleasant but not dangerous, and it's also information—you're learning what it is to be a soft wet creature in a soft wet world, which is what everything else here already knows.

**You will hear things that might not be there.** The humming from inside the root-bulbs. The rhythm of the ceiling mass. Sounds that might be your own blood, might be geological settling, might be something else entirely. The cistern's acoustics are designed—not intentionally, but through accumulated growth—to confuse source and location. Accept the uncertainty. Something is making those sounds; what it is matters less than the fact that you're hearing it.

**You will feel watched.** You are watched. The geckos' huge dark eyes track movement; the spiders sense vibration through their webs; even the bacterial films on the mineral spiral might be responsive to disturbance in ways we don't understand. But being watched here isn't surveillance or threat—it's mutual awareness, the same thing you're doing to them, the acknowledgment that you share space with other sensing beings.

---

## The Uncertain Things

The pulsing mass on the ceiling, the one that radiates heat: don't approach it directly. Not because harm is certain, but because its nature is unknown, and the creatures that live near it maintain a respectful distance. Follow their example. You can observe it from below, feel its rhythm in the air, wonder what it might be—but wondering from a distance is different from investigating up close, and investigation implies an authority over the place that you don't have.

The spiral structure in the pool's center: don't disturb the water near it. Whatever builds it, maintains it, extends it—that process is ongoing, has been ongoing for longer than you can imagine, and the pool's stillness is part of its conditions. You can look. You can appreciate. But the pool is not for wading.

The bulbs on the ancient roots: you may listen to them. Pressing your ear to their hollow warmth is intimate but not intrusive. Whether you hear humming or chewing or nothing at all, whatever you hear is real—real enough for this place, where the distinction between actual and imagined has worn thin along with every other boundary.

---

## Leaving

You'll know when it's time to leave because your body will reassert itself. Hunger or thirst or the need to urinate or simply the sensation of having been in one position too long—the demands of being a large mammal will eventually break through the cistern's timelessness.

Don't feel this as failure. You were never going to become a permanent part of this ecosystem. You don't photosynthesize; you can't eat the fungi safely; you need light and movement and air that doesn't feel like breathing soup. The cistern let you visit. That's the most it could offer; that's more than most places give.

Leave as slowly as you entered. Move through the root-choked passage with the same care, letting the tendrils brush your face, saying goodbye to the texture of this place against your skin.

---

## Afterward

You'll emerge into a world that seems too bright, too dry, too loud, too fast. This will be disorienting, possibly distressing. Sit near the entrance—not in it, but near—until your senses recalibrate. Drink water. Eat something simple. Let your eyes remember what full daylight looks like.

You'll carry the cistern's smell on your skin and clothes for days. The humidity will have worked its way into everything. Some people wash it off immediately; others let it fade naturally, a slow transition back to the surface world's dryness. There's no right answer.

What you won't carry, what you can't carry, is the cistern itself. The experience won't translate into stories that make sense to people who weren't there. The small revelations you might have had—about time, about patience, about what it means to be one living thing among countless others—these will feel true and important while you're there and will become difficult to articulate afterward.

This is appropriate. Some knowledge is local. Some experiences belong to the places where they happen and dissolve when removed from those conditions.

You went into a breathing darkness full of life. You stayed long enough to stop being only a visitor. You left, because you had to, because you're not made for that world.

That's the whole preparation, really: understanding that you're going somewhere that doesn't need you, that will continue without you, that will let you witness its completeness without offering you a permanent place within it. The cistern asks nothing of you except that you move slowly and don't bring light.

Everything else—the physical readiness, the right clothing, the mental preparation—is just making yourself capable of meeting those two simple demands.
