# Preparing for the Hatchery Cisterns

## Physical Conditioning and Health Considerations

The oppressive humidity will be your primary adversary. Before visiting, spend several weeks acclimating yourself to humid environments if you come from a dry climate—steam rooms, tropical greenhouses, anywhere that forces your body to regulate heat through mechanisms other than efficient evaporation. Your lungs will feel the weight of that air immediately, and the sensation of breathing something closer to warm broth than atmosphere can trigger panic in those unprepared for it.

Have your respiratory system evaluated. Any underlying conditions—asthma, chronic bronchitis, the lingering effects of past illnesses—will announce themselves aggressively in that environment. The combination of heat, humidity, and whatever organic particulates drift through the air from thousands of aquatic creatures creates conditions that strain healthy lungs.

Consider also your skin. Extended exposure to that level of moisture, combined with the mineral content of the water and air, will affect you. Bring barrier creams for any areas prone to irritation. Understand that you will not be dry at any point during your visit. Accept this before you descend.

## Clothing and Equipment

Forget anything that doesn't tolerate being soaked. Cotton will become a sodden second skin within minutes. Wool, surprisingly, may serve you better—it retains warmth even wet, and down there, despite the heat, you may find the constant dampness creates its own chill against your skin when you pause too long. Synthetic materials that wick moisture become paradoxical in an environment where there's nowhere for that moisture to go.

Rubber boots are non-negotiable. The keepers wear them for reason. The walkways are perpetually slick, the shallow pools sometimes extend unexpectedly across paths, and what seeps up through the floor grating carries minerals and organic matter you do not want prolonged contact with. Ensure the boots extend well above your ankles—higher if you plan to venture near the older vaults where the channels run less predictably.

A leather apron, if you can acquire one locally, provides protection not just from splashing water but from whatever you might brush against in those narrow passages. The keepers have refined their equipment over generations. Trust their judgment.

For light, bring nothing that requires you to keep it dry. The gas lamps illuminate the main junctions, but the spaces between them hold deep shadows, and the newer excavations may have no permanent lighting at all. Chemical light sources—the kind that activate when you bend them, if such technology exists in this particular branch of the multiverse—serve better than flames you must shield or electrical devices that will corrode. If you must carry a lantern, ensure it's designed for marine environments and understand that the glass will fog constantly.

## Navigational Preparation

The cisterns sprawl across three city blocks in a maze configuration. This cannot be emphasized enough. Study whatever maps exist, but understand they may be incomplete—the newer chambers are still being carved, and even the keepers' knowledge is primarily embodied, held in muscle memory and decades of familiarity rather than documentation.

Mark your path. Bring something that will adhere to wet brick and remain visible in dim light. Colored wax, perhaps, pressed into the mortar between bricks at head height. The gas lamps at every fourth junction provide a framework, but between those points, the uniformity of amber-lit brick and sweating stone can dissolve your sense of direction entirely.

Listen for the pumps. That rumble, felt more than heard, provides a kind of compass—the mechanical heart of the complex lies in a specific direction, and if you can orient yourself relative to it, you maintain at least one fixed point. The sound of pickaxes from the eastern excavations offers another landmark, though it comes and goes with the workers' shifts.

Do not rely on the water flow for navigation. It seeps up, drips down, moves through channels in patterns determined by geology and infrastructure too complex to intuit. The currents follow their own logic.

## Understanding the Inhabitants

The eels in the shallow channels are harmless individually—juveniles no thicker than a finger, concerned only with their own slow movements and whatever nutrients drift through the water. But do not wade through the channels unnecessarily. Thousands of small bodies responding to your intrusion creates unpredictable movement, and while they won't attack, the sensation of being surrounded by that much sliding, shivering life unnerves even the steadiest visitors. The water seeming to come alive around your ankles triggers something deep in the mammalian brain.

The salamanders in the older vaults demand more respect. Forearm-length, adapted to darkness, their milky eyes will not track your movements the way sighted creatures' would. They sense vibration, changes in water pressure, perhaps heat signatures. Move slowly around their pools. Do not cast shadows across the water suddenly—though their eyes are adapted to the dark, they may still perceive changes in the dim light, and startled salamanders can move faster than their drifting demeanor suggests.

The freshwater crabs in the corners are defensive rather than aggressive. Their shells, encrusted with the same pale mineral deposits that coat the pipes, provide camouflage against the calcified surfaces they inhabit. You may not notice them until you're close, and if you startle them, their response is to raise their claws and hold position rather than flee. Give them space. Do not reach into corners without looking carefully first.

The mother-fish requires a different kind of preparation entirely.

Forty years in the dark. Forty years of growth in an environment of constant warmth, abundant food, no predators, and no real stimulation beyond the patient rhythms of water and the occasional keeper checking her pool. What does that produce? Something patient. Something strange. Something that has learned to recognize the patterns of the world above her pool—the footsteps of keepers, the schedule of feeding, the particular ripples that different activities create.

She will know you're there before you see her. She may surface to breathe while you stand at the edge of her pool—they say she does this, that she's learned to associate the presence of observers with feeding time or simply with something different, a break in the monotony of her existence. No one agrees on what species she is anymore, which tells you that she's changed, that classification has become uncertain, that she exists now in a category of one.

Do not lean over the water. Do not trail your fingers in her pool. Whatever she is, whatever she's become, she is large and patient, and forty years of dark adaptation may have given her senses you cannot anticipate. Respect the boundary between her world and yours.

## Engaging with the Keepers

The keepers work in near-silence, communicating in hand signals refined over generations. This tells you several things.

First, they have created a complete language in gesture—complete enough to coordinate the complex work of maintaining this living system. This suggests a community with strong internal bonds and long traditions, where knowledge passes through apprenticeship and demonstration rather than documentation.

Second, they find silence valuable down here. Perhaps sound disturbs the creatures. Perhaps the acoustic properties of the cisterns make speech echo unpredictably, words bouncing off wet brick until they become unintelligible. Perhaps they've simply learned that words are unnecessary, that the work speaks for itself.

Third, and most importantly for you: they will assess you immediately and completely without asking a single question. Your equipment, your bearing, your movements will tell them whether you understand where you are. Arrive well-prepared and they may tolerate your presence, may even share knowledge through demonstration if not explanation. Arrive carelessly and you will find their silence a wall you cannot penetrate.

Do not attempt to learn their hand signals before you arrive—you will inevitably get something wrong, and a misapplied sign could mean anything from an insult to a false alarm about conditions in a particular chamber. Instead, communicate your intentions through obvious, universal gestures: pointing where you wish to go, showing empty hands to indicate peaceful intent, standing back and watching before you act.

If you must speak, do so quietly and accept that they may not respond in kind. Do not interpret their silence as rudeness. Interpret it as a different mode of being, one adapted to their environment as thoroughly as the creatures they tend.

## Food and Water

This may seem paradoxical in a place saturated with moisture, but bring your own water. What seeps through the floor, drips from the ceiling, and fills the breeding channels carries mineral loads and organic content from the entire system. The watercress in the overflow channels is bitter and mineral-tasting for a reason. The keepers may have developed tolerances over generations of working here; you have not.

Sealed containers are essential. Anything unsealed will absorb the humidity, will attract the attention of whatever small creatures drift through the air, will taste of the cisterns within hours. Metal containers may corrode; glass or ceramic serves better, though glass risks breakage on the slick walkways.

The keepers harvest that watercress for their own meals, which suggests that food can be safely consumed down here with proper precautions. But their digestions have adapted to this environment, and yours has not. Bring food that doesn't require preparation, that can be eaten quickly in the brief moments when you find a stable, relatively dry surface to rest.

## Psychological Preparation

This is perhaps the most important category, and the most difficult to address concretely.

The low ceiling—twelve feet at most—will press down on you. Not physically, but psychologically. Combined with the constant moisture, the dim amber light, the knowledge that the city goes about its business unaware above you, the cisterns create a particular kind of claustrophobia that has nothing to do with confined spaces and everything to do with existing in a world that operates by different rules.

The constant sound of water and life in aggregate never stops. There is no silence down here, only the absence of loud sounds. The drip, the whisper, the clank, the groan, the hiss—your mind will try to find patterns in it, try to separate signal from noise, and this is exhausting. Bring something to focus your attention when the ambient sound becomes overwhelming. A smooth stone to turn in your fingers. A memorized text to recite silently. Anything that gives your mind a channel other than interpreting every drip as potential significance.

The darkness between the gas lamps is not empty. It contains creatures, moisture, the slow transformation of stone and brick under the patient work of mineral deposits. You cannot see what happens in those shadows, but you can feel it—life continuing without observation, processes older than the city unfolding in the spaces between light. Some visitors find this deeply uncomfortable. Others find it strangely reassuring, evidence that the world doesn't require human attention to continue existing.

Decide which kind of visitor you are before you descend.

## Duration and Exit Planning

Plan for a shorter visit than you think you can handle. The environment is tolerable for hours, but it erodes your resilience in ways that aren't obvious until you try to leave and find yourself more exhausted than the physical demands justify. The humidity, the heat, the constant low-level attention required to navigate safely—all of it accumulates.

Know your exit routes. Know them cold, know them even if the gas lamps fail, know them by the feel of the walls under your fingertips and the direction of the pump-vibration. The keepers can navigate in total darkness if necessary; you cannot, but you should be able to find your way out with diminished light if some portion of the gas system fails.

Tell someone above when you descend and when you expect to return. The cisterns operate in their own time, seasons meaning nothing, the city above irrelevant to the work below. It would be easy to lose track of hours down there, to emerge blinking into daylight or darkness with no clear sense of how long you were below.

## Final Considerations

The whole complex exists in a state of becoming—this is perhaps the most important truth to internalize before you visit. The new chambers are still being carved while old ones slowly transform under the patient work of mineral and moisture. The stone grows its own architecture. The life adapts to the dark.

You are visiting a process, not a place. The Hatchery Cisterns you see will not be the Hatchery Cisterns that existed a generation ago, nor the ones that will exist a generation hence. The mother-fish continues growing, continues changing. The excavations push eastward. The mineral deposits thicken on every surface. The keepers pass their knowledge to apprentices who will inherit a slightly different world than the one their teachers knew.

Go with humility. Go with awareness that you are witnessing a collaboration between human intention and natural process that has been unfolding for generations. Go with respect for the keepers who have made this their life's work, for the creatures who know no other existence, for the slow geological and hydrological forces that shaped this space before anyone thought to use it for cultivation.

And perhaps bring an extra set of dry clothes to leave at the entrance above, for when you finally emerge and remember what it feels like to breathe air that doesn't weigh on your lungs like warm wool.
