# Preparing for the Hatching Plateau

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a place. You are visiting a *process*. The Hatching Plateau exists in a state of active becoming—geology, ecology, and something else entirely are all mid-emergence. This changes everything about how you prepare. You're not packing for a destination; you're packing for a birth you'll witness from inside the delivery room.

The plateau hasn't finished deciding what it is. This means the rules—physical, biological, perhaps ontological—are still being written. What's safe in hour one may not be safe in hour three. What's solid underfoot now may be negotiable later. You are not a tourist here. You are, whether you want to be or not, a variable in an equation that's still being solved.

---

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear and Movement

The basite is warm and actively sweating mineral-rich water. Standard hiking boots will fail you in multiple ways: they'll conduct heat uncomfortably, their treads will fill with mineral deposits that harden unpredictably, and they won't give you the tactile feedback you need to sense when the stone beneath you is "more certain of itself" versus still settling.

Consider instead: thick-soled sandals with drainage, or custom boots with ceramic-composite soles and mesh uppers. You want heat insulation from below but breathability above. The pools steam at knee-height, which means your lower legs will exist in a humid microclimate even when you're walking on dry stone.

More importantly: *learn to walk slowly before you arrive*. Practice moving through spaces with genuine attention to each footfall. The crawlers are inscribing something into the stone—instructions, perhaps, or territorial markers, or structural reinforcement. You don't want to interrupt their work carelessly. You don't want to step on them, obviously, but more than that, you don't want to scuff their secretions before they've hardened. Whatever they're doing, it seems to matter to the plateau's development.

### Breathing and Atmosphere

The air will be mineral-laden. That steam hanging at knee-height isn't just water vapor—it's carrying whatever the rock is exuding as it crystallizes. Expect a taste like wet limestone and copper, with undertones that shift as you move across the plateau.

A lightweight respirator mask with mineral filtration would be wise for extended visits, but—and this is important—*don't wear it constantly*. The atmosphere is part of the information here. The way the air changes near different incubators, the way it thickens or thins in the sound-pressure pockets, the subtle shifts in mineral composition as the plateau develops—these are data you'll want access to. Wear the mask when you need to rest your lungs. Remove it when you need to understand.

Bring saline rinse for your sinuses. You'll want it afterward.

### Thermal Considerations

The stone is "still warm from its own making." The pools steam. The mist hangs heavy. But there's no sun, and the light has no thermal component—it's illumination without heat. This creates a strange thermal landscape: hot from below, neutral from above, humid throughout.

Dress in layers you can shed quickly. Natural fibers will handle the moisture better than synthetics. Expect to be uncomfortable in a way that's hard to name—your body's thermoregulation systems will keep getting confused signals. Stay hydrated, but be thoughtful about drinking the pool water even if you're tempted. We don't know what those minerals do to human biology over time.

## Sensory Preparation

### The Sound

This requires the most preparation, and there's no way to fully prepare.

Thirteen incubators, each producing a different frequency, creating interference patterns across the plateau. Pockets of pressure and release. Zones where the sound becomes "almost tactile." This isn't background noise. This is an acoustic environment that will physically affect you—inner ear massage in some places, near-silence in others, and constant subsonic grinding beneath everything.

Before you go:
- Spend time with binaural audio, especially layered drones. Get your nervous system accustomed to immersive harmonic environments.
- Practice moving through spaces with variable sound pressure—concert halls, cathedrals, anywhere with complex acoustics. Learn to notice how sound changes with each step.
- Consider having your ears professionally cleaned. You want full sensitivity.

Bring high-quality earplugs with flat attenuation—not the foam kind that muffle everything, but the kind that reduce volume while preserving frequency relationships. You may need to dial down the intensity at times without losing the information.

Do *not* bring noise-canceling headphones. The sound is part of what's happening here. The incubators are communicating, or developing, or both. Cutting yourself off from that would be like visiting a birth with your eyes closed.

### The Light

Pearlescent. Directionless. Dim enough that your eyes never fully adjust. Shadows that suggest rather than fall.

This is going to be disorienting in ways that compound over time. Your visual system relies on shadows for depth perception, on light-source direction for spatial orientation. Here, you get neither. After a few hours, you may start to feel like objects are floating, like distances are uncertain, like the plateau is both smaller and larger than it should be.

Preparation:
- Spend time in diffuse light environments before you go. Overcast days. Rooms lit only by reflected light. Get your eyes used to working without strong directional cues.
- Practice estimating distances in low-contrast environments.
- Bring a small handheld light—not for general illumination but for occasional "reality checks" when you need to cast a real shadow and remind your visual system how space works.

Don't rely on the light for timing. You won't be able to tell how long you've been there by looking at the sky. There is no sky, or if there is, it offers no information. Bring a watch. Check it often.

### Touch and Proprioception

The stone sweats. The incubators pulse with *dimension*. The sound creates physical pressure in some locations.

Your sense of touch is going to receive information it doesn't know how to categorize. The incubators' oscillation "between states of presence" may feel like touching something that's intermittently not-quite-there. The pools may feel thicker or thinner than water should. The frame near the center—the one that converts light to warmth and makes your eyes disagree about its interior—may feel like standing near a strong magnetic field, or like nothing at all, depending on factors you can't predict.

Bring gloves you can remove easily. You'll want to touch things directly sometimes, but you'll also want protection available when the strangeness becomes too much. Cotton or thin leather—something that doesn't completely block sensation but provides a psychological buffer.

## Cognitive and Psychological Preparation

### Accepting Ambiguity

The organisms here exist in states of "not quite animal, not quite machine, not quite either kingdom of thing you have language for." The incubators breathe "not air but probability." The fauna may be part of the plateau or may have found it—the distinction may not be meaningful.

You need to arrive with your need-to-categorize impulse already softened. Practice sitting with uncertainty in your daily life. When you encounter something you don't understand, resist the urge to immediately file it into a familiar box. Let it remain undefined. Get comfortable with mental states like "this is a thing I'm perceiving" without needing to append "and it's a type of X."

If you can't do this, the Hatching Plateau will do it to you—but the forced version is much harder.

### Witnessing Without Intervening

This is perhaps the most important preparation.

Something is being born here. Thirteen somethings, at least, plus the plateau itself, plus possibly the fauna, plus whatever's happening with the frame. You are not here to help. You are not here to interfere. You are here to witness.

The transparent salamanders with their schematic-organs, the geometric jellyfish flattening against the incubators, the crawlers inscribing their instructions—these are all participants in a process that predates your arrival and will continue after your departure. You are not a participant. You are, at best, a tolerated observer.

Examine your helper impulses before you go. When you see something struggling, do you immediately want to intervene? When you see something you don't understand, do you want to poke it to learn more? When you see a process unfolding, do you want to optimize it?

All of these impulses will arise on the Hatching Plateau. Practice letting them arise and pass without action. The crawlers know what they're doing. The jellyfish have their own reasons. The incubators don't need your assistance.

### The Frame

Near the center. Three meters tall. A doorway or an arch or a constraint. Step through it and you might arrive in the same place—or you might not.

Here's my honest guidance: don't step through it.

I know this is difficult. The frame is clearly important. It's the only built structure on the plateau. The seven river-stones at its base—cool, from elsewhere, offerings or components—suggest that others have engaged with it before. The single sustained note it hums, outside the incubators' chorus, suggests enormous patience. It's waiting for something.

It may be waiting for *you*.

But the Hatching Plateau is a place of becoming, and the frame is a mechanism of transport or transformation. If you step through and arrive somewhere else, you may arrive somewhere that hasn't finished becoming yet. You may arrive in a state that hasn't finished becoming yet. The frame's patience may be the patience of something that doesn't care whether what emerges from it is still recognizably human.

Approach the frame. Study it. Feel the warmth it returns from the converted light. Listen to its note. But do not step through. Not on your first visit. Not until you understand more about what the plateau is becoming and what it wants from visitors who engage with its central mystery.

If you absolutely must interact with the frame: bring something to offer. Something from elsewhere, like the river-stones. Something cool and certain and finished—a counterweight to all this hot, sweating, undecided emergence. Place it at the base with the others. See if anything responds.

Then step back and wait.

## Practical Supplies

Beyond what I've already mentioned:

- **Water and food**: Bring your own. Don't drink from the pools or eat any of the fauna, no matter how curious you are about what the transparent salamanders taste like. You don't know what those minerals or those schematics will do inside you.

- **A journal**: Not electronic—something that can handle humidity and won't be affected by whatever fields the incubators might be producing. Write down what you observe. The shifting patterns, the changes in pool color, the behavior of the fauna. You'll want this record later, both to process what you experienced and to compare with future visitors' accounts.

- **A small mirror**: In an environment with no reliable shadows and directionless light, you may occasionally need to confirm that you still have a reflection. This sounds paranoid. Bring the mirror anyway.

- **Offerings**: If you want to engage with the frame, or even just demonstrate respect for the process you're witnessing, bring small objects to leave. Stones from rivers. Seeds. Anything that represents completed becoming from elsewhere—things that have already decided what they are.

- **A way to mark time**: I said this before but it bears repeating. You will lose track of time here. The light doesn't change. The sound is hypnotic. The process of becoming is absorbing to watch. Set alarms. Check your watch hourly. Decide in advance how long you're staying and hold to it.

- **Something grounding from home**: A photograph. A small object with personal significance. Something that reminds you who you were before you arrived. The plateau is a place of potential and specification, and it's possible—I don't know, but it's possible—that its influence extends to observers. Keep something nearby that reminds you that you've already decided what you are.

## During Your Visit

Move slowly. Breathe intentionally. Watch where the crawlers have been and where they're going. Find the sound-pressure pockets that feel right to you and spend time in them. Find the ones that don't and learn what the difference tells you about the plateau's geography.

Observe the incubators from a respectful distance. Note which ones are more opaque, which ones you can almost see through. Don't touch them. Whatever's inside is dreaming about a world that will solidify around it—don't introduce yourself into those dreams uninvited.

Watch the jellyfish when they descend to the incubators. What are they checking? What satisfies them? What makes them rise again?

Note how the plateau changes. The mineral shifts, the pattern reorganizations, the fauna behaviors. You're watching a world specify itself. The changes may be subtle but they're constant. Pay attention.

And when the sound becomes too much, or the ambiguity becomes too heavy, or the creeping sense that you're witnessing something you weren't meant to see becomes overwhelming—

Leave. Come back another time. The plateau will still be becoming. The incubators will still be pulsing. The frame will still be patient.

---

## After Your Visit

Expect a period of adjustment. Your senses have been bathed in strangeness—directionless light, harmonic pressure, visual ambiguity. Ordinary environments will feel almost aggressively definite for a few days. Shadows will seem too sharp. Sounds will seem too simple. The certainty of finished things may feel like a loss.

This passes.

Review your journal. Compare notes with other travelers if you can find them. Try to articulate what you witnessed without forcing it into categories that don't fit.

And pay attention to your dreams. The incubators dream of worlds that solidify around them. You were there, in that space of probability and becoming. Some of that may have imprinted. You may find yourself dreaming of hatching—your own or something else's. You may find yourself dreaming of the frame.

These are not warnings, exactly. Just things to notice. You witnessed a birth. Some part of that witnessing may stay with you, may become part of your own ongoing process of specification.

That's not a contamination. That's what happens when you enter a place that hasn't finished deciding what it is.

You become, briefly, part of the decision.
