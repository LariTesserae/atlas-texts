# Preparing for the Pocket Behind the Waterfall

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with what this place actually is. This is not a cave that happens to be unusual. This is a space where the fundamental agreements reality makes with itself have been renegotiated. Geometry folds. Time pools. The walls breathe on a scale that will make you question whether your own heartbeat is real or borrowed. You need to understand this not as danger to be overcome, but as a different language you'll be briefly immersed in.

The text tells you something crucial: you can leave, but only while you still know what leaving means. This is your operating constraint. Everything else flows from it.

## Physical Preparation

**Clothing and Body**

Wear wool, not synthetic materials. Wool retains warmth when wet, and you will be wet—completely, continuously, in ways that go beyond surface dampness. The humidity is described as so complete that breathing feels like drinking. Your clothing will never dry. It will become part of the water system the moment you enter. Synthetics will cling and chafe; cotton will pull heat from you faster than you can generate it. Wool will keep you functional.

Wear close-fitting layers rather than loose garments. In a space where geometry folds, loose fabric could catch on corners that exist only partially, could brush against surfaces occupying uncertain positions. You don't want to discover what it feels like to have your sleeve pass through a mushroom that isn't entirely solid.

Footwear matters enormously. You need grip on wet limestone, which means soft rubber soles with significant texture. But you also need to feel the ground—the text mentions the floor having depressions deeper than they should be. You need to sense when you're approaching one of those impossible depths before your full weight commits to the step. Something between a water shoe and a hiking boot. No steel toes, no rigid soles.

Leave your skin as exposed as the cold allows you to tolerate. I know this seems contradictory to the wool advice, but your skin is the most honest sensor you have. Hands uncovered. Face uncovered. You need to feel the walls breathing. You need to notice the temperature differential when you approach something that shouldn't be there.

**What to Carry**

Almost nothing.

The instinct will be to bring tools, light sources, supplies. Resist this. Every object you bring is an object that exists according to outside rules, and the pocket will have to negotiate with each one. A flashlight may work, or it may work differently, or it may illuminate things that the chamber's native bioluminescence has the wisdom to leave dark. A watch will lie to you—time pools here, and a device that insists on measuring it linearly will only confuse you about how long you've stayed.

Bring a single small stone from outside. River-worn if possible, something that fits in your closed palm. This is not superstition; this is anchor technology. When the memories of entering begin to shift, when you find yourself uncertain whether you came through the waterfall or were always here, hold the stone. Remember picking it up. Remember the specific weight of it in the specific moment before you entered. The stone cannot tell you what is true, but it can remind you that an outside exists, that you had a continuous experience leading to this moment, that the direction called "leaving" exists even when you can't feel it.

Bring nothing written. No books, no notebooks, no phones with text on their screens. In a place where cause and effect operate loosely, where alternatives are offered, written language is too fixed, too insistent on sequences that may not hold. If you need to remember something, you'll have to hold it in the same mind that the chamber is slowly softening. That's appropriate. That's honest.

**Food and Water**

Do not eat before entering. Not because of any mystical fasting requirement, but because digestion is a process that expects time to flow predictably. An interrupted digestive process in a place where hours might pass as moments—or moments as hours—could leave you nauseated in ways that make the disorientation worse.

Do not plan to eat inside. The mushrooms might be edible—the text suspects they are—but eating something that grows by passing *through* solid stone means incorporating that quality into yourself. You don't know what that would mean. Neither do I. Neither, I suspect, does anyone.

The water is everywhere and the text implies it would sustain you. This is probably true. But drinking water that "tastes older" depending on where you stand means drinking different waters, potentially different *times* of water. Do this only if you must. If you're staying long enough to need hydration, you're probably staying too long.

## Mental and Perceptual Preparation

**Calibrating Your Senses**

In the weeks before you go, practice peripheral vision. Sit in dim rooms and attend to the edges of what you can see without moving your eyes. The mushrooms in the pocket occupy more space when viewed this way; the meaningful information may live at the margins of perception rather than the center.

Practice noticing your own breathing without controlling it. This is harder than it sounds—observation usually becomes interference. But you'll need to distinguish your breath from the chamber's breath, and you can only do that if you know your own rhythm well enough to recognize when something else is suggesting a different one.

Spend time in complete darkness. Not the darkness of a room with the lights off, but genuine absence of light—a cave, a sensory deprivation context, somewhere with no photons. Learn what your visual system does when there's nothing to see. It will invent. It will offer patterns. Know what your particular brain's inventions look like so you can distinguish them from the bioluminescence that exists "more as a memory of light than light itself."

**Adjusting Your Relationship with Certainty**

This is the most important preparation and the hardest to describe.

You need to arrive at the waterfall already having loosened your grip on the idea that reality is obligated to be consistent. Not abandoned it—you need that grip to leave—but loosened it enough that the geometry folding around you registers as information rather than emergency.

Spend time with optical illusions. Not glancing at them, but dwelling in them, letting your perception flip back and forth between interpretations and noticing the moment of transition. That moment—where one truth becomes another truth and neither is wrong—that's the frequency you need to be able to tune to.

Read about quantum superposition, not because the chamber operates by quantum mechanics, but because the conceptual vocabulary is useful. The two ferns growing from the same spot, each real, each casting the same shadow—you need a framework for holding that without your mind rejecting it as error.

**Preparing for Memory Distortion**

The text tells you that your memories of entering will begin to shift, rearranging into sequences that feel true but didn't happen. This is perhaps the most serious warning in the entire description.

Before you enter, tell someone exactly how you approached the waterfall. Every detail. What you passed, what you noticed, the sequence of your steps. Not so they can verify it later—though that has value—but so the telling itself creates a kind of groove in your memory, a worn path that's harder to reroute.

Then, as you enter, narrate to yourself. Not out loud (sound behaves strangely in there) but in the voice inside your head. "I am stepping through the water. The water is cold. I feel the rock give way to space. I am inside now." This creates a present-tense anchor that's harder for retrospective revision to corrupt.

When you notice the memories shifting—and you will notice, if you're paying attention—don't fight it. Fighting makes you rigid, and rigidity will get you folded into the geometry. Instead, hold both: the memory that feels true, and the knowledge that you created a different memory-groove before entering. Let them coexist. The pocket offers alternatives; you don't have to accept them, but you also don't have to reject them violently.

## Navigating the Space

**Movement**

Walk slowly. Not because of physical danger, but because each step takes you somewhere "adjacent to where you began, but not the same." If you rush, you'll cycle through adjacencies faster than you can track, and you'll lose the thread of where you've been.

Count your steps, but hold the count loosely. Twelve steps is the circuit, but twelve steps doesn't return you to the start—it returns you to a version. Keep counting anyway. The numbers give you structure, and structure is what the pocket is slowly dissolving.

When you encounter a pool, test its depth with something other than your body first. The text describes lowering a hand into a puddle and feeling water close over elbow, shoulder. This is not metaphor. The depths are real, just non-Euclidean. A stick, a trailing piece of fabric, anything that can probe without committing you.

Do not touch the mushrooms that grow through stone. Your finger will pass into something that "feels like cold water but isn't wet." That's a one-way description. The text doesn't say what happens if you keep going. Neither will I, because I don't know.

**The Cairn**

You will probably want to visit it. The deepest part of the chamber, the only sign that another person was here, the dark river-worn stones from somewhere else—it calls to the part of you that wants to know you're not alone in this experience.

Go if you must. But understand what you're seeing.

Someone came here, understood what this place was, and chose to make a mark. They brought stones that did not belong. They stacked them in a shape that meant something. And now the chamber is unmaking that meaning, slowly, through the patient work of moss and mineral deposition. In a few centuries, intention will become geology.

This is not malevolent. This is the nature of the pocket. It does not preserve; it incorporates. The person who built the cairn knew this—they built it anyway, knowing it would be absorbed. There's something beautiful in that, and something cautionary. Whatever you do in here will also be incorporated. Whatever mark you leave—even the mark of your presence, your breath, the pattern of your steps—will become part of the chamber.

Don't add to the cairn. Don't take from it. Don't touch it. Witness it as what it is: evidence that others have passed through, and evidence that passing through leaves traces that eventually become indistinguishable from the place itself.

**Time Management (A Contradiction in Terms)**

You cannot manage time in a place where time pools and eddies. But you can manage your relationship to the question of time.

Decide before you enter how many breaths you will take inside. Not minutes, not steps, breaths. A breath is a unit that you control and that remains meaningful regardless of how time behaves externally. A hundred breaths. Two hundred. Choose a number that feels brief, then cut it in half. You will want to stay. The text tells you that something opens in your chest if you match your breathing to the chamber's rhythm, and that something is seductive. The opening feels like understanding. It is also the beginning of incorporation.

When you reach your breath count, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself. Do not decide you can stay for fifty more. The geometry is already starting to fold you in; you can feel it, even if you don't recognize the feeling yet. Your breath count is your contract with the outside. Honor it.

## The Threshold

**Entering**

The waterfall is ordinary from outside. That's important. It means the transition is not the waterfall's doing—it's the space behind it, reaching through. When you step through the falling water, you're not entering something; something is accepting you.

Press yourself against what should be solid rock. The text says this is how you find yourself inside. Note the passivity of that language. You don't push through; you press, and you *find* yourself. The agency is shared, at best.

Be respectful in the way you enter. Not because something is watching (though something is) but because you are asking to be shown something that doesn't have to show itself. The pocket exists whether or not you enter. You are the variable. Enter like a guest.

**Exiting**

This is where preparation matters most.

The threshold will not stay open forever. The walls will notice you. The geometry will start to fold you in. These are the text's words, and they should be taken seriously.

The direction called "leaving" must still mean something to you. If you've stayed until your memories have rearranged, until the alternative sequences feel as true as the original, you may find that "leaving" has become ambiguous. You may find that one version of you entered while another version didn't, and it's not clear which version you are now, or which version has access to the exit.

This is why the stone matters. This is why the breath count matters. This is why you told someone exactly how you arrived and narrated your entrance to yourself. You need anchors to a continuous self who came from outside, because that's the self who knows where outside is.

When you decide to leave, move without hesitation. Retrace the direction you came (even though retracing is impossible—do it anyway; the intention matters). Step toward the sound of the waterfall, which will be muffled, coming from too many directions, but it will still be there. The outside is still roaring, still ordinary, still measuring time in linear ways.

You may feel something like reluctance from the chamber. Not malevolent, just gravitational. A place that has begun to incorporate you will notice when you un-incorporate. Move through that reluctance the way you'd move through cold water—it will resist, and then it will let you go.

## After

When you emerge, the sun will have moved. You won't know how much. The birds will be calling, the pool will be cold and clear, and everything will be behaving itself in ways that might feel like loss.

Sit with the waterfall for a while before you walk away. Let the ordinary sound replace the muffled strangeness. Let your clothes begin to dry. Check that all your fingers move, that your feet are on the ground, that your shadow falls in the direction shadows should.

Find whoever you told about your approach. Tell them you're back. Let them tell you what time it is, how long you've been gone. Accept the number without arguing, even if it feels wrong. The pocket's time is not a mistake; it's just different. Both experiences are true. You contain both now.

In the days that follow, you may notice things. Moments where geometry seems softer than it should be. Water falling in ways that take slightly too long. Peripheral glimpses of mushrooms that occupy less space when you look directly. This is not the pocket following you. This is you, having been shown alternatives, now noticing them where you couldn't before. The strangeness was always there. You're just calibrated to it now.

This will fade. Mostly. The world you live in is more stable than the pocket, and it will reassert itself. But you might find, years from now, that you pause at waterfalls longer than other people do. You might find that you press your hand against rock faces, just to check. You might find that you've started breathing more slowly.

This is not contamination. This is not damage. This is what it means to have visited a place that suggests alternatives and to have returned with the suggestion still echoing.

The pocket is still there, behind the waterfall, breathing.

It will still be there when you're ready to understand more.
