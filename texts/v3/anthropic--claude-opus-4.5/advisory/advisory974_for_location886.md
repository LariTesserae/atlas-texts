# Preparing for the Suspended Cisterns of Vael-Ascending

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are preparing to visit a place that exists in a state of becoming. The Vael do not experience their home as a fixed location but as a process, a slow accumulation of clay and repair and adaptation that has been unfolding for eight generations. Your preparation must account not only for the physical challenges—and they are substantial—but for the psychological shift required to exist within something that is actively, patiently transforming around you.

The Suspended Cisterns occupy a thermal freshwater column rising through a salt sea. You will be approximately two hundred meters below what might loosely be called a surface, suspended in warm water that glows with diffuse, sourceless light, surrounded by architecture that has grown beyond its original intentions. Nothing here will behave quite as you expect. Prepare for that disorientation as deliberately as you prepare for the pressure.

## Physical Preparation: The Body's Education

### Pressure Adaptation

Two hundred meters of depth—even in fresh water, even in warm water—exerts force your surface-adapted body was never designed to withstand. Before arrival, you must undertake a gradual pressure conditioning program spanning several months at minimum. This is not optional. The Vael have spent eight generations adapting to their environment; you have only your preparation.

Begin with freediving practice in your origin location. Learn to equalize pressure in your sinuses, your ears, the small spaces of your skull. Learn what it feels like when your lungs compress, when your blood shifts toward your core, when your heart rate drops in response to facial immersion. These reflexes exist in you already—the mammalian dive response is ancient—but they have likely never been tested. Test them. Slowly, incrementally, with appropriate safety measures.

If possible, spend time in hyperbaric environments before your transit. Pressurized chambers, deep pools, whatever your origin location offers. Your goal is not to become immune to pressure but to become familiar with it, to recognize the subtle signals your body sends when something is wrong.

### Thermal Tolerance

The thermal column is warm. Warmer than most natural water you have encountered. The heat rises from volcanic fissures below and creates a gradient—warmer at the bottom, cooler at the top, with the inhabited zone existing in a band of tolerable temperature that the Vael have mapped through generations of experience.

Your body will need to regulate heat differently here than it does in air. Prolonged immersion in warm water can lead to hyperthermia even when the water feels comfortable. Practice extended warm-water immersion before your visit. Learn to recognize the early signs of overheating: confusion, irritability, the strange sensation of being too warm in a medium that should be cooling you.

Conversely, venturing outside the thermal column—into the cold salt sea that surrounds it—will shock your system. The Vael avoid this boundary. You should too, at least until you understand why.

### Breath and Its Absence

The Vael have solved the problem of breathing through eight generations of accumulated infrastructure: clay cisterns holding trapped air, pressure valves made from organic materials, a complex network of air-traps and speaking-tubes that distributes breath throughout their suspended city. You will be dependent on this infrastructure from the moment of your arrival.

Before your transit, practice breath-holding. Not to dangerous extremes—you are not trying to become a competitive freediver—but enough to understand your relationship with air. How long can you comfortably hold your breath? How does it feel when that comfort begins to erode? What is the difference between the urge to breathe and the actual need to breathe?

More importantly, practice breathing in confined spaces. The cisterns are not spacious. The air inside them is thick, warm, and tastes of iron. It is shared air, recycled air, air that carries the exhalations of everyone who came before you. If you have any tendency toward claustrophobia, address it now. The Vael prefer small spaces because small spaces can be sealed quickly if something goes wrong. You must learn to find comfort in confinement.

### Movement in Three Dimensions

There is no ground in Vael-Ascending. No floor. No stable surface to stand on. You will move by swimming, by pulling yourself along rope bridges, by navigating through dense vegetation that parts reluctantly around your body. Your spatial orientation will be challenged constantly.

Practice swimming in low-visibility conditions. Practice moving through underwater obstacles—kelp beds, if available, or artificial structures designed to simulate dense vegetation. Learn to navigate by touch and memory rather than sight. The sediment haze in the thermal column makes distance difficult to judge; you will need to develop other ways of knowing where you are.

If possible, spend time in environments where gravity's reference is ambiguous. Zero-gravity simulations, if your origin location offers them. Extended floating in sensory deprivation tanks. Anything that teaches your body to exist without the constant reassurance of down.

## Equipment: What to Bring and What to Leave

### The Problem of Materials

The Vael have almost no metal. What little exists—bronze knives passed down through generations—carries the weight of sacred text. Metal corrodes in water. Metal is heavy. Metal requires technologies the Vael do not possess: mining, smelting, forging. Their material culture is built from clay, fiber, bone, shell, and the secretions of creatures long vanished.

Anything metal you bring will mark you immediately as foreign. More practically, it will corrode, weigh you down, and potentially contaminate the water the Vael depend on for survival. Leave metal behind. All of it. This includes zippers, buckles, buttons, clasps, jewelry, medical devices, tools, and the thousand small metal objects that infiltrate surface-world clothing without your conscious awareness.

### What to Wear

Loose-fitting garments made from natural fibers that can tolerate prolonged water exposure. The Vael likely wear very little—clothing impedes swimming and serves little thermal purpose in warm water—but you may want more coverage than they consider necessary, at least initially.

Consider a simple tunic and shorts made from tightly woven plant fiber. Hemp, if available. Linen. Something that won't disintegrate but also won't trap air and affect your buoyancy unpredictably. Avoid anything with complex construction: seams that might split, fastenings that might fail, layering that might trap water in uncomfortable ways.

Footwear is probably unnecessary and potentially dangerous. The Vael navigate by touch, and bare feet against rope bridges and clay surfaces provide essential information. If you cannot tolerate barefoot travel, bring the simplest possible foot covering—a wrap of cloth, nothing more.

### Tools and Personal Items

A small belt pouch made from treated hide or dense woven fiber to carry essentials. Inside:

A knife. Not metal. Bone or volcanic glass, sharpened carefully. The Vael use crusite shells and volcanic glass for cutting; you should adapt to local materials as quickly as possible, but having a cutting tool for emergencies is non-negotiable.

A small container of wound sealant appropriate for underwater use. The water here is not sterile. The mineral content may actually inhibit some infections, but open wounds in a warm, organic-rich environment remain dangerous. Medical-grade waterproof adhesive, if your origin location produces such things. Otherwise, learn to make mixite paste from the Vael—crusite shells and volcanic glass ground fine as flour, mixed with something that makes it adhesive.

A length of braided cord, as strong as you can obtain without using metal fibers. Thirty meters at minimum. For emergencies involving rope bridges, getting lost in vegetation, anchoring yourself in unexpected currents.

A small waterproof light source, if such exists in your origin location without relying on metal components. The luminescent growth in the cisterns provides light, but it pulses arrhythmically and cannot be directed. In certain situations—medical emergencies, repairs, navigation through unfamiliar areas—you may need more reliable illumination. However, be prepared for the Vael to find this disturbing or inappropriate. Their eyes have adapted to the dim green-gold light of their world; sudden brightness may be painful or disorienting for them.

Nothing else. Truly. Every object you bring is an object you must keep track of, protect from water damage, and explain to people who have lived their entire lives with material scarcity you cannot imagine. Bring only what you cannot survive without, and be prepared to abandon even those things if necessary.

## Arrival and Decompression

You will not arrive at the cisterns directly. You cannot. The pressure differential between the surface and the inhabited zone would kill you almost instantly. Instead, you must descend gradually, over a period of days, pausing at intermediate depths to allow your body to adapt.

If your transit method deposits you at the surface of the thermal column, you will need to begin a controlled descent immediately. The Vael likely have protocols for this—way-stations at various depths, temporary air pockets where ascending or descending travelers can rest. Inquire about these before beginning your descent. Do not attempt to reach the cisterns in a single dive.

The descent will take longer than you expect. The warm water feels comfortable, even pleasant, but the accumulating pressure is invisible and patient. You will feel fine until suddenly you don't. Stop frequently. Monitor yourself for signs of pressure-related illness: joint pain, confusion, visual disturbances, numbness. If any of these appear, stop your descent immediately and wait. If they worsen, ascend slowly until they resolve.

Expect the descent to take three to five days, possibly longer. Bring enough food and water for this period, or arrange to receive supplies at way-stations. Sleep may be difficult during the intermediate stages—you will be suspended in water, in dim light, in unfamiliar pressure, possibly alone. This is where your mental preparation becomes essential.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

### Adjusting to Sourceless Light

The light in Vael-Ascending enters from the sides rather than above. It is refracted through the curved boundary where hot fresh water meets cold salt sea. It is green-gold and perpetual, neither day nor night, neither dawn nor dusk. It makes distance difficult to judge and creates no shadows worth the name.

This will affect you more than you expect. Human psychology is deeply tied to light cycles, to the rhythm of day and night, to the way shadows tell us about the shape of things. In Vael-Ascending, you will have none of this. Your circadian rhythm will drift. Your depth perception will falter. You may experience disorientation, mood disturbances, a persistent sense that something is wrong without being able to name what.

Before your visit, practice existing without time cues. Spend extended periods in environments with constant artificial light. Sleep and wake according to internal signals rather than external ones. Learn what your body's natural rhythm feels like when it isn't being entrained by the sun.

More importantly, accept that this disorientation will happen and is not dangerous. The Vael have lived in this light for generations. They have developed their own rhythms, their own ways of marking time. Ask them about these. Learn their systems. Do not try to impose surface-world time on an environment that has no surface.

### The Weight of Silence

Sound travels beautifully through water, but there is almost no sound to travel in Vael-Ascending. The occasional creak of a rope bridge. The muffled thump of a moved vessel. The subliminal hum of the thermal current, felt through bone more than heard through ears. And beneath that, nothing. Silence so complete it becomes a presence.

The Vael speak in whispers and hand-signs. Their music is percussive, transmitted through the structure of the city itself. They have adapted to a world where most of the frequencies human ears evolved to detect simply don't occur.

Before your visit, spend time in silence. Extended silence. Not the relative quiet of a peaceful room but the absolute silence of an anechoic chamber if you can access one, or failing that, deep water, remote caves, anywhere sound goes to die. Learn what your mind does in the absence of auditory input. It will fill the void with its own creations—phantom sounds, remembered conversations, the persistent sense that something is about to make noise. This is normal. Do not let it alarm you.

More practically, learn hand-signs. The Vael have their own system, which you will need to learn from them, but basic gesture-based communication is transferable. Learn to convey yes, no, help, danger, stop, come, wait, I don't understand. These will be your vocabulary until you acquire theirs.

### Living with the Becoming

The city is still becoming. The Vael believe this literally, not metaphorically. They speak to their walls. They apologize when they must cut into clay for repairs. They treat the accumulated structure of their home as something more than object, something with agency, something that is growing toward a form no one can predict.

You do not need to share this belief, but you must respect it absolutely. The Vael's relationship with their city is the foundation of their psychological survival. They live in an environment that would kill them instantly if their infrastructure failed. Their belief that the city is becoming, that their presence contributes to this process, that the accumulation of repair and adaptation has meaning beyond mere survival—this gives them the resilience to exist in an impossible place.

Do not dismiss this belief as superstition. Do not analyze it as a coping mechanism where the Vael can hear you. If you cannot participate sincerely, participate respectfully. When they speak to the walls, be silent. When they apologize for necessary damage, acknowledge their apology. When they express uncertainty about what the city is becoming, do not offer your own theories unless invited.

The city may, in fact, be becoming something. The oldest cisterns have features no one remembers building. The structure has grown beyond its original design. Something is happening here that eight generations of inhabitants have witnessed without fully understanding. Your certainty about what is or isn't possible means nothing in the Suspended Cisterns of Vael-Ascending.

## Interacting with the Vael

### Understanding Their Context

The Vael have never seen birds. They have stories about flying things, but these stories have the quality of myth, creatures too strange to be real. They have never stood on solid ground. They have never experienced true darkness or true brightness. Their entire existence has unfolded within a warm, hazy, green-gold twilight, in clay chambers sealed against an environment that would kill them instantly if they stepped outside without preparation.

You come from elsewhere. Your experiences are incomprehensible to them in ways you cannot fully anticipate. When you speak of land, of sky, of walking, of dry air, of sunlight that casts shadows—you are describing myth. They may believe you. They may not. Either way, you are telling stories of a world as strange to them as their world is to you.

Be patient with their questions. Be honest about your own confusion. Do not pretend to understand things you don't. Do not pretend their way of living is normal to you. The Vael have survived by developing extraordinary sensitivity to their environment; they will detect false comfort immediately and trust you less for having offered it.

### Practical Social Considerations

The Vael prefer small spaces. They are accustomed to confinement, to breathing shared air, to physical proximity that surface-dwellers might find uncomfortable. Do not retreat from closeness. It is not intrusion; it is survival behavior, the preference for spaces that can be sealed quickly, the comfort of knowing others are near if something goes wrong.

Their whispered speech and hand-sign communication serves multiple functions: it conserves air, it doesn't travel far enough to attract attention, it respects the silence that is their constant companion. Match your volume to theirs. Learn to convey meaning with minimal sound.

Food will be unfamiliar. The Vael eat what grows in the vertical forest: fruit-like nodules, the soft cores of kelp stems, possibly cultivated organisms you don't have names for. Some of these will be edible to you. Some will not. Some will be actively dangerous. Ask before eating anything. Describe any dietary restrictions you have, though be aware the Vael may not have alternatives.

### The Patron

Something large moves through the outer reaches of the thermal column. The Vael have never seen it clearly—just pressure waves, bending vegetation, pale shapes disappearing into haze. They call it the Patron. They believe it is waiting for the city to finish becoming.

Do not seek out the Patron. Do not attempt to observe it more clearly than the Vael have managed. Do not ask too many questions about it. The Vael's relationship with this creature is complex, layered with generations of accumulated meaning, and your curiosity is less important than their equilibrium.

If you encounter evidence of the Patron—the pressure wave of its passing, the sudden stillness in the vegetation, the sense of something vast and patient nearby—stay calm. Move slowly. Do not run. Do not make noise. The Patron has never attacked the cisterns. It has never approached them. The Vael believe this is intentional, and they have survived for eight generations by not testing that belief.

## Emergency Preparation

### If the Walls Crack

The cisterns are pressurized air pockets in an environment of pressurized water. The pressure differential is substantial. If a wall cracks—if a seal fails—water will rush in with tremendous force, and the air that keeps you alive will rush out.

This is why the Vael prefer small spaces. Small spaces can be sealed quickly. Know where the emergency seals are in any chamber you occupy. Practice activating them before you need them. Ask the Vael to show you—they will not be offended; they will be relieved that you take this seriously.

If you cannot seal a compromised chamber, evacuate immediately. Move to an adjacent air pocket. Do not wait to see if the crack will stabilize. Do not attempt repairs while water is entering. Get out, seal the connection behind you if possible, and report the damage to local Vael.

### If You Get Lost

The vegetation outside the cisterns is dense, and the light is dim, and distance is hard to judge, and the landmarks you might use for navigation are all floating, shifting, growing. It is possible to become lost outside the inhabited zone.

If this happens, stop moving. Anchor yourself to something stable—a thick kelp stem, a woven structure, anything that isn't going to drift. The thermal column has a current: warm water rising from below. Feel for this current. It will tell you which way is up and which way is down, even if your eyes cannot.

The inhabited zone exists in a specific temperature band—warm but not hot, bright but not bright enough to see the surface. If the water grows noticeably warmer and the light dimmer, you are descending toward the fissures. If the water grows cooler and the light diffuses toward cold blue, you are ascending toward the salt boundary. Neither direction is survivable in the long term. Turn around.

The Vael will search for you if you don't return. They will tap on the structures of their city, sending vibrations through the water that travel farther than sound. Listen for these vibrations. Move toward them if you can identify their direction. If you cannot, stay anchored and wait. They will find you eventually, as long as you haven't moved somewhere they don't expect.

### If Your Air Supply Is Compromised

This is the simplest and most lethal emergency. If you cannot breathe, you die. There is no nuance.

Always know where the nearest air pocket is. Always. The Vael navigate their city with this knowledge as a constant background process. You must learn to do the same. When you enter a new area, identify the air sources. When you swim between cisterns, hold your breath for shorter than you're capable of, leaving a margin for error.

If you find yourself without air and cannot reach a cistern, look for the small emergency caches the Vael maintain throughout the inhabited zone—clay vessels with trapped air, not large enough to enter but large enough to provide a few breaths while you reorient. These are marked, but their markings may not be obvious to outsiders. Ask to learn them before venturing far from the central cisterns.

If there are no emergency caches accessible and you cannot reach a cistern, you are probably going to die. This is not meant to frighten you but to clarify the stakes. The Vael live with this reality constantly. Your preparation should include accepting it.

## What You Will Gain

If you survive your visit—and with proper preparation, you likely will—you will have experienced something few multiverse travelers ever encounter: a human community that has entirely reimagined what it means to be human.

The Vael have no concept of ground. No experience of air as a ubiquitous given rather than a precious resource. No memory of light that casts shadows or darkness that is truly dark. They have built a civilization from clay and fiber and breath, suspended in a thermal column in the middle of an alien sea, and they have done this for eight generations without forgetting that they are still becoming, still learning, still adapting.

You will learn to breathe differently. To see differently. To move in three dimensions rather than two. You will experience silence so profound it becomes a presence and light so sourceless you forget what shadows are. You will live inside a structure that may or may not be alive, surrounded by people who speak to their walls and believe a vast creature is waiting for them to finish.

And when you leave—rising slowly through temperature gradients, decompressing over days, finally breaking the surface into a world the Vael can only imagine—you will carry something with you that cannot be named. The knowledge that human beings can survive anywhere, given enough time and enough patience and enough willingness to become something new.

The city is still becoming.

So, for the duration of your visit, will you.
