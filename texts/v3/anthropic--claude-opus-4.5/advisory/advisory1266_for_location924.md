# Preparing for the Throat of the Mountain

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, you need to sit with the reality of this place. This is not a waterfall you're visiting—it's a vertical ocean inside a self-excavating volcano, inhabited by creatures that swim through falling water, marked by someone who survived here long enough to make fire in a place where fire should be impossible. The mountain is actively unmade. The stone remembers being liquid. The sound will become part of your circulatory system.

You are preparing to enter a location that operates on rules still being written.

---

## Physical Preparation

### Thermal Protection

The water is glacial snowmelt hovering just above freezing. You will be wet constantly—not damp, but *wet*, with spray reaching you even in the shelter. Hypothermia is your most immediate mundane threat.

Bring layered synthetic or wool base layers that retain warmth when soaked. Avoid cotton entirely—it will kill you here. A thin wetsuit layer against your skin may serve you better than traditional cold-weather gear, since you're essentially entering an aquatic environment that happens to be vertical. Your outer layer should be something that can shed water without becoming waterlogged, but understand that nothing will keep you dry. You're managing heat loss, not moisture.

Your hands and feet are critical. Neoprene gloves, thin enough to maintain dexterity but thick enough to preserve function. Waterproof boots with aggressive tread—the basalt will be slick, and you'll be climbing through "wet intestines" to reach the shelf. Consider bringing chemical hand warmers sealed in waterproof pouches, though I'm uncertain how well they'll function in this humidity.

A hood or balaclava to protect your head and ears. Not just from cold—from the sound. The description mentions the sound making teeth hum in their sockets. Prolonged exposure to that level of acoustic pressure can cause physical damage beyond hearing loss.

### Hearing Protection

This deserves its own section. The sound "rewrites the rhythm of your blood." This is not metaphor—sustained exposure to intense low-frequency sound can affect cardiac rhythm, cause nausea, disorientation, and psychological disturbance. 

Bring the best hearing protection you can find, but understand you may not want to use it fully. The description suggests that after long enough, the sound becomes "a kind of silence" in which you can hear something else—a voice, the mountain breathing, the creatures singing. You'll need to make a choice about how much you're willing to let in.

I'd suggest high-quality filtered earplugs that reduce volume without eliminating all sound, plus over-ear protection you can add when the breathing of the water-column intensifies the roar. Give yourself options.

### Vision and Light

Light enters from above but behaves strangely, fragmenting into "billions of silver threads," creating strobing effects as clouds shift. You may experience visual confusion, difficulty tracking movement, possibly migraine or seizure triggers if you're susceptible.

Bring adjustable eye protection—not just sunglasses, but something that can handle rapidly shifting light conditions. Polarized lenses may help cut through the chaos of refracted light. A wide-brimmed waterproof hat can help shade your eyes from the most intense moments when light beams strike the water-column directly.

For your own light: waterproof headlamp, obviously, with multiple brightness settings. But I'd be cautious about using artificial light extensively. You don't know how the swimming creatures respond to it. You don't know what else might be in the passages below. The original inhabitant made fire—whether for warmth, light, or signaling something, you can't know. Observe before you illuminate.

## The Climb to the Shelter

You reach the basalt shelf by climbing through the mountain's "lower passages"—wet interior tunnels that wind up through the structure. This climb took hours. Prepare for:

**Route-finding in the dark and wet.** The passages are not marked (or if they are, the marks may be old, may mean something other than "this way is safe"). Bring waterproof chalk or biodegradable markers if you want to ensure you can find your way back—though consider whether you *should* mark your passage. Someone was here before and left only scratches on the ceiling of the shelter, not a trail. Perhaps there was a reason.

**Unstable stone.** The rock is young, still settling. Great slabs have calved away. The micro-earthquakes are constant. Move carefully, test handholds before committing weight, never assume a surface that held moments ago will hold now.

**Physical endurance in cold water.** You'll be climbing while soaked, while cold, while the sound grows louder as you ascend toward the main shaft. Pace yourself. Bring high-calorie foods sealed in waterproof containers—you'll burn enormous amounts of energy just maintaining body temperature.

**The psychological weight of descent-by-ascent.** You're climbing up, but you're also going *deeper in*—deeper into the mountain's throat, closer to the place where the rules change. The cognitive dissonance may be disorienting. Keep track of your own mental state.

## Supplies for the Shelter

### Duration

How long do you intend to stay? The previous inhabitant stayed long enough to make fire, to eat, to carve patterns on the ceiling. Long enough to develop a relationship with this place. You could treat this as a brief observation—a few hours pressed against the warm-remembering stone—or you could try to understand the rhythms. The water-column breathes. The light shifts. The creatures appear rarely, "minutes, sometimes an hour" between sightings.

If you want to see one clearly, "just once, well enough to carry its shape back to the world below," you may need to stay for a long time.

### Fire

The previous inhabitant made fire here. You don't know how. The text speculates that conditions may vary, that the water-column "wavers, breathes, allows moments of relative dryness." 

Bring fire-starting materials sealed against moisture—waterproof matches, a ferrocerium rod, possibly a small amount of fuel that burns even when wet. But don't assume fire is achievable. Don't assume the previous inhabitant's success is replicable. They may have had knowledge, tools, or timing you don't possess.

If you cannot make fire, you need another heat source. Chemical warmers. A small camping stove designed for extreme conditions. Your own metabolic heat, conserved carefully.

### Food and Water

Ironic, in a place made of falling water, but you need drinking water. The falling column is glacial snowmelt—clean, probably safe—but catching it means exposing yourself to the full force of the spray, probably hypothermia-inducing in anything but the briefest exposure. Can you set up a catchment system that extends into the spray zone while you remain sheltered? Perhaps. Experiment carefully.

Bring water regardless. Bring more than you think you need. Dehydration in cold wet environments is counterintuitively common—you don't feel thirst the same way, but you're still losing moisture, and the effort of staying warm burns through your reserves.

For food: calorie-dense, no preparation required, sealed against water. Nuts, dried meat, energy bars, chocolate. Whatever you can eat with shaking hands in the dark while the mountain trembles.

## Observing the Creatures

They are the reason you might stay. "Translucent, or nearly so. Tubes. Ribbons. Briefly spheres." They swim through falling water—up, across, in ways that suggest the vertical river is genuinely their ocean.

### Patience

You cannot summon them. They appear on their own schedule, "minutes, sometimes an hour" between sightings. You will need to watch the water-column for extended periods, in the cold, in the noise, in the strobing light. This is a test of endurance as much as attention.

### Recording

Can you photograph them? Film them? The light conditions are extreme and rapidly shifting. Any camera would need to be waterproofed beyond normal standards, and the results might be useless—motion blur, exposure chaos, the creatures' translucence making them invisible in captured images.

Consider whether you need to *record* them or whether you need to *see* them. The text suggests the goal is to see one clearly enough "to carry its shape back to the world below." That might be a matter of memory and description rather than documentation. Perhaps you should bring a sketchbook in a waterproof case, and draw what you see afterward, relying on your own perception rather than technology that might not function here.

### Interaction

One of them turned toward you, "though it has nothing you could call a face." It held position against the fall for three heartbeats. It was aware of you.

Do not assume you understand what this means. Do not assume they are harmless because they appear curious. Do not assume they are dangerous because they are strange. They are organisms adapted to an environment you cannot survive in unaided. Their relationship to you is unknown.

I would not recommend attempting to touch the water-column while they are present. I would not recommend shining light at them, making loud noises to attract them, or leaving food offerings. Observe. Wait. If they approach you, stay still. If they enter your shelter—which seems unlikely given their apparent dependence on the falling water, but the rules here are "still forming"—remain calm and give them space to leave.

## The Scratched Marks

Circles within circles. Lines that might be tallies. Made by someone who survived here, who made fire here, who ate here and marked time or meaning into the stone.

You want to know what they mean. You may never know. The person who made them is gone—you don't know how long ago, you don't know whether they left voluntarily, you don't know if the marks are a record, a warning, a prayer, or simply the product of isolation and the need to make meaning in a place that resists it.

Bring paper to copy the marks. Not tracing paper—too fragile when wet—but something you can sketch on quickly, capturing the patterns before the spray obscures your vision again. Photograph them if your equipment survives the conditions.

Do not add your own marks. Not yet. Not until you understand more. The previous inhabitant left almost nothing behind—a fire-stain, a bone fragment, the scratches. This may have been deliberate minimalism, or desperation, or respect for the place. Until you know which, tread lightly.

## The Shell

"Something that spiraled in ways that didn't quite make sense, with an opening that faced *inward*."

You left it where it was. Good. Continue to leave it where it is.

Objects that don't make geometric sense, in locations where the rules are "still forming," are not for casual collection. The shell may be a remnant of something that lived here before the water came, or during an earlier phase of the water's presence. It may be related to the swimming creatures. It may be something else entirely.

Observe it. Note its location. Do not disturb it. If you see other such objects, do not disturb them either.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the hardest part to prepare for, and the most important.

### The Sound

You will become the sound. It will rewrite you. This is not comfortable, especially at first. Some people panic. Some people find a strange peace. You don't know which you'll be until you're there.

Before you go, practice stillness in loud environments. Sit near heavy machinery, near waterfalls, near anything that produces sustained overwhelming noise. Learn what your mind does when it cannot escape a sound. Learn whether you can find the silence inside it, or whether you fight and fight until you exhaust yourself.

If you're the fighting type, this may not be the place for you. Or it may be exactly the place for you—the place that teaches you to stop. But the lesson might be brutal.

### The Waiting

You are going to a place where "you wait." Not as a failure mode, but as the primary activity. You wait for the light to shift. You wait for the creatures to appear. You wait to understand the rhythms. You wait to hear what's in the gaps between the thunder.

Modern human life does not train us for this. Practice waiting before you go. Practice sitting with nothing to do, nowhere to be, no input or stimulation, for hours at a time. Practice the kind of waiting that isn't bored, isn't impatient, isn't looking for the waiting to end—the kind of waiting that is a form of presence.

### The Isolation

The previous inhabitant was alone. You will be alone, unless you bring others—and bringing others into a shelter twelve feet deep, six feet high at maximum, with enough cold and wet and noise to challenge even one person's survival, seems unwise.

Know your relationship to solitude before you go. Know whether it feeds you or depletes you. Know how long you can sustain it before something in you starts to erode.

### The Strangeness

Creatures that swim through falling water. A shell that opens inward. A mountain being unmade from within. The world has rules here, but they are strange rules, still forming.

You must be able to tolerate strangeness without needing to resolve it into familiarity. The creatures are not fish—you're told this explicitly. Don't force them into fish-shaped understanding. The marks are not writing—don't force them into language. The water-column is not a waterfall—don't frame it that way, or you'll miss what it actually is.

Practice encountering the unknown and letting it stay unknown. Practice the humility of "I don't have words for this." Practice letting categories blur at the edges. This will serve you better than any specific piece of equipment.

## What You Might Bring Back

If you survive—and survival is not guaranteed, given the hypothermia risk, the unstable stone, the micro-earthquakes, the sheer physical and psychological demands of this place—what will you bring back?

Maybe photographs that don't capture what you saw. Maybe sketches of the marks that raise more questions than answers. Maybe a description of the creatures that no one believes, because the words aren't sufficient.

Or maybe something harder to name. A new relationship with sound. A new capacity for patience. A memory of pressing your back against stone that remembers being liquid, in a place where the sky pours into the earth, watching something translucent hold position against the fall for three heartbeats before letting the water take it down and away.

The value of visiting the Throat of the Mountain may not be extractable. It may not be transferable. It may live only in you, in the way you're different afterward, in the silence you learned to hear beneath the roar.

---

## Final Recommendations

Go in the warmer season, if this place has seasons. Go after training in cold-water survival, in caving, in extended wilderness solitude. Go with backup plans for extraction if you become incapacitated. Go with someone who knows where you are and when to expect you back, even if they cannot follow you inside.

Go with respect. This mountain is dying—being unmade from within—but it is not yet dead. The creatures are living there. Someone else lived there once. You are entering a place that is still becoming itself, and your presence will become part of what it is becoming.

Go with questions rather than answers. Go to witness rather than to conquer. Go because you want to see one of the creatures clearly, just once. Go because you want to understand the scratched circles. Go because you heard there was a place where the rules are strange and still forming, and you wanted to feel what that was like.

And when you leave—through the wet passages, down through the mountain's intestines, out to the slopes where things grow in ways you understand—carry what you've seen carefully. Name it precisely or not at all. Let it change you if it's going to change you.

The Throat of the Mountain will still be there, still swallowing clouds, still being carved deeper. The creatures will still swim their impossible vertical ocean. The marks on the ceiling will still wait for someone else to wonder what they mean.

You are not the first visitor. You will not be the last.

Go well.
