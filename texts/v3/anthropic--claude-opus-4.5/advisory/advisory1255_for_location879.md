# Preparing for the Frozen Market Beneath the Glacier

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with the description and understand the nature of this place. This is not a destination you visit and leave unchanged. The Frozen Market exists in a temporal pocket—not frozen in time, but operating on a timescale so elongated that your presence there will feel like dropping a stone into still water. You will create ripples simply by being something new in a place where nothing new has arrived for generations beyond counting.

The market is not hostile. But it is complete. It has no need of you, no space designed for you, no role waiting for you to fill it. You must decide before you go whether you intend to observe or participate, and you must understand that participation may mean absorption.

## Physical Preparation

### Thermal Management

The cold is described as profound but survivable, with body heat from the crowd providing marginal warmth. Do not overdress. This seems counterintuitive, but the danger here is moisture accumulation from exertion followed by rapid cooling. You want:

- A base layer of fine merino wool or silk—something that wicks moisture and retains warmth when damp. Synthetic materials may mark you as profoundly alien in ways that attract unwanted attention.
- A middle layer you can remove if the press of bodies grows too warm. Wool felt would be ideal and would help you blend with the locals.
- An outer layer of something wind-resistant for the journey to and from the crack, which you will remove once inside.
- No cotton. Cotton kills in cold, wet environments. It absorbs moisture, loses all insulating capacity, and will leech heat from your body.

Your hands and face will be exposed for trade and communication. Accept that frost will form on your eyelashes. Bring a small container of rendered animal fat—tallow or lanolin—to protect your lips and the skin around your eyes from cracking. This also serves as a trade good and a gesture of cultural alignment.

### Managing the Crack

The entrance is shoulder-width, requiring you to slide through sideways. This means:

- Nothing on your back wider than your body. No frame packs.
- Soft-sided containers worn at your front or distributed in pockets.
- A belt pouch system rather than a backpack.
- Removing your outer layer and pushing it through ahead of you, then pulling it after.

Consider bringing a small amount of rope or strong cord to lower items through if the passage has any vertical component. The description suggests it "opens into" the chamber, implying possible descent.

### Hydration and Food

The water is available but communal—drawn from glacial melt collected in clay channels, drunk from shared ladles. You will need to decide whether to drink from these sources. The water is described as tasting of minerals and time, suggesting high mineral content and possible biological activity from the ecosystem that has developed. If you have a sensitive digestive system, bring water purification tablets, though using them openly might cause offense. Better to bring a small container and purify privately, or simply accept the local water as part of the experience and prepare for possible gastric adjustment afterward.

Food exists in the market—preserved meats packed in rendered fat—but may not align with your dietary needs or restrictions. Bring dense, compact nutrition that doesn't require preparation: hard cheeses if you can eat dairy, dried meats if you can eat meat, dense nut-and-fat preparations if you need plant-based options. All of these will also function as trade goods.

## Trade Goods and Currency

The market operates on bone tokens and direct barter. You cannot bring currency from other worlds and expect it to function. You must bring objects of genuine utility or interest.

### High-Value Items to Consider

**Metal tools or implements**, if the market lacks metallurgy. A single steel needle could be worth more than anything else you carry—but also could be so valuable as to be suspicious, marking you as something other than a lost traveler. Small metal items are better: fish hooks, small scissors, pins.

**Pigments or dyes**. The description mentions undyed wool that has yellowed with age. Color may be precious here. Bring small quantities of stable, non-toxic pigments in concentrated form. Indigo, ochre, carmine—colors that come from the natural world and won't seem impossibly alien.

**Fine thread or cordage** of unusual strength or fiber. Spider silk equivalents, if you can source them. The market values sinew thread and undyed wool, so anything that outperforms these materials in strength or fineness would be noteworthy.

**Seeds or spores** are risky. The ecosystem is closed and has been for uncountable generations. Introducing new organisms could be catastrophic or could be the greatest gift anyone has ever brought. Know your own ethics before deciding.

**Stories and songs** may have value, but the market's relationship with sound is complicated. They speak in murmurs because loud sound risks fracturing the ice above. A whispered story, told leaning close to a merchant's ear, might be worth something. A song might be impossible to deliver.

### What to Accept in Trade

You want to leave with something, both as proof of your experience and as a connection to this place. The bone needle mentioned in the description is ideal—small, portable, functional, and worn smooth by ten thousand hands. It carries the weight of the market's history.

Consider also:
- Pressed moss arrangements that might be writing—even if you cannot read them, they are artifacts of a unique culture
- Moth-wing images, fragile but extraordinary
- Small quantities of preserved food, if you can transport them safely
- Ice-cores sold as medicine or oracle—though these will melt unless you have extraordinary cold-storage capacity
- Figurines carved from the teeth of unknown animals

Do not acquire the moss or lichen itself. Do not acquire living creatures from the water channels. These belong to the ecosystem and removing them may be both ecologically damaging and culturally offensive.

## Behavioral Preparation

### Sound Discipline

Practice being quiet. Not silent—silence is unnatural and may make others uncomfortable—but quiet. Learn to murmur. Learn to communicate with gestures, expressions, small movements of the hands. The market has developed its own communication system adapted to the acoustic constraints of the space, and you will need to learn it by observation and imitation.

Before you enter, spend time in quiet spaces. Libraries. Monasteries. Places where soft speech is the norm. Retrain your voice to sit in your throat rather than project from your chest.

### Physical Proximity

This is not a market where personal space exists. You will be pressed against strangers. Items will be passed over your head and across your body. You may need to remain wedged in a gap no wider than your body for extended periods.

If you have difficulty with crowds or close physical contact, this is not a place for you. If you can adapt, prepare by spending time in densely packed spaces—crowded transit, busy markets in your own world, standing-room-only events. Learn to relax into compression rather than fighting it.

### Time Perception

Nothing changes in the Frozen Market. The same goods circulate. The same families hold the same stalls. The light is constant. There is no day or night, no season, no weather.

You will lose track of time. This is not a metaphor or a warning about distraction—your sense of duration will become unreliable within hours. Bring a timing device that does not rely on you checking it, something that can vibrate against your skin at set intervals to remind you how long you have been there. Decide before you enter how long you intend to stay and set your device accordingly.

If you do not do this, you may look up and discover that what felt like an afternoon was actually a week. Or a month. The description makes clear that you *can* stay, can grow moss-furred and wide-eyed, can forget the concept of weather. The market will not expel you. It will simply absorb you, slowly, thread by thread, until you are no longer a visitor but a permanent fixture.

### Language and Communication

The market has its own language, presumably developed over the countless generations of isolation. You will not speak it. You may be able to learn fragments of it during your visit, enough to conduct simple trades, but true fluency would require the kind of extended stay that risks absorption.

Bring a small notebook and writing implement. Visual communication—drawing what you want, sketching quantities, illustrating concepts—may serve you better than attempting verbal negotiation. Keep your drawings simple and clear. Offer the notebook and implement themselves as trade goods if appropriate; writing technology may be novel here.

### The Moss-Covered Merchants

The description mentions merchants who have sat so long in their stalls that they have disappeared beneath living mounds of moss, "visible only as shapes beneath the green." These individuals are still present, still alive in some sense, still part of the market's economy.

Do not assume they are dead or comatose. Do not attempt to clear the moss from them. Do not treat them as obstacles or scenery. They may still be trading, in their own way, on their own timescale. Approach them with the same respect you would offer any other merchant, even if they cannot respond in ways you recognize.

### The Children

The merchants' children keep translucent shrimp-creatures as pets in jars of ice-melt. Children are often the most curious about strangers and may approach you first.

Be gentle. Be patient. Do not offer them items from your world without first consulting their parents—gift-giving customs vary, and what you intend as friendly could be seen as attempted corruption or debt-creation. Let the children observe you. Answer their whispered questions as best you can. They may become your translators and guides if you earn their trust.

### The Things in the Moss

You will see the vegetation ripple sometimes, something tunneling beneath, but no one seems alarmed. Take your cue from the locals. These creatures are part of the ecosystem, tolerated or ignored. Do not investigate them, do not attempt to uncover them, do not react with visible fear or disgust. They are simply part of what the market is.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Weight of Permanence

This is the hardest thing to prepare for. The Frozen Market is not merely old—it is permanent in a way that most places are not. It has always been a market. It will always be a market. The ice may close in a thousand years, but a thousand years is not soon.

You are temporary. Your whole civilization is temporary. Everything you know and love and fear exists in the blink of an eye compared to the market's duration.

Some travelers find this comforting—to witness something that endures, something that will outlast all their concerns. Others find it annihilating, a reminder of their own insignificance that persists long after they leave.

Know yourself. Know how you respond to confrontations with the eternal. If you have a tendency toward existential despair, prepare coping strategies before you enter. If you find transcendence in permanence, prepare to feel more alive than you ever have.

### The Temptation to Stay

The market offers something that almost nothing else in the multiverse can offer: stability. Certainty. A role that never changes, a place that never shifts, a community that has endured beyond memory and will endure beyond imagining.

You can stay. The description is explicit about this. You can wedge yourself into a gap and begin to trade. You can grow moss-furred and wide-eyed. You can forget the concept of weather.

Decide before you enter whether you are at risk of this temptation. If your life outside is unstable, chaotic, painful, or meaningless, the market's offer may be more seductive than you expect. This is not necessarily wrong—perhaps staying is the right choice for some travelers—but it should be a choice, not a gradual surrender to entropy.

If you intend to leave, set your timed reminder. Commit to honoring it. Tell yourself, before you enter, that when the vibration comes, you will begin moving toward the crack, no matter how comfortable you have become, no matter how natural the market feels, no matter how alien the outside world seems in your memory.

### The Needle in Your Pocket

You will leave with something. The description ends with a bone needle found in the pocket, "worn smooth by ten thousand hands before yours."

This object will be your proof and your anchor. It will remind you that the market was real. It will also carry the market's weight—all those hands, all those generations, all that time compressed into a single small object.

Be prepared for the needle to change your relationship with your own world. Things will seem more temporary, more fragile, more rushed. You will find yourself handling objects differently, wondering how long they will last, how many hands will hold them. The needle will be a splinter of the eternal embedded in your mortal life.

Some travelers find this enriching. Others find it unbearable. Know which you are likely to be.

## The Exit

When you leave—if you leave—the crack will still be there. The passage will still be shoulder-width. The wind outside will still be screaming.

You will emerge into a world of change, of weather, of time that moves at a perceptible rate. The contrast will be disorienting. Your eyes will struggle with variable light after the constant glacier-glow. Your ears will ring with the wind after the hush. Your body will feel loose and unmoored after the press of the crowd.

Give yourself time to readjust. Find shelter from the wind. Eat something warm. Speak aloud, just to hear your own voice at full volume again.

And check your pocket for the needle. Turn it over in your fingers. Feel the smoothness worn by ten thousand hands.

Then begin the journey back to whatever you call home, carrying a piece of permanence with you, forever.
