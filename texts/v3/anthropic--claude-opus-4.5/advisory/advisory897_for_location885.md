# Preparing for the Singing Pillars of Koreth-Vastness

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with this truth: you are preparing to enter a place that will assault the most fundamental assumptions your body makes about the world. You have lived your entire life in an environment where sound is informational, where silence exists, where you can speak and be heard, where the ground stays still beneath your feet. Koreth-Vastness will strip all of this from you. This is not a place you visit casually. This is a place you prepare for the way you might prepare for a vision quest, a surgical procedure, or a profound grief—with the understanding that you will not be quite the same person when you emerge.

The altitude alone would make this a serious undertaking. The thin air of the lower stratosphere means your blood will struggle to carry oxygen. But the altitude is almost incidental compared to the sound. The sound is the thing. The sound is everything.

---

## Physical Preparation: The Body as Instrument

### Cardiovascular and Altitude Conditioning

Begin training at least six months before your journey. Your cardiovascular system needs to operate efficiently on significantly less oxygen than it's accustomed to.

**Altitude simulation:** If you have access to altitude training masks or hypoxic tents, use them. Sleep at simulated altitude for increasingly long periods. Your body needs time to increase red blood cell production, and this cannot be rushed without risking dangerous complications.

**Cardiovascular endurance:** Long, slow distance training—hiking, cycling, swimming—will serve you better than high-intensity work. You need a heart that can maintain steady output for hours under stress, not one optimized for brief explosive efforts. Aim to be comfortable hiking for eight hours at moderate elevation before you attempt the plateau.

**Core and balance training:** The constant subsonic vibration will challenge your vestibular system and your ability to stay upright. Exercises that improve proprioception—standing on unstable surfaces, yoga, slacklining—will help your body adapt more quickly to ground that never quite stays still.

### Pressure Acclimatization

Your sinuses, inner ears, and other air-filled cavities will experience significant stress from both the altitude and the acoustic environment.

**Practice pressure equalization:** Learn proper techniques for clearing your ears. The Valsalva maneuver is a start, but also learn the Frenzel and Toynbee techniques. You'll need to equalize frequently as pressure waves from the deeper bass notes pass through you.

**See an ear specialist:** Have your eardrums examined for any weaknesses or perforations. Even small vulnerabilities could rupture under the acoustic conditions. If you have any history of ear infections, vertigo, or hearing problems, discuss this journey seriously with a medical professional.

**Dental check:** Air pockets in teeth can cause excruciating pain under pressure changes. Have any cavities filled and any questionable teeth examined before departure.

### Fasting and Digestive Preparation

The subsonic frequencies will agitate your internal organs. Travelers report nausea, cramping, and involuntary bowel movements in the first days on the plateau.

**Clean your diet** in the weeks before arrival. Reduce processed foods, alcohol, and anything that tends to cause gas or digestive upset. Arrive with a settled stomach.

**Learn to eat small amounts frequently** rather than large meals. Your digestion will be disrupted; don't give it more than it can handle.

**Bring digestive aids:** Ginger for nausea, activated charcoal for gas, electrolyte supplements for the dehydration that comes with altitude and wind exposure.

## Hearing Protection: The Most Critical Equipment

This is not optional. This is not a suggestion. Unprotected exposure to the acoustic environment of Koreth-Vastness will cause permanent hearing damage within hours and complete deafness within days. The indigenous people have adapted over generations; you have not.

### Layered Protection

You need multiple layers of hearing protection designed to work together:

**Custom-molded earplugs** as your base layer. Have these professionally fitted. They should seal completely and attenuate across all frequencies as much as physically possible. Standard foam earplugs are insufficient—they don't block low frequencies effectively, and the bass notes here will shake your skull regardless.

**Over-ear protection** as your second layer. Industrial-grade earmuffs rated for the loudest environments. These go over your earplugs.

**Bone conduction dampening:** This is the hard part. Sound this powerful doesn't just enter through your ear canals—it vibrates through your skull directly into your cochlea. Some travelers report success with padded helmets or balaclavas lined with dense, vibration-absorbing material. Others use medical-grade bone conduction blocking devices developed for industrial applications. Research what's available in your home world.

**Active noise cancellation:** If you have access to technology that can generate counter-frequencies, this can help with some of the more predictable tones. However, the chaotic nature of the wind means this will only provide partial relief during stable moments.

### Accepting Imperfect Protection

Even with the best equipment, you will hear the pillars. The deepest bass notes cannot be stopped by anything you can reasonably wear—they will pass through your body as vibration, and you will perceive them as sound even though they never entered your ears. You are not preparing to experience silence on the plateau; you are preparing to experience *survivable* sound. There is a significant difference.

## Equipment for Survival

### Shelter

You will not survive the plateau without shelter, and you cannot carry shelter adequate to protect you across the whole plateau. Your strategy must involve moving between protected locations.

**Emergency bivouac:** Carry a small, extremely wind-resistant emergency shelter. This isn't for sleeping—it's for surviving if you're caught far from proper shelter when conditions worsen. It should be able to be deployed quickly, anchored securely to stone, and should incorporate some sound-dampening properties.

**Identify the indigenous shelters:** Before venturing far onto the plateau, learn where the wind-shadow pockets are. The people who live here have spent generations mapping acoustically quiet zones. Their hospitality practices are unknown to me, but bringing appropriate trade goods (more on this below) and approaching with obvious respect may earn you guidance.

**Never sleep in the open.** The combination of acoustic exposure, temperature swings, and wind will kill you or drive you mad.

### Clothing

**Wind resistance is paramount.** Every seam, every gap, every loose edge will catch the wind and be pulled at relentlessly. Your clothing should fit close to your body without restricting movement. Think of what climbers wear in exposed mountain conditions, but optimized even further for wind.

**UV protection:** The thin air filters almost none of the sun's ultraviolet radiation. Cover all skin. Your exposed face will need heavy sunscreen reapplied constantly, as the wind will strip it away.

**Layering:** Temperature swings between sun and shadow are extreme. You need to be able to add and remove insulation quickly while moving.

**Secure everything:** Nothing you bring should be capable of blowing away. Straps, clips, and tethers on every item. Practice handling all your gear with thick gloves on—you'll be wearing them constantly.

**Membrane eye protection:** Your eyes will be under constant assault from dust, crystal particles, and the sheer drying force of the wind. Goggles that seal completely are essential. Bring multiple pairs and lens options for different light conditions.

### Navigation

**Traditional compasses** should work, but verify this with travelers who've returned. Some mineral compositions can interfere with magnetic readings.

**Landmarks will be confusing.** The pillars all look similar, and the light plays tricks. If you have access to any form of positioning technology that works in this multiverse location, bring it. If not, you must learn to navigate by the patterns of sound—certain pillar groupings produce recognizable chords.

**Mark your path.** The indigenous people may have feelings about this; observe what they do. But you need some way to retrace your steps. The labyrinthine terrain can disorient quickly.

### Water

**The intermittent rains are your lifeline.** Bring water collection gear—broad, lightweight catchment surfaces that can be deployed quickly when clouds release. The rain comes suddenly and ends abruptly; missing a collection opportunity could leave you dangerously short.

**Carry enough to survive between rains.** This may mean several liters per day, more in the drying wind. Your pack weight will be significant.

**The mineral pools are not safe to drink** without treatment. The crystal deposits indicate high mineral concentrations that could damage your kidneys. Bring filtration adequate to remove dissolved minerals, not just particulates and pathogens.

### Food

**Caloric needs will be extreme.** Cold, altitude, and the constant energy expenditure of moving in wind and recovering from vibration stress will burn through your reserves quickly. Bring dense, nutritious foods that don't require preparation.

**The indigenous people eat the dried algae and trade for lowland goods.** Learn what they might value in exchange for food or hospitality. The algae is apparently nutritious and safe for outsiders to consume.

**Don't expect to hunt or forage** unless you've been taught specifically what's safe. The ecology here is specialized and unfamiliar.

## Communication and Social Navigation

### Learning the Gesture Language

**This may be the most important preparation you do.** The indigenous people have abandoned spoken language for movement and touch. You cannot communicate with them otherwise—and more importantly, you cannot communicate with yourself otherwise. Your own voice will be inaudible to you much of the time.

Seek out other travelers who have returned. Ask if any of them learned the gesture system. Even a basic vocabulary could save your life—ways to signal distress, to ask for shelter, to express gratitude, to indicate that you intend no harm.

If no one can teach you, then at minimum develop your own system of unambiguous hand signals with any traveling companions. Agree on clear signs for danger, stop, follow me, I need help, I'm okay, and water.

### Traveling Alone vs. With Companions

**I strongly recommend against traveling alone** to Koreth-Vastness. The isolation of being unable to speak, combined with the sensory assault and physical dangers, could break you psychologically in ways you won't anticipate. Having someone you trust—someone who can communicate with you through touch, who can notice if you're becoming disoriented or unwell, who can help if you're injured—dramatically improves your chances.

That said, choose your companion carefully. This journey will strain any relationship. You will be exhausted, in pain, potentially frightened, unable to discuss what you're feeling. Choose someone whose presence you find grounding even in silence.

### Approaching the Indigenous People

**You are entering their home.** They have developed a complete culture adapted to a place that will be merely survivable for you, at best. Approach with humility.

Observe before acting. Watch how they move through the space, how they interact with each other, how they relate to the pillars and the wind. They know things about this place you cannot learn from any preparation—they've internalized it into their bodies over lifetimes.

**Bring trade goods** that cannot be obtained on the plateau. Metal tools, if they use them. Fabric, if theirs is limited. Medicine, though learn first what they need. Something beautiful from your world that might bring them joy—though be thoughtful; they live in a place of extraordinary terrible beauty already.

**Do not assume they want you there.** They may have good reasons for being wary of outsiders. If you sense you're unwelcome, leave. There may be places on the plateau where visitors are permitted and others where they're not.

## Psychological Preparation: The Mind Under Siege

### Understanding Acoustic Trauma

Sound at the intensities and frequencies present on the plateau doesn't just affect your ears—it affects your brain directly. You may experience:

- **Disorientation and vertigo** from your vestibular system being continuously stimulated
- **Difficulty thinking clearly** as your auditory processing consumes cognitive resources
- **Mood disturbances:** anxiety, depression, euphoria, rage, all arising without clear cause
- **Intrusive thoughts** as your pattern-seeking brain tries to find meaning in the noise
- **Dissociation** as your mind attempts to protect itself by disconnecting from your body
- **Hallucinations** in extreme cases, particularly if you become sleep-deprived

Know that these are normal responses to abnormal conditions. They don't mean you're losing your mind—they mean your mind is responding to input it was never designed to handle. Having a framework to understand this can help you weather it.

### The Silence That Isn't

**You will discover how much of your identity depends on hearing yourself.** Your inner monologue, your ability to subvocalize, your sense of being a coherent self speaking words in your own head—all of this will be disrupted. Some travelers report that the wall of external sound eventually silences their internal narration entirely, leaving a strange blank awareness they find either peaceful or terrifying.

Practice meditation before you go. Become comfortable with states of mind that don't depend on verbal thought. Learn to observe your experience without needing to narrate it.

### The Temptation of Meaning

**The patterns in the pillar-songs are real, but they may not be for you.**

The indigenous people have built a mythology around the plateau as a place of cosmic argument, of a world debating itself. You will be tempted to find meaning in the chords you hear, to believe you're receiving messages, to feel certain that the music is communicating something important directly to you.

Maybe it is. I can't rule that out. But I can tell you that your brain, stressed and altered by the environment, will impose meaning on noise regardless of whether meaning is there. You may feel moments of transcendent insight, absolute certainty that you've understood something profound. This may be real. It may also be your exhausted, acoustically-battered brain generating compelling hallucinations.

Carry a way to record your thoughts—writing, if you can manage it with gloved hands in the wind, or drawings, or some other method. When you leave the plateau, you can examine what you thought you learned and see whether it survives the return to ordinary consciousness.

### The Harmony Moments

Prepare yourself for the possibility that you will experience the brief harmonies—the moments when the chaos aligns and the plateau sings something that people describe as transcendent.

There is no way to prepare for what this will feel like. But you can prepare for its aftermath. People report being fundamentally changed by these moments. They describe grief when the harmony ends. They describe an inability to fully return to ordinary life. They describe feeling as though they've heard something they were always meant to hear, and everything else is now just noise.

This may or may not happen to you. If it does, understand that the grief is natural. The moment passed; you cannot hold it. But you can carry the fact that it occurred, that such things are possible, that you were present when the world said something beautiful.

## Practical Itinerary Suggestions

### Acclimatization Approach

Don't ascend to the plateau directly if there are lower approaches. Spend several days at intermediate altitudes if possible. The peoples of the lowlands who trade with the plateau's inhabitants may have guidance about the safest routes up and the best places to stop along the way.

### Initial Days on the Plateau

Plan to spend your first days in or near one of the acoustically quieted shelters. You will be exhausted and possibly ill. The subsonic vibrations will disturb your sleep even in the quiet zones. Don't push yourself to explore until you've stabilized.

Use this time to observe. Watch the wind patterns. Begin to learn the rhythms of the plateau. Let your body start its adaptation.

### Venturing Out

Move during the calmer periods if you can learn to predict them. The indigenous people will know when conditions are relatively stable. Never travel so far that you can't reach shelter if conditions deteriorate.

Have a purpose, but hold it loosely. Why are you here? To experience the harmony moments? To study the ecology? To learn from the people who live here? To prove something to yourself? Know your intention, but be prepared to let the plateau teach you something you didn't come to learn.

### Departure

**Have a plan for leaving** that doesn't depend on conditions you can't control. Know where the descent routes are. Don't wait until you're completely depleted to begin your exit.

Give yourself time after leaving to decompress. The silence of the lowlands may feel strange, even uncomfortable. Your ears may ring. Your body may continue to vibrate with phantom sensations. Your thoughts may be quieter than you're used to. Be gentle with yourself. You've been somewhere that humans weren't designed to go.

---

## Final Thoughts

The Singing Pillars of Koreth-Vastness exist at the edge of what we can experience without being destroyed by it. They offer something that cannot be found anywhere else—a place where the world itself is making sound, where geology has become music, where chaos flirts constantly with pattern and meaning.

You may find something sacred there. You may find only suffering. Likely both.

Go prepared. Go humbly. Go knowing you're not entitled to survive this, that the plateau owes you nothing, that every moment you spend there is borrowed from an environment that doesn't care whether you exist.

And if you hear the harmony—if you're present for one of those moments when the thousand voices briefly speak as one—know that you've witnessed something true. Hold it. Let it change you.

Then come home, and tell the rest of us what the world was saying.
