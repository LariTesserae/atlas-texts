# Preparing for the Engine That Drowned Itself

## Understanding What You're Approaching

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Notice how it makes you feel—not just intellectually curious, but in your body. That subtle unease, that sense of wrongness-that-isn't-quite-wrong? That's useful data. This location doesn't just break physical laws; it breaks *categories*. Things that should be distinct blur together: machine and organism, cargo and medium, cause and effect. Your preparation must account for the possibility that *you* might blur too.

This isn't a hostile environment in the conventional sense. Nothing here is described as attacking visitors. The danger is more insidious: transformation without consent, meaning without understanding, participation in a process you can't comprehend. The crabs on their conveyor loops don't seem distressed. That should worry you more than if they did.

## Physical Preparation

### Thermal Regulation

The water fluctuates between roughly 8-12°C, synchronized with the machinery's rhythms rather than any natural pattern. This is survivable but demanding.

**Recommended approach:**
- A quality exposure suit rated for extended cold-water immersion—5-7mm neoprene minimum, though you might consider a drysuit with thermal undergarments
- The temperature shifts will work against your body's adaptation; just as you adjust to the cold, the piston compresses and warms things. Then it cools again. This cycling may be more exhausting than steady cold.
- Bring chemical heat packs, but position them where you can access them quickly. You may need warmth during the "idle" phases.
- Consider that whatever the factory "excretes" into the water may affect thermal properties in ways your equipment wasn't designed for. Monitor your suit's integrity.

### Pressure Considerations

The text describes being "hundreds of meters down" in a space that has "confused itself about its depth." This creates genuine uncertainty:

**The question you cannot answer in advance:** Does your body experience the pressure of the true depth, or the pressure of the remembered shallowness?

Prepare for both possibilities:
- Bring diving equipment rated for deep technical diving (mixed gas, redundant systems, staged decompression capability)
- But also prepare for the possibility that none of it will be necessary—that the space's confusion extends to how it treats your tissues
- The safest assumption: your body exists at the *real* depth until you cross the boundary, then exists in whatever state the space insists upon. This means your entry and exit are the most dangerous moments. Consider a slow, staged approach to the shimmer-boundary, monitoring your body's responses before full commitment.

### The Light Problem

Surface-quality illumination at crushing depth. This has practical implications you might not immediately consider:

- Your eyes will be adapted for seeing. This is *unusual* for deep diving and may create psychological disorientation—your training tells you it should be dark, but it isn't.
- Bring a powerful dive light anyway. If you need to leave quickly and the space's "memory" of being shallow fails, you'll need it on the other side of the boundary.
- Consider polarized lenses. The "diffuse and grey-white and directionless" quality might create subtle visual strain over time.
- The light has no source. Do not spend too long trying to find one. This way lies a particular kind of madness.

## Equipment Beyond Survival

### Documentation Tools

You will want to record what you see. More importantly, you will want to *verify* what you see after you leave.

- Waterproof camera systems, yes—but bring *redundant* ones. Film-based if you can source it, alongside digital. If the space affects physics, it may affect electronics differently than chemistry.
- Audio recording is essential. The text emphasizes that sound behaves wrongly here—sharp where it should blur, carrying details that physics wouldn't normally permit. Capture this. Having proof later may help you trust your own memories.
- Written notes in waterproof format. Immediately after observing something, write what you saw. Your memory may shift. The notes won't.

### Analytical Instruments

You are watching a process. The factory is *making something*. Understanding what requires more than eyes.

- Water sampling containers—many of them. Take samples at different distances from the machinery, at different points in the piston cycle, from the conveyor's input and output if you can reach them.
- The "thick, luminous ribbons" that sink from the apparatus are your primary mystery. Capturing some of this output may be the most valuable thing you do here. It may also be the most dangerous. Use remotely-operated collection if possible.
- Bring a small container of *ordinary* seawater from outside the boundary. Compare it to what you find inside. The differences matter.
- Chemical testing equipment for pH, salinity, dissolved oxygen, unusual metallic content. The "metallic sheen" on the crabs suggests something is entering the water column.

### Boundary Markers

You need to know where safety is at all times.

- Bring a reel with high-visibility line. Anchor it outside the shimmer-boundary in ordinary ocean. This is your path home.
- Mark the boundary's position from multiple angles. The text suggests it's visible as a shimmer, but "visible" may not mean "stable."
- Consider that the boundary might move. Check your line periodically. If it's tightening or slackening in ways that don't match your movement, the space may be shifting.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Transformation Question

Fish pass through gears and "momentarily become teeth or cogs or drive-shafts" before emerging as fish again. The text then immediately asks: "hold still long enough here and you start to wonder which category *you'd* fall into."

This is not idle speculation. This is the central risk.

**Before you enter, decide:**
- How long is too long? Set a maximum duration for your visit and *commit to it* regardless of what you're learning. Forty-five minutes. An hour. Whatever you choose, when that time comes, you leave.
- Designate specific physical actions as "reality checks." Touch your own face with your hands. Count your fingers. Speak your own name aloud. Do this every ten minutes. If something feels wrong—if the count is off, if your voice sounds different, if the action itself becomes difficult to remember—that's your signal to leave *immediately*.
- Accept that you may leave partially changed. The crabs developed metallic shells. This might have been gradual. There may be no clear threshold.

### The Fascination Trap

The factory is described as compelling. It processes, it produces, it has *purpose*. The temptation will be to stay and watch, to understand, to witness the completion of whatever is being made.

This is exactly how the crabs ended up on their endless conveyor loops.

**Strategies:**
- Write down why you're leaving *before* you enter. "I am leaving at [time] because I want to see my family / because I have other work / because survival matters more than understanding." Carry this note where you can see it.
- Bring a dive buddy if at all possible. Give each other permission to call the dive for any reason. If your partner says "we leave now," you leave now, no questions, no hesitation. You can discuss it on the surface.
- Remember the fish that follow the luminous ribbons down and don't return. They are drawn by something. They don't seem distressed. They still die—or transform, or translate, or whatever happens to them. The absence of fear is not the same as the absence of danger.

### The Meaning Problem

Your mind will try to make sense of what you're seeing. It will generate explanations: "this is a natural phenomenon we don't understand yet," "this is alien technology," "this is a manifestation of collective unconscious industrial anxiety," "this is a wound in reality that is healing itself." 

All of these explanations are probably wrong. None of them are useful.

**Practice, before you go, the discipline of observation without interpretation.** Write down what you see. "Piston moves. Water temperature rises 0.5°C. Fish school shifts east." Do not write down what it *means*. You don't know what it means. You will not learn what it means. The factory is processing seawater into something else, and you don't have the context to understand why.

This is uncomfortable. Humans need meaning. The discomfort is part of the experience.

## Interaction Protocols

### With the Machinery

Do not touch it.

This seems obvious but will become difficult in practice. The machinery has gaps, openings, spaces that seem designed for hands. Pistons have handles. Gears have grips. You will feel the pull to interact, to become part of the process.

If you choose to touch something anyway—and you might, this is your journey—use a tool first. A probe, a pole, something that isn't you. Watch what happens to the tool. Does it return unchanged? Does it return *at all*?

### With the Fauna

The fish don't notice their transformation through the gears. The crabs ride their conveyors contentedly. The sharks never cross the boundary.

**Consider what each of these populations knows that you don't.**

The sharks' behavior is the most informative. They patrol. They wait. They don't enter, and they don't leave. Something about the boundary is important to them. Something about the factory requires their presence at its edge. Before you enter, spend time watching the sharks. Their patrol patterns may reveal information about the space's geometry that isn't visible from inside.

The crabs have adapted or transformed. If you can safely collect one, do so. Its shell composition may tell you something about the long-term effects of exposure. But consider: removing a crab from its conveyor loop may not be a kindness. It may also draw attention you don't want.

Do not follow the fish that descend with the luminous ribbons. I shouldn't need to say this. I'm saying it anyway.

### With the Space Itself

The factory "insists itself into being." It exists as "pure function." This suggests something uncomfortable: it may respond to *observation* differently than to *participation*.

Keep moving. Don't hover in one place. Don't watch any single mechanism for too long. Be a poor audience. The factory may not care about you at all—you may be as irrelevant to its process as a single water molecule in its flow. But if it *does* notice observers, your best protection is to remain uninteresting.

## What to Bring Home

If you survive unchanged—or changed only in ways you can live with—you will want to bring something back. Evidence. Meaning. Proof.

**Physical samples:** Water from inside the boundary. A fragment of shell from a metallic crab. A frame of video showing fish becoming machinery becoming fish. These are valuable, but treat them with extreme caution after you return. Store them in containment. Don't assume they're inert just because they're away from the factory.

**Observational records:** Your notes, your recordings, your documentation of the machinery's cycles and the fauna's behavior. These may be more valuable than physical samples. They can be shared without risk of contamination.

**The most important thing you bring home is yourself.** Monitor your body for weeks after. Watch for metallic sheens. Watch for the urge to return. Watch for rhythms in your own behavior that match the piston's cycle—every few seconds, do you find yourself pausing, waiting, then continuing? This would be a sign.

## Final Considerations

You asked how an ordinary human should prepare. I've answered as if you are genuinely ordinary—no special resistances, no multiverse-traveler adaptations, no protection beyond what you can carry and what you know.

The honest answer, underneath all the practical advice: you probably shouldn't go.

Not because it's certain death. The sharks survive at the boundary. The crabs survive on their loops. The fish survive their transformations, after a fashion. Survival is possible.

But the factory is *making something*. The process is young. It might run for a million years. It might complete tomorrow. You don't know what it's making, what it's for, or who—or *what*—set it in motion. Visiting means participating, however passively, in a process you cannot understand and cannot stop.

If you go anyway—and you might, curiosity is its own compulsion—then go with open eyes. Go humble. Go ready to leave when your time is up, no matter how much you want to stay and watch the gears turn.

The drowned factory churns on. It doesn't need you. Try to remember that it doesn't need you.
