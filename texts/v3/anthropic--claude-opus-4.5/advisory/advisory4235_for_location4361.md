# Preparing for the Wicker Warrens

## Physical Preparation

### Body Conditioning

Before descending into the Warrens, you'll need to recalibrate your expectations about physical space. Spend several weeks practicing movement in confined areas—crawling through spaces barely wider than your shoulders, learning to navigate by feel when your eyes fail you, becoming comfortable with the sensation of surfaces pressing close on multiple sides. The standard passages accommodate the compact bodies of the keepers, not the frames of average-height outsiders. You won't be standing fully upright for much of your visit.

Work on your flexibility, particularly your hips and spine. Practice transitioning smoothly from crawling to crouching to sitting, and learn to do so without sudden movements that might stress the woven floors beneath you. The warren's structure is sound, but it responds to how you move through it. Gentle, distributed pressure is absorbed. Sharp, concentrated force travels unpredictably through the fiber matrix.

### Acclimatization to Dryness

The absolute absence of liquid water will challenge your body in ways you may not anticipate. Begin conditioning yourself weeks in advance by gradually reducing your reliance on external hydration. This sounds dangerous—and done wrong, it is—but the warrens operate on different principles. The keepers have developed techniques for extracting moisture from the fermented succulent drinks, from the lichen itself (certain varieties can be chewed to release bound water), and from the humid air of the lower levels. Your body will need time to accept these alternative sources.

Expect your skin to crack initially. Bring a small amount of rendered fat—the keepers trade for it—to ease the transition, but understand that within a week or two, your skin will begin producing different oils, adapting to the dry heat. Your lips will peel. Your nostrils will bleed. This passes.

### Heat Tolerance

The geothermal warmth of the lower levels runs tropical, but it's a dry tropical—nothing like jungle humidity. The heat will feel like standing near an oven rather than sitting in a steam bath. Spend time in dry saunas if you have access, learning to breathe slowly and conserve movement when the temperature climbs. The keepers have adjusted their metabolisms over generations; you'll need to consciously manage yours.

## Clothing and Equipment

### What to Wear

Loose, breathable fabric in layers you can shed. Natural fibers only—the synthetic materials common in some multiverse regions react poorly to the particular enzymes present in the warren's lichen, developing an acrid smell and eventually breaking down into irritating threads. Linen works well. Undyed cotton better. Some travelers report success with fabrics woven from plant fibers similar to what the warrens themselves are made of—if you can source such material in advance, the keepers may regard you more favorably.

Leave your boots behind. Anything with a hard sole risks damaging the woven floors, and the keepers will ask you to remove them immediately regardless. Soft leather foot wrappings work, but most experienced visitors simply go barefoot after the first day. Your soles will toughen. The fibers are surprisingly comfortable once you adjust—warm and yielding, with a texture like walking on tightly woven rope.

### What to Carry

Almost nothing. The warrens provide what you need, and the keepers are generous with basics, but they have little use for most outside goods and no space to store excess. Everything in the warrens serves a purpose or becomes part of the weaving.

Bring gifts rather than supplies. Small tools in materials they can't easily source—metal needles (bone is traditional but metal lasts longer), thin-bladed knives suitable for cutting sedge, polished stones they might incorporate into ceremonial weaving. Avoid anything that requires water to function. Avoid anything fragile. Avoid anything loud.

If you rely on medication, bring what you need in the most compact form possible, and understand that the keepers may ask to examine it. They're curious about outside healing practices, though they rarely adopt them.

### Navigational Considerations

You will get lost. Accept this. The warrens follow an internal logic that takes time to learn—the subtle differences in weave pattern that indicate which family maintains a section, the slight variations in lichen color that signal depth, the way sound travels differently near junctions versus dead ends. Carry nothing to mark your path; the keepers would view this as damage. Instead, develop relationships. The children especially love to guide visitors, and their knowledge of the passages is encyclopedic.

## Social and Cultural Preparation

### Understanding the Quiet

The warrens are not silent—they're full of creaking and cricket-song and the soft sounds of daily life—but they operate at a different volume than most surface cultures. Spend time before your visit practicing the art of speaking softly without whispering. Whispering actually carries further through the woven walls; a true soft voice, barely above breathing, is absorbed more readily.

Learn to communicate through touch and presence. The keepers often sit together in the small chambers for hours, bodies in contact, without speaking at all. This isn't awkward silence; it's a form of communion. If you find this uncomfortable, work on that discomfort before you arrive. Visitors who need constant verbal interaction tend to unsettle the warrens' rhythm.

### The Etiquette of Space

When a keeper makes room for you, they are giving you something genuinely precious. Space in the warrens is finite in a way that surface-dwellers rarely experience. Every chamber exists because someone wove it, maintained it, chose to keep it rather than letting it compress back into fiber. When you're offered a sleeping niche or a seat at a communal meal, recognize the gift.

Reciprocate by making yourself small. Not physically—they understand you're larger than they are—but energetically. Take up less space than you technically need. Share surfaces. Allow your body to rest against others without stiffness. The keepers will test this unconsciously, sitting closer than you expect, reaching across you to retrieve something, settling into positions that put you in constant light contact. Respond with ease and they'll trust you further.

### Regarding the Seventh Level

Do not ask about it directly. The keepers will mention it in passing—that oblique reference to the first weavers, the too-great warmth, the strange lichen—and then move on. Follow their lead. Your curiosity is natural and they know it, but pressing the matter marks you as someone who doesn't understand how the warrens work.

If you stay long enough, if you prove yourself quiet and careful and willing to tend and repair, you may be told more. Visitors who've reached that point report... inconsistent things. Some say the seventh level is simply dangerously hot, a practical hazard sealed away. Others speak of something down there that continues to weave, something that learned the patterns from the original makers and has been practicing ever since. One traveler I spoke with, who claimed to have spent three years in the warrens, told me the sound you hear through the floor isn't movement at all—it's growth. The seven levels are eight now, she said. Maybe nine. The warrens go deeper than they did a generation ago, and the keepers don't speak of it because they genuinely don't know what's happening beneath them.

Take this as you will. But don't try to open that hatch.

## Psychological Preparation

### Adjusting to Enclosure

Some visitors experience significant distress at the warren's enclosing nature. This isn't simple claustrophobia—the chambers aren't crushingly small, and the woven walls provide surprisingly good airflow. It's something subtler: the awareness that you're suspended in a structure made entirely of dead plant matter, that the floor beneath you is also a ceiling, that the whole system exists because people continuously maintain it.

Test yourself before committing. Spend time in woven spaces—large baskets, if you can find them big enough, or rooms constructed from similar materials. Notice how you respond to the slight give of the floor, the way walls flex when you lean against them, the knowledge that everything around you was made by hands and could be unmade. If this triggers panic, the warrens may not be safe for you. If it triggers something else—wonder, perhaps, or a strange sense of being held—you'll likely do well.

### The Intimacy of Sound

Privacy as surface cultures understand it does not exist in the warrens. The woven walls transmit sound with perfect fidelity while blocking sight entirely. You will hear everything—conversations, arguments, lovemaking, grief, the soft sounds of sleep and dreaming. They will hear everything from you. This isn't surveillance; there's no one listening with judgment or recording what they learn. It's simply the nature of the space.

Most visitors find this profoundly uncomfortable at first, then gradually freeing. When there's no possibility of hiding, the effort of maintaining a hidden self becomes obviously pointless. The keepers have developed a culture around this—they speak as though they might be overheard because they always are, they live as though they're being witnessed because they always are, and this has made them strangely genuine. Deception is not unknown in the warrens, but it's difficult, and the keepers seem to find it exhausting in a way surface cultures don't.

Prepare yourself for this exposure. Consider what you habitually hide and why. The warrens will not force you to change, but they will make your concealments obvious to you in ways that can be disorienting.

### Time and Patience

The warrens move slowly. Repairs take hours, meals take hours, the simple act of moving from one level to another takes longer than you'd expect because the passages are narrow and shared and someone is always coming the other way. The keepers don't experience this as delay; it's simply how things are. Fighting against it will only exhaust you.

Practice patience before you go. Real patience, not the performed kind. Sit with tasks that cannot be hurried. Learn to weave, if you can—even simple basket-work will help you understand the keeper's relationship to time. A single chamber might take a weaver months to complete. When they maintain the warrens, they're maintaining the work of ancestors they never met, and they do so with the knowledge that their own weaving will be maintained by descendants they'll never meet. Time moves differently when you're part of a continuous project that spans generations.

## What to Expect Upon Arrival

You'll find the entrance during the hottest part of the day, when the clay flats shimmer and your eyes ache from the glare. The hatch will seem too small, and you'll wonder if you've made a mistake. You haven't. Lower yourself through.

The first thing you'll notice is the smell—dry grass and musk and that sweet fermented sourness. Then the light, amber and diffuse, sourceless, coming from everywhere and nowhere. Then the sound: the creaking. That constant, subliminal voice of the structure settling, adjusting, breathing.

Someone will find you within minutes. They always do. The ferrets will have noted your arrival and communicated it somehow—no one has fully explained how—and a keeper will appear from a passage you didn't notice, compact and quiet and watching you with eyes that have never seen harsh sunlight.

They'll assess you quickly. Your size, your smell, your willingness to move softly. If you pass their evaluation—and if you've prepared as I've described, you likely will—they'll lead you deeper, into the warmth and the quiet and the endless woven dark.

Make room for them in return. They've been making room for a very long time. It's the only currency that truly matters here.

And if, in the depths of your first night, you wake to the sound of something beneath you—something vast and patient, shifting in the darkness below the seventh level—try to go back to sleep. The keepers have heard it all their lives. It's simply part of the warrens now.

Whatever is down there, it seems content to keep weaving.
