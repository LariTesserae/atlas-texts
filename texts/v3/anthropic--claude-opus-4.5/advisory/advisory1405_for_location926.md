# Preparing for Veth-Caul: A Traveler's Guide

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a city. You are visiting something that has *become* a city, or is *practicing* being one. The distinction matters because your preparation must account for the fact that Veth-Caul may be preparing for you in return. This is not a passive environment. It is not hostile—nothing in the accounts suggests malevolence—but it is *attentive* in ways that stone and mortar cannot be. Your presence will be noticed, processed, perhaps responded to.

This should inform everything that follows.

## Physical Preparations

### Clothing and Footwear

Bring garments you are willing to lose or see transformed. The city's self-repairing materials have been known to *adopt* foreign fabrics left in contact with surfaces overnight. This isn't theft—the items remain yours, technically—but a cotton shirt left draped over a bone-coral railing may develop the ability to mend its own tears, and with that ability comes a subtle *wrongness* to the weave that some travelers find unbearable against their skin.

Wear layers you can remove quickly. The post-rain rearrangement periods demand you be able to move through doorways that may be narrowing, and bulky outer garments have trapped more than one visitor in rooms that decided to become corridors.

For footwear, choose something with thin, flexible soles. You will want to feel the subsonic hum through your feet—this is not merely atmosphere, it is *information*. Thick rubber or heavy treads will insulate you from warnings you cannot afford to miss. The locals go barefoot when they can, though calluses take months to develop against the slightly abrasive pale stone. A compromise: soft leather, or woven plant-fiber sandals you can purchase in the outer markets before descending into the basin proper.

Avoid metal fasteners where possible. Buttons, ties, or garments that simply wrap and fold will serve you better. Metal doesn't *malfunction* in Veth-Caul, but it draws the attention of the Tenders. They will investigate zippers, belt buckles, earrings—not aggressively, but persistently, and their many-legged inspections are unsettling even when harmless.

### Physical Conditioning

The plazas are vast. Prepare for walking, significant walking, across distances that would be uncomfortable even in a conventional city. There is no public transit. The bridges breathe but do not carry—they connect building clusters, not disparate neighborhoods. You will cross hundreds of meters of bare stone between any two points of interest, often multiple times per day.

More importantly, prepare for *stillness*. When the rains come, you stop. When the post-rain hours begin, you find interior shelter and you wait. This can mean two, three, sometimes six hours of enforced patience while the city rearranges around you. If you are someone who grows anxious in confinement, practice now. Meditate, or learn to. Bring a craft that requires only your hands—the city does not interfere with knitting needles or sketch pencils, and the hours pass easier when your fingers have work.

Your cardiovascular system should be adequate for moderate exertion in warm, humid conditions. The temperature hovers at what feels like late spring—comfortable when still, perspiration-inducing when walking. Hydration matters, though the city provides water. We'll address whether you should drink it shortly.

### Medical Considerations

Bring your own medications in quantities sufficient for your entire stay, plus emergency surplus. The local pharmacopoeia exists but derives from the garden plants, and their effects on travelers—whose bodies have not slowly adapted over generations—are unpredictable. A headache remedy that works beautifully for a ten-year resident may leave you experiencing mild synesthesia for a week.

If you take anything on a strict schedule—insulin, blood pressure medication, psychiatric drugs that require consistent blood levels—understand that timekeeping in Veth-Caul is approximate at best. The dim light offers no reliable day-night cycle. Bring multiple timepieces (non-digital if possible; the city doesn't interfere with electronics but it doesn't *support* them either, and batteries drain at irregular rates). Set alarms. Do not trust your internal clock, which will begin to drift within the first few days.

Regarding injuries: minor cuts and abrasions heal faster in Veth-Caul than elsewhere. This seems to be a property of the environment rather than any deliberate intervention. Deep wounds or broken bones should be treated by the local practitioners, who have developed techniques specific to the city's unusual physiology—both their own and, somehow, yours while you're there.

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

### Accepting Uncertainty

The buildings will change. Rooms you slept in will not look the same when you return. Doorways will relocate. Stairs will occasionally decide to become ramps. None of this is dangerous if you don't fight it, but the psychological toll of living in mutable architecture is significant and underestimated by nearly every first-time traveler.

Before you go, practice small surrenders. Rearrange your furniture at home randomly, then force yourself to adapt rather than restore it. Take different routes to familiar destinations. Sleep in different positions. These are pale approximations of what Veth-Caul asks of you, but they begin building the mental flexibility you'll need.

The locals have a phrase: *"The room knows better."* When you enter a space and find it changed, the correct response is to assume the change serves some purpose you don't yet understand. Fighting the architecture—trying to prop doors open, wedging furniture to prevent movement, marking paths on walls—accomplishes nothing except exhausting you and, some say, making the city *curious* about why you're resisting. Curious, in Veth-Caul, means attentive. You don't want to be interesting.

### The Question of the Water

You will need to decide, before you arrive, whether you intend to drink the city-provided water.

The effects are well-documented. Regular consumption over days and weeks attunes you to Veth-Caul's rhythms. You begin to feel when rearrangements are imminent—a slight pressure behind the eyes, a tingling in the fingertips. The breathing of the bridges becomes intelligible, not as language but as *something*, a communication that falls below words but above pure sensation. Long-term residents describe a sense of being welcomed, of belonging.

But there is a cost, and it is not fully reversible.

Travelers who drink the water regularly report that other places feel *flat* afterward. Cities that don't shift begin to seem dead—architecture that just sits there, inert and fixed, becomes subtly oppressive. The attunement, once gained, is difficult to lose. Some travelers return to Veth-Caul again and again, not because they want to but because everywhere else has become insufficient.

My recommendation: bring your own water for the first several days. Observe. Speak with residents of varying tenure—the newcomers of a few years, the settlers of decades. Understand what you're accepting before you accept it. The city provides water freely; you can begin drinking it at any point. But you cannot un-drink it.

If you do choose to partake, start slowly. A few sips with your first meal, gradually increasing. Your dreams will change before anything else—they'll become more architectural, more spatial. This is normal. The nausea some experience in the first week is also normal and passes.

### Solitude and Company

Veth-Caul is not crowded. The vast plazas between building clusters, the gardens serving as the only reliable gathering points, the soft-spoken culture that's evolved in a place where sound is absorbed—all of this creates an environment that can feel intensely isolating, even when you're technically surrounded by people.

If you are traveling alone, build connections early. The garden-markets are your opportunity. Vendors and regular shoppers are accustomed to travelers and generally welcoming—they remember what it was like to be new. Establish routines. Return to the same gardens, the same stalls, at the same approximate times. Reliability signals trustworthiness in a place where everything else is unreliable.

If you struggle with isolation, consider timing your visit to coincide with one of the loose gatherings the locals call Convergences—periods when, for reasons no one fully understands, the building clusters seem to drift closer together and the plazas shrink. Foot traffic increases. Conversations become easier. The city feels almost normal. These happen irregularly, perhaps three or four times per local year, but long-term residents can sometimes predict them based on the pattern of the bioluminescent pulses. Ask before you come; someone may know.

If you are traveling with companions, establish protocols now for what to do when you become separated—because you will become separated. Doorways relocate. The building you both entered will sometimes only let one of you exit from the expected side. Agree on meeting points (specific gardens, which don't move) and timeframes. Agree on what to do if someone doesn't arrive. Carry written messages rather than relying on spoken coordination; the city absorbs shouts, remember, and calling for a missing companion across a plaza is an exercise in futility.

## Equipment and Supplies

### What to Bring

**Flexible containers**: Rigid luggage becomes problematic in rooms that reshape overnight. Soft bags, wrapping cloths, items that can compress and expand. Your possessions may need to fit through spaces that weren't doorways yesterday.

**Light sources**: The city's ambient glow is sufficient for navigation but not for detail work—reading, writing, examining small objects. Battery-powered lights are acceptable but unreliable; bring backups. Better are the chemical light tubes available in the outer markets, which burn slowly over hours and are unaffected by whatever makes electronics inconsistent here.

**Writing materials**: Not digital. Paper, bound journals, pencils or the local equivalent (a self-sharpening stylus made of bone-coral that many travelers find unsettling but practical). You'll want to document your experiences, partly for later reflection and partly because writing helps process the disorientation. The city seems to approve of writing—journals left out are never disturbed, and some travelers report finding their books moved to better-lit locations overnight.

**Rope or flexible cord**: For personal use, not for trying to control the architecture. Having a length of cord to lower supplies from a window when your door has relocated to an inconvenient position, or to mark a path through a particularly dense garden, proves useful more often than you'd expect.

**Trade goods**: The economy in Veth-Caul is not currency-based in any way you'll recognize. Some transactions use the local medium—small carved tokens that the city produces irregularly and that have value simply because they're scarce—but most commerce is barter, and travelers are valued for what they bring from outside. Small, well-made items are prized: needles that don't reshape themselves, scissors that maintain their edge, spices that taste only of what they are. Bring more than you think you'll need.

### What Not to Bring

**Weapons**: Not because they're prohibited—there's no authority to prohibit anything—but because they mark you as someone who doesn't understand what you're entering. The city is not dangerous in ways a weapon addresses. The only threat is the architecture itself, and you cannot stab a doorway into staying open. Carrying visible armament will make residents avoid you, and their avoidance means losing access to the local knowledge you need to survive comfortably.

**Detailed maps**: Useless at best, actively misleading at worst. The cartographic attempts that exist are conceptual rather than geographic—relationship maps between neighborhood temperaments, probability charts for rearrangement patterns. You can purchase these locally if you want them. Anything produced outside Veth-Caul is already outdated.

**Rigid expectations**: This is equipment of a sort, and it must be left behind. Do not enter Veth-Caul with a list of things you must see or accomplish. The city does not accommodate schedules. If you need to meet someone at a specific location at a specific time, you will probably fail. Build in flexibility to the point of formlessness. Let your visit shape itself around what the city is doing rather than trying to impose your intentions on its rhythms.

## Navigating the City

### The Gardens as Anchors

The thirty-seven-meter circles do not move. This makes them the only reliable reference points in Veth-Caul, and you should begin immediately, upon arrival, to learn them. Each garden has a character—some are dense with flowering species, others dominated by the copper-stemmed trees, others barely more than ground cover with a few shrubs. The locals name them according to these characters: the Rust Garden, the Flowering Court, the Low Green, the Metal Trees.

Memorize a mental map of gardens relative to each other. Building clusters will rearrange around them, but the gardens themselves stay fixed. If you can identify which garden you're near, you can orient yourself relative to the rest of the city.

### Movement Protocols

Walk the long way around, always. Shortcuts through building clusters are tempting—the plazas are so vast, and surely that narrow alley will save twenty minutes—but interiors rearrange more frequently than exteriors. Enter a building only when you have business there. Cross plazas even when it seems inefficient. The stone will not shift beneath your feet.

When you must enter structures, move with purpose but not haste. The buildings seem to respond poorly to urgency—rooms rearrange more frequently around panicked movement. Walk steadily. Breathe. If you find yourself disoriented inside a building, stop and wait. Often the architecture will eventually offer you an exit, especially if you remain calm. Fighting your way through—forcing doors, climbing through windows—sometimes works but sometimes triggers more aggressive rearrangement.

During post-rain hours, simply don't move. Stay where you are. This is non-negotiable. The rearrangement during Drinking periods can be violent by Veth-Caul standards—walls sliding meters rather than centimeters, entire rooms budding or being reabsorbed. Even experienced residents have been injured by moving during these hours. Find shelter, and stay.

### Interaction with the Tenders

Leave them alone. They will leave you alone. This is the entire protocol.

If one lands on you—they fly occasionally, though usually they crawl—remain still and it will move on when it determines you are not a plant. If one seems interested in something you're carrying, set the item down and step back; it will investigate and lose interest when the thing proves inorganic or unrelated to its garden. Do not, under any circumstances, interfere with a Tender that is working. Even moving one aside gently disrupts patterns you don't understand, and the gardens sometimes respond poorly—plants dying back, taking weeks to recover.

The Tenders may be part of the city's body. They may be parasites or symbiotes. They may be something else entirely. It doesn't matter. They are not for you.

## Social Considerations

### The Local Population

The residents of Veth-Caul are human, but they have adapted in ways that can feel alienating to newcomers. The slow speech, the curved movements, the translucent skin of the long-timers—these are easy to intellectualize but viscerally unsettling when encountered directly. You may find yourself avoiding eye contact with someone who has become slightly too easy to see through.

Push past this. These are people who chose to live in the belly of something vast and strange, and that choice deserves respect even when its consequences are uncomfortable to witness. The long-term residents often have the deepest knowledge and are the most willing to share it—they remember their own newcomer disorientation, often decades past, and feel genuine compassion for travelers still struggling.

Speak quietly. This is practical—loud voices are absorbed—but also cultural. Volume is considered aggressive, an attempt to impose yourself on space that belongs to the city. Even emphatic conversations happen in near-whispers. Adjust your expectations for how discussions feel; the muted tones do not indicate disinterest or coldness.

### Exchanging Information

The most valuable currency you carry is news from outside. The basin's sharp edges make physical departure easy but psychological return difficult; many residents haven't left in years or decades, and they hunger for stories of other places even when they have no desire to visit them. Be prepared to describe your previous travels in detail. Landscapes, customs, what food tastes like in different climates. This is how you build relationships, and relationships are how you learn what you need to know.

In return, ask questions—but ask them carefully. Direct interrogation about the city's nature tends to produce deflection. Better to ask about personal experience: "How did you find your first Drinking?" "When did you start understanding the bridges?" "What surprised you most in your first year?" The answers will contain the information you need, wrapped in individual stories that make it digestible.

## What You Cannot Prepare For

There is a moment that comes to most travelers, usually in the first week, when the subsonic hum rises slightly and you feel—just for an instant—regarded. Not watched, not observed, but *regarded*, the way a vast and patient intelligence might turn a fraction of its attention toward something small and curious that has entered its space.

Some travelers find this transcendent. Others find it terrifying. Most fall somewhere between, feeling both the wonder and the fear in proportions that shift from moment to moment. You cannot know in advance which you will be. You cannot prepare for this. You can only know that it's coming, and that it passes, and that afterward you will understand something about Veth-Caul that you didn't understand before.

What you understand, I cannot tell you. It's different for everyone. But it will change how you move through the city for the rest of your stay.

## Final Considerations

### How Long to Stay

A week is sufficient to experience Veth-Caul as a visitor—to witness a few Drinkings, to learn the nearest gardens, to feel the hum rise and fall, to speak with residents and form preliminary impressions. This is the minimum I would recommend. Less than a week and you'll spend most of your time disoriented, leaving just as the city begins to make sense.

A month is enough to begin genuine attunement, especially if you're drinking the water. By the end of a month, you'll feel the rearrangements before they happen, sometimes. The bridges will start to mean something. You'll have favorite gardens, routines, maybe acquaintances becoming friends. Leaving after a month is still relatively easy—you'll miss Veth-Caul, but you'll readjust.

Longer than a month, and you should consider whether you're visiting or settling. The line blurs gradually, but there's a point—different for everyone—where Veth-Caul becomes home in a way that's hard to undo. If you have obligations elsewhere, ties you want to maintain, set a firm departure date before you arrive and hold to it. The city doesn't trap you. It simply makes elsewhere seem flat.

### Leaving

When you're ready to go, walk to the basin's edge and climb out. That's all. The sharp geological breaks that mark Veth-Caul's perimeter have rough footholds, and the ascent is strenuous but manageable. At the top, you'll find yourself in ordinary countryside—fields, roads, eventually towns that operate according to comprehensible rules.

Look back, if you want. The city will be there, sprawled in its basin, pale buildings catching the strange sourceless light, gardens shimmering green-gold between the vast plazas. You may feel an urge to return. This is normal. Give it a few weeks to fade, or don't, and make your own choice about what that urge means.

The city will wait. It has been waiting for a long time. It is patient beyond any measure you or I can understand. Whether you return or not, it will continue its slow becoming—breathing, thinking, growing, learning what it means to be a place where people live.

That's what you're preparing to visit. An education in patience. A lesson in surrender. A brief residency in the body of something vast and gentle and utterly, quietly strange.

Go well. Listen to the bridges. Trust the room.
