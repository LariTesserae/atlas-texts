## Preparing to Visit The Nest That Grew Backwards

### Before You Leave: Mental and Spiritual Preparation

**Sit with contradiction for a while.** In the weeks before your visit, practice holding two incompatible ideas in your mind without trying to resolve them. Watch optical illusions without blinking. Read stories that don't end. The hollow operates on logic that predates human reasoning—your sanity will fare better if you've loosened your grip on the need for things to make sense.

**Examine your relationship with observation.** The creatures there watch. The hollow itself seems to watch. You will be seen in ways you may not be accustomed to. Spend time beforehand being genuinely looked at—by animals, by strangers, by your own reflection held too long. Notice what feelings arise. The hollow will amplify them.

**Make peace with slowness.** Time there moves reluctantly. If you are someone who fidgets, who checks phones, who measures worth by productivity—practice stillness now. Sit in a room and do nothing for an hour. Then two. The hollow will ask more of you than this, but you should at least know what it feels like to let moments pass unconvinced.

---

### Physical Preparations

**Clothing and Body**

Wear natural fibers—cotton, wool, linen. Synthetics feel wrong against the humidity, and there's something about the hollow that seems to *notice* petroleum-derived materials with what might be distaste. Tight-fitting layers work better than loose ones for the entrance squeeze. Remove jewelry, especially anything metallic that might conduct the strange condensation.

Eat lightly beforehand. The air is so humid that breathing becomes almost like drinking—a full stomach may rebel. Hydrate well before arrival, but bring no water with you. The hollow provides, in its fashion, and carrying water from outside seems to introduce a wrongness, a dissonance between what you've brought and what weeps from the walls.

Cut your nails short. You'll be touching the stone, the moss—possibly involuntarily, as your hands reach toward the axis without your permission. Long nails catch on the liverworts and disturb patterns you cannot see but can somehow feel disrupting.

**What to Bring**

A small, simple light source—a candle stub or single match, not a flashlight. Electric light behaves badly there, flickering in rhythms that don't match its power source. Candlelight at least fails in ways you can understand.

Something to mark your own passage that can join the other left things: a smooth stone from your home, a small woven ring of grass you've made yourself, a clean bone honestly obtained. Do not bring these as offerings—the hollow doesn't want worship. Bring them as *contributions*, as acknowledgment that you, too, have observed something and wish to leave evidence that observation occurred.

A notebook and pencil (not pen—ink runs in the humidity, and there's something about the hollow that resists permanent marks made by visitors). You may want to record the scratched marks in the soft stone. You may want to sketch the spiral geometry. You probably won't succeed—the drawings will look wrong when you review them outside—but the attempt itself is valuable.

**What Not to Bring**

Cameras or recording devices. They capture what the eye sees, not what the hollow shows, and the discrepancy between the two will unsettle you more than the hollow itself.

Anything alive that isn't you. No companion animals, no cut flowers, no seeds. The hollow has its own ecosystem, and introducing external life feels like bringing a lit match into a room full of sleepers. Wrong. Potentially dangerous in ways you won't understand until too late.

Strong scents. No perfume, no scented soap, no essential oils. The air there is the air—warm, wet, faintly mineral, entirely itself. Foreign smells don't dissipate properly. They hang and pool and seem to attract the attention of the clicking thing in the deep crack.

### Navigating the Approach

**Finding the Entrance**

Follow the condensation. Trust your skin more than your eyes. When droplets form on your forearms without explanation, you're close. The crack in the basalt will not look like an entrance—it will look like a mistake the mountain made and forgot about. Squeeze through anyway. Turn your shoulders sideways. Do not think too hard about whether the crack was that narrow a moment ago.

**Entering**

Move slowly through the entrance passage. Let your eyes adjust not just to darkness but to the different way the hollow organizes space. The shift is subtle but significant—like switching from reading one language to another. Give yourself time.

When you emerge into the hollow proper, crouch immediately. The ceiling is lower than it appears, and the geometry will try to convince you there's more room than exists. Trust your body's sense of where stone is.

---

### Behavior Inside

**The First Moments**

Stillness first. Do not move to the back wall, do not approach the axis, do not touch anything. Simply be present. Let the hollow notice you. Let the creatures observe. You are entering a space that has been here since before the mountain—show it the respect you would show anything ancient and patient.

Breathe slowly and accept that breathing will feel strange. The humidity coats your throat, your lungs. It is not harmful, only unfamiliar. You are not drowning. You are not even swimming. You are simply breathing air that has different priorities than the air outside.

**Moving Through the Space**

When you move—and you will, eventually, called toward the axis like everything else here—move along the paths the creatures have already established. The millipedes trace routes through the moss-spirals. Follow their logic. Where they curve, curve. Where they pause, pause.

Do not try to touch the axis itself. There is nothing there to touch—it is a direction, not a destination. But your hands may reach toward it anyway, the same way your hands might reach toward a fire for warmth. Let them reach. Just don't try to grasp.

If you set something down, accept that it will move. This is not theft or malice. The hollow is still very slowly happening, and everything within it drifts toward that happening. If you need to keep track of an object, keep it in your hands or against your body. Objects set down belong to the hollow's geometry now.

**Observing**

Look at the salamander. Really look. Note which side of its head the eyes are on. Look away. Look back. Accept what you see without trying to reconcile it with what you saw before. This is not the salamander changing—this is you observing a creature that exists in a space where position is more negotiable than you're accustomed to.

Study the scratched marks in the soft stone. Do not try too hard to find meaning. Let meaning find you, or not. The marks may be counting. They may be the absence of stone. Both can be true in the hollow. Record what you see in your notebook, knowing the recording will be inaccurate.

Listen for the sound that isn't quite sound—the note from the unborn instrument. It comes from the direction of the axis. It may be the hollow breathing. It may be the hollow thinking. It may be you, hearing your own blood move through your ears and interpreting it through the hollow's strange acoustics. The distinction matters less than you'd expect.

**Duration**

You will not know how long you've stayed. Time moves reluctantly—this means it can be persuaded, but not predicted. What feels like an hour may be three. What feels like three may be one. Your best guide is your body: when thirst arrives that the humid air cannot satisfy, when hunger makes your hands shake, leave. Do not try to push past these signals. The hollow doesn't mind your staying, but you are still a creature with needs, and the hollow will not remind you of them.

### The Creatures

**The Millipedes**

They follow the spiral paths because the geometry compels them, but there's something in their too-many segments that suggests the geometry compels them because they followed first. They may have been here as long as the hollow. Do not touch them, but do not fear them. If one crosses your hand where you've rested it on the moss, stay still. It is navigating. You are terrain.

**The Salamander**

It watches. That is its purpose, or its nature, or the role the hollow has assigned it. Meet its gaze if you can find its eyes. Look away when it blinks. What you see when you look back is true, no matter how it contradicts what you saw before. The salamander is not lying to you. The salamander is simply being salamander, in a place where being is less fixed than outside.

**The Moths**

They are patient beyond patience. Their antennae tracking your movements is not predatory—it is documentary. They are recording you the way you might record the marks on the stone. Let them. Being observed by the moths is a fair exchange for being allowed to observe the hollow.

**The Clicking Thing**

Do not approach the deep crack where the hollow narrows to darkness. Do not shine your candle toward it. Do not call out, do not tap the stone to elicit response. Something is there. It may be a beetle. The hollow permits it to remain unnamed, and you should extend it the same courtesy. If the clicking grows louder, it is time to leave. Not because the clicking thing is dangerous—you have no way of knowing whether it is—but because the hollow is telling you something, and listening when a place speaks is simple wisdom.

---

### Leaving

**Timing Your Exit**

The hollow will not trap you, but it will make leaving feel like an afterthought. You may find yourself thinking, "just another moment," again and again, each moment bleeding into the next with the reluctance that characterizes time there. Set an intention before you enter: when you notice yourself thinking "another moment" for the third time, leave. Do not wait for a fourth.

**The Physical Exit**

The crack may feel narrower than when you entered. It probably isn't—but your sense of your own size will have shifted, or your sense of what "narrow" means. Turn sideways. Move slowly. Do not panic if the rock seems to press closer than before. The mountain grew around the hollow because it couldn't incorporate it. It will not close on you. It cannot. The contradiction is still unresolved, and you are part of that irresolution now, briefly. You will fit.

**The First Moments Outside**

Sit against the basalt for a time before descending. The ordinary air will feel thin, almost insufficient, after the humidity of the hollow. Colors may seem too bright or too flat. Sounds will feel like intrusions—the wind, birdsong, your own footsteps. This is normal. Your senses recalibrate. Give them time.

Do not immediately check how much time has passed. It will unsettle you either way—too much or too little will both feel wrong. Wait until you've descended the young mountain, until you're back among things that cast shadows in the correct direction. Then check, if you must. But consider not checking. Consider letting the time inside be its own thing, unmeasured, unreconciled.

**Integration**

In the days after, you may notice small wrongnesses in the world. Water running slightly upward in a glass before you catch it. Shadows disagreeing about direction, then agreeing again when you look closely. Objects drifting slightly toward empty points in rooms. These fade. The hollow's geometry is persistent but not contagious—it stays where it is, mostly, though it leaves impressions on those who visit.

If you dreamed the marks on the stone and wake uncertain whether you've decoded them, that is normal. They were not meant to be decoded. They were meant to record that observation occurred. Your observation now joins them, part of the hollow's long, slow accumulation of seeing.

If you feel called to return, wait at least a season. The hollow doesn't change quickly, and neither should your relationship with it. Let it settle into memory. Let memory do its strange work of preservation and transformation. If you still feel called after the season turns, go back. Bring a new stone. Trace the millipede paths again. Listen for the unborn note.

The hollow doesn't mind. It was here before the mountain. It will be here after. Your visits are nothing to it, or everything, or something in between that you won't have words for. That's fine. Words are young, too. The hollow remembers when there was only observation, only the slow spiral inward, only the patient waiting for everything to finally arrive at the center.

It hasn't arrived yet. But it's getting closer. And now, having visited, so are you.
