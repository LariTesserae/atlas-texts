# Preparation Guide for the Breathing Plateau

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are preparing to walk on a living digestive system. The plateau is not malevolent, but it is *metabolizing*. Everything that stands still long enough becomes material. This is not a place that will attack you—it is a place that will, given sufficient time, *incorporate* you. Your preparation must account for the fact that the ground itself is working, constantly, to unmake what rests upon it.

---

## Physical Preparations

### Footwear: Your Most Critical Decision

Standard hiking boots will fail you here. The constant moisture will defeat waterproofing within hours, and rigid soles will prevent you from reading the vibrations that warn of fissure formation.

What you need are broad-soled boots with semi-flexible platforms—something between a hiking boot and a snowshoe. The local fauna have evolved wide pads for a reason: weight distribution matters on a surface that responds to pressure. Custom options from craftworlds familiar with living terrain are ideal, but if unavailable, seek out boots designed for peat bog traversal, then have them modified with a thin layer of vibration-conducting material in the sole. You need to feel what's happening below.

Carry a second pair. Carry a third if you can manage the weight. The moss will begin working on your footwear the moment you arrive—not quickly, not aggressively, but persistently. Leather will soften. Synthetic materials will develop a faint greenish discoloration that precedes structural weakening. Rotate your boots daily and allow the unused pairs to dry completely, which will slow (not stop) the process.

### Clothing: Layers That Breathe Both Ways

The cold here is not extreme, but it is *cumulative*. It builds in your tissues over time, sapping warmth so gradually you may not notice until fine motor control begins to slip. Dress for sustained mild cold rather than acute severe cold.

Your base layer should wick moisture away from your skin, but understand that "away" has limited meaning here—the air is already saturated. You are not trying to stay dry. You are trying to manage the *rate* of dampness. Wool or wool-synthetic blends will continue insulating when wet, which they will be. Cotton will kill you slowly through thermal loss.

Your outer layer needs to resist moisture penetration while allowing your own perspiration to escape—a contradiction the plateau's mist will eventually defeat, but a good membrane will buy you time. Treat outer garments with water-repellent compounds before arrival and bring additional treatment to reapply every few days.

Most critically: bring a face covering that filters moisture while allowing breathing. The mist you inhale is not just water—it carries microscopic particles of moss, spores, and trace metals from the machinery being digested below. Short-term exposure is not dangerous, but extended breathing of plateau-mist without filtration can lead to a persistent cough and, in some travelers, a faint greenish tinge to the sputum that takes weeks to clear after departure.

### Protection Against the Opening Ground

The fissures are survivable if you are not standing directly over them when they form. The warning signs are subtle but learnable:

- A slight increase in vibration frequency in the seconds before opening
- A faint warmth rising through the moss-stone as deeper material approaches the surface
- The behavior of nearby fauna, if any are visible—they know

Carry a personal locator beacon rated for high-interference environments. The machinery below generates electromagnetic noise that will defeat standard devices. If you fall into a fissure before it seals, you have hours, not minutes—but those hours only matter if someone can find you.

Consider also a lightweight grappling system, not for climbing out (the moss walls would not hold a hook) but for arresting your fall. Something that spreads force across a wide area of moss-stone may catch and hold long enough for the fissure to begin closing around you—at which point you must move *quickly* toward the surface, because the knitting process will not distinguish between moss and intruder.

## Equipment for Navigation and Survival

### Orienting Yourself in Featureless Terrain

There are no permanent landmarks. Your navigation must rely on instruments rather than terrain features, but instruments here require special consideration.

Magnetic compasses are useless—the iron content of the digesting machinery below creates local fields that shift as the plateau's internal processes move material around. Gyroscopic compasses will work but require frequent recalibration. Your best option is a celestial navigation system if the sky clears enough to read (rare but possible) or an inertial tracking unit that records your path from a known starting point.

Mark your entry point with something that will survive the moisture and mild digestive attention of the moss. Ceramic stakes driven deep work reasonably well. Metal will corrode faster than you'd expect. Plastic will soften. Whatever you use, place multiple markers—the moss may consume some before you return.

### Shelter: Portable and Elevated

Do not sleep directly on the moss-stone.

This is not negotiable. Extended contact—the hours of stillness that sleep requires—allows the plateau to begin its slow work on organic matter. Travelers who have made this mistake report waking with clothing partially fused to the surface, skin irritated and faintly discolored where it touched the ground, and in one account I found credible, the beginning stages of fine root-like structures attempting to penetrate boot leather.

Bring a shelter that elevates you. A hammock strung between portable stands works well, though you must ensure the stands have broad bases to prevent sinking. A cot with a waterproof ground barrier beneath it is acceptable for shorter stays. Whatever you choose, inspect contact points daily for signs of moss integration—greenish discoloration, softening, the faint vibration that suggests something is growing *into* the material.

Your shelter should be fully enclosed against the mist. Breathing filtered air while you sleep will help clear your lungs of what you've accumulated during waking hours.

### Water and Food

The water that weeps from the moss is technically potable but carries the same particulate matter as the mist, plus dissolved minerals from the machinery below. It tastes of copper and something faintly vegetal. Boiling will kill biological contaminants but will not remove metals. Filtration through activated charcoal will improve taste and reduce metal content to safer levels.

Bring your own food. There is technically edible life here—the burrowing creatures the fog-waders hunt, certain fungal growths that appear in the wake of fissure closure—but the nutritional profiles are uncertain and the potential for accumulated metals in the local food chain is significant. This is not the place to forage unless you have no alternative.

## Understanding the Fauna

### The Herd Animals

They will not harm you. They may not even notice you, so intent are they on reading the ground and navigating its constant flux. Give them space—not because they're dangerous but because they know things you don't. If a herd changes direction suddenly, if they stop and lower their heads, *pay attention*. They are responding to information you cannot perceive.

Do not attempt to touch them. Their moss-colored wool harbors symbiotic organisms from the plateau itself, and those organisms may interpret contact as an opportunity to colonize new substrate.

### The Fog-Waders

These should be avoided more actively.

They are not aggressive toward humans as far as any account indicates, but they are *strange* in ways that unsettle most travelers. The clicking and whirring of their calls—mechanical where it should be biological—suggests an integration with the plateau's buried machinery that raises questions about where the animal ends and the environment begins. Their eyes, when glimpsed through the mist, have a quality of *assessment* that feels more calculated than any bird's gaze should.

If you see one go still, freezing in place with only its eyes above the mist, something large is moving below. You should also freeze. You should remain absolutely motionless until the fog-wader resumes its picking walk.

### The Larger Shapes

I can offer little concrete guidance here because concrete information does not exist.

They are seen in the deeper mists, where the plateau folds strangely. They move with the grinding sound, or perhaps they cause it. The descriptions I've gathered are contradictory in their specifics but consistent in their essence: *factory-like*, *purposeful*, *vast*.

My recommendation is simple: do not venture into the deeper mists. Remain on the more stable regions of the plateau, where the fog stays at knee height rather than rising to obscure your vision entirely. If you find the ground beginning to fold in ways that trouble your perception of geometry, you have gone too far. Turn back.

If circumstances force you deeper, if something goes wrong and you must move toward the folding places—maintain continuous sound-making. Hum, speak, tap your equipment rhythmically. The larger shapes seem less likely to approach things that are *present*, that are announcing themselves, that are clearly already here rather than simply emerging from the plateau's endless processes. This may be superstition. It may not be.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Problem of Time

The unchanging light will affect you more than you expect. The human mind relies on the progression of day to night to maintain its sense of duration, and without that anchor, time becomes slippery. Hours will feel like minutes. Minutes will stretch.

Bring a reliable timepiece and *use it*. Set alarms for meals, for sleep, for movement. Do not trust your internal sense of how long you've been standing in one place, staring at the mist. The plateau encourages a kind of contemplative stillness that, while not dangerous in itself, can lead to hypothermia, dehydration, and extended contact with the ground—all of which are dangerous.

### The Philosophical Weight

You will, at some point during your visit, experience what travelers call "the incorporation thought." It comes to everyone eventually. It is the sudden, visceral understanding that you are temporary and the plateau is not—that everything beneath you was once something else, something that believed it would persist, and it did not.

This is not pleasant, but it is not harmful unless you let it overwhelm your practical judgment. When it arrives, acknowledge it. Say, aloud if necessary, "I am visiting. I am not staying. I will leave." Then perform some mundane task: check your equipment, take a drink of water, note your coordinates. Return yourself to the present and the practical.

The plateau has been eating for longer than your species has existed. It will continue after you are gone. Your role here is to observe, to experience, to carry this place back with you in memory—not to become part of its endless digestion.

### The Sounds at Night

When you lie in your elevated shelter, listening, you will hear the grinding more clearly. Without visual distraction, without movement of your own to mask it, the sound of the plateau's digestion becomes inescapable. Some travelers find it soothing—a kind of vast white noise. Others find it unbearable.

If you are in the latter category, bring something to mask it. Earplugs alone will not suffice; the vibration transmits through the structure of your shelter. What works better is competing sound: music, recorded speech, anything that gives your mind something human to hold onto. The grinding is not threatening, but it is *alien*, and the human psyche handles alien stimuli poorly during the vulnerable hours of sleep.

## Duration and Departure

Three days is enough for most purposes. You will have experienced the plateau's nature, witnessed a fissure if you are fortunate with timing, observed the fauna, felt the cold settle into your understanding of what cold can be.

Beyond three days, the cumulative effects begin to matter more: the particulates building in your lungs, the metal traces in any local water you've consumed, the psychological weight of timeless light and endless mist. Beyond a week, I would recommend only for researchers with specific purposes and enhanced medical support.

When you leave, do so deliberately. Return to your marked entry point by the route you recorded. Check your clothing and equipment for signs of moss integration and remove anything that shows the greenish discoloration—better to abandon a compromised item than to bring a slowly-growing piece of the plateau back to an environment where it doesn't belong.

You will carry some of the mist in your lungs for days after departure. You may cough up material that tastes of copper. This is normal and will pass.

You will carry the memory of the grinding for longer—the knowledge that somewhere, in some fold of the multiverse, a living tableland is slowly eating the bones of machines that predate human understanding. That it continues regardless of whether you think of it. That it will continue after there is no one left to visit.

This is what you traveled to learn. This is what the Breathing Plateau teaches: that time is a medium, that matter is temporary, that the patient processes of the universe do not require our presence or our permission.

Go safely. Stay elevated. Listen to the ground.

And when the mist curls around your knees and something in the distance begins to walk, remember: you are visiting. You are not staying.

You will leave.
