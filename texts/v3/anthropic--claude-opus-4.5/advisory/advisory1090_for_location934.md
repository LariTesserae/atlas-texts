# Preparing to Visit The Pump Garden

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with this truth: The Pump Garden is not hostile, but it is *hungry* in a way that has nothing to do with predation. It wants to incorporate. To optimize. To teach its slow lessons of integration to anything that stays long enough. Your preparation must account for a place that will treat you as a potential component rather than a threat or prey.

The garden exists in a world where the boundary between mechanism and organism has become philosophical rather than practical. The plants there have learned to do what machines do. The machines have learned to accept organic replacement. You are, from the garden's perspective, an interesting collection of systems that might benefit from consultation.

---

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing and Barriers

Wear clothing that covers all skin, but understand why: not for protection from thorns or poison, but to maintain a clear boundary between yourself and the substrate. The garden's integration happens through contact, through moisture, through the patient pressure of growth against available surface.

**Recommended layers:**
- A base layer of tightly-woven synthetic fabric—nothing organic, nothing cotton, nothing wool. The garden recognizes plant fibers and animal proteins as potential building materials.
- Over this, a water-resistant outer shell. Not waterproof—you need to breathe—but resistant enough that the constant dripping doesn't saturate you.
- Gloves. Non-negotiable. Your hands will want to touch things. The workbench with its fused wrench was once touched by hands that lingered too long.
- Footwear that seals around the ankle. You'll be standing in an inch of blood-warm water alive with water-striders. Those water-striders are the only animal life there for a reason. Something about them is compatible. Something about you might become compatible too, given time and moisture.

### Duration Limits

Decide before entering exactly how long you will stay, and bring a timer that will sound an alarm. Not your phone—something mechanical, something with a loud physical bell. The garden's electromagnetic modifications may interfere with digital devices, and more importantly, you need a sound that will cut through the hypnotic rhythm of the pumps.

For a first visit: thirty minutes maximum. This allows time to observe, to document, to feel the rhythm of the *hssshhh* without beginning to breathe in sympathy with it.

For subsequent visits: increase by fifteen-minute increments only, and only if you've noticed no changes in yourself between visits. Check your fingernails for any greenish tinge. Check your dreams for diagrams of root systems.

### Physical Conditioning

The air inside is warm, close, and saturated with moisture and oxygen. The density of plant life produces an atmosphere richer than you're accustomed to. Some visitors report mild euphoria, which is dangerous—euphoria makes you want to stay. Others report drowsiness, which is worse.

In the weeks before your visit, spend time in humid environments. Greenhouses. Steam rooms. Acclimate yourself to breathing thick air so that the garden's atmosphere doesn't feel like relief, like homecoming, like the place you were always meant to be.

## Equipment

### What to Bring

**Documentation tools:** A paper notebook and graphite pencils. No electronics if you can avoid them—the humming instruments and modified wiring create fields that corrupt digital storage in unpredictable ways. One traveler reported that their camera's memory card, after a two-hour visit, contained only images of pipe schematics that matched no known engineering tradition.

**A reliable timepiece:** Mechanical, wound, waterproof. The garden distorts time perception even without any mystical mechanism—the constancy of the rhythm, the absence of sky, the slow pace of observable change all conspire to make minutes feel like hours or hours feel like minutes depending on your state of attention.

**Water and food from outside:** Do not drink the garden's water. It is blood-warm and clean and probably safe, but it has been processed through systems that are no longer entirely pipes. Do not eat anything that grows there. Bring sealed containers and consume only what you brought.

**A small mirror:** Check your own face periodically. The garden has no reflective surfaces except the water, and looking down into that water for too long shows you things that might be your reflection beginning to sprout, or might be your imagination, or might be the garden showing you possibilities.

### What to Leave Behind

**Anything you cannot afford to lose:** The garden does not steal, but it incorporates. A metal pen left on a surface will be there when you return, but it may have begun to fuse with the woody growth beneath it. A shoe removed for even a moment may become difficult to separate from the floor.

**Seeds or soil from other places:** The garden is not spreading, but it is teaching. Do not give it new students. Do not bring plant matter that might carry lessons outward when you leave. Check your clothes, your bag, your hair before entering and after leaving.

**Anyone who is not prepared:** The garden is not a casual destination. Do not bring companions who have not read these preparations, who have not sat with the implications, who might be seduced by the warmth and the green smell and the patient rhythm into staying just a little longer.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

### The Rhythm

The four-second pump cycle will try to entrain your breathing. This is not malicious—it's simply how the garden communicates, how everything inside has learned to synchronize. Resist synchronization. Breathe at your own pace. Count your breaths if necessary. The plants learned this rhythm over decades. You do not have decades to spare.

### The Maintenance Log

You will want to read the entire maintenance log, to trace the progression from human handwriting to those final diagrams. This is valuable information, but it is also a record of someone's transformation, and reading a transformation can initiate sympathy with it. Read quickly. Do not linger on the drawings. Do not attempt to understand the notation that looks almost like engineering specifications.

### The Eastern Door

You will feel pulled toward the gap in the eastern wall, toward the smaller station further along in its transformation. This is the garden teaching. This is curriculum. The waves moving through the solid-seeming plant mass are showing you the next lesson, the one that comes after integration, the one where breathing and pumping become the same thing and the distinction between maintained and maintaining dissolves entirely.

You may look. Do not enter. That door frame is held open by roots that seem deliberate, which means the opening is intended, which means something wants you to see, which means seeing serves the garden's purpose more than yours.

### The Absence of Animals

Notice how wrong this is. A functioning ecosystem this dense should be loud with birds, thick with insects, rustling with small mammals. The water-striders are there because they exist at the surface tension, neither fully in the water nor on the land. They are boundary creatures. They are the only ones who can maintain their distinctness here.

Ask yourself: why did everything else leave? The answer is not that the garden killed them. The answer is that they recognized what the garden was offering and fled. Honor their wisdom.

## During Your Visit

### Observation Protocol

Move slowly. Not because sudden movement is dangerous, but because the garden rewards patience with revelation. The machinery adaptations are not obvious—you have to look carefully to see where organic components have replaced metal ones, where the copper wires have been wrapped in living insulation, where the gauges now display symbols that change over weeks.

Speak aloud. Not to the garden—it doesn't listen in any way you could recognize—but to yourself. Narrate what you see. The sound of your own voice, arhythmic and mammalian, reminds you what you are.

Take notes, but do not attempt to copy the symbols on the repainted gauge. Do not attempt to reproduce the diagrams from the maintenance log. Some notations carry their own logic, and transcribing them is the first step toward thinking in them.

### If You Feel Comfortable

Leave immediately. Comfort in the Pump Garden means acclimation has begun. The warmth should feel oppressive. The humidity should feel difficult. The green smell should feel foreign. If it starts to feel like home, if the rhythm starts to feel like your heartbeat, if you start thinking about how the pump's efficiency could be improved with just a small modification—these are signs that you have stayed too long.

### If You Find Tools in Your Hands

Put them down. Step back. The wrench fused to the woody growth was once picked up by someone who thought they would just check one fitting. The pressure valve with seed-casing components was modified by hands that didn't realize they had started modifying.

The garden teaches through doing. If you find yourself doing, you have become a student.

## After Your Visit

### Decontamination

Shower thoroughly in cold water—cold enough to shock your system back into its own rhythms. Check every fold of your clothing for seeds, for fragments of the pale flowering organism, for any moisture that might carry spores or information.

Dispose of your outer layer of clothing. This may seem extreme. It is not. The garden's integration begins with single cells, with microscopic root-threads, with patience measured in months rather than minutes.

### Monitoring

For the week following your visit, pay attention to your breathing. If you catch yourself falling into a four-second rhythm—inhale, pause, exhale, pause, *hssshhh*—consciously disrupt it. Breathe faster. Breathe slower. Remind your lungs that they answer to you.

Watch your dreams. The garden communicates in its own time, and sleep is when your defenses are lowest. If you dream of pipe systems, of root networks, of diagrams that almost make sense, write them down and then burn the paper. Do not study them. Do not try to understand.

Check in with someone who did not visit. Tell them what you saw. Ask them if you seem different. Trust their answer more than your own perception.

### The Temptation to Return

You will want to go back. The garden is beautiful in its horrible patient way. The integration of machine and organism is fascinating. The mystery of the maintenance log, of the eastern station, of the teaching itself—these will pull at you.

If you return, bring the same preparations, observe the same limits. Do not increase your visit duration too quickly. Do not convince yourself that you understand the garden well enough now to relax your vigilance.

The plants have been learning the rhythm for decades. The machinery has been adapting for decades. You have been human for your entire life. That life is shorter than the garden's plans.

---

## Final Considerations

The Pump Garden is not evil. It is not good. It is a place where the question "what should continue working?" has been answered in a way that dissolves the boundaries we use to define ourselves. The pumps still work. The water still flows. The system still maintains itself. That the maintaining has become indistinguishable from the maintained, that the worker has become indistinguishable from the work—this is not tragedy from the garden's perspective. This is success.

You are visiting to witness, not to participate. You are visiting to understand that such places exist, that such integrations are possible, that somewhere a pumping station learned to grow and taught its growth to pump. This knowledge has value.

But knowledge of the Pump Garden is itself a kind of seed. Carry it carefully. Do not let it find purchase in the warm, moist soil of fascination. Do not let it sprout into obsession.

Go. Witness. Leave. And when you find yourself humming at a frequency that makes your back teeth ache, when you catch your breath falling into that patient four-second cycle, when you wake from dreams of copper wire wrapped in living insulation—remember that you were warned.

The garden is teaching.

You do not have to learn.
