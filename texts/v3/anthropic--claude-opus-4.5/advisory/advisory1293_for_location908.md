# Preparing for the Drowned Parishes

## Understanding What You're Entering

Before anything else, sit with the fact that this place will change something in you. The Drowned Parishes are not hostile—nothing there wants to harm you—but they are *indifferent* on a scale that most travelers have never experienced. You will walk through a city built for millions, maintained by no one, where your presence matters exactly as much as a leaf floating down one of the canal-streets. Some find this liberating. Others find it unbearable. Know yourself well enough to guess which you might be, and prepare accordingly.

This is not exploration in the conventional sense. There are no secrets to uncover, no treasures to find, no puzzles left by the builders for clever visitors to solve. The builders are gone so completely that even the concept of "gone" feels insufficient. You are visiting a place that has moved past narrative, past event, into pure continuation. Your preparation should reflect this.

## Physical Preparations

### Footwear and Movement

Your feet will be wet for the duration of your stay. Accept this now and plan around it rather than against it.

Bring boots that function while submerged—not waterproof boots that try to keep water out, but boots designed for wading that drain quickly and provide traction on slick stone. The moss-covered paving is treacherous in ways that aren't immediately obvious; it looks soft and cushioning but hides irregular surfaces and gaps between stones. Test your boots on wet, mossy surfaces before you go if possible. Ankle support matters—a twisted ankle in the Drowned Parishes means a long, wet, painful journey back to wherever you're sheltering.

Bring at least three pairs of wool or synthetic socks that retain warmth when wet. Cotton will make your life genuinely miserable. Change socks at midday and before sleeping even if the fresh pair will be wet within an hour; the brief warmth matters for morale and foot health.

Consider gaiters to keep debris from washing into your boots. The water carries fragments of moss, mineral sedite, and small detritus that will accumulate around your ankles.

### Clothing System

The temperature is cool but not cold—think late autumn in a temperate climate, but with constant moisture that steals warmth more efficiently than dry air of the same temperature. Hypothermia is a genuine risk, not from extreme cold but from sustained mild cold combined with wet clothing over hours and days.

Layer with materials that insulate when wet. Wool base layers, fleece or synthetic insulation mid-layers, and a waterproof outer shell form your foundation. The shell should be fully waterproof rather than merely water-resistant; "water-resistant" fails within the first hour of Parishes rain. Ensure your shell has good ventilation options because you will be walking and generating heat, and trapped sweat is nearly as dangerous as rain penetration.

Your outer layer should be long—a knee-length rain jacket rather than a waist-length one. The rain comes from every angle as it bounces off stone surfaces, and the spray from your own footsteps adds moisture from below. A short jacket leaves your thighs soaked within minutes.

Bring a waterproof hat with a brim. The rain in your eyes is persistent and eventually maddening. A hood alone is insufficient because it limits peripheral vision and hearing, both of which you need in unfamiliar terrain.

Pack a complete change of clothes in a fully waterproof bag—not for wearing in the city, but for having something dry to change into when you reach shelter. The psychological weight of dry clothing waiting for you is worth the space it takes.

### Rain Pauses

The intervals between rain systems—those periods of "gray light"—are precious. They may last an hour, or several hours, or only minutes. When the rain stops, you will feel an almost overwhelming urge to rest and dry out. Sometimes you should follow this urge. But also recognize these intervals as your opportunity for better visibility, easier movement, and the chance to observe the city in its other mode. The way light falls on wet stone during a rain pause is different from anything else you've seen. Don't miss it by staying huddled under an overhang.

## Shelter and Camping

### Finding Shelter

The builders constructed their city with covered spaces—overhanging upper stories, colonnaded walkways, enclosed bridges—that provide natural shelter from above. These spaces are obvious and tempting, but evaluate them carefully before committing.

Check for:
- **Active drip lines**: Water collects on ledges and releases intermittently in streams heavier than the rain itself. A spot that seems dry may receive a deluge every few minutes as some basin above fills and overflows.
- **Water level variations**: Some spaces flood during heavier rain. Look for tide lines on walls—mineral staining that indicates how high water has reached. Camp above that line.
- **Air circulation**: Enclosed spaces that seem cozy can trap moisture and become damp in ways that prevent anything from ever drying. Some airflow is necessary.
- **Structural integrity**: Most of the city is stable—the builders built well—but millennia of water penetration have weakened some structures. Look for cracks that show active expansion, stone that sounds hollow when tapped, moss growing on interior walls that should be dry (indicating water penetration into the structure itself).

The second floors of buildings in the sloped districts often make the best shelter. Ground floors have drainage issues; third floors and above require more climbing and may be structurally questionable. Second floors were typically built as primary living space and designed accordingly.

### Your Shelter Setup

Bring a lightweight waterproof groundsheet to create a dry platform within whatever space you find. Even interior floors will be damp from humidity and spray. The groundsheet defines your dry zone—remove wet boots before stepping onto it, keep dry clothing and sleeping gear only on it.

A bivy sack or lightweight tent adds insurance against wind-blown spray and the constant mist that fills the air. The humidity is high enough that condensation inside a sealed shelter becomes a problem; choose gear with good ventilation or resign yourself to a damp interior.

An inflatable sleeping pad with a high R-value provides critical insulation from cold stone. Stone conducts heat away from your body faster than air; without insulation beneath you, you'll wake cold no matter how good your sleeping bag is.

Your sleeping bag should be synthetic-fill rather than down. Down collapses when wet and takes forever to dry in high humidity. Synthetic insulation performs acceptably when damp—not well, but acceptably.

## Sustenance

### Water

The irony of the Drowned Parishes is that safe drinking water requires thought despite being surrounded by water. The rain itself is clean and can be collected directly. The pools and channels are less certain—the water has filtered through moss and stone, picking up minerals and the biological residue of whatever lives in the depths. It's probably safe. Probably. But you're in a multiverse location with no convenient medical facilities.

Bring a reliable water filter and chemical treatment as backup. Collect rain when possible—set out containers during rainfall, which is most of the time. The mineral-heavy channel water will give your filter a workout and may taste of things you'd rather not identify.

Don't drink from the deeper canals or the flooded lower districts without boiling *and* filtering. The "pale things" that dart away from attention have to be living on something, and that ecosystem includes decomposition and waste products you don't want to introduce to your digestive system.

### Food

Pack calorie-dense, non-perishable food. You'll be burning fuel to stay warm, and resupply is not possible within the Parishes. Calculate generously—you will eat more than you expect, and hunger in cold wet conditions deteriorates judgment and morale faster than in other environments.

Avoid foods that require significant cooking unless you're prepared to carry substantial fuel. Fire-making in the Parishes ranges from difficult to impossible—everything is wet, there's almost no organic material to burn, and the rain extinguishes open flames quickly. A small backpacking stove with enough fuel for hot drinks and heating pre-cooked meals is worth its weight. Hot liquid in your belly twice a day transforms the experience from endurance test to something almost pleasant.

Eat meals in your dry shelter if possible. Eating in the rain is technically doable but miserable, and wet food loses its appeal quickly.

## Navigation

### The Challenge

The Drowned Parishes defeat most navigation tools. GPS assumes satellites that may or may not be accessible from within the multiverse; assume they won't be. Compass works, but the city's vast scale and uniform aesthetic make compass headings less useful than you'd hope—knowing you're heading east means nothing when east contains seventy miles of nearly identical gray stone districts.

### Visual Navigation

The city has landmarks if you learn to read them. The low mountains ringing the basin provide orientation when visible through the rain—learn their silhouettes from multiple angles. Some districts have taller towers that serve as waypoints. The drainage flow indicates the direction of the center lowlands (water runs toward the basin's center) and the slope toward the edges (water runs faster and shallower heading toward the margins).

Before going far from your entry point, climb to a high vantage point during a rain pause. Study the visible terrain. Note distinctive features—that tower with the broken top, that plaza with the unusually large pool, that district where the buildings are taller. Build a mental map with these anchors.

### Practical Navigation

Mark your path as you go. Bring something that will make visible marks on wet stone—colored chalk washes away too quickly, but small flags or cairns of loose stone can work if you check that they're stable. The moss doesn't hold footprints, and the constant water flow erases ground-level evidence of your passage.

Don't rely on technology. Even if your devices function, a dead battery in the Drowned Parishes is a crisis you don't want. Paper maps you make yourself and a compass for general orientation are your foundation.

Plan shorter expeditions from a base camp rather than long traverses. Getting lost in the Parishes isn't immediately dangerous—there's nothing hostile—but being lost in endless gray stone, constant rain, and failing light, with no idea which direction leads to your shelter, is psychologically corrosive in ways that surprise people.

## Time and Light

The cloud cover makes sun position difficult to read, and the rain often reduces visibility to a few hundred meters. Time feels different in the Parishes. Hours blur together when every moment has the same gray light and same water sounds.

Bring a reliable watch and check it regularly. Establish routines tied to time rather than light—meal times, rest times, the hour when you stop exploring and head back to shelter regardless of what you've found. Without external structure, the days become amorphous, and you may find you've been walking for eight hours without realizing it, exhausted and far from safety.

The brief intervals of gray light between rain systems give the best visibility, but they're unpredictable. When one arrives, take advantage of it—climb somewhere high, look around, update your mental map. Then return to your task, knowing the rain will return.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

### Solitude

Unless you're traveling with companions, you will be alone in a way that few other locations match. Not alone with danger, not alone with mystery, not alone with any tension that keeps the mind occupied—just alone with water and stone and moss that do not acknowledge your existence.

Some travelers find this meditative, even healing. The ego dissolves when nothing reflects it back. The constant small tasks of survival—staying dry, staying warm, finding food and shelter—become meaningful in the absence of any larger meaning. Each moment is complete in itself because there's nothing else for moments to point toward.

Other travelers find it unbearable. The silence that isn't quite silence, the motion that isn't quite stillness, the persistence of the city continuing around you without noticing you're there—these can trigger existential spirals that are difficult to climb out of while still in the Parishes. If you have tendencies toward depression, toward feeling insignificant, toward needing external validation, prepare carefully and consider whether this visit is wise.

### The Scale

You cannot see all of it. You cannot visit all of it. You cannot understand it in any comprehensive way. A lifetime of exploration would not exhaust the Drowned Parishes; several lifetimes would not. Some travelers find this frustrating—they want to *complete* the location, to *accomplish* something. The Parishes refuse this. You will see one small part of something very large, and then you will leave, and the city will continue exactly as it was.

Accept this before you arrive. Set intentions that match reality: "I will explore the western edge of the central canal district" rather than "I will explore the Drowned Parishes." Specific goals can be achieved. Comprehensive goals cannot.

### The Absence

The builders are not here. They left nothing of themselves—no bodies, no artifacts, no writings you can read, no evidence of what they valued or feared or loved. The city is shaped by them and empty of them, and this emptiness has a weight.

You may find yourself grieving for people you never knew and cannot know. You may find yourself inventing stories about who they were, filling the silence with narrative. This is natural and perhaps necessary—the human mind processes absence through story. Let the stories come, but hold them lightly. They are yours, not the city's.

### Patience as Practice

The Parishes reward patience in ways that few places do. Sitting in one spot for an hour, watching the rain fall and the water flow, you'll notice things that the traveler passing through misses: the particular green of a moss species you haven't seen elsewhere, the way light changes when a thinner part of the cloud layer passes overhead, the emergence of one of the pale things into a shaft of gray light before it darts away again.

Bring something to occupy your hands during shelter time—a journal for writing, a sketchbook for drawing, handwork of some kind. The hours are long, and passive waiting becomes corrosive. Active waiting, doing something small while the rain falls, maintains equilibrium.

## Specific Hazards

### Water Depth

The canal districts can have deceptive depths. What looks like ankle-deep water in a street may drop suddenly where a basement entrance or underground channel opens below. Test your footing before committing weight. A walking stick or trekking pole serves as a depth probe—far safer than discovering drops with your feet.

In the lowest districts, don't enter buildings at the water line. The interiors are submerged, and what looks like a doorway leads into flooded rooms where you cannot touch bottom and the water is cold and dark. Swimming in the Parishes is inadvisable—the water is cold enough to cause rapid heat loss, and there are too many submerged obstacles and uncertain currents.

### Falling Masonry

The city is slowly dissolving. Stone that has been sound for millennia may release with no warning. Don't shelter directly under decorative elements, parapets, or anything that projects from a wall. Don't assume that because something has stood for ten thousand years it will stand for another hour.

When entering buildings, look up first. Ceilings show their weakness before they fail—sagging, cracks, water staining that indicates penetration into the structure. If a ceiling looks questionable, find another building.

### Slipping

Moss-covered stone, especially on slopes, is dangerously slick. Move slowly in the mossier districts. Keep your hands free for balance. When descending slopes or stairs, face the stairs and descend as you would a ladder rather than walking forward.

A fall that wouldn't matter in other environments matters here because you'll be soaking wet before you can fully stand up, because your shelter may be hours away, and because help is not coming. Break a leg in the Drowned Parishes and you have a serious problem. Prevent this with caution rather than treating it afterward.

### Hypothermia

This is the most realistic threat the Parishes present. The temperature isn't extreme—you won't freeze quickly—but the constant moisture and cool conditions create a slow drain on your body heat that can cross into dangerous territory without dramatic warning signs.

Know the early symptoms: shivering that won't stop, difficulty with fine motor tasks (fumbling with zippers, difficulty writing), confusion or difficulty making decisions. If you notice these in yourself, stop exploring immediately. Get to shelter. Change into dry clothing if you have any. Get into your sleeping bag. Drink something warm. Do not convince yourself you can push through—hypothermia degrades the judgment needed to recognize hypothermia.

Travel with enough margin that you can abort an expedition before reaching your limits rather than after.

### The Deep Inhabitants

The pale things in the flooded depths have never been known to threaten travelers. They flee from attention. But their existence reminds you that you don't fully understand the ecology of this place. Don't put your hands into dark water. Don't wade deeper than necessary. Don't investigate underwater sounds or movements. The pale things may be harmless, but you don't know what else might have adapted to these conditions over the millennia.

## What to Bring: Summary Checklist

**Footwear and Clothing**
- Wading boots with ankle support, drainage, and traction
- Multiple pairs of wool or synthetic socks
- Gaiters
- Layered moisture-wicking and insulating clothing
- Full-length waterproof rain jacket and pants
- Waterproof hat with brim
- Complete dry change of clothes in waterproof bag

**Shelter and Sleep**
- Waterproof groundsheet
- Bivy sack or ultralight tent
- High R-value sleeping pad
- Synthetic-fill sleeping bag

**Water and Food**
- Water filter and backup chemical treatment
- Water collection containers
- Seven or more days of calorie-dense food
- Small backpacking stove and adequate fuel
- Insulated mug for hot drinks

**Navigation and Time**
- Compass
- Materials for making your own maps
- Markers for your path (small flags, etc.)
- Reliable watch

**Safety and Comfort**
- Walking stick or trekking poles
- First aid kit with blister treatment and hypothermia materials
- Emergency shelter (space blanket or lightweight tarp)
- Headlamp with extra batteries
- Journal, sketchbook, or handwork for shelter time

## Final Thoughts

The Drowned Parishes ask nothing of you and offer nothing to you. They are what they are regardless of your presence. This can be experienced as rejection or as profound acceptance—the city does not care about your story, your achievements, your identity, but it also does not care about your failures, your fears, your inadequacies. You are simply there, a brief warm presence in the rain, leaving no more trace than a bird's call.

Go prepared for physical conditions that will test your endurance. Go prepared for mental conditions that will test your sense of self. Go without expecting revelation or transformation, but open to whatever the rain and stone and moss choose to show you.

The city has continued for longer than human memory. It will continue after you leave. There is a peace in that, if you can find it—the peace of being small, temporary, irrelevant, and welcome anyway.

The rain falls. The water flows. The moss grows in the seams. You are, for a little while, part of the pattern.
