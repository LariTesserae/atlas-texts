# What Lives in the Incubation Vent

---

**The Threadkeepers**

They are not one species but a behavior that has emerged across several—a convergent solution to the vent's central problem, which is that everything here is always moving and nothing is stable.

The first Threadkeepers you notice are the small ones: insects with elongated abdomens that trail silk behind them as they navigate the fissure. They don't build webs for catching prey. They build *infrastructure*. Their lines run horizontally across the vent at irregular intervals, anchored to the warm stone on both sides, creating a grid of reference points in the vertical dark. Other creatures use these threads for navigation, for rest, for the laying of eggs in locations that won't shift when the rock decides to grow another few millimeters.

But the insects learned this behavior from something older.

Forty meters above the gantry, in a section of the vent too narrow for your shoulders, lives the thing the researchers in the notebook called *Der Faden*—The Thread. The name is written in German, then crossed out, then written again with a question mark. Their sketches show something like a worm, something like a ribbon, something like a rope that has learned to move. It is perhaps three meters long when fully extended, though it rarely extends fully. Mostly it loops and coils through a vertical territory it has claimed, its body pressed into crevices, its presence marked by the silk it leaves behind.

The silk is not silk. Under magnification—there's a note about this, water-damaged but legible—the fibers are mineral-organic hybrids. The Thread secretes something that crystallizes in contact with the vent's humid air, creating lines stronger than spider silk and conductive to vibration. The Thread does not have eyes. It has its network. Every footfall, every wingbeat, every shift in the growing rock reaches it as information. It knows the vent the way your tongue knows the inside of your mouth.

It eats, but not often. The cord-salamanders avoid its territory. The insects that become tangled in its fibers are absorbed slowly, over days, their bodies converted into more thread-stuff. But mostly it simply *maintains*. When a anchor point fails—when the rock cracks and a line goes slack—the Thread travels to the break and repairs it. When new fissures open in the growing stone, the Thread explores them, claims them, webs them into the larger network.

There are other Threads. Three, perhaps four, in other sections of the vent. The researchers found evidence that they communicate through their shared infrastructure—vibrations passed from network to network, a language written in tension and release. They may coordinate. They may compete. They may simply coexist, each tending its own section of the dark, each building toward something no single organism will live to see complete.

The small insects that trail silk behind them—they hatched in the Thread's territory. Some of them carry fragments of Thread-silk in their spinnerets, hybrid material they couldn't produce on their own. They are not offspring. They are not parasites. They are something the vent has made: a relationship that has no name in the biology you know.

**The Lung-Moths**

The ones clustered on the walls near the surface—the ones so thick they obscure the rock—are sleeping. They sleep during the vent's exhale phase, when warm air rises past them toward the cold. They wake during the inhale, the brief reversal that happens when conditions above shift, when cold air falls into the fissure and the temperature differential momentarily inverts.

During the inhale, they fly.

Not upward, toward the light. Downward, into the warmth, against the current that usually carries everything toward the surface. They are enormous for moths—wingspan wider than your hand—and their flight is laborious, a constant battle against the physics of the space they've chosen to inhabit. They fly because that's when the pollen falls.

Not true pollen. The coral-growth, the pale branching structures that have colonized the upper reaches—they reproduce through spores, but their spores are heavy, loaded with mineral content, unable to ride the vent's exhale the way lighter particles would. The spores sink. They sink through the brief windows when the air reverses, drifting down through the fissure toward the moss zones below, where they will land and fail to germinate in conditions too wet and too warm for them.

The lung-moths intercept them.

Their bodies are covered in structures like feathers, like ferns, like the gills of mushrooms—branching surfaces that maximize area. The spores stick. A single moth in flight might carry thousands of them, a visible coating that changes the color of its wings from wet slate to something paler, something that glows faintly with the bioluminescence the spores carry from their parent structures.

The moths don't eat the spores. They *breathe* them.

Their respiratory systems—documented in the notebook with increasing excitement, diagrams drawn and redrawn as understanding developed—are not like other insects'. The tracheal tubes that carry oxygen to their tissues have evolved to also carry something else: mineral compounds, extracted from the spores, deposited slowly in their wing structures. The wings of old lung-moths are heavier than they should be, stiff with accumulated crystal. Eventually they can no longer fly. They crawl to the warmest parts of the vent, wedge themselves into crevices, and die with their wings spread.

The wings don't decompose. The chitin breaks down, but the mineral lattice remains—a structure like frozen lace, anchored to the rock, catching light and moisture. The coral-growth colonizes these structures preferentially. The dead wings become scaffolding for new life, carrying the spores back upward through generations of deposition, slowly building the vent's upper reaches into something more complex than stone.

The lung-moths are not pollinators, exactly. They are not decomposers. They are a circulatory system, moving material from height to depth and back again, their brief lives a single pulse in a process that spans centuries.

**The Harmonics**

The clicking birds—the swift-like things that hunt on the wing—are only part of a larger phenomenon. The vent's acoustics are not incidental to its ecology. They are *used*.

The birds click because clicking works. The narrow walls, the irregular surfaces, the humid air—they create an environment where sound bounces in predictable ways, where a trained ear can map the space more accurately than any eye. But the birds are newcomers to this strategy. The real masters are older and stranger.

In the deepest accessible reaches of the vent, below the gantry, below the moss zones, where the rock is warmest and the mineral seep is thickest, something lives in the walls. Not on them. In them. The stone is not solid—it's porous, riddled with bubble-chambers from volcanic gases that escaped as the formation cooled. These chambers connect through passages too small for anything with a skeleton. What lives in them has no skeleton.

The researchers called them the Choir, though they never saw them directly. What they saw was evidence: holes in the rock that seemed to breathe, membranes stretched across openings that vibrated when sound passed through. What they heard was the sound those membranes made.

The vent's constant thrum is not just insects. Underneath the wing-drone, underneath the clicking and shuffling, there's a bass note—a frequency you feel more than hear, a vibration that resonates in your chest cavity. This is the Choir. The membranes are their voice. They produce sound continuously, a tone that shifts in pitch as conditions change, as the rock warms or cools, as the air pressure rises or falls.

Other organisms respond to this tone. The insects move away from certain frequencies, toward others—herded, guided, their migrations shaped by a sound they may not consciously hear. The cord-salamanders follow. The birds adjust their clicking to avoid interference. The entire vertical ecosystem flows around a framework of sound produced by something that has never been seen, that may be one organism or many, that may be aware or may simply be alive in the way that a heart is alive—pumping, rhythmic, essential.

The researchers tried to find them. The notebook documents expeditions into side-fissures, attempts to trace the membrane-holes back to their source. The expeditions ended. The notes don't say why. The last entry about the Choir is a single sentence, half-obscured by water damage: *"We think it knows we're listening."*

**The Graf**

Eighty meters above the gantry, past the section claimed by the Thread, past the lung-moth colonies, there's a ledge. It's not natural—the rock has been worked, chipped and smoothed to create a platform perhaps a meter wide and two meters long. Something lives there.

The researchers called it the Graf—the Count—though again, it's not clear if this is a species name or something more specific. Their notes suggest they observed it for weeks. Their sketches show something mammalian: small, compact, furred in grey-white that blends with the unfinished rock. It has hands, of a sort. It uses them.

The Graf collects.

Its nest—platform, den, workshop—is crowded with objects. Dead insects, arranged by type. Shed feathers from the clicking birds. Fragments of lung-moth wing, the crystalline remnants. Threads of silk from the Threadkeepers' network. Pieces of the pale coral-growth. Mineral deposits pried from the walls. Items from the abandoned research station: a lens, a coil of wire, a piece of laminated paper with most of its text worn away.

These objects are organized. Not randomly, not haphazardly—sorted into categories that shift over time. The researchers watched the Graf rearrange its collection constantly, moving items from pile to pile according to some system they couldn't decode. It would spend hours examining a single fragment, turning it over in its hands, holding it up to what little light reached its ledge. Then it would place the fragment somewhere specific and move on to the next.

It noticed the researchers. It watched them back.

When they left equipment unattended—a temperature probe, a sample vial, a pencil—the Graf took it. Not immediately. It would wait, sometimes for days, until the object was clearly abandoned. Then it would climb down from its ledge with careful, deliberate movements, retrieve the item, and add it to the collection.

The researchers tried leaving things deliberately. A mirror. A piece of colored glass. A watch that no longer worked. The Graf took all of them. It examined the mirror longest, holding it at various angles, watching its own reflection with an attention that the notebook describes as *"uncomfortably focused."*

There is only one Graf. The researchers never found evidence of others, no breeding population, no young. It may be very old. It may be the last of something. It may be something else entirely—a scout, a remnant, an emergence.

The last note about the Graf is not about the Graf directly. It's about the collection. The researcher writes: *"I think it's making something. Not building—not a nest, not a tool. Making something out of understanding. It looks at each piece and it looks at the whole and it's trying to find how they fit. It's trying to find the pattern. I think it's been trying for longer than we've been watching. I think it's been trying for longer than we've been here."*

---

**The Unfinished**

The vent is still forming. This is not metaphor. The rock is young, geologically speaking—centuries old at most, a crack that opened when the mountain shifted, a wound that filled with warmth and moisture and the possibility of life. The things that live here have not finished becoming what they will be.

The insects are diversifying. Every season, every generation, there are variants—slightly different wing structures, slightly different feeding behaviors, slightly different responses to the Choir's bass note. Most variants fail. Some persist. The vent is a crucible, selecting for whatever works in this specific, unrepeatable environment.

The moss is spreading. The rust-red variety has begun appearing in the mid-reaches, where it never grew before. The black variety, the one that smells of copper, is developing structures that might be fruiting bodies, or might be something else—the researchers weren't sure.

The coral-growth is climbing. Year by year, centimeter by centimeter, it follows the lung-moths' crystal deposits upward toward light it will never reach. It doesn't need the light. It just grows toward it, an orientation that served some ancestor in some other place, repurposed here into a slow vertical migration that will continue until the vent closes or the growth finds something else to orient toward.

And underneath all of this, the Choir hums. The frequency shifts. The ecosystem adjusts. The Thread repairs its network. The Graf adds another fragment to its collection. The lung-moths sleep, waiting for the breath to reverse.

You are watching something emerge. Not an ecosystem in equilibrium—a becoming, a process, a draft of what life might look like in this particular crack in this particular mountain. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here knows what it will be.

The gantry groans beneath you. The bolts have shifted another fraction of a millimeter. The rock is growing around them, incorporating them, making them part of the vent whether they wanted to be or not.

You are a visitor. You are also evidence—another data point, another encounter, another object for the Graf to contemplate if you leave anything behind. The vent doesn't care about you, but it will notice your passage. Something will. Something is noticing everything, cataloging everything, trying to find how it all fits.

The light flickers. The insects flow. The breath continues, in and out, in and out, the mountain exhaling warm air into the cold, the cold falling briefly back into warmth.

You climb.
