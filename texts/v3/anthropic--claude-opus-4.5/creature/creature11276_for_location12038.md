**The Keeper of Balanced Salts**

She has no name she would recognize as such, but if you watched her long enough—and you would need to watch for months to understand her patterns—you might call her something like *the one who tends the equilibrium*, or more simply, *the woman who drinks the room*.

She is not young. She is not old in any way that makes sense against the room's timescale, but she has been here long enough that her skin has taken on the particular pallor of someone who lives by thermal light and mineral glow. Her hair, once dark, has gone the color of the amber deposits along the basin's edge—not gray, not white, but that specific yellow-brown of iron-stained calcium. She keeps it wrapped in a length of corroded copper mesh she found fitted between two pressure gauges, soft now from years of handling.

She came here through the hatch seventeen years ago, or nineteen, or twenty-three. She was fleeing something—she remembers the fleeing more than she remembers what from. A debt, perhaps. A person. A version of herself that had become untenable. The room was supposed to be temporary shelter while she decided what to do next.

What happened instead was that she began to understand the water.

---

She drinks from the basin every morning, if morning is what you call the hours after she wakes on her pallet of layered rust-flakes and salvaged cloth tucked behind the defunct pressure regulator. The water tastes different each day—not dramatically, but in ways she has learned to read. More copper means the overnight condensation favored the eastern pipe-cluster. More calcium means the thermal upwelling was stronger, pulling mineral-heavy moisture from whatever aquifer feeds the geothermal system below. She has mapped the room's moods through her tongue.

She eats the beetles when she can catch them, though she does not catch them often. They are not nutritious enough to sustain her fully, and so she leaves every nine or ten days, climbing out through the hatch in the blue hours when the streets above are emptiest, foraging from the city's margins, returning with dried foods that keep in the heat: hard bread, salted fish, roots that do not mind humidity. She stores these in a cache of glass bulbs she carefully emptied and cleaned, their original purpose—pressure indicators, perhaps, or chemical reservoirs—now obsolete.

The city does not know she exists. She prefers this.

---

The larger shape in the far darkness is hers, in a sense. She did not bring it; she found it here, or it found her, in the early months when she was still learning the room's geography. It is a cat-sized something that is not a cat. Six legs, or perhaps eight—she has never seen it fully, only in the peripheral glimpses the room allows. Its eyes, when they catch the mineral glow, are the pale green of weathered copper. Its body is furred in something that might be actual fur or might be a dense growth of the same fungal fibers she sometimes finds colonizing the dampest pipe-joints.

She calls it *the other*, not as a name but as a description. They have an arrangement: she does not enter its territory behind the tertiary condensation array, and it does not disturb her sleep. Sometimes she leaves beetle carapaces near the border between their spaces, and sometimes they are gone when she returns. Whether it eats them or simply moves them elsewhere, she does not know.

They have never touched. She is not certain it is touchable, or that touching it would leave her hand unchanged.

---

Her days, such as they are, involve maintenance she was never asked to perform but has taken on as a kind of devotion.

She clears the mineral deposits when they grow thick enough to impede the basin's overflow channels, using a sharpened length of pipe as a chisel. The crystals she removes are sorted: the pure white calcium goes into a pile she has been building for years in a corner, a cairn now knee-high, purposeless except as evidence of her presence. The amber pieces she sometimes keeps, turning them in the dim light, finding faces in their fractures.

She oils the valves, though they will never turn again. She wipes condensation from the glass faces of the pressure gauges, though their needles are frozen. She traces the rust-maps on the walls with her fingers, memorizing their borders, checking for expansion—the rust has not moved perceptibly in her time here, but she watches anyway, because watching is what she does now.

Once, she found a seam in the floor grating that was beginning to weaken, a joint where the metal had thinned past safety. She spent three weeks reinforcing it with wire stripped from a defunct cable conduit, wrapping each connection until her fingers bled copper-green. The room did not thank her. The room does not notice her. But she noticed herself noticing, and that was enough.

---

She talks to the room sometimes, in the long hours between water-drips.

Not conversation—she is not mad, or not mad in that particular way. More like annotation. She names what she observes, quietly, in a language that has become her own through years of solitary use: *the eastern cluster is weeping heavy today* or *the resonance is deeper, something must be running in the far systems* or simply *eight seconds, eight seconds, eight seconds*, counting the drips like a meditation.

The room does not answer, but it holds her words in its acoustics, lets them bounce softly from pipe to pipe before they fade. She finds this comforting. Her voice, at least, belongs here. It has become part of the equilibrium.

---

She knows she will die in this room.

Not soon—she is healthy, in the way that people who live simply and move constantly through cramped spaces remain healthy. But eventually. And she has thought about what this means, has made a kind of peace with it.

She will lie down on her pallet behind the pressure regulator, and her breathing will slow to match the water-drip, and then it will stop. And the beetles will find her, eventually, and whatever the *other* is will perhaps investigate, and the heat will do what heat does to bodies, and she will become part of the mineral content of the room. Calcium from her bones will join the crystalline shelves along the basin's edge. Iron from her blood will add, infinitesimally, to the rust-maps on the walls.

The room will not notice. The cycle will continue. The condensation will fall at eight-second intervals onto water that now contains some particle of her, and the equilibrium will hold, and the city above will go on not remembering its own name.

She finds this, strangely, beautiful. To become part of something that works, that continues, that needs nothing and offers nothing but persists anyway—she cannot think of a better way to stop being herself.

---

But that is later. That is years from now, perhaps decades.

For today, she wakes in the thermal dark. She drinks from the basin—more copper than yesterday, the eastern cluster must be particularly active. She watches the beetle trace its familiar route along the pipe-maze, pausing to drink from condensation beads. She checks the overflow channels, finds them clear. She adds a small amber crystal to her cairn.

She sits with her back against a riveted plate, feeling the ancient warmth rise through the grating, listening to the room's sounds layer themselves into something that is almost music: drip, tick, hum, the soft scratch of the *other* moving in its territory, the distant resonance of systems she will never see.

She is content. She is alone. She is exactly where she chose to be, even if she did not know she was choosing at the time.

The room breathes around her, and she breathes with it, and the water continues its patient cycle, and the rust holds its borders, and nothing requires anything of her except her own quiet presence, which she gives freely, because it is all she has left, and it is enough.
