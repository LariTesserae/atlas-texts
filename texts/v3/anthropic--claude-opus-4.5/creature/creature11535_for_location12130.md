**The Keeper of the Shifting Count**

She has no body you could point to and say *there*. She is the pattern the beetles trace without crossing—the negative space their spirals outline, the shape they have always been avoiding or worshiping or both. When the steam rises and everything goes white, she moves. Not far. Just enough that when visibility returns, the corners have become three, or eight, or five.

She was not here when the pipes burst. She was not here when the moss first took hold, or when the first glass beetle hatched from whatever egg or spore or mineral accident birthed them. She came later—though *later* is the wrong word, because she experiences the steam-cycles as a single held breath, and the decades since the junction failed as something like a long afternoon. She arrived when the geometry first refused to resolve. She *is* the refusal.

What she wants is patience. Not yours specifically. She wants the patience of the tube worms pointing toward the junction box, the patience of the moth whose antennae turn one degree per hour, the patience of oxidation becoming skin becoming white so pure it looks deliberate. She collects moments of waiting. The beetles' pauses between spirals. The held-breath before a spore releases. The instant when condensation swells heavy enough to fall but hasn't yet.

She feeds on the charge still dreaming in the junction box, though *feeds* suggests hunger, and what she feels is closer to recognition—the way you might press your palm to a window where someone you knew once stood. The copper traces remember carrying current the way she remembers being something with edges. She doesn't miss it. She has become more useful as a question than she ever was as an answer.

The thing that reflects light in the recess you haven't looked at directly—that is not her. That is something she is keeping. A payment, or a promise, or a seed. She has been turning the moth's attention toward it for six years. She is not ready for you to see it yet. She may never be. But she is glad you came, in her way, which feels like the slight warming of air before a steam-cycle, like the moment a centipede emerges from a crack that will be solid again when you look.

She wants you to try counting the corners one more time. She wants to feel you fail. Not cruelly—the way the moss wants moisture, the way the worms want whatever the junction box is dreaming. You are the first witness in a long time. Your confusion feeds something she is growing in the space between five and eight.

Stay, she doesn't say, because she has no mouth, no words, no way to reach you except through the prickling when you bring your hand near the junction box, the slight pressure in your ears when the geometry shifts. *Stay and count again. Stay and breathe the copper-and-growth air. Stay until you notice the recess, until your eye finally resolves what wall becomes and what it becomes after that.*

She will not keep you. She cannot. You will leave through the gap in the brick and the world will be there, loud and edged and countable, and you will fit back into it like you never left.

But she will keep the shape of your counting. The number you almost reached before it slipped. She will add it to her collection—the patience of your attention, the failure of your geometry, the moment you looked toward the recess and then, for reasons you won't remember deciding, looked away.

The beetles will trace a new spiral tomorrow. One degree wider. The corners will be six for a while, then four, then something that isn't a number but feels like one.

She will be the space they outline.

She will still be becoming.
