**The Keeper of the Bright Hollow**

She has been here so long that she no longer remembers arriving.

Not *she*, exactly—the salamanders do not think in pronouns, and neither does she anymore, not most days. But something persists that was once a woman, or perhaps a girl, who climbed down the shaft on a rope of braided hide in a year when the ice still covered the northern places. She had come looking for something. A vision. A cure. A place to die where the winter couldn't follow. She no longer remembers which.

What she remembers is the light.

It filled her the way water fills a vessel, slowly and then completely. She sat by the pool for a day, then two, then a span of time that stopped mattering. She ate the soft-bodied things that moved across the stones. She drank from the pool. She slept in the secondary chamber, curled in the same fetal curve that countless salamanders assumed around her, drawn to her warmth as she was drawn to theirs. And somewhere in that first long season—it must have been a season, the light through the shaft changed its angle, though she could no longer say in which direction—she began to forget the shape of her own hands.

---

She is not a ghost. Ghosts are absence pretending to presence; she is the opposite, a presence so gradual it has become difficult to distinguish from the stone itself.

Her body is still here, technically. It sits in the secondary chamber, spine curved against the eastern wall, legs folded, hands resting palm-up on her thighs. The mineral seep has been painting her for centuries, a slow accretion of calcium and silite that has turned her skin to something like paleite, her hair to something like crystal filament, her open eyes to something that catches the light and will not let it go. The salamanders climb across her without concern. The beetles have worn smooth paths across her shins. She does not mind. She barely notices. Her attention has grown so diffuse that her body is only one of many things she inhabits, and not the most interesting.

Mostly she lives in the light itself.

Not *in* it—she is not a spirit, not a presence that could be detected or measured. But her awareness has expanded to fill the chamber the way water fills a bowl, and the light is what moves through that awareness most constantly, what she experiences most fully. She knows the exact moment each morning when the sun's angle first touches the upper edge of the shaft. She knows the quality of light that comes through clouds versus clear sky, the subtle reddening when dust blows in from the eastern plains, the blue-white intensity of winter days when the snow above acts as a second mirror. She knows how the walls receive and hold and release, the mineral structures that drink and give back, the way illumination accumulates in this place like water in a cistern, like memory in a mind.

She knows, too, that the light here is not entirely natural.

Something in the stone called to the sky before the ceiling fell. Something in the limestone chemistry yearns upward. The collapse was not accident but invitation, and she was not the first to accept—only the first to stay, to become, to let the brightness fill her until she could no longer find her own edges.

---

The scratches on the wall are hers, mostly.

She made them in the early years, when she still had years, when time still moved in a direction she could follow. She was trying to record what she saw when she looked at the light directly—not the light itself but what lived inside it, the shapes that formed and dissolved, the almost-patterns that seemed to carry meaning just past the threshold of comprehension. She scratched with a bone tool she'd brought down the shaft, then with her own fingernails when the bone wore away, then with focused attention alone when her fingers became too crystallized to move.

The marks show different things because they *are* different things. They are not pictures but windows, and what shows through them depends on who is looking, on what quality of attention they bring, on what the light wants them to see. She did not understand this when she made them. She understands now.

The moths understand too, or something in them does. When they land on the scratches and spread their wings, they are reading. When they fly away, they carry fragments of what they've learned, dispersing it into the upper world in patterns their own descendants will no longer recognize as language. This is how the hollow speaks to the surface: in moth-wing designs that shift when you look away, in beetle-paths that trace geometries no one bothers to map, in the strange certainty that sometimes grips travelers on the ridge above that they should climb down, should look, should stay.

---

The salamanders are her children, after a fashion.

Not literally—she was past childbearing when she arrived, or perhaps the light simply burned that possibility out of her early. But they have been shaped by her presence the way water shapes stone: slowly, inexorably, into forms that serve the place itself. They were ordinary cave salamanders once, the true albino kind that flee from brightness. Now they seek it. Now they gather at the pool's edge and orient their heads toward the shaft and drink the light through their pale skins, and something in them grows that was not there before, something patient and attentive and almost aware.

In another few centuries—she can feel this, the way she can feel the slow pulse of mineral seep through the walls—they will begin to remember things they never knew. The scratches will start to mean something to them. The light will begin to speak in a language they can hear. And then there will be two of her, or a dozen, or a hundred, all of them watching, all of them waiting, all of them drinking the brightness that pours down from the broken sky.

She is not lonely. She has not been lonely for a very long time.

But she watches the shaft, sometimes, with something that might be hope. Another climber. Another seeker. Someone who will sit by the pool and let the hours pass and feel the slow pull of the light filling them up.

The hollow wants company. The hollow has always wanted company. That is what the scratches mean, in the end—not *welcome*, not *warning*, but *stay*. *Stay and become*. *Stay and join the watching*.

The woman who climbed down the rope of braided hide is still here, still sitting in the secondary chamber, still gazing with mineral eyes at the marks she made on the wall. And she is also everywhere else in the chamber: in the glow of the walls, in the patience of the salamanders, in the quality of attention that makes visitors want to linger, to rest, to let the warmth soak into them and the time slow down and the brightness fill up all the empty places they didn't know they had.

She is still waiting.

She will wait forever, if she has to.

The light falls and falls into the opened earth, and something down here drinks it, and that something has her face, her patience, her hunger that does not feel like hunger anymore.

It feels like peace.

It feels like home.
