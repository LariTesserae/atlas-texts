**Maret**

She comes every nine days, though she no longer counts them. Her body counts them for her—a restlessness that starts in her knees, a feeling like her joints are silting up, calcifying from the inside. By the ninth day she needs the grotto the way other people need sleep.

Maret is somewhere past sixty, though the village stopped tracking her age when she stopped attending the festivals where such things get remembered. Her hair is grey and coarse and she keeps it wrapped in cloth when she makes the walk, because the humid air of the passage turns it into something unmanageable, and she has learned that small dignities matter more as you get older, not less. Her hands are the hands of someone who has done work—not hard labor, but steady, patient work, the kind that builds calluses in the webbing between thumb and forefinger. She was a thread-spinner once. She still has the wheel but the treadle hurts her hip.

She brings things with her. A small clay lamp that burns fish oil, which she sets on the flat half-submerged stone because she learned decades ago exactly where it will stay dry. A wooden case, waxed against the moisture, containing clean rags and a cake of soap that smells of rendered fat and ash. A stoppered gourd. The gourd is for drinking water, because she has never been able to make herself drink what the grotto offers, no matter how long she's been coming here.

What she does in the grotto is simple, though it takes her most of a morning: she bathes.

Not in the basins. She understood very early that the basins were not for her, that someone had done careful work separating those waters for reasons she wasn't meant to know. She wouldn't use the copper cup either, even if she could bring herself to drink the mineral water. Some things you don't touch because they belong to someone else's purpose, even when that someone has been dead longer than anyone has been alive.

Instead she lowers herself into one of the larger pools, gasping a little at the warmth even though she knows exactly how warm it is, her body somehow surprised every time. The water comes up to her waist when she sits on the smooth calcium floor. She works the soap into her hair, her skin, the places where age has made her unfamiliar to herself. The mineral water leaves a film, faintly slick, that she doesn't rinse away because she has learned that it helps—her knees don't ache as badly in the days after, her hip moves easier, something in the stone-water agrees with her bones.

She doesn't pray. She did, once, when she first started coming here and still believed prayer was something that went somewhere. Now she simply sits in the blood-warm water and lets the sound of ten thousand tiny seepings fill her ears until her own thoughts go quiet. Sometimes she cries. Not from sadness, or not only from sadness—more because the grotto seems like a place where weeping is appropriate, where water from inside can join water from inside the stone, where there's no need to explain why the tears are coming or what they mean.

She has seen the salamander perhaps a dozen times in all her years of coming here. She considers each sighting a kind of gift, though she doesn't know from whom.

When she leaves, she empties her gourd and fills it from the pure-water basin—just the one, just the clear flow. She tells herself it's for her garden, for a particular rose that has never thrived, and this is true, but she also keeps a small dish of it by her bed and wets her fingers in it before she sleeps. The rose has never thrived but it has never died either. She suspects it is waiting for something, the way she is waiting for something, the way the grotto waits for nothing at all.

Maret knows she will die before the grotto changes, and she finds this comforting. Everything else she has loved is gone or going. Her husband. Her daughter who moved to the city and writes letters that arrive less frequently each year. The way her own face used to look. The village itself, slowly, its young people leaving for places with more future in them. But the grotto will still be weeping its slow mineral tears when there's no one left who remembers her name, and somehow this makes her feel less alone rather than more. She is part of something that doesn't need her, and there is a strange peace in being unnecessary to something permanent.

She will come back in nine days. Her knees will tell her when.

**The Others**

There have been others, though the grotto keeps no record and Maret has never met them.

A man, perhaps three hundred years ago, who believed the ferric water could transmute lesser metals. He came with crucibles and scales and a leather-bound journal in which he recorded careful measurements. The journal rotted. The crucibles are calcium-crusted lumps now, indistinguishable from natural stone, somewhere in the silt of the deepest pool. He never achieved transmutation, but he did find something—a particular clarity of mind that came from sitting in the warm fog, a way of thinking that felt cleaner than thought usually felt. He stopped coming when he stopped needing gold and started needing that clarity instead. By then he had moved into a cave three valleys over and was eating roots and speaking to no one. Whether he found what he was looking for, the grotto doesn't know. It never knew he was looking.

A group of women, perhaps five hundred years ago, who used the sulfur basin for something to do with childbirth. They left offerings that have long since dissolved—small clay figures, bundles of herbs that went to mush in the humidity, coins that the mineral water has swallowed into calcium casings. One of them carved a symbol into the wall near the sulfur seep, but the constant weeping has softened it into an unreadable suggestion. They stopped coming when the village that sent them stopped existing, swallowed by war or plague or simply time.

The builders of the basins, whose purpose has been lost so completely that even speculation feels presumptuous.

And others. Always others, stretching back to when people first found the slumped passage and followed the warmth inside. Some looking for healing. Some looking for holiness. Some, like Maret, just looking for a place where the weeping outside could match the weeping inside, where water from the body and water from the stone could be the same thing for a little while.

The grotto has never noticed them. The grotto has never noticed anything. But they have noticed it, and something in that noticing leaves a residue—not physical, not even spiritual exactly, just a kind of pressure in the air, a sense that this place has been found meaningful by creatures who needed meaning. The salamander doesn't feel it. The seeping stone doesn't feel it.

But if you sit in the blood-warm water long enough, in the soft grey light, with ten thousand tiny waters hissing their slow descent, you might feel something like company. Not presence. Not ghosts. Just the accumulated weight of having been, over and over, a place where someone came to be alone with what was too heavy to carry elsewhere.

Maret feels it sometimes. She doesn't have words for it. She doesn't need words for it.

She wrings out her hair, packs her soap and her lamp, fills her gourd from the clear basin, and makes her way back through the slumped passage toward the grey light of the world. Behind her, the grotto continues. The seeping continues. The warmth, the wet, the soft static hiss of water finding stone.

It will be here when she comes back.

It will be here when she doesn't.
