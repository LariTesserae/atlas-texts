**The Stillwater People**

They are not a single creature but a dispersal—a loose kinship group of perhaps thirty individuals spread across several square miles of the basin, close enough to hear each other's long calls across the wet stone, far enough apart that they rarely touch.

They move on four limbs when crossing open rock, hands and feet splayed wide to distribute weight across the smooth hardpan, but rise to two when wading through the ankle-deep channels, arms held slightly out for balance, fingers trailing in the water as if reading it. Their skin is the color of the pale ochre stone with darker mottling that echoes the mineral veins—not camouflage exactly, because nothing here hunts them, but a kind of rhyme with the landscape, a family resemblance earned over generations beyond counting.

They are smaller than you might expect. The largest adults stand perhaps to your shoulder when upright, and their bodies have a quality of *reduction*—limbs slender, torsos narrow, skulls smoothed to essential curves. Like the basin itself, they seem to have arrived at what they always meant to become. No bulk for the sake of bulk. No ornamentation. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted for the grey days under cloud cover, pupils wide even at noon, and their ears are small, pressed close to their heads, because in this place of constant sound the challenge is not hearing but selecting, filtering, finding meaning in the chorus.

---

They eat what the basin offers, which is nearly everything and never quite enough of any one thing to make them specialists. Frog eggs scooped from still pools and swallowed in handfuls, the texture like cool tapioca. The tender growing tips of rushes, stripped and chewed to sweet pulp. Algae scraped from warm rocks with the edges of flat stones—the only tools they use with any regularity, and even these they rarely carry, just pick up and set down as needed. The dark shapes that slide through deeper channels they catch sometimes, when the creatures are sluggish in the morning cool, pinning them with quick hands and biting behind the head. The meat is oily and dense and they eat it immediately, not because they couldn't preserve it but because the thought of preservation has never occurred to them. Why store what seeps up endlessly from the rock?

They drink constantly, almost without noticing, lowering their faces to the water-films or cupping handfuls to their mouths while doing other things. Their bodies run warm and damp; their skin never fully dries. If you touched one—and they would let you, more from indifference than trust—they would feel like the smooth stone after rain, blood-warm and slightly slick.

---

Their voices are mid-range and carrying, built for the same acoustic environment as the frogs. They speak to each other across distances in long, vowel-heavy calls that slide between tones—not singing, exactly, but not speech as you would recognize it either. More like weather. The calls communicate location, intention, general state: *I am here, I am moving toward the eastern pools, I am content* or *I am here, I am still, something is not right*. Up close, in the rare moments when two or three of them come together beneath one of the solitary trees, the calls compress into something more like conversation—shorter, softer, punctuated by silences during which they watch each other's faces with those large dark eyes, reading expressions you cannot read.

They do not name each other. Or rather, they name each other constantly, provisionally, descriptively: *the one near the warm seep*, *the one whose child died in the last rains*, *the one who found the eel-run*. Identity is situational, relational, a matter of where you are in the basin and what you have done recently enough to be remembered. The very old are sometimes called by chains of such descriptions that have accreted over decades, litanies of location and event that serve as a kind of history, but even these are not fixed—they shift as the speaker's relationship to the subject shifts, as the relevant past recedes or surges forward.

---

They gather fully only when the afternoon rains come.

It happens the same way each time. The clouds thicken, the light goes grey-green, and the frogs fall silent in anticipation. Across the basin, the dispersed people begin to move toward the solitary trees—not rushing, never rushing, but converging with a slow inevitability, drawn by something older than decision. By the time the first drops fall, fat and blood-warm, twenty or thirty of them have collected in the wide shadows, sitting or squatting on the darker, damper rock, their mottled skin beading with rain they do not wipe away.

They do not speak during the rain. They sit together, shoulders sometimes touching, and they listen. The rain on stone, on water, on the canopy above them. The resumed chorus of frogs. The particular sound of water finding its level, trickling into fissures, spreading in films. Their faces, when you can see them through the grey curtain of rain, are slack and inward, eyes half-closed. They are not meditating or praying—those words carry intention they would not recognize. They are simply *being rained on together*, which is the closest thing they have to ritual, to religion, to the acknowledgment that they are not alone in the world.

When the rain stops—and it always stops, after an hour or two, the clouds thinning and the even light returning—they disperse again without ceremony. They wade back out into the channels, following the water toward their preferred pools and seeps, and soon the basin holds only their distant calls, indistinguishable from the frogs unless you know what to listen for.

---

They raise their children slowly, in the way of creatures with few predators and long memories. A child stays close to its mother for years, learning the basin's geography through her feet—which stones are warm, which channels run cool, where the eels congregate in the season when the water drops. The fathers are present but unattached, drifting through the mother's territory for a season or two before moving on, contributing food and occasional protection but not continuity. The children grow into the dispersal gradually, their calls stretching across greater distances, their returns to the mother less frequent, until one day they simply do not return and are thereafter encountered only as *the one who was born near the orange lichen*, *the one whose mother knew the deep pool*.

They do not bury their dead. They do not need to. The basin takes them back—the warm water, the patient chemistry of dissolution, the frogs and eels and dark sliding shapes. A body left on the wet stone is bones within a season, and the bones wear down like everything else,ite given over to the same processes that smoothed the mountains to blue-grey suggestions on the horizon. The living do not mourn in any way you would recognize. They speak the dead's names less frequently, then not at all, and the names fade as names do when no one is speaking them. What remains is the basin, and the chorus, and the rain.

---

They are not wise. They have no accumulated knowledge beyond what each generation teaches the next: where to find food, how to read the water, when to gather under the trees. They have no stories about the distant past because the past is indistinguishable from the present—the basin has been this way longer than they have been anything, and it will continue being this way after they are nothing again. They do not wonder about the blue-grey rises on the horizon or what might lie beyond them. They do not make art or music beyond their functional calls. They do not dream, as far as anyone could tell, of anything other than water and stone and the bodies of their scattered kin.

But they are *content*—and that word, with all its quiet weight, is the truest thing that can be said about them. They have fitted themselves to a world that asks little and offers just enough, and in that fitting they have found something that is not happiness, exactly, but is not suffering either. They are the basin's thought about itself, if the basin could think. They are what remains when everything that could be stripped away has been stripped away.

If you walked toward them across the warm stone, following the water-channels, they would watch you come with their large dark eyes. They would not flee or approach. They would wait, and if you stopped near them, they would return to what they were doing—scooping frog eggs, chewing rush-stems, listening to the chorus. You could stay among them for days, for weeks, and they would neither welcome nor resist you. They would simply continue being what they are, and in time you would understand that this is not indifference but something older and more generous: the patience of creatures who have never needed to decide whether the world is good or bad, because the world is simply *there*, wet and warm and endlessly seeping, and they are in it, breathing water, waiting for the rain.
