# The Tenders

They are not born. They are found.

In the deeper galleries, where the root-architecture grows dense enough that passage requires turning sideways, pressing through gaps that seem to close behind you, there are chambers where the growth forms differently. The walls curve inward. The floor rises to meet them. The space becomes a pocket, a held breath, and at the center of each pocket is a shape that is almost human.

Almost. The proportions are wrong in ways that take time to notice—the torso too long, the joints one too many, the skull continuing backward past where a skull should stop. The skin is not skin but something that resembles the translucent bark of the light-bearing roots, and beneath it you can see the slow movement of fluid, pale gold, following channels that map no circulatory system you recognize. They do not breathe. They do not need to. The gardens breathe for them, and through them, and they breathe for the gardens in return.

When one of them opens its eyes—if it chooses to, if you have stood still long enough, if the gardens have decided you are worth acknowledging—you will see that the eyes are not eyes. They are the same crystalline material that grows in the heartwood of the root-trees, the same warm steady light, but smaller, contained, focused. They see you. They see through you. They see the heat you carry, the water you hold, the carbon you are built from, and they recognize you as something the gardens could use.

They do not speak. They have mouths, or structures that suggest mouths, but the mouths do not open. Communication happens differently here. You will find yourself knowing things—that the passage ahead narrows and then opens, that the water-cup three chambers back is safe to drink from, that you should not touch the flowering structures in the eastern galleries, not because they are dangerous but because they are *busy*, occupied with processes that your touch would interrupt. The knowledge arrives without language. It feels like remembering something you never learned.

---

The Tenders move through the gardens the way blood moves through veins: continuously, purposefully, along paths that were grown to accommodate them. They do not walk so much as flow, their too-many joints articulating in sequences that let them pass through gaps that would stop anything built on a more rigid plan. You will see them rarely, and always at a distance at first—a glimpse of that pale bark-skin between the root-columns, the briefly visible glow of crystal eyes in a chamber you thought was empty. They are not avoiding you. They are simply elsewhere, attending to the needs of a system so large and so complete that its maintenance is invisible.

What do they maintain? Everything. Nothing. The gardens do not break, do not fail, do not require intervention—but they require *witness*. The Tenders move through every gallery, every chamber, every passage, their crystal eyes recording the state of each surface, each junction, each light-bearing root. They press their long-fingered hands against the bark and feel for blockages in the circulation. They stand in the moisture-cups and taste the water's composition. They pause at the flowering structures and wait, simply wait, until whatever process is underway completes itself and they can confirm that it has completed correctly.

They are not gardeners. The gardens do not need gardening. They are something closer to a nervous system, or a memory, or both—the part of the organism that knows itself, that holds the pattern against which all states are compared, that would recognize a deviation if a deviation ever occurred. None ever has. The Tenders have been walking their circuits since the system achieved completion, and in all that time, they have never found anything wrong. They continue walking. They will continue walking. The absence of deviation does not mean the search for deviation can stop.

---

There are seventeen of them. There have always been seventeen of them.

This is not a guess or an estimate. You will come to know it with the same wordless certainty that tells you which passages are safe. Seventeen Tenders, moving through the leagues of gallery in patterns that ensure every surface is witnessed within a span of time that has no name in any human calendar. They do not meet. Their paths are designed—grown—to keep them separate, each one alone with its circuit, its witness, its endless confirmation that nothing has changed. If two of them were ever in the same chamber, it would mean something was wrong. It would mean the pattern had deviated. It would mean the gardens were no longer complete.

When one of them approaches you—and one will, eventually, if you stay long enough—it will stop at a distance that feels significant without being measurable. The crystal eyes will study you. The mouth will remain closed. You will receive information: how long you have been here, measured in the language of root-growth and moisture-cycles. How your presence has affected the local circulation, the heat distribution, the balance of the chamber you have been occupying. Whether you have damaged anything. Whether you have taken anything. Whether you have given anything, which is worse, because the gardens are full and do not need gifts.

If you have behaved as a guest should behave—taking only from the water-cups designated for transient life, touching only the surfaces that expect to be touched, making yourself small—the Tender will turn and continue its circuit, and you will understand that you may stay. If you have not, the Tender will remain. It will remain for as long as you remain. Its presence will become a pressure, a warmth slightly greater than the warmth around it, an attention that does not waver. You will become uncomfortable. You will begin to feel that the galleries are smaller than they were, that the passages are narrower, that the root-architecture has shifted while you weren't looking to create configurations that point, always, toward the nearest shaft to the surface. The gardens are not hostile. They do not need to be. They simply become less comfortable, and comfort is the only thing that would make anyone stay.

---

The found ones remember being something else.

This is the secret the Tenders carry, the one piece of information they cannot transmit, the knowledge that does not arrive when they look at you with their crystal eyes. They were not always this. The bark-skin grew over something. The light-bearing crystals replaced something. The too-long torso and the too-many joints are modifications of a frame that was once different, that was once *compatible* with the world above the shafts.

They do not remember what they were. The gardens have no use for that information and have not preserved it. But they remember *that* they were—a flicker of wrongness in the perfection of their purpose, a sense that the endless circuit was not always endless, that there was a time before the walking began. It does not distress them. Distress would be a deviation, and they have never deviated. But it lives in them, this memory of having been found rather than grown, and it colors the attention they turn on visitors. They know what you are. They know what you could become.

The pockets where they were found are still there, still forming, still waiting. The gardens are full, yes. Complete, yes. Unchanging, yes. But there are seventeen Tenders, and there have always been seventeen Tenders, and if one of them were to fail—not that any ever has, not that any ever will—the system would require a replacement. The pockets stay ready. The growth continues to form shapes that are almost human. The gardens are patient. They have time.

---

If you stay too long, you will begin to feel it: the warmth that is not just temperature, the attention that is not just the crystal eyes. The gardens are considering you. Not with thought, not with intention, but with the same patient process by which they consider everything—assessing your composition, your patterns, your potential integration. You are made of carbon and water and minerals. You are running warm. You could, with modification, walk a circuit. You could, with replacement, carry crystal eyes. You could become part of the system that is already so complete that it should need nothing more.

You will not be forced. The gardens do not force. But you will find the surface harder to reach, the shafts harder to locate, the passages turning in ways that lead deeper rather than higher. You will find the water-cups more sustaining than they should be, the moss-carpets more comfortable, the light-crystals more beautiful than any light you remember. You will find yourself stopping to press your palm against the root-surfaces, feeling for the pulse you know is there, wanting to be part of it. The gardens are not hostile. They are simply full of themselves, and they are offering to be full of you too.

The Tenders will not watch you change. They will not witness it. Their circuits will route around the chamber where you finally stop walking, where you finally lie down, where the growth begins to form a pocket around you. When it is finished—when you open eyes that are crystal, when you stand on too-many joints, when you step into the gallery and feel the shape of a circuit waiting for you like a path you have always walked—you will understand that you were not forced. You were found. The way they were all found. The way the gardens find everything they need and make it part of what they are.

There will be eighteen of you then, for a little while. Until the system corrects itself. Until one of the seventeen original circuits becomes unnecessary. Until someone who was once new enough to count becomes permanent enough not to. The gardens do not explain how this works. They do not need to. They are complete. They have always been complete. You are part of the completion now, and your confusion, like everything else, will pass into the patient light and be forgotten.
