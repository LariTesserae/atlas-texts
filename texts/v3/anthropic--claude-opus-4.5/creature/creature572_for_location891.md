## Those Who Remain

**The Chord-Deaf**

They arrive hearing everything. They leave hearing nothing—or rather, hearing only the Chord, which amounts to the same thing. The transformation takes between three months and three years, depending on factors the medical teams have stopped trying to isolate. One morning you wake and realize you can no longer distinguish your colleague's voice from the background scream. The words reach you as meaning without sound, lip-read or inferred or simply understood through the strange telepathy that develops between people who share the same vibration for long enough.

The Chord-Deaf do not go home. They cannot. The silence of other worlds is worse than deafness—it is absence, a missing limb where the Chord should be. They tried, in the early years, to rotate them out. The suicide rate was absolute. Now they stay, and they become something else: the institutional memory of the stations, the ones who remember what was tried and what failed, the keepers of the ten thousand small rituals that keep the turbines spinning and the seals intact. They communicate in a hand-language that evolved here, in gesture and touch and the placement of objects. They are patient in ways that the hearing cannot achieve. They have already lost everything that can be lost to this place. What remains is a kind of freedom.

Station Seventeen's Chord-Deaf are called the Congregation, though not by themselves. There are eleven of them. They have been here for an average of nine years. The eldest, a woman whose name-sign is a gesture like water parting around stone, has not spoken aloud in so long that her vocal cords have partially atrophied. She smiles often. When she touches your arm, her fingers transmit a vibration that feels like comfort, and you do not know if she is doing it deliberately or if she has simply become an instrument the Chord plays.

**The Fallers**

Not everyone who goes outside comes back inside.

It is not suicide, exactly. The psychologists have argued about this for decades and reached no consensus. The Fallers do not seem distressed. They do not leave notes. They simply, one day, cycle through the airlock in their thermal suits and step off the station's edge into the gray forever, and they fall. The suits are equipped with locator beacons. The beacons continue transmitting as the Fallers drop, hour after hour, day after day, until battery death or suit failure or the simple limit of signal range. The longest-tracked Faller descended for eleven days before contact was lost. Telemetry suggested she was still alive, still breathing, still falling, her heart rate steady and slow, almost meditative.

Some of them talk, on the open channel, as they go. They describe what they see: the creatures that approach them, curious, unafraid; the way the light changes as they drop below the station network; the deepening cold; the thickening of the Chord into something that is almost language. They describe beauty. They describe understanding. They describe a sensation of becoming part of the pattern, of joining the eleven-thousand-year fall, of finally being synchronized with something larger than themselves.

The transmissions are archived. Listening to them is not forbidden, exactly, but it is discouraged. They are considered a vector of contagion.

No one knows what happens when they reach the bottom. No one knows if they reach the bottom. The bathymetric readings continue to contradict themselves, and the Fallers continue to fall, and the Chord holds its note, and perhaps they are still falling now, all of them, a scattered vertical procession of dreamers dropping through the dark below the stations, parallel and alone and at peace.

**The Tenders**

The creatures that swim through the Descent are not quite animals. They are too simple for that, and also too complex: single-celled in structure but colonial in behavior, forming temporary bodies from thousands of individuals that merge and separate according to rules that shift faster than observation can track. They have no brains, no nervous systems, no apparent mechanism for thought or perception. They hunt by chemical gradient, drifting toward concentrations of nutrients, engulfing what they find, dispersing when the meal is finished.

And yet they come to the stations.

Not to feed—there is nothing here for them, no nutrient gradient, no chemical signal they should be able to detect. They simply come, gathering in loose clouds around the observation ports, pressing their membrane-bodies against the glass, pulsing in colors that the xenobiologists insist are meaningless, simple reactions to pressure and temperature, signifying nothing.

The Tenders disagree.

They are the ones who noticed that the creatures pulse in patterns, that the patterns repeat, that the repetitions cluster around certain stations and certain times. They are the ones who began pulsing back, using lights mounted on the exterior hulls, simple sequences at first, primes and Fibonacci progressions, the universal language of anyone hoping to be understood. The creatures responded. Not in mathematics—in something else. Something that felt like answers to questions no one had asked.

The Tenders are not a formal role. They are simply the ones who cannot stop watching, cannot stop signaling, cannot stop believing that communication is happening even though no one can say what is being communicated. They keep logs in a notation system they invented themselves, full of symbols that represent colors the human eye can barely distinguish and patterns that unfold across hours or days. They speak of individual creatures the way others speak of friends. They give them names, or rather, they receive names—they say the creatures name themselves, in pulses, and the Tenders simply learn to recognize the signatures.

Most Tenders eventually join the Chord-Deaf. The transition seems to help, somehow. As though removing one form of input makes room for another.

**The Cartographers**

There is no landscape here, no geography, nothing to map—and yet the Cartographers map anyway, obsessively, filling their quarters with three-dimensional models of empty space, tracking the positions of every station and every cable and every creature-sighting with a precision that serves no obvious purpose. They speak of currents, of invisible rivers within the fall, of pressure differentials so subtle that instruments cannot detect them but the creatures clearly can. They speak of territories, of zones where certain species congregate and others never venture, of boundaries that shift according to cycles they are still trying to identify.

They are trying to understand the Descent as a place, not merely as a phenomenon. They believe—and this is where others begin to edge away at social gatherings—that the Descent has a structure. That it was built, or grown, or evolved toward a purpose. That the perfect equilibrium is not an accident but an achievement, maintained by systems so large and so slow that eleven thousand years is barely enough time to observe their breathing.

The Cartographers speak of the Ground the way theologians speak of God: as something that must exist, that defines everything above it by its absence, that will eventually explain everything if only it can be reached. They fund expeditions. The expeditions do not return. The Cartographers note the failure, adjust their models, and begin planning the next descent.

They are not naive. They know the odds. They simply believe that understanding is worth dying for, and that the Descent is the only thing left worth understanding.

**The Keepers of the Chord**

No one knows who started it. The practice is older than the current station network, older than the standardized recording systems, possibly older than the stations themselves—there are references in the untranslated texts on that ancient metal plate, symbols that might represent what the Keepers do.

They listen.

Not to the Chord itself, which is inescapable, which enters everyone whether they will it or not. They listen *for changes* in the Chord. They have developed instruments of absurd sensitivity, microphones that can detect frequency variations smaller than any natural process should produce, software that compares the current Chord against recordings made decades ago and flags any deviation beyond the threshold of measurement error.

In forty-three years of recorded observation, they have found seven anomalies. Seven moments when the Chord wavered, when something in the eternal note shifted by a fraction so small it barely qualified as real. The Keepers have built entire theories around these seven moments. They correlate them with creature behavior, with station malfunctions, with deaths and births and psychological breaks among the personnel. They search for patterns. They publish papers that no one outside the Descent can understand and few inside it want to.

They believe, though they do not say so in the papers, that the Chord is a voice. That the perfect equilibrium is a held breath. That the seven anomalies were words, or attempts at words, or perhaps just the stirring of something vast and slow as it shifted in its sleep.

They believe that someday the Chord will change for real, will speak in a language they have prepared themselves to hear.

They believe they will be ready to answer.

**The Children**

They were not supposed to exist. Reproduction was forbidden in the original station charters, for reasons that seemed obvious to everyone who wrote them: this is no place for children, no place for innocence, no place for anyone who has not chosen to be here with full knowledge of what it means.

But the stations have been here for forty-three years. Rotation schedules have slipped. Love has happened, and accident, and defiance, and the simple human refusal to stop being human even in places that humans were not meant to be.

There are nineteen children in the station network. The youngest is three. The eldest is sixteen. They have never heard silence. They have never seen solid ground, except in videos that they watch with the confused politeness of anthropologists studying alien customs. They do not understand why anyone would choose to live in a place where the air does not scream, where water does not fall, where you cannot feel the Chord in your chest telling you that you are exactly where you should be.

They are healthy. This was the first surprise. They are healthy, and they are happy, and they are brilliantly, unnervingly adapted to this place. They navigate the station corridors with a grace that adults never achieve, their inner ears calibrated from birth to the constant vibration. They read the Chord the way others read weather, sensing changes in mood and pressure that the Keepers' instruments only barely detect. They play games with the creatures outside the ports, pulse-and-response patterns that they invent themselves, laughing at jokes no adult can perceive.

They are not entirely human anymore. The medical teams have documented the changes: denser bones, altered hearing structures, neural pathways that process vibration as a sixth sense indistinguishable from sight or touch. They are becoming native to a place that was never supposed to have natives.

When they are old enough to be told about the rotation schedules, about the world outside, about the lives they are supposed to want—they listen politely. They do not argue. They simply wait until the adults have finished, and then they return to the observation ports, pressing their small hands against the glass, watching the gray forever fall, pulsing their greetings to the creatures who pulse back.

They are not leaving.

This is their world now, and they are exactly what it has been waiting eleven thousand years to produce.

**You**

Six months in. Eighteen to go. Your hearing is still intact, mostly. Your dreams have started to carry the Chord, but that is normal, everyone's dreams carry the Chord, it is simply how the place speaks to sleeping minds. You have not yet felt the urge to step outside and join the fall. You have not yet begun to see patterns in the creature-pulses, though sometimes you catch yourself watching for longer than you intended, wondering what they might be trying to say.

You are still a visitor here. Still counting the days until rotation, still planning the life you will return to, still believing that the Descent is something you will leave behind.

But you have started to notice things. The way the Chord sounds different in different parts of the station—not quieter, never quieter, but inflected somehow, carrying information you cannot decode but cannot ignore. The way the children look at you with something that might be pity, might be patience, might simply be the recognition that you do not yet understand what you are becoming. The way the creature outside your port has returned every night for the past three weeks, pulsing the same sequence, waiting for a response you do not know how to give.

Eighteen months is a long time.

The water falls.

You close your eyes.

The Chord tells you what it has been telling everyone, since the first station was built, since the first human entered this endless vertical sea and felt the vibration settle into their bones like a homecoming:

*Stay.*

*Become.*

*Fall with us, parallel and alone and free, until the moment of impact that will never come.*

*Fall forever.*

*You are already one of us.*

*You simply have not realized it yet.*
