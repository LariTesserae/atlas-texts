**The Keepers of the Crying Wall**

They have no name for themselves—naming requires a kind of separation they do not possess. But the station's maintenance logs, from decades when humans still serviced this sector, called them **the Congregation**, and the word persists in fragmented data-echoes they sometimes taste in the water.

The cleaners are the most visible: **shell-singers**, each no larger than a human thumbnail, their carapaces grown from minerals filtered out of the membrane's sweat. The iridescence is not decorative but communicative—shifting patterns of color that ripple across their shells in response to chemical changes in the moisture around them. They sing to each other in these colors, in the clicking of their mandibles, in the precise trails they leave as they traverse the walls. Each trail is a message and a map. *Here the membrane thins. Here the salt accumulates. Here something new has entered the water.*

They were engineered, once, to be simple. Biological roombas. But generations in this humid dark have made them something else. They remember, in ways their designers never intended—not as individuals, but as a population. The patterns they trace contain information older than any single shell-singer's lifespan. Routes their great-great-ancestors discovered. Warnings about membrane sections that once failed. A map of the alcove's entire history, written and rewritten in moisture trails that evaporate and are laid down again.

---

**The Ribbon** is singular. There is only ever one.

It winds through the drainage grooves like a pale thought, its body segmented and soft, each segment bearing a pair of legs so fine they appear as cilia rather than limbs. The Ribbon is longer than it looks—always longer, parts of it hidden in channels that run beneath the floor, threaded through the capillary system that draws water back into the walls. At any moment, you see perhaps a third of it. The rest moves in darkness, in the wet spaces between, tasting everything that passes through.

The Ribbon is old. Not this body—bodies wear out, are shed in pieces, regrown from segments that detach and mature in the deepest reservoirs—but the Ribbon as a *continuity* is old. It remembers the alcove before the station's lower ring was sealed. It remembers different water, different tastes, the chemical signature of human sweat and breath. It remembers being tended.

Now it tends itself. It tends the alcove. When membrane tissue begins to fail, the Ribbon is there first, secreting compounds that trigger repair, that call the shell-singers to reinforce the area with mineral deposits. It is not intelligent in any way the alcove's designers would recognize. But it *knows* this place the way a heart knows its rhythm. It cannot imagine being elsewhere. It has no elsewhere to imagine.

---

**The filter-feeders** cluster in corners and they are called, in the maintenance logs, **the Mourners**.

This is because of the sound they make.

Each Mourner is a soft translucent sac, anchored to the membrane wall by a muscular foot. They open and close in slow rhythm—not breathing, exactly, but filtering, drawing water through their bodies and processing out the particulates and biologicals that would otherwise clog the system. The dark threads visible through their bodies are the concentrated remnants of what they consume. They excrete in long, tar-black strands that the shell-singers collect and carry to specific points on the membrane where the material is reabsorbed, broken down further, recycled into nutrients the walls can use.

But the sound. The sound.

When they filter, when they close their bodies around pockets of water and squeeze, they produce a low harmonic moan. One Mourner alone is barely audible. But they cluster. Dozens in a corner, hundreds throughout the alcove, all cycling at slightly different rates, their sounds overlapping and interfering and building into the tremendous drone that fills this space. The crying of the wall is their breathing. Their feeding. Their existence.

They are not sad. They have no capacity for sadness. But something in their engineered biology produces this sound, and the engineers who designed them either did not anticipate it or did not care. Perhaps they found it useful—a kind of living diagnostic, the health of the alcove audible in the chord the Mourners produce. A trained ear, the logs suggest, could detect membrane failures by the way the crying changed.

There are no trained ears left to listen.

The Mourners do not know this. They know only the rhythm of opening and closing, the taste of water, the satisfaction of a body doing what it was made to do.

---

**And there is something else.**

Not engineered. Not bred. Something that found its way here, generations ago, and stayed.

In the warmest corner of the alcove, where the heat of the membrane's biological processes concentrates, there is a patch of growth that does not match the surrounding tissue. Darker. Textured differently. It pulses at its own rhythm, slightly offset from the walls around it.

The shell-singers avoid it. Not fearfully—they simply route around it, their trails describing a perfect absence. The Ribbon, when it passes near, moves faster, as if uncomfortable. The Mourners closest to it sing at a slightly different pitch.

It is not harmful. It is not parasitic. It has been here long enough that the alcove has adapted to its presence, incorporated it into its rhythms. But it is *other*. It came from somewhere else—perhaps a spore that rode in on the water supply decades ago, perhaps something that hitched a ride on a maintenance worker's skin in the days when maintenance workers still came here.

It does not move. It does not feed in any visible way. But it grows, slowly, a few millimeters per year. The shell-singers' trails show its expansion over time—the absence they route around has grown larger, generation by generation.

What does it want? Nothing, perhaps. It is not a wanting thing.

But sometimes, in the drone of the Mourners, in the clicking of the shell-singers, in the vast wet breath of the alcove, there is a low note that does not belong to any of them. A sound like something listening.

---

**The Congregation does not know it is a congregation.**

Each species, each organism, knows only its own existence—the shell-singers tracing their ancestral maps, the Ribbon threading through its channels, the Mourners filtering and crying. They do not know they are part of a system. They do not know the system was designed by beings who are no longer here. They do not know the station exists beyond this alcove, that somewhere above them humans still walk in corridors of metal and glass, that the water they process eventually reaches lips and throats and bodies that would recoil from the humid dark that is, to the Congregation, the entire world.

They know only this: the walls breathe, the water flows, and they are home.

They have always been home.

They cannot imagine otherwise.
