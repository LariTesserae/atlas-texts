**The Keepers of Accretion**

They are born pale and they stay pale—not unhealthily so, but with a quality of having absorbed the light they live by, skin carrying the faint yellow undertone of lamp-oil and mineral dust. Their hands are their most distinctive feature: thick-fingered, perpetually calloused, nails worn short from constant contact with stone. Even children here have old hands.

They call themselves the Merata, which in their soft-spoken tongue means something like "those who remain while changing." An outsider might find the name paradoxical. The Merata do not.

---

The first thing you notice about them is how they move. Close. Always close. They navigate the narrow streets in a kind of choreographed intimacy, bodies turning sideways to pass, hands briefly touching walls or shoulders for orientation. Personal space, as surface-dwellers understand it, does not exist here—there is no room for it, and no cultural expectation of it. A Merata child learns to sleep in a pile of siblings, to eat with elbows tucked, to conduct every interaction within arm's reach of another person.

This closeness has made them extraordinary readers of faces and breath. They speak in near-whispers, yes, but more communication happens in the slight flare of nostrils, the tension around eyes, the minute shifts of weight that signal mood or intention. Lying to a Merata is not impossible, but it requires a discipline of micro-expression that few outsiders possess. They find surface people exhausting to talk to—all that distance, all that volume, all that *missing* information.

Their voices, when they do use them, are remarkable instruments. Trained from birth to carry meaning in minimal sound, Merata speech is dense with tonal variation, with consonants softened to near-silence, with a vocabulary rich in words for quiet things. They have eleven terms for different qualities of dripping water. They have no word for "echo" that does not also mean "consequence."

---

**The Stoneborn Trades**

Every Merata belongs to one of the great work-families, though "family" here is a fluid concept—membership comes through apprenticeship as often as birth, and the lines blur over generations. The work-families are:

The **Carvers** maintain livable space against the endless encroachment. They work in teams during dry periods, chipping back doorframes, clearing lamp-niches, shaving floors level. It is brutal, endless, Sisyphean labor, and the Carvers approach it with a grim satisfaction that borders on religious devotion. Their prayer, if it can be called that, runs: *The stone gives. We give back. The stone gives.* They are muscular, compact, and tend toward a dark humor about impermanence.

The **Shapers** are the Carvers' opposite number—they work *with* the mineral growth, guiding it, encouraging it. They coat wooden frames with calcium paste to create new formations. They embed objects in walls-to-be: tools, symbols, the small treasures of families wanting to make permanent what time would otherwise dissolve. The Shapers understand the solution's flow, the temperature variations that affect deposition rate, the precise chemistry of encouragement. They are patient beyond what seems humanly possible, working on projects their grandchildren will complete.

The **Tenders** maintain the fungus gardens and root cellars, the pale fish populations, the lichen beds that provide dyes and medicines. They are the closest thing the Undercity has to surface-dwellers in temperament—attuned to growth cycles, to living things, to the particular needs of organisms. Many Tenders develop a pallid flush to their skin from years working in the phosphorescent glow of certain cultivated fungi.

The **Keepers of the Drip** are the priests, if the Merata have priests. They monitor the moisture cycles, predict wet and dry periods, maintain the vast oral tradition of flow patterns and mineral moods. They speak even less than other Merata, conserving words as others conserve space. When a Keeper speaks, markets pause. When a Keeper sings—rare, reserved for moments of genuine crisis or celebration—the sound carries through the caverns like something geological, like the voice of the growing stone itself.

The **Measurers** are the newest work-family, only four generations old. They track the walls. Every wall. They maintain the great charts showing growth rates by location, by season, by the mysterious long rhythms no one fully understands. They are why the Undercity persists—without their predictions, the city would have been choked by its own architecture generations ago. Measurers are often strange, abstracted, more comfortable with numbers than faces. Other Merata treat them with a mixture of respect and gentle pity.

**How They Live**

A Merata home is perhaps eight feet wide and extends back twenty, thirty, sometimes forty feet into carved stone. Families live in sequence: the eldest near the entrance, the youngest in the deep back chambers, everyone passing through everyone else's space a dozen times daily. Privacy is achieved not through walls but through a complex social grammar of *not-seeing*—certain hours, certain activities, certain conversations are understood to be invisible to those who pass through. Children learn this grammar before they learn to walk.

They sleep in shifts, the homes too small for everyone to lie down at once. A family of twelve might have four sleeping at any time, arranged in the rotating schedule that childhood stories and adult jokes both reference constantly. *May your sleep-shift never meet your enemy's* is a blessing. *I'll tell you during third-sleep* means the information is either unimportant or extremely important—context determines which.

They eat communally, in the great halls where the cavern opens wide enough for hundreds to gather. The food is fungal, root-based, supplemented by fish oil and the occasional surface trade goods that filter down through the well shaft. It is bland by outside standards, nutritious by any measure, and spiced with fermented lichens that give it a sour, complex undertone. Merata who travel to the surface often struggle with the *intensity* of food there, the assault of flavors that seems to them almost violent.

Marriage—though they do not use that word—is a matter of adjacent sleeping arrangements formalized through witnessed vows. Two people agree to wake together, which is more intimate here than it sounds: to wake next to someone is to see them at their least composed, in the vulnerable moment before the social grammar of not-seeing engages. Divorce is the simple matter of shifting sleep positions, though families usually spend months negotiating space before it comes to that.

---

**The Matter of the Dead**

When a Merata dies, the body is washed in mineral solution and placed in an alcove-to-be—a niche the Shapers have prepared, where the limestone is growing fastest. Over months, sometimes years, the stone closes over. The dead become part of the wall, their shapes sometimes visible as subtle bulges in the growing surface, more often lost in the mineral accumulation entirely.

But not lost. Never lost. The Keepers of the Drip maintain the memory, singing the names of the wall-dead in their rare ceremonies, tracing their fingers over stone that was once a grandmother, an uncle, a child who died too young. The Merata do not visit graves. They lean against their ancestors, hang their lamps in alcoves their great-great-grandparents now form the back of, live always in the company of generations turned to architecture.

This shapes their psychology in ways difficult for outsiders to grasp. Death is not an ending but a transition to a different form of presence. The wall where your mother rests will narrow your doorway over decades—she is still mothering, still shaping your life, still requiring accommodation. Grief here is measured not in stages but in stratigraphy: the bands of mineral deposit that mark the years since someone you loved became stone.

---

**The Children of the Drip**

Merata children are quiet—not suppressed, but quiet, having absorbed the culture's ambient hush from birth. They play in the narrow gaps between buildings, games of touch and texture and precise physical coordination. They learn early to feel the difference between mineral-slick and water-slick, between stone that is growing fast and stone that is dormant. By age seven, most can estimate a surface's deposition rate by touch alone.

Their education happens through apprenticeship, following the work-families, but all children learn the basics: Carving to survive, Shaping to build, Tending to eat, Keeping to predict, Measuring to plan. They grow up understanding that their city is an argument between human intent and mineral emergence, and that neither side ever fully wins.

Adolescence is marked by the First Embedding—each young person chooses a small object, something meaningful, and works with a Shaper to place it in a growing wall. Over the following years, they watch the stone claim it. By the time the object is fully enclosed, invisible, made permanent through incorporation, the adolescent is considered adult. They have learned, viscerally, what it means to give something to the stone and have the stone give it back changed.

---

**The Elders and the Long View**

The oldest Merata, those past seventy or eighty years, begin their own preparation. They spend more time in the alcove-galleries, touching the walls where friends and family became stone, choosing their own resting places. They work with Shapers to prepare niches, sometimes over years, adjusting the position as the stone grows, waiting for the shape to feel right.

There is no fear in this. There is something closer to anticipation—the satisfaction of joining a structure you have spent your life maintaining, of becoming literally foundational to the world your grandchildren will inherit. The very old Merata often develop a quality of geological patience, sitting for hours in the dripping quiet, their silence no longer social convention but something deeper, something that has stopped distinguishing between the speed of human thought and the speed of mineral accumulation.

They tell their stories to the Keepers of the Drip, who commit them to the song-memory. They choose what objects will be embedded near their alcoves, what tools or tokens will keep them company in the long mineral night. They say goodbye over months, sometimes years, the process of dying stretched to match the process of becoming stone.

When the end comes, it is usually peaceful. The family gathers in the narrow home, close as always, and the elder breathes their last surrounded by the soft-voiced presence of everyone they love, in a city that will slowly incorporate their body into walls that will themselves be slowly incorporated into the living stone.

---

**The Outsiders' View**

Traders from the surface find the Merata unnerving. It is not the silence, or the closeness, or the pale skin and worn hands. It is the *timescale*. Speaking with a Merata elder, you realize they are tracking conversations that began before you were born and will continue after you die. They ask about your grandfather's health, having met him once fifty years ago. They reference agreements made with your trading house generations back, expecting you to honor terms you have never heard of.

The Merata remember everything, because the stone remembers everything, and they have learned to think like the stone thinks: in layers, in accumulation, in the slow patient recording of every thing that passes.

Some outsiders find this wisdom. Others find it terrifying—the sense that you are speaking with something not quite human, or human in a way that has drifted far from the surface-dweller norm. The Merata notice this discomfort. They have eleven words for different qualities of dripping water, but they have only one word for the fear outsiders feel in the Undercity, and it translates roughly as: *the sound of someone realizing they are temporary*.

---

**What They Want**

More space. Always more space. The Carvers' endless work buys time, but the Measurers' charts show clearly that the city is losing ground, centimeter by centimeter, generation by generation. Some caverns that held neighborhoods fifty years ago are now solid stone, families displaced to the ever-more-crowded terraces.

They could expand deeper, following the caves down into unmapped darkness. Some have. The Deep Merata, they are called, families who pushed past the known boundaries and are now rumored to live in caverns where the deposition is slower, where space is more plentiful, where something in the chemistry of the water has begun to change them in ways the surface-connected Merata whisper about and try not to imagine too vividly.

They could return to the surface, or at least trade more actively with it. Some do—a constant slow emigration of young people who cannot bear the closeness, who want to shout, who want to see the sky. The Merata let them go without judgment. The stone gives. You give back. Some things you give back to the outside world.

But most stay. Most will always stay, because this is their home and their ancestors and their future, because the work of the Carvers and Shapers and Tenders and Keepers and Measurers is never finished, because the city needs them and they need the city and both are still becoming what they will eventually be.

The Undercity will last another five hundred years, perhaps less, perhaps more. The Merata do not know how it will end—whether they will be pushed out by the growing stone, or find new caverns, or adapt in ways they cannot yet imagine. They think about it. They plan for it. But they do not fear it, because they have spent two hundred years learning to live inside something that is still learning what shape it wants to be, and they have become, themselves, a people comfortable with incompleteness.

They descend. They remain while changing. They whisper in the cool damp dark, and the stone listens, and remembers, and grows.
