**The Tenders**

They have no name for themselves—naming requires a sense of separation from one's environment that they have never developed. They are simply what lives here, as the moss is what grows here, as the rain is what falls.

You would not see them at first. Perhaps not for days. They move through the drowned streets with a slowness that registers as stillness unless you watch for a very long time, gray shapes in gray water, their skin having taken on the mineral tones of the stone they have lived among for generations beyond counting. They are not hiding. They simply do not move at speeds that mammalian attention is calibrated to notice.

When you finally perceive one, it will be in the middle distance—a figure standing thigh-deep in a flooded plaza, facing a wall where water traces its dark calligraphy down the facade. Watching. No, not watching. *Attending*. The figure's hands rest on the surface of the water, fingers spread, not moving, and you realize it has been standing there since before you arrived, and will be standing there after you leave, and that this is what it does. This is what they all do.

---

They are tall and terribly thin, their proportions elongated in a way that suggests bodies optimized for stillness rather than motion—less mass to maintain, less heat to lose to the constant cool water. Their limbs are long and their joints slightly wrong, bending at angles that allow them to fold into remarkably small spaces when they rest, which they do in the sheltered corridors where the rain sounds more intimate, more enclosed. They tuck themselves into window alcoves and doorways, becoming architectural, becoming part of the city's geometry until some slow imperative moves them to unfold and wade to their next position.

Their skin is not smooth. It has developed a texture over the generations, something like the roughness of weathered stone, and in the creases and hollows of their bodies, moss grows. The same moss that carpets the streets—it has colonized them too, soft green patches in their armpits and the hollows of their throats and along their spines where moisture collects when they stand still for weeks at a time. They do not remove it. Perhaps they cannot feel it. Perhaps they welcome it, this proof that they have achieved the stillness they seem to revere.

Their eyes are large and pale, adapted to the gray light and the dim interiors where they sometimes linger, and they blink rarely—watching the water's movement, the rain's patterns, the slow creep of mineral staining down a wall. When they do blink, it is deliberate, a reset, as though they are marking the passage of some span of time invisible to faster minds.

---

They tend the city. This is the closest translation for what they do, though it implies more intention than they seem to possess. They tend it the way moss grows—not with purpose, but with presence, with patient persistence.

You might see one working at a drain grate, fingers probing the silted channels with a slowness that makes the work appear ceremonial. It is not ceremony. It is simply the speed at which they live. Over the course of a day, it will clear perhaps a handful of debris from the grate, enough to improve the flow slightly, enough to keep the system adequate rather than letting it fail entirely. Then it will move on—or rather, over the course of another day, it will gradually shift its position toward another grate, another slight blockage, another small intervention in the city's hydrology.

Others stand in the flooded plazas with their hands on the water's surface, and at first this seems purposeless, contemplative, like meditation. But if you watch long enough—and you would have to watch for a very long time—you might notice that the water moves slightly differently around their fingers. That they are reading something in its flow, some information about the levels and pressures throughout the system, some understanding of where the water is moving too fast or too slow or pooling where it should drain. They are sensors as much as actors, their stillness a form of listening.

In the canal-districts of the central lowlands, they move through the dark water like pale reeds, only their heads above the surface, navigating by some internal map of the submerged streets. They know where the water runs deep and where hidden steps rise toward walkways. They know which flooded basements connect to which underground channels. They know the city's water the way a body knows its own blood.

---

They do not speak, not in any way you would recognize as speech. But they are not silent.

When two of them meet—which happens rarely, as their territories are large and their movements slow—they will stand facing each other in the rain for what might be hours, their hands making small movements in the water between them, their pale eyes fixed on each other's faces. Something passes between them. Information, perhaps. The state of the drains in one district, the level of the pools in another. Or something less practical—recognition, acknowledgment, the simple confirmation that another exists.

Sometimes, in the deep quiet of the flooded nights, a sound rises from the canals. Not a voice, exactly. Something more like the building resonance of water moving through pipes, a low harmonic that seems to come from everywhere at once. The Tenders make this sound, though how is unclear—perhaps by positioning themselves at specific points in the drainage system, perhaps by some vibration of their strange bodies, perhaps by means that have no analog in the biology of faster creatures. The sound carries across districts, across the vast breadth of the city, and other Tenders pause in their slow work to listen. To receive.

It might be language. It might be something older than language, some form of communion that predates the need for words. Whatever it is, it coordinates them—ensures that when one drain fails beyond recovery, others will gradually shift their paths to compensate, that the city's water will continue to flow through alternate channels, that the slow losing battle against siltation will be fought on the fronts where it matters most.

---

They do not remember the builders.

This is not a loss of history but an absence of the capacity for history. The Tenders live in an eternal present tense, each moment complete in itself, the past existing only in the shapes it has left behind—the streets that were made for walking, the windows made for looking through. They do not know that these shapes once had other purposes. They know only that the streets carry water and must be kept clear, that the windows let in light and rain, that the plazas collect what drains from the surrounding slopes.

If they evolved here—if they are the descendants of something that was here when the builders still walked, some creature of the basin's edges that found opportunity in the abandonment—they carry no memory of that transformation. If they came later, finding the empty city and making it theirs, they carry no memory of arrival. They simply are, as the city simply is, as the rain simply falls.

Some of them are old. This is visible in the depth of their weathering, the thickness of the moss that grows in their creases, the slowness of their already-slow movements. The oldest stand nearly motionless in the deepest parts of the canal-districts, their lower bodies lost in the dark water, their upper bodies becoming something like statuary, stone-colored and stone-still. They are still alive—their pale eyes still move, tracking the rain, reading the water—but they have achieved a stillness so profound that the city has begun to claim them. Mineral deposits form on their surfaces. Moss grows thick enough to blur their features.

They will stand there until they stop. There is no ceremony around this, no acknowledgment of ending. One day a Tender will simply fail to blink, fail to shift, fail to respond to the deep harmonic calls that coordinate their kind. The water will continue to flow around them. The moss will continue to grow. Eventually, perhaps, they will become indistinguishable from the stone—another shape in a city full of shapes, another form that once had purpose and now simply persists.

---

In the shallow water of a flooded courtyard, something pale and quick darts away from your attention. The things that live in the deep channels, the flooded basements, the darker places where the Tenders do not go.

These are their young.

Not children in any developmental sense—they do not grow into Tenders the way a child grows into an adult. They are a different phase, a different form of the same existence, and the transformation between them is not growth but *settling*. The young are fast because they have not yet learned stillness. They flicker through the submerged streets, feeding on whatever feeds in these waters, living a life of movement and reaction that would be unrecognizable to their future selves.

And then, at some point that varies for each individual, something shifts. A young one will slow. Will stop. Will stand for the first time in water it has only ever moved through, and feel the current against its skin, and discover that there is information in that feeling. That the water speaks, if you are still enough to listen.

The settling takes years. A flickering juvenile becoming a merely quick adolescent becoming a slow adult becoming a Tender, patient and gray and growing moss in its creases. The memory of speed fades as the capacity for speed fades, until the mature Tender cannot recall what it was to move fast, to react, to flee. Until stillness becomes not a choice but a nature.

The young do not interact with the settled. They inhabit different layers of the same city—the dark quick spaces below, the gray slow spaces above—and if they recognize each other as the same kind, they show no sign of it. Perhaps they don't. Perhaps, to the fast-moving young, the still figures in the water above are simply part of the landscape, as the stone is part of the landscape, as the rain is part of the landscape. Perhaps, to the Tenders, the flickers in the deep water are simply another thing that moves, like the rare birds, like the rain itself—noticed, not understood, let pass.

---

There are places in the city where the Tenders gather.

Not often. Not in numbers that would constitute a crowd by any ordinary standard. But certain plazas, certain junctions in the canal system, certain rooftops where the rain pools before cascading down to the streets below—these places will sometimes hold three or four or five of them at once, standing in their characteristic stillness, arranged in patterns that might be geometric or might be arbitrary.

They are not conferring. They are not communing, exactly, though the low harmonic sound sometimes rises from these gatherings, thrumming through the water and the stone. They are simply *being together*, in the way that moss grows in colonies, in the way that rain falls in fronts. There is something in their nature that requires occasional proximity, some need that is met by the presence of others of their kind, though what that need is and how presence meets it remains opaque.

After hours or days, they disperse. Slowly. One will begin to move, and then another, and then another, until the plaza stands empty again except for the water and the rain. They return to their territories, their drains, their patient watching. The city continues.

---

If you stayed here long enough—truly long enough, years rather than days—you might come to understand them. Or you might come to understand that understanding is not the right frame, that the Tenders exist in a mode that does not translate into comprehension, that they are simply what lives here in the way that the water is simply what flows here.

They do not wonder about the builders. They do not wonder about you, if you come. They notice—their pale eyes track movement, register presence—but noticing is not the same as curiosity. They will continue their slow circuits through the drowned streets whether you are there or not. They will tend the drains, read the water, make their low harmonic calls across the vast basin. They will settle and slow and eventually stop, becoming stone, becoming city.

This is not a tragedy. It is not even a story, really—stories require change, and the Tenders have found a way of existing that minimizes change, that resists change, that maintains the city in its state of purposeless persistence for as long as the rain continues to fall.

And the rain continues to fall.

And the water continues to flow.

And somewhere in the gray distance, a Tender stands thigh-deep in a flooded plaza, hands resting on the surface of the water, reading currents that have been flowing since before it was born and will flow after it becomes stone, and it does not think about this because it does not think, it only tends, it only persists, it only is.
