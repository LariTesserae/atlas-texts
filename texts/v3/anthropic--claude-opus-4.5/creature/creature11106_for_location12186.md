**The Clinging-Folk**

They are born with hands that grip. This is not metaphor—the midwives check the reflex before they check the breath, curling the newborn's fingers around a length of cable, watching for the automatic clench that means the child will survive. Those born without it do not last long here, though no one speaks of this directly. The fog takes what it takes.

By the time they can walk, they can climb. By the time they can speak, they know the sounds that mean *hold fast* and *weight shifting* and *cable singing*—this last a warning that a line is under stress, that something above or below is about to give way. These are not words in the usual sense. They are tones, pitched to cut through fog and engine-hum, passed from mouth to mouth along the catwalks faster than any message could travel by lift. A child learns them before learning the name of the mountain. The mountain has no name. It simply is.

---

Their skin takes on the color of what surrounds them: not through any biological mechanism, but through years of fog-damp and mineral deposit, through handling rusted iron and copper-green pipe, through sleeping in rooms where the walls weep moisture that carries trace metals from the stone. By middle age, a cliff-dweller's hands are stained in patterns as distinctive as fingerprints—you can tell which district someone works in by the particular mottling of their palms. The pipe-fitters of the middle tiers are ochre to the elbow. The cistern-keepers of the upper reaches carry a pale verdigris in the creases of their knuckles. The boiler-workers are marked by a fine black dust that never quite washes out, settling into the whorls of their skin until they seem to be wearing gloves of soot.

Their clothes are layered and practical and never quite dry. Oilcloth over wool over linen, each layer serving a purpose: the linen wicks moisture away from skin that would otherwise never fully dry; the wool retains warmth even when wet; the oilcloth sheds the worst of the fog-drip that falls from every surface above. Hoods are universal, shaped to direct water away from the face while leaving peripheral vision clear. You learn to watch your periphery here. You learn that looking down is less dangerous than failing to notice movement beside you—a swinging crane-arm, a cable under tension, a platform tilting as weight shifts.

---

They speak in a dialect that has grown as accretive as the city itself, bolted together from the languages of every group that ever sought refuge on the cliff face. Some words are clearly maritime—*hawser*, *belay*, *make fast*—carried here by people who understood that a vertical mile is just another kind of sea. Other words come from mining communities, from the terminology of shafts and levels and the slow extraction of useful things from resistant stone. Still others are unique to this place, invented to describe phenomena that exist nowhere else: *drip-song* for the sound that signals fog-thinning, *lift-logic* for the intuitive understanding of how the cage network moves, *gray-hours* for the long stretches of afternoon when the light dims but does not fail, when the city hangs between day and night and time seems to thicken like cooling metal.

They do not raise their voices. This is not politeness—it is survival. Sound carries strangely here, bouncing off stone and metal, finding paths through the cable network that amplify it in unexpected ways. A shout in one district might arrive in another as a whisper, or as a boom that startles a worker on a narrow ledge. Conversations are conducted at close range, mouths near ears, the intimacy of communication enforced by the architecture. Strangers stand closer together here than they would anywhere else, because any greater distance means not being heard.

---

The social structure mirrors the physical one: layered, vertical, organized around access. Those who live in the upper reaches control the water—the catch-basins that feed the cisterns, the overflow channels that must be opened or closed according to the fog's rhythm. They are not wealthy in any conventional sense, but they are essential, and they know it. The middle tiers house the workers who keep the city moving: the lift operators, the pipe-fitters, the cable-minders who spend their days walking routes that would terrify a newcomer, checking tension and wear and the slow creep of vine growth into mechanisms. The lower reaches are for the newcomers themselves, for those who have not yet proven they can be trusted with the city's more delicate systems, for the old and the injured and the ones who have lost their nerve for heights.

But this hierarchy is porous in ways that matter. A cistern-keeper's child might apprentice to a boiler-worker, might spend twenty years in the steam and soot before returning to the upper tiers with knowledge that makes the water systems more efficient. A lift operator who loses a hand in a cable accident might descend to the lower reaches, might find work there that requires patience rather than grip, might eventually become one of the elders who remember which floors the diagrams don't show.

Because the city has floors that the diagrams don't show. Everyone knows this. No one finds it strange.

---

The elders—and there are always elders, even in a place where accidents are common and the fog takes what it takes—gather in a district that exists only intermittently. It appears on no map because it is not always there: a cluster of habitation pods accessible via a lift-cage that arrives, sometimes, at a floor number that should not exist. The elders do not summon this floor. They simply wait in the cage, patient as the stone itself, until the cage decides to take them where they need to go.

What do they discuss there? The city's rotation, perhaps—the slow drift of active districts around the cliff face, the pattern that emerges when you track which areas are abandoned and which are thriving across decades. The writing in the cable routes, which changes so slowly that only the very old notice it changing at all, and which might be instructions or might be a record or might be the city thinking its own thoughts in a language no human taught it. The thing in the deep fog, which the elders have seen—some of them—and which they describe not with fear but with a kind of professional respect, the way you might describe a colleague whose methods you don't understand but whose results you cannot argue with.

They emerge from these meetings with no pronouncements, no decrees. They simply return to their districts and make small adjustments—a word to a pipe-fitter about rerouting a section of line, a suggestion to a crane operator about shifting cargo at a different time of day. The adjustments accumulate. The city responds. No one designed this system of governance. It simply emerged, the way the city itself emerged, through the accretion of many small decisions that somehow add up to a coherent whole.

---

Children play in the gaps between structures, on platforms too narrow for adults to traverse comfortably. They are watched, always, but not by their parents—by the city itself, or so it seems, by the attention of everyone who happens to glance their way as they climb and swing and test their grip against surfaces that would kill them if they fell. Falls happen. Not often, but often enough. The community gathers when a child falls, gathers in silence, gathers not to mourn in any conventional way but to witness—because death here is not a failure of the community or the city or the child. It is simply the fog taking what it takes, the drop claiming what the drop claims. The body is recovered if possible, given to the boilers if it can be found, allowed to vanish into the deep gray if it cannot.

After, there is a meal. There is always a meal after. The community shares what it has, which is never much—preserved vegetables grown in window-boxes, protein from sources no one examines too closely, bread baked in ovens that run on the heat vented from the industrial works. The meal happens in whatever space is available, on platforms lit by the dim yellow glow of gas-lamps, and no one speaks of the child who fell. They speak instead of the child's grip, of the strength of it, of the first time they noticed that the child could hold their own weight on a cable. They speak of what the child might have become. They speak until the fog rolls in thick enough to muffle conversation, and then they disperse to their pods, and the city continues its murmur around them, and the lifts continue to rise and descend, and the drip-song changes pitch as the night settles in.

---

There is a word in their dialect for which no translation exists: *verthan*. It describes a state of being that is something like faith and something like resignation and something like the calm that comes from understanding your own smallness against geological time. To feel *verthan* is to look down at the drop and feel no fear—not because you are brave, but because you have understood that the drop is simply a fact, as much a fact as the stone or the fog or your own heartbeat. You will fall or you will not. The city will endure or it will not. The stone beneath it all will endure regardless, will still be here a billion years hence, worn perhaps a millimeter smoother, but essentially unchanged.

The elders say that *verthan* is the city's gift to those who live in it. Others say that *verthan* is what the city extracts in exchange for shelter, the price you pay for belonging to a place that belongs to the stone. Both of these are true. Neither of these is complete.

---

In the dead districts, where the green has taken over entirely, there are still people. Not many. Not the kind you would recognize immediately as people. They have lived there so long, in the spaces where the vines have woven themselves through everything, that they have begun to move the way the vines move—slowly, deliberately, with a patience that seems botanical rather than animal. They speak, when they speak, in a register so low it is almost inaudible, and the fog seems to part around them as if in deference.

The city's active population knows they are there. Messages are sometimes left at the borders of the dead districts—requests for guidance, offerings of food that disappear overnight, questions about the cable-writing or the lift-floors that should not exist. Responses come irregularly, in forms that require interpretation: a vine growing suddenly toward a particular junction, a shift in the pattern of abandoned platforms that suggests movement should be directed one way rather than another.

Are these people? Are they something the city has made of people? Are they something that was always here, waiting in the stone, adopting human form as a convenience?

No one asks. Asking would be a violation of *verthan*. The dead districts exist. Their inhabitants exist. The city exists. The stone exists. The drop exists. You learn it by living here.

---

In the end, what you notice about the people of the Clinging City is not their strangeness but their ordinariness. They love each other. They argue over small things—whose turn it is to clear the catch-basin filters, whether a particular cable route should be rerouted or repaired. They tell jokes that make no sense outside this context, that rely on shared knowledge of drip-songs and lift-logic and the particular quality of light on a day when the fog lifts just enough to see the valley floor, impossibly far below. They grow old if they are lucky. They fall if they are not. They teach their children to grip.

And they do not look down. Not because they are afraid of what they might see, but because looking down is no longer necessary. The drop is not below them. The drop is all around them—above and below and on every side, held at bay by cables and catwalks and the ancient stone and the constant murmur of maintenance that keeps the city clinging to the cliff.

They are at home here. They are at home in a way that suggests home is not a place of safety but a place of understanding—understanding that you belong to something larger and stranger than yourself, that you are temporary and it is not, that your grip will hold or it will not, and either way the fog will roll in and the lifts will rise and descend and the city will continue its slow rotation around an axis that exists only in the aggregate of many lives lived in vertical space.

They do not ask what the city wants from them. They already know. It wants them to hold on.
