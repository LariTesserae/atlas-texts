## The People of the Slow Descent

They call themselves nothing. The concept of a collective name implies the existence of an Other substantial enough to require distinction, and the salt flats below have been empty of human movement for so many generations that the need for self-naming has atrophied like an unused limb. When pressed by the rare visitor—traders from the distant ridge-settlements who arrive perhaps twice in a lifetime, following routes marked by cairns their ancestors built—they will say only "we are here" or "we are the ones who remain," statements of location rather than identity.

---

**The Sap-Keepers** form the closest thing to a distinct caste, though the word implies a rigidity that doesn't exist here. They are simply the families who have accumulated, over centuries, the deepest knowledge of the dust-root vines—which bladders are ready for harvest, which tendrils are sick and must be pruned before the illness spreads, where new growth should be encouraged and where it threatens to destabilize the stone itself. 

A Sap-Keeper can read a vine the way a scholar reads text. This one is stressed: see how the silver hairs lie flat instead of lifting toward the wind? This bladder will be bitter; the amber has a greenish cast that speaks of mineral imbalance. This tendril has found a crack in the stone and is widening it—in fifty years it will calve off a section of the inhabited cliff face if left unchecked. They carry curved bone knives worn to translucence by generations of handling, and they move through the vine-curtains with an ease that looks like the vines are parting for them, though it is simply that they know where the gaps are.

The eldest Sap-Keeper currently living is a woman called **Therru**, though this is not a name in the way outsiders understand names—it is a sound she has answered to since childhood, given to her by her mother based on the particular creak of the rope bridge outside their home on the morning of her birth. Therru is old enough that she has outlived the ability to climb to the highest reaches of the settlement, but young enough that her hands remain steady when she punctures bladders. She keeps her bone awl in a pouch made from kheth-leather—one of the few permitted uses of the creatures, harvesting the hides of those who die naturally—and the pouch is so old it has taken on the texture of stone.

Therru remembers when the vines failed. Not completely—nothing here happens completely—but there was a period, three generations of the vine-cycle ago, when the bladders swelled only to half their usual size and the sap ran thin and cloudy. Seventeen people died before the vines recovered. She was a child then, and she remembers the particular quality of hunger that comes from drinking something that fills the stomach but does not nourish. She teaches the younger Sap-Keepers to watch for the early signs: a certain brittleness in the silver hairs, a delay in the bladders' first swelling. Whether they will see such a failure in their lifetimes, she cannot say. But they will be ready.

---

**The Bridge-Minders** maintain the connections between chambers, between families, between the rings of habitation. This is not prestigious work but it is necessary—the rope bridges fray, the wooden ladders split, the carved handholds in the cliff face wear smooth and must be re-chiseled. A Bridge-Minder's hands are permanently roughened from hemp fiber andite basite dust, the creases of their palms mapped in gray.

They work in pairs, always in pairs, because a fall from these heights offers no second chances and the thermal winds that rise from the salt flats are unpredictable. One works while the other watches, ready to grab a wrist or a belt or a fistful of clothing. The intimacy this creates is particular: Bridge-Minders choose their partners carefully, and the partnerships often outlast marriages, outlast blood-bonds, become the defining relationship of a life. Two old men on the middle ring, **Koss** and **Evaru**, have mended bridges together for sixty years. They finish each other's gestures now. Evaru will reach for a tool and Koss will already be placing it in his hand. Koss will lean out over a frayed rope and Evaru's grip will find his belt before Koss has consciously decided to lean.

They speak rarely when they work—there is no need—but in the evenings they sit together on the platform outside their adjacent family-chambers and talk for hours in low voices about nothing in particular. The quality of the light today. A kheth they saw doing something unusual. The way a particular bridge is aging, whether it will need replacement in their lifetimes or their children's. These conversations are not exchanging information; they are the simple pleasure of being heard by someone who has heard you ten thousand times before and has not yet grown tired of it.

---

**Children here** grow up vertical. They climb before they walk confidently on flat surfaces—there are so few flat surfaces that the skill is almost decorative. A child of five can navigate the rope bridges without holding the guide-ropes, can scramble up a ladder faster than an adult, can find handholds in the cliff face that seem invisible to older eyes. They play games that would terrify parents in any other settlement: racing along the vine-highways, swinging from tendril to tendril, dropping from platform to platform in controlled falls that stop the heart of any visitor who witnesses them.

The children know the kheth by individual markings—this one has a notch in its left ear, that one walks with a slight limp, the big female with the pale patch on her chest has raised four litters in the vine-cave behind the third-ring granary. They name the kheth, though they would never admit this to adults who might consider it inappropriate attachment. The names are descriptive and often unkind, in the way of children's names: Snot-Nose, Loud-One, the Screamer, Old Biter. The children follow the kheth through the vines, learning the settlement's hidden geography—the spaces between spaces, the routes that bypass the main platforms entirely, the secret rooms where the vines have grown so thick that adults cannot fit through the gaps.

A girl called **Mirin**—perhaps nine or ten, though ages here are tracked loosely if at all—has become something of a legend among the settlement's children for having followed a kheth all the way to the bird-nesting grounds above the highest human habitation. She will not say what she saw there, only that the birds are larger than she expected and that they watched her with an intelligence she found uncomfortable. The other children believe she is lying or exaggerating. Mirin does not argue with them. She has started collecting the small, glossy feathers that sometimes drift down from above, and she keeps them in a pouch she will not let anyone else touch.

---

**The Shapers** are those who carve new chambers from the stone, who widen fissures into homes, who cut the channels that direct rainwater—on the two or three days per year when rain actually falls—into the cisterns that supplement the vine-sap. They are fewer now than they once were. The settlement is not growing; it has not grown in living memory. The existing chambers are sufficient, and the work of the Shapers has shifted from expansion to maintenance: smoothing a floor worn uneven by generations of feet, reinforcing a ceiling that has developed a worrying crack, carving the memorial texts that mark important deaths.

The memorial texts are the closest thing the settlement has to written history. They spiral around the interior of certain chambers—the oldest, most central ones—in a script that has evolved over the centuries from pictographic to abstract. The earliest texts are still readable as images: a figure standing, a sun symbol, wavy lines that might represent the vine-sap or might represent something else entirely, something forgotten. The later texts are pure pattern, meaningful only to those who have learned the current form of the writing. A Shaper named **Corin** has spent most of his adult life studying the older texts, trying to reconstruct what they say. He believes one of them describes the last time anyone saw liquid water. He is probably wrong, but the belief gives his life a shape that satisfies him.

Corin is unmarried, childless, absorbed in his work to a degree that his family finds alternately admirable and concerning. He sleeps in a small chamber he carved himself, just large enough for a sleeping mat and his collection of stone fragments—pieces chipped from the cliff face during his investigations, each one labeled with a mark indicating where it came from. He believes the stone itself holds memory, that the basalt plug was once hot enough to flow, and that the things it witnessed in that molten state are somehow encoded in its crystalline structure. He has no way to read this encoding. He simply believes it exists, and the belief is enough.

---

**The dead** do not leave, exactly. The burial practice—wrapping in fresh-cut vine tendrils, allowing the slow incorporation into the living plant—means that the ancestors are physically present, distributed throughout the vine-mass that covers the cliff face. A tendril that a child swings from might contain, somewhere in its fibers, the dissolved remains of a great-grandmother. A bladder being harvested for sap might have grown from a node that was once, decades ago, pressed against a beloved uncle's face.

This is not spoken of as horrifying or even as particularly sacred. It is simply true, in the way that all the truths here are simply true. The dead become the vine; the vine becomes the sap; the sap becomes the living. The cycle is visible if you choose to see it, invisible if you choose not to. Most people choose not to, most of the time. But there are moments—harvesting a bladder from a tendril you know grew through your father's wrappings, drinking sap in the chamber where your mother's body was placed—when the knowledge surfaces, and something complicated moves through you that is neither grief nor comfort but partakes of both.

An old man named **Destru** tends the burial-places. This is not his only role—he has a family, grandchildren, the ordinary obligations of life—but it is the role that defines him to others. He knows where every body has been placed for the past four generations. He knows which vine-masses are thick with the dead and which are still relatively unpopulated. He knows the stages of incorporation: the first softening of the wrappings, the slow invasion of root-fibers through cloth and flesh, the gradual dissolution that leaves nothing but a faint density in the tendril, a slightly different texture that a knowing hand can feel.

When Destru himself dies, he has already chosen his place: a young vine on the settlement's eastern face, still thin enough that his body will constitute a significant portion of its eventual mass. He likes the idea of becoming a large fraction of something rather than a small fraction of something larger. It is the closest thing to ambition that exists here.

---

**Visitors**, when they come, are absorbed into the settlement's rhythm whether they intend to be or not. There is nowhere to stand apart, no inn or guesthouse, no public space distinct from private space. A visitor is taken in by whichever family is deemed most appropriate—based on criteria the visitor is never told and probably wouldn't understand—and is expected to participate in the household's life for the duration of their stay.

The last visitor, a trader from the ridge-settlements who arrived eight years ago with a pack of dried fish and mineral dyes, stayed for three months. She had intended to stay for days. But the rhythm of the place caught her—the slow meals, the unhurried conversations, the particular quality of attention that people here give to every task—and she found herself unable to leave. When she finally did depart, she wept, though she could not have said why. She has not returned, but she sends messages sometimes, carried by other traders: she is well, she remembers them, she dreams sometimes of the vine-light and the moth-whisper and wakes disoriented in her own bed.

The family who hosted her still speaks of her, not with longing but with the mild interest one gives to any memorable variation in routine. The children she played with are adolescents now, and they remember her as a figure of impossible energy, always wanting to do the next thing, always asking what would happen next. They found her exhausting and wonderful. They hope she will return someday. They do not expect her to.

---

**In the evenings**, when the light has dimmed to rust and the moths have begun their rising, the settlement's people emerge onto the platforms and bridges to sit together in the relative cool. Conversations drift between groups, mixing and separating like the moth-clouds themselves. Someone is always humming—not songs exactly, but melodic phrases that others pick up and vary, a kind of continuous musical conversation that has no beginning or end.

The kheth emerge too, traveling their vine-highways in family groups, occasionally pausing to observe the humans with their bright, unconcerned eyes. A child reaches out to touch one and is gently redirected by a parent's hand—not forbidden, just guided away, the taboo enforced through soft redirection rather than prohibition. The kheth moves on, unbothered, and the child watches it go with an expression of pure, patient longing.

Above, in the darkness where the birds nest, something shifts and calls once, twice, then falls silent. Below, the salt flats hold their faint phosphorescent glow, the ghost-light of a world that has forgotten what water was. Between these two darknesses, the settlement clings to its dying stone, and the vines pour down in their silver-green curtains, and the people breathe the strange air that has sustained them for longer than memory, and they do not hurry, and they do not fear, and they do not ask what comes next. 

What comes next will come. That is the only truth that matters here: what comes next will come, and when it does, they will still be here, or they will not, and either way the stone will continue its slow surrender to the wind, and the vines will continue their slow climb toward a sun that barely warms them, and the world will go on being what it is, which is enough.

Which has always been enough.
