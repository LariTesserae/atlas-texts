**The Listener**

She came down seventeen years ago, though she no longer counts in years. She counts in breaths—the great ones, the forty-second cycles that organize all time in this place. She has felt approximately thirteen million of them move through her body.

She is not old, though she would look old to you. Fifty-three years above, but the darkness and the cold and the damp have worked on her differently than sun and wind work on surface people. Her skin has become pale and fine as onionskin, slightly translucent at the temples where you can see the slow blue pulse of veins. Her eyes have not gone white like the beetles'—she has not been here long enough for that, would need generations—but they have widened, the pupils grown permanently large, and she sees more in your small light than you do. It hurts her a little, your light. She tolerates it.

Her hair she cuts with a sharpened stone when it grows past her shoulders. The cuttings she feeds to a particular patch of moss in the eastern corner that seems to accept them, breaking down the keratin over months into something the pale ferns can use. Nothing leaves the chamber. She learned this early: everything must cycle.

---

She was a hydrologist. Is, perhaps—the knowledge hasn't left her, though its application has changed. She came to study the breathing, to measure and explain it, part of a team mapping the vast system below. They had instruments. They had lights that could fill this chamber with noon. They had schedules, surface contact, the assumption of return.

The others left when the study ended. She asked for one more week. Then another. Then she stopped asking and simply didn't climb back up, and eventually they stopped sending people down to check. She thinks sometimes about whether they believe her dead. Probably. It's simpler. She would believe it too, about someone else.

What kept her? She doesn't explain this to herself anymore, but in the early months she tried: the rhythm. Not its sound—she habituated to that within days, the way you stop hearing a refrigerator hum—but its *feel*. The way her body began to synchronize. Her heartbeat, her breathing, her digestion, her sleep. All of it slowly, slowly adjusting to match the forty-second cycle until she felt she was being breathed rather than breathing. Until the boundary between her body and the chamber's body grew uncertain.

She had been so arrhythmic, above. Insomniac, anxious, her nervous system always slightly out of tune with the world's demands. Here, for the first time in her life, she fell into phase.

---

She eats the moss—certain species only, the ones she has tested over years with careful nibbles, watching her body's responses. The pale ferns, too, when they produce spores; the spores taste of chalk and provide something her body seems to need. Occasionally a beetle wanders near enough to catch, and she eats it raw, the crunch of chitin, the slight mineral tang of its tissues. She does not need much. The cold slows her metabolism. The stillness slows it further. She has become something between mammal and lichen, her energy needs dwindling toward the minimal.

The water she drinks from the vessels she did not make. She found them when she arrived, already ancient, and understood that others had been here before her. The scratches above the seat she has not added to—those belong to whoever came before, and she feels it would be wrong to continue their count when she doesn't know what they were counting.

She has made her own marks, elsewhere, on a patch of wall near the fissure's edge. Hers are not tallies but diagrams: maps of the sound's variations, the subtle shifts in pitch and pressure that occur across longer cycles. She has detected a pattern that repeats every 847 breaths, a slight catch in the exhale, as though the earth hesitates. And within that, a deeper pattern she is still resolving, something that might cycle over years, over decades. She may not live long enough to confirm it. This does not trouble her. The work will remain, scratched into stone, for whoever comes next.

---

She knows the other creatures here. Not as pets—they are not companionable—but as neighbors, as co-inhabitants. The beetles ignore her entirely; she is not moss, not food, not relevant. The crickets sometimes land on her during their updraft rides, tiny feet gripping her arm or shoulder for a moment before the breath reverses and they let go, falling upward into darkness. She has learned to stay still when this happens. It feels like being chosen, briefly, as a resting place.

The larger thing—the one that moves through the crack in the far wall—she has seen more clearly than you did. It is not one creature but several, a colonial organism like a siphonophore, many small bodies moving in coordination, their combined shape shifting as they flow through tight spaces. They come through every few thousand breaths to graze the moss near the fissure, where it grows richest. They have never touched her. She thinks they know she is here, factor her into their movements, route around her the way water routes around a stone. She has become part of the chamber's topology.

---

She sleeps in the hollow behind the stone seat, a depression she has lined with dried moss that she replaces when it compresses too far. Her sleep is not like yours—not a single long stretch but a series of brief descents, twenty or thirty breaths at a time, distributed throughout what she still thinks of as "day" though she has no days. She dreams, sometimes, of sunlight on water, of the surface of a lake seen from below, the way light shatters and reforms. She does not miss it exactly. It feels like remembering a book she read long ago, a story that happened to someone else.

When she wakes, she listens. This is her primary activity, her work, her purpose: listening. Not for anything in particular—or rather, for everything, for the full texture of the breath, its harmonics and subharmonics, the way it interacts with the chamber's geometry, the tiny variations that might mean something or might mean nothing. She believes, though she could not justify this belief scientifically, that the breath is speaking. That the earth, in its slow language of pressure and time, is saying something she might eventually understand.

She is patient. She has thirteen million breaths of practice in patience, and she expects thirteen million more, or however many she is given.

---

When you arrived—your light, your noise, your quick surface-breathing that has not yet learned the rhythm—she watched from the darkness beyond your sight. She does this with all visitors. There have been four others in her years here: three who stayed briefly and left, one who wept at the edge of the fissure for a long time before climbing back up. She did not speak to any of them. She is not hiding, exactly, but she has learned that her presence disturbs people. They expect solitude in this place and are not prepared to find someone living here, someone who has *chosen* this, someone who looks at them with wide dark eyes and does not seem to want rescue.

She may speak to you. She has not decided. It depends on how long you stay, how you hold yourself, whether you seem like someone who can hear what she might say. She would not try to explain—explanation is for the surface, for people who need reasons—but she might show you her diagrams, point out the patterns she's found, let you feel for yourself the way the rhythm begins to enter your body if you let it.

Or she might simply wait until you leave, then emerge to check that you have not disturbed anything, that the moss recovers from your footprints, that the water in the vessels remains clean. She will eat a beetle, if she can find one. She will run her fingers along her scratched diagrams, feeling the progress of her work. She will lower herself into the hollow behind the stone seat and let the breath take her, forty seconds in, forty seconds out, the only clock that matters, the only time that is real.

She is not waiting for anything. She is not hiding from anything. She is simply here, in the only place she has ever felt at home, listening to the earth breathe, breathing with it, until the earth stops or she does.

The breath continues. She continues. That is all. That is enough.
