**The Keeper of the Cairns**

She is old in the way the ledge is old—not ancient, but weathered past the point where years matter. Her hair is the color of the lichen, that gray-green-white that refuses to commit to any single shade, and she wears it cropped close to her skull where the wind can't catch it. Her face is a map of exposure: cheeks roughened to leather, eyes permanently narrowed against mist and brightness, lips cracked and healed and cracked again in patterns like the stone itself.

She does not live here always. No one could. But she lives here *enough* that the goats do not startle when she comes, that the swifts have learned the shape of her and subtract her from their calculations of threat. She climbs up from the valley perhaps once in a turning of the moon, sometimes more often when the pressure builds behind her breastbone, sometimes less when her knees refuse the steeper pitches. She knows seventeen routes to the ledge. She invented four of them.

Her hands are her most remarkable feature—broad across the palm, fingers thick and blunted, nails worn to nothing. They are hands that have stacked every cairn on this plateau, including the ones that are leaning now, the ones she built when she was young and did not yet understand how stone settles against stone over decades. She does not remember what the first cairn was for. A death, she thinks. Or a survival. The distinction blurs at this altitude.

She carries very little when she climbs: a leather satchel worn soft as skin, containing dried meat, a fire-steel, a small knife, a coil of cord. The cord is important. She has used it to lower herself to nests where injured swifts tangle in their own constructions, to retrieve kids that have miscalculated their footing on the ledge's edge, to mark her path through cloud so thick she could not see her own feet. The cord is thirty years old and has been repaired so many times it is more knot than rope.

She builds the cairns for the dead, but not only for the dead.

Some mark places where she has seen things—a particular quality of light through breaking cloud, a hawk killing a swift in mid-air and carrying it upward rather than down, a pattern in the lichen that looked, for one season only, like a face she once knew. Some mark promises she has made to herself: *I will return. I will climb this high again. I will not let the valley keep me.* Some mark nothing she can name. She simply knows, arriving at a particular spot, that a cairn belongs there. The knowing lives in her hands before it reaches her mind. She has learned not to argue with it.

The goats know her by smell, which is considerable—woodsmoke and wet wool and something sharper underneath, the scent of a body that has stopped apologizing for itself. The lead female, a heavy-horned creature with a black blaze across her muzzle, sometimes stands near while she works, watching with those unsettling horizontal pupils. She has named this goat *Assessor* because of the way it seems to judge the quality of each cairn. She talks to it while she stacks stones. The goat does not respond, but it does not leave either.

She knows the birds by their calls better than by their appearance. She can distinguish fourteen separate species in the cacophony, can tell you which are nesting, which are hunting, which are simply screaming because they are alive and full of the furious energy of small winged things. The red-throated ones—she calls them *grinders* for their ice-on-ice voices—she has watched for forty years and still cannot predict. They appear and disappear according to laws she has not deciphered. She respects them for their opacity.

At night, in the incomplete shelter she did not build but has maintained, she lies on the smooth stones and listens to the settling and dripping. Her body aches in ways she has stopped cataloging. The cold works into her joints, and she lets it, breathing through the discomfort the way she has learned to breathe through most things. She does not sleep well here. She has never slept well here. That is not why she comes.

She comes because the ledge asks nothing of her that she cannot give.

Down in the valley, there are people who want things from her—answers, explanations, the performance of emotions she may or may not feel. There are structures that require maintenance, relationships that require tending, a body that requires the particular ministrations of warmth and dryness and soft places to rest. She is not ungrateful for these things. She returns to them. But here, on the crying ledge, she is only what the stone and water and screaming birds need her to be: a creature that climbs, a stacker of cairns, a brief warm presence in a place that will forget her the moment she descends.

She has thought about dying here. Not morbidly—practically, the way she thinks about most things. The logistics of it. Whether her body would be found, and by whom. Whether the goats would investigate, the birds descend. She has decided it would not be the worst way. But she has also decided it is not yet time. There are more cairns to build. The leaning ones need restacking before they fall entirely. And there is a spot near the eastern edge, where the view opens to the highest of the distant peaks, where she has felt the knowing gathering for years now but has not yet understood what shape the stones should take.

When the clouds part, she stands at the ledge's edge and looks down at the world she will return to. It seems very small from here, and very busy, and very concerned with things that do not concern her. She has made her peace with this distance. She is not above the world, not better than it—she is simply *apart* from it, in the way the ledge is apart, in the way the goats and swifts and lichens are apart. They have all found the same solution to the problem of existence: climb high enough that the noise becomes a different kind of noise, one you can fold yourself into and disappear.

She turns back to the newest cairn, three stones high and waiting for its fourth. Her hands know the weight she needs before she finds it. The mist closes in. The birds scream. Somewhere below, the mountain weeps from its secret aquifers, pressing water up through fractured stone to emerge here, at this cupped hand of basalt, where a woman with lichen-colored hair stacks rocks for reasons she has stopped trying to explain, even to herself.
