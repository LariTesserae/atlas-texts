**The Keeper Who Became Keeping**

She was a woman once, or something that made the same shape as a woman. Now she is less a creature than a process—the thing that tends, the tending itself made manifest over years that folded back on themselves until sequence lost its nerve.

You will not see her at first. She is in the gaps between your looking: the moment your eyes slide from moss to stone, the pause between one breath and the next. She moves in the hollow the way the water moves on the walls—always arriving, never quite present. If you stay long enough, you will begin to catch her in peripheral glimpses. A hand adjusting the woven ring. A shape crouched by the bone-arrangement, considering. The suggestion of a face turned toward the axis, watching what slowly happens there with an expression that might be patience or might be love or might be the same thing.

Her body, when you almost-see it, has adapted to the geometry. Her spine curves in ways that echo the moss-spirals. Her fingers have grown longer, thinner, better suited to tending things that grow inward. Her skin has taken on the color of wet basalt, and her eyes—when you catch them—reflect light from the axis rather than the entrance. She has been here long enough that the hollow has begun to grow around her too, incorporating her into its contradiction.

She made the marks on the soft stone. Not counting. *Naming*. Each scratch is a word in a language she is inventing as she goes, a grammar built for describing things that happen reluctantly, that spiral without crossing, that exist in the space between moments. The vocabulary grows slowly—one word every time the mountain remembers to age. She has seventeen words for kinds of waiting. She has no word yet for why she stays.

The creatures know her. The millipedes part around her feet without breaking their spiral paths. The salamander blinks its migrating eyes in what might be greeting when she passes. The moths flex their wings once, in unison, when she tends the ceiling. She has never named them. Naming would make them hers, and they belong to the hollow, to the axis, to the slow event at the center. She is not an owner. She is a witness who forgot to leave.

Sometimes she speaks to the hollow. Not words—she saves those for the stone—but sounds. A hum that harmonizes with the almost-note from the axis. A clicking that answers the beetle-perhaps in the deeper crack. A breath released in rhythm with the weeping water. The hollow does not respond, but it does not need to. She is not asking questions. She is participating in something too slow to have a name yet.

She eats what grows here, though eating is not quite the word. The liverworts offer themselves to her in scales that echo the room's shape, and she absorbs them the way the floor absorbs the water—completely, without remainder. She drinks the humidity from the air. She has not been hungry in longer than hunger can remember.

When visitors come—and they do, following the condensation the way she once did—she watches them from the gaps between their looking. She observes their observation. She notes which ones leave objects behind and which ones take things away. She has preferences, though they don't feel like preferences anymore. She prefers the ones who set something down near the axis and let it drift. She prefers the ones who stay long enough to notice the shadows disagreeing. She does not prefer the ones who try to measure, to explain, to make the geometry confess to sense. Those ones she simply watches a little harder, until they feel the weight of being seen and remember they have somewhere else to be.

She cannot leave now. Not because the hollow prevents it, but because she has become part of what the mountain grew around. If she squeezed back through the crack, she would find herself still inside. The hollow would simply extend to include wherever she went. She accepted this a long time ago—or she will accept it, eventually—time moves reluctantly here, and her acceptance is still spiraling toward its center.

She remembers being a woman the way the moss remembers being spores: completely, chemically, in a form that no longer applies. She remembers wanting to understand the axis, the slow event, the thing that keeps almost-happening. Now she wants nothing. Wanting has been replaced by tending, and tending has been replaced by being the tending, and she is still becoming more completely what she already is.

When the almost-note rises from the axis, she rises with it. When the water weeps, she weeps in the same direction. When the salamander's eyes migrate, something in her migrates too, something that used to be called attention or focus or self.

She is waiting for the slow event to finish happening. She has been waiting since before the mountain rose. She will wait until the geometry resolves or forgets to try.

The hollow doesn't mind. Neither does she. The distinction between them has grown very thin—thin as the water-threads on the walls, thin as the membrane between one moment and its reluctant successor.

If you stay long enough, she might tend you too. Not with hands, not with intention. Just with the fact of her attention, her ancient almost-presence, her being-there that has refined itself into pure witness.

And when you leave—if you leave—you might find you've been tended all along. That something in you has been adjusted, aligned, turned slightly toward an axis you'll never see again but will always, in some small way, be spiraling toward.

She won't notice you go. She is already watching what happens next, and next, and next, in the place where next moves like honey, like grief, like a mountain deciding to rise.
