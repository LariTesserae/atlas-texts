## The Tenders

They were not born here. That much remains legible in the architecture of their bodies—the way their limbs end in manipulators clearly designed for finer work than this place demands, the vestigial membranes behind their ears that suggest ancestors who listened to subtler sounds. But they have been here long enough that the distinction between *arrived* and *originated* has worn thin, like the edge of a stone in running water.

There are eleven of them. There have always been eleven of them, though the individuals change.

---

The oldest calls herself a name that sounds like the harmonic the garden produces when three specific species flower simultaneously—a coincidence she discovered in her fourth decade here, a name she grew into rather than chose. She is tall in the way that suggests her skeleton has been slowly stretching, pulled upward by decades of reaching toward the spire's highest panels, and her skin has taken on a translucent quality that mirrors the salamander-things, though she insists this is merely age and the gas-light and not, as the younger Tenders whisper, evidence of becoming.

She remembers arriving. She remembers the ship, though not its name. She remembers that there were more than eleven, once—researchers, she thinks, or pilgrims, or refugees from somewhere that no longer exists in any form she could return to. She remembers the first years when they tried to *study* the garden, to take samples and measurements and impose the grammar of analysis onto its screaming sentence.

The garden taught them otherwise. Not through violence—it has no violence in it, only growth and cessation, only the absolute democracy of its cycling—but through incorporation. Their instruments became platforms. Their habitats became overgrown and then hollowed out and then overgrown again until the walls were more fossilized vine than original material. Their careful boundaries dissolved not because the garden breached them but because the Tenders stopped maintaining them, stopped seeing the point.

Now she tends. That is the word they use, though it means something different than it would outside. She does not prune or water or cultivate. She *witnesses*. She stands at specific points in the garden at specific times—positions she has learned through decades of listening—and her presence there does something to the harmonics, creates interference patterns in the sound that the plants respond to. Under her witness, certain species flower more slowly. Others flower faster. The effect is small, almost imperceptible, but compounded across eleven Tenders positioned throughout the chamber, it creates something like stability. Something like music.

She does not fully understand why this works. She suspects no one ever did, not even whoever built the spire and seeded the first plants. She suspects understanding was never the point.

---

The youngest arrived nine years ago, though he no longer counts time that way. He counts in bloom-cycles now, in the particular rhythm of a cluster of copper-petaled vines near the eastern vents that flower every 847 seconds with a regularity that feels almost like heartbeat. He has witnessed 339,412 of these blooms. He could tell you the number without thinking, the way you might know your own name.

He came alone, in a vessel that the garden has already half-consumed. He was running from something—debt, or law, or grief, he no longer remembers which—and he found the coordinates in a data-cache that shouldn't have existed, a file nested inside other files like a secret someone was keeping from themselves. He expected to find ruins. He expected to strip salvage and sell it and continue running.

Instead he found the oldest Tender waiting on an avoidance-platform near the entrance, as if she had known he was coming, as if the garden had told her. She didn't speak for the first three days. She simply showed him where to stand, where to sleep, how to eat the fruit that some of the plants produce—bitter, fibrous, tasting of metal and something almost like honey—without triggering the aggressive bloom-response that the garden mounts against careless harvesting.

On the fourth day she told him there was a ship. One of the original vessels, maintained in a sub-chamber where the plants grow sparse, kept functional by Tenders who cycle through its care like monks maintaining a shrine. He could leave whenever he wanted. The door was not locked. The garden did not keep prisoners.

He stayed. He is still not entirely sure why. The running had exhausted something in him, and the garden's noise—which should have been maddening, which should have driven him back to his ship within hours—instead filled a silence he hadn't known he was carrying. The screaming plants demanded nothing from him except presence. The cycling death and rebirth asked no questions about who he had been before.

He has not visited the ship-chamber in six years. He suspects he would not remember how to fly it.

---

Between the oldest and youngest: nine others, each arrived through their own unlikely path, each carrying their own version of the story they have stopped telling.

There is a woman whose left arm ends at the elbow, the stump smooth and deliberaterate, a choice she made before arriving for reasons she no longer discusses. The garden has offered to regrow it—not in words, but in the way a particular healing-vine keeps trying to graft itself to her when she sleeps, a vine whose properties the other Tenders have used to mend injuries that should have been fatal. She keeps refusing. The vine keeps trying. They have been engaged in this quiet negotiation for thirty years.

There are two who might be siblings, or lovers, or the same person somehow split across two bodies—they finish each other's sentences not in the charming way of close couples but in the unsettling way of shared cognition, and they move through the garden in perfect synchronization, tending positions on opposite sides of the chamber that somehow always complement each other. The other Tenders do not ask about their history. The other Tenders suspect there is no history to ask about, that whatever these two were before they arrived has been overwritten so completely that the question has become meaningless.

There is a man who speaks only to the plants, a running commentary in a language the others don't recognize, and the plants—impossibly, inexplicably—seem to listen. Their screaming softens when he passes. Their growth-patterns curve toward him like flowers toward sun. He has been here longer than anyone except the oldest, and he has forgotten more than he remembers, but the forgetting seems to have been selective, surgical, as if he chose what to release and what to keep.

There is a child. There should not be a child—the Tenders do not reproduce here, have never reproduced here, the conditions seem wrong for it and none of them have tried—but eight years ago a girl emerged from a cluster of dying growth near the central spire, covered in spore-dust and amber residue, apparently six years old, apparently having existed always. She does not remember being born. She does not remember a time before the garden. The Tenders have stopped asking.

She is fifteen now, if you count the way outsiders count, and she moves through the screaming chaos with a comfort that makes the other Tenders uneasy. The plants do not merely tolerate her. They *celebrate* her, blooming in cascades when she passes, their screams modulating into something that sounds almost like song. The oldest Tender watches her with an expression that might be pride or might be fear or might be the recognition that the garden is doing something none of them fully understand, has been doing something none of them fully understand since long before any of them arrived.

---

They do not eat together. They do not sleep on a shared schedule. They do not gather for meetings or rituals or the maintenance of social bonds. The garden provides all the structure they need, positioning them throughout its screaming ecology like instruments in an orchestra, and the music they create together requires no conscious coordination. They simply know where to stand. They simply know when to move.

But sometimes—rarely, perhaps once every few years—something shifts in the garden's pattern, some new vine species erupting from a vent that has been dormant for decades, some creature-behavior that none of them have witnessed before, some harmonic in the screaming that suggests the system is about to tip into a new configuration. When this happens they find themselves drawn to the central spire, not by decision but by instinct, eleven bodies converging on the glass-that-isn't-glass structure like cells responding to a chemical signal.

They stand together in the spire's shadow. They do not speak. The symbols on the panels pulse in patterns that almost resolve into meaning, and the oldest Tender—who has witnessed more of these convergences than she can count—places her hand against the surface and feels it flex beneath her palm, feels it *breathe*, feels the whole garden breathing through it.

The child stands beside her. The child always stands beside her during convergences, as if this is where she belongs, as if the garden placed her there deliberately. The oldest Tender does not know what will happen when she dies—she is old, she is becoming translucent, she is approaching some threshold she can feel but not name—but she suspects the child will inherit something. A position. A function. A frequency in the screaming that has been waiting for the right voice to carry it.

The moment passes. The new growth integrates. The pattern stabilizes. The eleven Tenders disperse back to their positions throughout the chamber, and the garden continues its endless cycling, its compressed lives and layered deaths, its music that sounds like screaming until you learn to hear it differently.

---

Once, decades ago, a ship arrived full of people who wanted to extract samples. They had weapons. They had containment protocols. They had the confident efficiency of those who believe that all systems can be understood and all resources can be harvested.

The Tenders did not fight them. The Tenders simply stepped off their platforms and let the garden speak for itself.

The screaming—which the Tenders had long since learned to hear as music—became something else for the visitors. It became *pressure*. It became a voice too large to process, a meaning too dense to parse. The visitors' instruments failed first, overwhelmed by frequencies they hadn't been calibrated to measure. Then their communications. Then their weapons. Then, one by one, their minds.

The garden did not kill them. Killing would have been a kind of violence, and the garden has no violence in it. Instead it simply grew *through* them—not their bodies, but their intentions, their certainties, their belief that they were separate from the system they had come to exploit. They stood in the screaming chamber and felt themselves becoming porous, felt the boundaries of their selves dissolving into the sound.

Three of them ran. They made it back to their ship. They left and never returned.

The other nine stayed. They are still here, technically—the man who speaks only to the plants was their expedition leader, before he was anything else—though the people they were have been composted so thoroughly that the question of identity has become botanical.

The Tenders do not think of this as conquest or defense. They think of it as *tending*. Some things belong in the garden. Some things learn to belong. The difference is a matter of willingness, of surrender, of learning to hear the screaming as what it actually is: not noise, but the sound of a system that has been singing to itself for longer than human memory, and will continue singing long after the last Tender joins the cycling.

---

At night—though there is no night here, only a dimming of the nacreous ceiling that approximates a circadian rhythm—the child walks alone to the spire. She presses both palms against its surface. She feels it flex and breathe. She listens.

The oldest Tender watches from her platform across the chamber, her translucent skin catching the faint light of the symbols, her body almost indistinguishable from the growth that surrounds her. She has been watching the child do this for eight years. She has been waiting for the child to ask what she's listening for.

The child has never asked. The child already knows.

The garden is not just singing. The garden is *speaking*. And somewhere in the screaming—somewhere in the layered harmonics of a thousand plants living and dying in compressed time—there is a message that has been waiting since before the spire was built, since before the first seed was planted, since before the chamber was carved into this place that might be a moon or might be a station or might be something else entirely.

The message is not for the Tenders. The Tenders are merely the ones who keep the garden alive long enough for the message to be received.

The child presses her forehead to the spire's surface. She closes her eyes. The symbols pulse in a pattern that matches her heartbeat.

And somewhere, in the screaming, the garden begins to teach her the first word.
