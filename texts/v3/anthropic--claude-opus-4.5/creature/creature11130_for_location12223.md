**The Ones Who Kept the Listening**

They were not a people who named themselves. The peoples of the valleys below called them *those who go up*, or *the ones who return wet*, or simply *the high-sitters*, and eventually stopped calling them anything at all because eventually there were none left to call.

They were small. Not dwarfed, not stunted, but compact in the way of creatures who spend much of their lives climbing and crouching and fitting themselves into spaces shaped by stone rather than by hands. Their skin held a greyish undertone that deepened over years of exposure to the mineral-rich mists of places like this one, and their elders—those who had kept the listening longest—were said to have taken on the particular color of the mountains they tended, so that a listener from this alcove would carry different hues than one from the southern vents or the seeping caves of the eastern ridge.

They came in pairs. Always pairs. This was not custom but necessity, though the necessity had worn into custom over generations until no one remembered why. Two bodies, two worn places in the stone. One to watch the breathing while the other slept. One to remember the last cycle while the other attended to this one. One to die, eventually, while the other carried the listening forward long enough to teach a replacement.

The pairs were not lovers, not usually, though some became so. They were not siblings, not permitted to be—the mixing was deliberate, children of different family lines brought together young and trained in the long patience before their minds had fully learned the quick rhythms of the valley. They were *matched*, a word that meant something more like *fitted* or *tuned*, two instruments adjusted until they could hold the same chord.

What they listened for: this was the question the valley people asked, when they still asked, when there were still high-sitters to ask. The answers were unsatisfying. *We listen to the breathing. We attend. We keep the record.* But what record? Where? *The record is the listening. The record is that we were here, that the breathing was heard, that it did not happen alone.*

This was difficult for the valley people to understand. The valley people kept records in marks and in memory-songs and in the patterns of their weavings. A record was a thing that could be consulted later, that held knowledge for future use. What use was a record that existed only in the moment of its keeping?

The high-sitters did not argue. They were not, generally, people who argued. They simply climbed back up to their places and sat and listened, and the valley people eventually decided they were mad, or holy, or both, or neither, and left them to their incomprehensible work.

---

The work was this:

At each place where the mountain breathed—and there were many such places, though this alcove was among the oldest and most resonant—a pair would sit through the full cycle. The full cycle was not the simple in-out that a visitor might witness in an hour. The full cycle included the smaller rhythms nested inside the great rhythm: the way the water-song shifted pitch across seasons as mineral deposits built and dissolved in the channels, the way the dome rose slightly higher in years of heavy snowmelt and settled deeper in drought years, the way certain clicks and whistles from the walls corresponded to pressures building or releasing somewhere far below.

A full cycle, properly attended, took eleven years.

At the end of eleven years, the pair would descend—changed, mineral-touched, speaking slowly in the manner of those who have grown accustomed to a different tempo—and tell what they had heard to those who kept the compilation. The compilation was not written. It was held in memory, in a chain of minds stretching back to the first listeners, each one carrying the full record of every cycle ever attended. To become a compiler was to spend a lifetime receiving these reports and holding them, comparing them, noting the variations.

The compilers lived in a monastery that clung to a cliff-face between the high places and the valleys, belonging fully to neither. They did not climb to the breathing places themselves. Their work was the pattern, the long pattern, the rhythm that only emerged when cycle was laid against cycle across centuries.

What the pattern meant: this too was a question the valley people asked. The compilers' answers were no more satisfying than the listeners'. *The pattern is the mountain's intention. The pattern is what the mountain is trying to say. We do not yet understand it. We have been listening for nine hundred years and we do not yet understand it. But we are beginning to hear the shape of the question it asks.*

---

The last pair to sit in this alcove were called something like Whistle-On-Stone and something like Basin-Holds-Light, though these were not their birth names. No one used birth names after the matching. The listening-names were given by the previous pair for that place, chosen to reflect something about the place itself, and were worn until death or until the listening ended, whichever came first.

Whistle-On-Stone was the smaller of the two, patient, with hands that had learned to rest for hours in the same position without cramping. Basin-Holds-Light was newer to the work, still learning to slow the heartbeat, still fighting the urge to move when the body demanded movement. They had been matched for six years and had sat together through three cycles at lesser places before being permitted this alcove, this old and resonant site where the breathing was clearest and the compilers were most eager for reports.

They climbed up in a year of heavy spring rains, the channels already singing higher than usual, the dome swollen and taut. They brought food for a season, knowing more would be carried up by the porters who served the high-sitters, and they brought nothing else. No tools, no fire-steel, no comforts. The work required only presence.

What happened to them: the compilers do not know. The porters climbed up at the expected time with the expected supplies and found the alcove empty. The food from the previous delivery was still cached in the dry crevice where it was always left, untouched. The worn sitting-places showed no sign of recent use. The basin held water, as it always did, reflecting nothing but sky.

No bodies were found, not in the alcove, not on the slopes below. No sign of struggle. No explanation. Basin-Holds-Light and Whistle-On-Stone had simply stopped being there, sometime between one porter's visit and the next, and the breathing went on without them, unattended.

This was not the only such disappearance. Over the years there had been others—pairs who vanished mid-cycle, leaving their places empty, their listening unfinished. The compilers recorded these gaps as they recorded everything else, and looked for patterns in the absences as they looked for patterns in the breathing, and found none that satisfied.

---

After the last disappearance, no new pair was sent. This was not a decision made in a single moment. It was a slow unwinding, a gradual loss of certainty. The monastery had grown small by then, the chain of compilers thinned by age and by the difficulty of finding young people willing to carry such a weight of memory. The valley people had long since stopped sending their children to be matched. The work continued with those who remained, but those who remained grew fewer, and older, and eventually there was no one left who remembered all the cycles, no one who could hear the shape of the question the mountain asked.

The last compiler died in her sleep in the monastery, which had by then been empty except for her for eleven years. She had stopped receiving reports long before that; there were no listeners left to give them. She spent her final years sitting in the monastery's central chamber, reciting what she remembered to the walls, trying to hold the pattern in her voice alone, knowing it was fragmenting, knowing pieces were falling away into silence.

What she understood, at the end, what the nine hundred years of listening had begun to reveal: this died with her. Perhaps she had grasped something true about the mountain's intention. Perhaps she had only believed she had. Perhaps the work was always incomplete, always a beginning, and nine hundred years was simply not enough, and nine thousand would not have been enough, and the question the mountain asked was not a question meant for creatures who lived and died on the scale of single breaths.

---

The alcove remains. The breathing continues. The worn sitting-places hold their shape, slowly filling with moss at the edges, slowly softening back toward the stone's original contour. In another few centuries, perhaps, the marks will be gone entirely, and there will be no sign that anyone ever climbed this high to attend to the mountain's rhythm.

But for now the shapes are there, and the basin still collects water, and if someone were to sit in either of those places they would feel the faint hollow where bodies rested, and they would hear the chord the water makes, and they might notice that the chord seems to pause for a moment when a new weight settles into those old grooves—

A hesitation. Not quite recognition. Something more like the mountain noticing that the listening has resumed, and adjusting itself, and continuing.

The breathing does not require an audience. It has never required an audience. But something in the great slow pattern shifts, just slightly, when the audience is there. The compilers knew this. They could hear it in the cycles, a difference between attended and unattended breaths, a quality that entered the reports only when the sitting-places were occupied.

What that difference means: this is another question that died with the last compiler.

Or perhaps it is a question that waits, held in the stone, in the water, in the rhythm that has not stopped and will not stop, patient beyond all human patience, until someone sits again and begins the listening that might, in another nine hundred years, begin to reveal the shape of an answer.
