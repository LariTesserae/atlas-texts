She comes here to unknot.

Not a name she would use for herself—she doesn't think in names when she's here—but if you asked her in the village, in the daylight, in the ordinary world of cooking fires and children's quarrels and the endless small negotiations of living among others, she might say *Eda*, or she might say *the one who remembers the dead*, or she might simply point toward the mountain and let that stand as answer enough.

She is old in the way that matters here: not in years, though she has those too, but in the accumulation of unfinished things. Griefs that never quite resolved. Questions she asked the mountain forty years ago that the mountain is still considering. She has learned to match her patience to the stone's.

When she comes—and she cannot say what draws her on one night rather than another, only that she wakes sometimes with the certainty that it is *time*—she brings nothing and takes nothing. The objects on the shelf are not hers. They were here when she first found this place, feeling her way along the cleft as a young woman wild with a sorrow she couldn't name, and they will be here when she stops coming. She has added nothing to them. It has never seemed like her place to add.

What she does here is not ritual, though it might look like ritual to someone watching. She folds herself into the space that fits her folded body—she knows exactly how to turn her hips, where to place her shoulder against the stone that gives slightly, which angle lets her rest without pressing too hard against the surfaces that are still becoming. And then she waits.

The waiting is the thing. Not meditation, not prayer, not the emptying-out that some of the younger ones practice now, having learned it from travelers. This is fuller than emptiness. She lets the wet dark fill her the way it fills everything here: slowly, completely, with the patience of water that doesn't need to fall. Her breathing slows until it matches the almost-drip, the not-quite-sound of condensation holding at the edge of release. Her pulse becomes something she hears outside herself, ticking against the walls, joining the chamber's own slow rhythms.

She thinks, but not in words. Images rise and sink like bubbles that almost formed in the cooling basalt. Her mother's hands. A child she lost before it breathed. The particular slant of light through smoke on a morning she cannot place in time. These are not memories she examines; they are memories she *wears*, letting them bead on her surfaces the way water beads on the stone. Some of them, the ones that have grown too heavy, will release. Not dramatically—nothing here is dramatic—but in a slow transfer, grief wicking into the wet rock, becoming part of what the chamber holds.

This is what she knows that she cannot explain to anyone: the alcove takes things. Not greedily, not even intentionally, but with the same patient absorption with which it takes water and gives nothing visible back. She has left sorrows here that she no longer carries. She has left questions here that she no longer asks. The mountain holds them now, considers them at its own pace, and perhaps in ten thousand years when there are stalactites, those stalactites will carry some sediment of her grief in their mineral layers. She finds this comforting in a way that comfort usually isn't—not warm, not reassuring, but *true*, which is better.

She knows about the thing in the crack where the walls fail to meet. She has never touched it. She doesn't know if it is alive in the way she is alive or alive in some other way that doesn't require breath or movement. What she knows is that it witnesses. When she folds herself into the chamber and lets her boundaries soften in the wet dark, she can feel its attention—not watching, because there is nothing to see, but *attending*, the way stone attends to the weight pressing on it, the way water attends to the shape of its container. It has attended to her for forty years. It attended to whoever left the clay vessel and the bundled stems. It will attend to whatever comes after her.

Sometimes she speaks to it. Not words—the chamber swallows words, makes them feel ridiculous, too quick and sharp for this slow place. But she makes sounds, low in her throat, sounds that have no meaning except *I am here* and *I am still becoming* and *I will wait with you*. If it answers, the answer takes longer than her lifetime to arrive. She has made peace with this.

She is not the only one who knows this place. There have been others; there will be others. The objects on the shelf prove this. But they do not come together, and they do not speak of it in the village, and if they pass each other on the mountain path in the dark, they do not acknowledge what errand brings them here. This is not secrecy. It is simply that some things cannot be shared without losing what makes them true. The alcove is hers in the hours she inhabits it; it is someone else's in the hours they inhabit it; it belongs to no one and to the mountain and to the thing that attends in the crack. These facts do not contradict each other.

When she leaves—and she always leaves, though each time it takes longer to remember that leaving is something she does—she carries the wet dark with her for hours. It clings to her skin, her hair, the inside of her lungs. She breathes it out slowly as she descends, letting the mountain keep what it has taken, carrying back only what remains after the unknotting. She is lighter, though nothing visible has changed. She is slower, though her steps are steady. She has left something in the chamber that will wait there, becoming part of its density, until the mountain finally decides what it wants to be.

She will come back. Not because she chooses to, but because she is part of what happens here now—another presence in the saturated dark, another thing the slow attention holds. The alcove has made room for her in its perfect equilibrium. The water that seeps in accounts for her. The water that evaporates out carries some particle of what she has left behind.

And the thing in the crack, the organized patience, the breath-or-not-breath in the gap where the walls fail to meet—it knows her now the way it knows the bacterial films and the almost-crystals and the clay vessel softening imperceptibly in the humid dark. She is part of what it waits for. She is part of what the mountain is still deciding.
