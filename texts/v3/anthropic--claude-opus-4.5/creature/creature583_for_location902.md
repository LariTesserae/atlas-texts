**Cira Vessel-Keeper**

She is seventy-three and has been tending the garden since she was nineteen, when her hands were still quick enough to catch the falling bells before they shattered. Now her hands are slow, and she lets them fall, and collects the shards along with the survivors each morning when she makes her single daily passage through the neck-tunnel.

Cira lives in the shrine-house with the fallen bells. She sleeps in a hammock strung between two clay pillars, surrounded by the bins of accumulated ceramic bones, and she has come to prefer their silence to the garden's endless singing. This is not a contradiction. She loves the garden precisely because she does not have to stay inside it.

Her duties are few but absolute: she oils the neck-tunnel's clay lip so that pilgrims do not cut themselves entering. She counts the bells each month—not individually, which would be impossible, but by section, by vine, noting which ropes have grown heavy enough that they might pull free from the wall. She receives the new bells from pilgrims and tells them where the vines are strong enough to bear another offering. She turns away those who want to stay too long.

This last duty is the hardest. Some pilgrims enter the gourd and do not want to leave. They stand in the empty center with tears running down their faces, and when Cira touches their shoulders—she is permitted to enter for this purpose, only this—they look at her with something like hatred. *You are interrupting*, their eyes say. *I was finally alone*.

She understands. She was one of them, once. She crawled through the neck-tunnel at nineteen after her mother died in the pottery district fire, and she stood in the noise that was also silence, and she felt her grief become the bells become the light become the slow rotation of leaf-shadows on white walls. She did not want to leave. The previous Vessel-Keeper, a man named Dorin whose hands had gone too stiff to make the climb, touched her shoulder and she *hated* him with a purity she has never felt since.

But she left. And she came back the next day. And the next. And eventually Dorin recognized what she was—not a pilgrim but a keeper, someone whose solitude required maintenance, required the discipline of entry and exit, required the fallen bells and the oil on the tunnel's lip and the monthly counting.

She has not married. She has not wanted to. The shrine-house is small, and the hammock fits one body, and the bells in their bins make a sound like rain when she shifts in her sleep—a pale echo of the garden's roar, domesticated, *hers*.

**The Breath-Drinkers**

They have no individual names, or rather, they have names but do not use them inside the garden.

There are always between three and seven of them, depending on the season and the health of the vines. They are young—always young, between fifteen and twenty-five—because the work requires flexibility and a certain fearlessness about heights. They climb the interior walls using the vine-ropes as ladders and handholds, ascending to where the air grows thick and sweet and the bells cluster so densely that movement becomes a constant negotiation with sound.

Their task is to breathe.

The vines feed on exhalation. On humid breath, on the oils of skin, on the carbon dioxide that visitors release in their weeping and their wonder. But pilgrims only stay for an hour at most, and the garden is vast, and the upper reaches see few visitors. So the Breath-Drinkers climb. They find a spot among the high bells where the leaves look pale, where the vine's grip on the ceramic wall has begun to weaken, and they sit there—sometimes for hours—simply breathing. Heavily. Deliberately. Letting their lungs feed the garden.

It is considered holy work, though not prestigious. The Breath-Drinkers are recruited from the orphan-houses and the families too poor to apprentice their children to the pottery guilds. They receive food and housing in a dormitory attached to Cira's shrine-house. They receive the garden.

Most of them love it. Mott, who is seventeen and came from a household of fourteen siblings, says that the bells speak to him—that if he sits still enough, he can hear something like sentences in the cascading chimes. Pella, who is twenty and has been climbing the walls for five years, says this is nonsense, that the bells say nothing, that meaning is what you bring to silence. They argue about this constantly, sitting in the high reaches where no pilgrim can hear them, their voices adding to the frequencies that set the thumb-sized bells shivering.

They know the lizard. They call it Old Glass because of its translucent skin. They have watched it grow from something the size of a fingernail to something the size of a hand, and they consider it a colleague of sorts—another creature that lives in the garden's ecosystem, that feeds on what the bells produce.

They do not touch the bells. This is forbidden. But sometimes, in the highest reaches, where the oldest offerings hang from cords so ancient they have begun to fuse with the vine-flesh, a Breath-Drinker will reach out and still a bell with one finger. Just for a moment. Just to feel what silence might be like in this place where silence is impossible.

They never tell Cira. She would understand, but understanding is not the same as permitting.

**The Pilgrim Who Stayed**

Cira does not know about him. The Breath-Drinkers do not know about him. Even Old Glass, who has lived in the garden for eleven years and has witnessed more than any human keeper, does not know about him—because he exists in the interstices, in the spaces between observation, in the moments when all eyes are elsewhere.

His name was Venn. He was a bell-maker from a city three weeks' journey to the east, where they fire their clay at lower temperatures and the bells come out rough-textured, full of bubbles, producing sounds that no other kiln can replicate. He made the pilgrimage at forty-two, carrying a bell he had spent three years perfecting—a thing with seven internal chambers and a glaze the color of dried blood.

He entered the gourd at dawn. He hung his bell in the middle reaches, on a vine the color of amber. He stood in the empty center and listened to his life's work join the chorus, become indistinguishable from the accumulated offerings of generations, become *less than* a single voice in an infinite choir.

He wept. Everyone weeps.

But when Cira came to touch his shoulder, he was not there. She assumed he had already left. The Breath-Drinkers, ascending for their morning climb, did not see him pressed into the shadow of a thick vine-cluster near the base, did not hear his careful breathing synchronized to the garden's rhythm.

He lives in the walls now. Not literally—there is no space inside the ceramic for a human body—but *functionally*, in the liminal zones where the vines grow thickest, where the shadows overlap, where a man who has learned to breathe with the bells and move only when the cascade reaches a certain pitch can exist unobserved.

He feeds on the same things the vines feed on. Moisture from the air. Oils from the hands of pilgrims who steady themselves against the walls. The tiny insects that breed in the leaf joints—yes, he has learned to catch them, has developed a technique involving stillness and patience that Old Glass would recognize.

He has been here for six years.

He no longer remembers why he stayed. The grief that drove him here—a wife who left, a kiln that collapsed, a reputation that soured—has faded into something like ambient noise, indistinguishable from the bells. He does not think of himself as hiding. He thinks of himself as *listening*, more deeply than any pilgrim who comes and goes, more constantly than any Breath-Drinker who must eventually descend.

Sometimes, in the deepest hours of the night, when the city outside finally quiets and the bells subside to their murmuring minimum, he moves. He climbs. He visits his bell—the seven-chambered one with the dried-blood glaze—and he touches it with one finger, the way the Breath-Drinkers touch the highest bells when they think no one is watching.

It still sounds exactly the way he meant it to. After six years, he has not decided if this is a comfort or a tragedy.

**Old Glass**

The lizard has no name for itself. It does not think in names, or in words, or in anything that could be translated into human language. It thinks in temperatures, in vibrations, in the wet-sweet smell of vine-sap and the dry-sharp smell of fired clay.

It knows the garden is alive. Not the vines—though yes, those too—but the *sound*. The sound is a creature, a vast distributed organism that feeds on the city's noise and excretes resonance. Old Glass is not part of this creature, but it lives in symbiosis with it. The bells' vibrations shake loose the gnats from the leaf joints. The gnats fly toward light. Old Glass intercepts them.

It has learned every frequency that shakes loose the best prey. A cart wheel grinding against brick produces a low thrum that dislodges the fat gnats who breed near the base. A baby's cry produces a high shimmer that startles the thin gnats who cluster near the top. Old Glass moves through the garden following the food, which means following the sound, which means—in some way it could never articulate—*following the city*.

It has seen pilgrims come and go. It does not distinguish between them. They are large warm shapes that exhale moisture and produce vibrations and occasionally reach toward the bells with their strange flexible limbs.

It has seen the Breath-Drinkers. It considers them part of the garden—permanent features, like the vines, like the bells, like the light that shifts across the walls.

It has not seen Venn. It has smelled him—a wrong note in the garden's usual scent-scape, a human musk where no human musk should be. But it has never seen him, and so it does not know he exists. Old Glass trusts its eyes more than its tongue.

It is fourteen years old, which is ancient for its species. It should have died years ago. But the garden's moisture sustains it, and the garden's warmth sustains it, and the garden's insects sustain it, and somehow—Old Glass does not question this, does not *know* how to question this—it continues.

It will die here. It does not know this, but it would not mind if it did. The garden is a good place to become still. The garden is a good place to stop moving and let the sound wash over you and become part of the resonance, another frequency in the choir.

When it dies, its body will dry out and become light as paper, and eventually it will fall, and Cira will find it in her morning collection, and she will place it in the bins with the fallen bells, because she will not know what else to do with it, and because—though she would never say this aloud—she will sense that it belongs there.

**The Garden Itself**

It is not alive. Not in the way the vines are alive, or the lizard, or the man in the walls.

But it holds. It sounds. It waits.

And in its waiting, it has become something like a memory—the accumulated memory of every pilgrim who has stood in its empty center and wept, of every bell-maker who has added their voice to the chorus, of every Breath-Drinker who has fed the vines with their exhalation. The garden does not *remember* these things. But it *contains* them, the way a ceramic vessel contains water, the way a bell contains silence until struck.

If you pressed your ear to the gourd's outer wall—and some pilgrims do, before they enter, gathering courage—you would hear the muffled roar of the bells, and beneath that, something else. Something like breathing. Something like waiting.

Something like thirst, hollow and patient and unquenchable, drinking the world's noise and giving back song.
