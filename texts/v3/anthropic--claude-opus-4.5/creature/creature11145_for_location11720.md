They came here to process grief.

Not metaphorically—literally. The plateau's mineral seepage contains compounds that, when extracted and refined, produce a substance that softens the sharp edges of loss. Not erases it. Not numbs it. Softens it, the way water softens stone over centuries, wearing away the jagged places until what remains is smooth enough to hold without cutting yourself.

The factories were built by a consortium of peoples who had experienced too much death too quickly—a plague, a war, a collapse, something that left whole generations carrying weight they could not put down. They came here because the plateau was empty, because the heat and moisture kept casual visitors away, because the work of extraction required exactly the kind of focused, repetitive labor that made it possible to get through one hour and then another. They built the channels to manage the water. They built the walkways to manage themselves. They built infrastructure for a population of the bereaved, and the bereaved came, and for a while the city functioned exactly as intended.

---

The workers did not speak to each other about what they had lost.

This was not a rule. It was simply what happened. You came here because the weight was too heavy to carry alone, but also too heavy to share—too specific, too yours, too much a thing that language would only flatten into something bearable and therefore false. So you worked beside others in the steam and the heat, and you knew they were carrying their own weights, and the knowing was enough. The silence between you was not empty. It was full of everything neither of you would say.

They developed hand signals for the work. Practical things: *pressure rising*, *filter clogged*, *shift ending*. But also other things, things that had no practical application: *I am still here*, *today is hard*, *I dreamed of them again*. A whole vocabulary of gesture that meant nothing and everything, that let you communicate without communicating, that acknowledged the presence of another person's grief without requiring you to witness it directly.

The crabs were here before them. The plateau crabs, filtering their channels, building their mineral shells. The workers watched them at first with curiosity, then with something like recognition. Creatures that processed what the plateau gave them. Creatures that extracted what they needed and let the rest flow past. Creatures that clicked their small sounds into the silence and expected no response.

Some workers began to feed them. Not food—the crabs needed nothing but the mineral water—but attention. Sitting by the channels during rest periods, watching the crabs work, letting the clicking become a kind of company. The crabs did not respond to this attention in any obvious way. They continued their filtering, their slow movements, their patient chemistry. But they did not avoid the watching workers either. They accepted being witnessed, the way the plateau accepted the factories, the way the silence accepted the small sounds that interrupted it.

---

The long-bodied creatures came later.

No one is certain when. They were simply there one day, moving through the abandoned sections of the city, navigating the machinery as if they had always known it. The workers who first saw them were afraid, then curious, then something else—something that had no name in the vocabulary they had developed.

The creatures pressed their heads together when they met. Long moments of contact, then separation, then continuing in different directions. The workers watched this and understood something, though they could not have said what. A greeting? A transfer of information? A mutual acknowledgment that each was still carrying what it carried, still moving in the direction it was moving, still present in the steam and the heat and the silence?

The workers began to do the same.

Not pressing heads together—that would have been too strange, too animal, too much an admission of what they had become. But pausing when they passed each other. Meeting eyes for a moment, or not meeting eyes, depending on what each could bear. A brief stillness in the flow of bodies through the city, an acknowledgment that lasted exactly as long as it needed to last, and then movement resuming. The long-bodied creatures did not teach them this. They simply demonstrated that it was possible.

---

The population thinned over time, but not through departure.

Some workers finished their processing. The grief did not disappear—it never disappeared—but it became something they could carry back to wherever they had come from, something that fit inside them instead of displacing everything else. They left the plateau and returned to lives that were shaped by loss but not consumed by it. The factories had done what they were built to do.

Others stayed. Not because their grief was too large—though some griefs were too large—but because they had become part of the plateau's chemistry. They had sweated in the heat for so long that they no longer noticed the heat. They had breathed the mineral air until their lungs expected it. They had spent so many hours in the productive silence that ordinary sound felt like violence against their ears.

These workers spread out. The city had been built for density, but they no longer needed density. They needed space—the kind of space where you could work alone for hours and then encounter another person and pause, and acknowledge, and continue. They moved into the abandoned districts, the distant factory complexes, the structures that had been built for a population that would never come. They maintained what they needed and let the rest accumulate minerals, let the plateau reclaim what it could.

Their children—some had children, either brought with them or born here—grew up in the silence. They learned the hand signals before they learned spoken language. They learned to work the machinery, to extract the grief-softening compounds, to process what the plateau provided. They did not carry the specific losses their parents carried, but they carried the knowledge of those losses, the shape of them, the weight that had become their inheritance.

---

The sky-things arrived when the second generation reached adulthood.

Or perhaps they had always been there and only now descended low enough to be seen. They circled on the thermals, their wrong number of wings catching currents that rose from the heated stone, their subsonic calls making the workers' teeth ache in a way that felt almost like recognition. The plateau crabs paused when the pulses came, tracking upward with their eyestalks, and the workers learned to pause too—to let the sound that wasn't sound move through them, to feel it in their bones before returning to their work.

The sky-things never landed. They never approached. They only circled, and called, and waited. The workers developed theories: the creatures were waiting for the right conditions, the right temperature, the right concentration of minerals in the air. They were waiting for the factories to produce enough of the grief-softening compound that it saturated the atmosphere. They were waiting for the workers themselves to become something—processed enough, softened enough, ready for whatever came next.

The theories did not matter. What mattered was the circling, the calling, the patience that extended across years and then decades. The sky-things became part of the landscape, as much as the crabs, as much as the long-bodied creatures, as much as the factories themselves. You looked up and they were there. You looked down and you continued your work. The plateau provided, and you processed, and something watched from above, and this was simply how things were.

---

The deep pool dwellers do not interact with the surface.

The workers know they are there—shapes moving below the mineral-clouded water, serpentine or tentacular or collective, impossible to resolve into certainty. The pools steam at their edges, heated baths that no one enters, that would cook an unprotected body within minutes. Whatever lives in that heat has adapted to conditions that should support nothing. Like the crabs. Like the workers themselves.

Some of the older workers believe the pool dwellers are processing something too. Not grief—or not only grief—but something else, something that the plateau provides in its depths that it does not provide on its surface. The deeper you go, they say, the more concentrated the chemistry becomes. The more powerful the compounds. The more thorough the softening.

They do not try to reach the pool dwellers. They do not try to communicate. They only acknowledge, in the vocabulary of gesture that has no words for it, that something else is down there, carrying what it carries, doing what it does. The acknowledgment is enough. It has always been enough.

---

A worker stands on metal grating above a mineral stream.

She has been here for eleven years. She came carrying the death of her sister, who was also her twin, who was also the person she had expected to grow old beside. The weight of that loss had been too heavy to carry alone, too specific to share, too much a thing that compressed her into someone she did not recognize. She came to the plateau because she heard it could help. She stayed because it did, and then because she no longer knew how to leave.

Her hands move through the familiar motions of her work—adjusting valves, monitoring pressures, extracting what can be extracted and letting the rest flow past. The steam rises around her. The crabs click in their channels below. Somewhere in the haze, a long-bodied creature is moving between structures, navigating machinery that has become landscape, carrying whatever it carries in its glistening silence.

She does not think about her sister constantly anymore. She thinks about her sometimes—in the mornings, when the haze is thickest, when the white sky presses down like a hand on her chest. She thinks about the way her sister laughed, the specific sound of it, the way it made space in any room for joy to enter. She thinks about the absence of that sound, the silence that replaced it, the silence that she has learned to fill with clicking and pulsing and the constant run of water over stone.

The grief has not disappeared. It has softened. It fits inside her now, a smooth weight she carries without cutting herself, a presence that shapes her without crushing her. She is not the person she was before her sister died. She is not the person she was when she arrived. She is someone else, someone processed, someone who belongs to the plateau now as much as the crabs belong, as much as the creatures belong, as much as the mineral formations belong that grow in branching structures at the edges of pools.

Another worker passes on the grating nearby. They pause. They meet eyes for a moment—or not, depending on what each can bear. A brief stillness, an acknowledgment that lasts exactly as long as it needs to last, and then movement resuming. The other worker continues toward the distant factory where she tends her own machinery, carries her own weight, processes her own portion of what the plateau provides.

Above them, something circles on thermals, patient and strange. Below them, something moves in the mineral depths, serpentine or tentacular or collective. Around them, the city continues—sparse within its own sprawl, built for a density that exists in the spaces between workers rather than in their proximity, infrastructure maintained and inhabited by people who have learned that presence does not require closeness, that acknowledgment does not require words, that grief can be processed without ever being spoken.

The plateau sweats. The factories breathe. The woman returns to her work, her hands moving through motions that have become as natural as breathing, as constant as the water that runs warm beneath her feet. She is carrying what she carries. She is still here. Today is hard, or today is easier, or today is simply today—another unit of time in which the work continues, in which the silence holds what it holds, in which the softening proceeds at exactly the pace it needs to proceed.

She does not know if she will ever leave. She does not know if leaving is something she wants, or something she fears, or something that has simply become irrelevant—a question from a different life, asked by a person she no longer entirely is. She knows that the plateau will continue whether she stays or goes. She knows that the crabs will click, that the creatures will move, that the sky-things will circle and call and wait for conditions that may or may not ever arrive.

She knows that her sister is dead, and that this knowledge no longer tears her open every time she touches it. She knows that this is what she came here for. She knows that this is enough.

The water runs. The steam rises. The work continues.

Everything continues.
