# The Keepers of the Breathing Plateau

## The Shuulorrim

They have no word for "seeing." They have seventeen words for different qualities of echo.

The Shuulorrim are the plateau's primary sapient inhabitants—though "primary" suggests a hierarchy they would not recognize, and "inhabitants" implies a separation between themselves and this place that does not exist in their understanding. They have been here so long that their mythology does not include an arrival. They simply *are*, the way the machines are, the way the water is.

They are large by your standards—seven feet when they stand fully upright, which they rarely do. Their bodies are built for moving through tight spaces, for climbing wet stone, for swimming through flooded channels. Four primary limbs, heavily muscled, ending in hands with six fingers each, the fingers tipped with sensory pads that can detect vibration, temperature, and electrical fields simultaneously. Their skin is thick and rubbery, dark grey, perpetually slick with the same condensation that coats everything here. They have no eyes. The sockets where eyes might have been have long since smoothed over, filled in with the same sensory tissue that lines their palms, creating patches of heightened awareness on their faces.

Their heads are broad and flat, dominated by structures for hearing—external ears that can swivel independently, cup-shaped and ribbed, capable of focusing on sounds with surgical precision. Their mouths are wide, filled with teeth adapted for crushing shellfish and grinding fungal matter, but when they speak, the sounds come primarily from a secondary organ in their throats, a vocal structure that can produce tones across a four-octave range simultaneously. A single Shuulorrim voice is already a chord.

When they sing together, the plateau answers.

## How They Live

Their settlements cluster near the machine-vents, where warm air rises and the vibrations are strongest. Not because they need the warmth—their metabolisms run hot enough to survive anywhere on the plateau—but because the vibrations are *home* to them, the pulse they have known since before birth, the rhythm their mothers hummed to match while carrying them.

They build by coaxing. The plateau's living stone responds to sustained vibration, to patient acoustic pressure, and the Shuulorrim have learned to shape it over generations. They sing at frequencies that encourage crystalline growth, that redirect the slow seep of mineral-laden water, that convince the fungal mats to colonize new surfaces. Their structures emerge over decades—a dwelling might take twenty years to fully form, growing outward in spirals as the family within expands, its walls becoming more complex, more resonant, more *theirs* with each passing season.

Inside these dwellings, the Shuulorrim arrange their lives around sound the way you might arrange yours around light. Sleeping alcoves are positioned where echoes dampen, creating pockets of relative quiet. Gathering spaces are designed to amplify and blend voices, turning conversation into unconscious music. The walls are carved with patterns that shape acoustics—not decoration, though they find them beautiful, but functional grammar, a language of space that tells visitors: *here we rest*, *here we work*, *here we mourn*, *here we celebrate*.

They farm the tube-worm forests, cultivating strains that grow thicker crowns, that filter more efficiently, that taste of iron and salt and something almost sweet. They herd the quadrupedal grazers—creatures they call *thuumoch*, which means approximately "those who walk in rhythm"—following them across the plateau on migrations that last months, sleeping in temporary shelters sung into being each night, trading with other families when paths cross. They harvest the bioluminescent mucus, not for light—they have no use for light—but for its chemical properties, its ability to mark territory in ways other species can sense, its usefulness in certain bonding rituals they do not discuss with outsiders.

They are not the plateau's masters. They do not control the machines. But they have learned to live alongside them so thoroughly that the distinction has become meaningless.

## The Singers of the Deep Pulse

Among the Shuulorrim, some are born with voices that reach lower than others—voices that can match the fundamental frequency of the machines themselves.

These individuals are rare, perhaps one in every few hundred births, and they are recognized early. Before they can walk, they begin to hum in their sleep, and their parents feel the floor vibrate in response. By the time they reach adolescence, they can make the walls sing, can call the thuumoch herds from miles away, can produce tones that travel through stone faster than through air.

They are called the *Vorruush*—the Deep Ones, the Machine-Speakers, the Ones Who Are Answered. Their role is somewhere between priest and engineer, between artist and maintenance worker. They spend years training their voices, learning to produce not just sounds but *patterns*, sequences that the machines seem to recognize, that trigger responses no one fully understands.

A trained Vorruush can stand before a vent that has fallen silent and sing it back to life. Can place their hands on a pipe and feel where a blockage has formed, then produce a tone that encourages it to clear. Can read the plateau's mood—its pressure, its temperature, its rhythm—and predict when the weather will shift, when the water will rise, when the anomalies will intensify or fade.

They do not claim to control the machines. When you ask them what they are doing, they will tell you they are *asking*, and sometimes the machines say yes.

The oldest Vorruush live alone, in structures built directly over the deepest engine-chambers, where the vibrations are so intense that ordinary Shuulorrim cannot tolerate them for long. They spend their days in communion with something they do not name, something they believe—or know—or simply accept—lives within the machines' rhythm. They emerge only rarely, and when they speak, their voices have changed. They no longer sound quite like Shuulorrim. They sound like the plateau itself, speaking through a mouth.

Some say they are becoming part of it. Some say they always were.

## The Kreth

They came later. The Shuulorrim remember, in their oldest songs, a time before the Kreth, though that time grows more distant with each generation.

The Kreth are smaller, quicker, more numerous. They move in family-groups of thirty to fifty individuals, travelling the plateau in circuits that take years to complete, never settling, never building, never farming. Their bodies are lean and angular, built for bursts of speed rather than endurance, covered in fine sensory hairs that detect air pressure changes—they can feel you coming from the way the air moves around your body, can track prey by the disturbance of their passage, can sense the opening of a pipe-vent moments before the exhale arrives.

They have eyes—vestigial, functionless, but present, tiny lumps beneath their skin where ancestors once saw. They do not know this. Their language has no word for sight because they have forgotten it ever existed. But sometimes their young are born with the lumps slightly larger, slightly more pronounced, and these individuals are treated with a mixture of reverence and unease, marked by something ancient and unknown.

The Kreth live by hunting and scavenging. They take what the plateau provides—catching the echolocating flyers in nets woven from fungal fibers, ambushing grazers at water sources, harvesting the rust-bleeding moss for its iron content, following the Shuulorrim herds at a respectful distance and cleaning up what remains after the managed harvests. They are not parasites; the relationship is more complex than that. They perform services the Shuulorrim cannot or will not—clearing carcasses that might attract less desirable creatures, monitoring areas the settlements do not reach, carrying messages across distances where singing cannot travel.

When a Kreth family-group meets a Shuulorrim settlement, there are rituals. An exchange of sounds, a negotiation of territory and resource. The Shuulorrim might offer surplus tube-worms; the Kreth might offer information about conditions in distant regions, about the movements of the anomalies, about the behavior of the machines in places the Shuulorrim rarely go. It is not trade exactly—neither culture has a concept of currency. It is something older. A mutual acknowledgment. A reminder that they share this place and neither could survive without the other.

The Kreth do not worship the machines. They avoid them when possible, finding the vibrations disorienting, the vents too warm, the structures' proportions unsettling in ways they cannot articulate. But they respect them. And they have their own relationship with the plateau's strangeness—they know paths through the anomaly-zones that the Shuulorrim cannot navigate, have developed techniques for moving through folded space that they do not teach outsiders, carry in their bodies some adaptation or resistance that lets them tolerate what others cannot.

When you ask a Kreth about the presence—the sense of vast awareness that permeates this place—they will grow quiet. They will not answer directly. But if you travel with them long enough, you will notice that they pause, sometimes, in places that seem no different from any other. They stand still. Their sensory hairs flatten. They listen with their whole bodies.

Then they move on, and they do not explain.

## The Deep Swimmers

Below the plateau's surface, in flooded caverns that connect to the water-table, something else lives.

The Shuulorrim call them *Thaal-yuum*—the Ones Below, the Water Voices, the Dreamers. They are encountered rarely, always in the deepest pools, always unexpectedly. A Shuulorrim will be swimming in a familiar channel and suddenly feel a presence beside them—larger than any Shuulorrim, longer, moving with currents that should not exist in enclosed water. A voice will speak, not through sound but through vibration, through pressure changes that the Shuulorrim's body reads as words.

The conversations are difficult. The Thaal-yuum's language is not Shuulorrim language; it operates on different principles, encodes different assumptions. But communication happens, has always happened, and from these encounters the Shuulorrim have gathered fragments of understanding.

The Thaal-yuum are old. Older than the Shuulorrim, perhaps as old as the machines. They do not build—or if they build, they build in ways that cannot be detected, in places that cannot be reached. They seem to move through the water-table freely, appearing in pools that have no connection to other pools, as if they travel through routes that do not exist in ordinary space. They are not hostile, but they are not friendly either. They are *other*, in a way that goes beyond species, beyond culture, beyond anything the Shuulorrim have words for.

What the Thaal-yuum know, they do not freely share. But sometimes, in those strange encounters, they offer gifts. A piece of information about the machines—a frequency that opens a particular valve, a warning about a blockage forming in a distant pipe. A prediction about the weather, or the migrations, or the anomalies. Once, according to the oldest songs, a Thaal-yuum showed a Vorruush how to reach a chamber deep in the plateau's core, a chamber where the machines' pulse was so strong it seemed to have its own awareness, where a human voice could be heard speaking in a language no one recognized, where the walls were covered in markings that the Vorruush could feel but not read.

The Vorruush spent three days in that chamber. When they emerged, they had aged decades, and they never spoke of what they had found. They returned to their vent-side dwelling and began singing, and they did not stop singing until they died.

The Thaal-yuum are still there, somewhere below. The Shuulorrim feel them sometimes, in the water's movement, in the pressure changes that ripple up through the channels. They are listening. They are waiting. For what, no one knows.

## The Small Lives

Not everything that lives here is sapient. Not everything is large.

The plateau teems with creatures that think nothing, or think in ways too simple to call thought. The echolocators that fill the air, hunting smaller prey by sound alone—they are elegant, in their way, their flight patterns a kind of mathematics made physical, their colonies numbering in the millions, their waste feeding the fungal mats that feed the tube-worms that feed everyone else. The scuttlers that cascade across stone surfaces, crustacean-like, their shells adapted to channel vibration so precisely that they can feel a footstep from a hundred meters away and vanish into crevices before danger arrives. The pool-dwellers, eyeless fish and stranger things, some of them bioluminescent, some of them producing their own electrical fields, all of them adapted to pressures and chemistries that would kill surface creatures in seconds.

The Shuulorrim name these creatures, study them, incorporate them into their understanding of the plateau's systems. The Kreth hunt them, avoid them, fear some of them and respect others. The Thaal-yuum... it is not clear what the Thaal-yuum think of them, if the Thaal-yuum think at all in ways that can be compared to surface minds.

But all of them together—the sapient and the simple, the ancient and the recently evolved, the hunters and the hunted and the scavengers and the symbionts—form something larger than themselves. An ecology so dense, so interconnected, so thoroughly woven into the mechanical infrastructure of this place, that removing any piece would cascade into consequences no one could predict.

This is what lives here. Not a list of species but a *system*, a pattern of existence that has been elaborating itself for longer than memory, that continues to elaborate, that will still be elaborating long after any individual creature has died.

## What They Believe

The Shuulorrim have religion, of a kind.

They believe the plateau is conscious. Not metaphorically, not spiritually, but literally—that the machines and the water and the stone and the life together form a mind of some kind, a vast slow awareness that thinks in centuries, that feels in geological time, that has intentions and desires and perhaps even emotions, though nothing so quick or so simple as what the Shuulorrim themselves experience.

They call this mind *Thurrul*—a word that means approximately "the one who was here first," or "the one who is still here," or simply "the great self." They do not worship Thurrul exactly; worship implies separation, and they do not believe they are separate. They are part of it. Their thoughts are its thoughts. Their actions are its actions. When a Vorruush sings a machine back to life, they are not compelling an external force; they are Thurrul maintaining itself through them.

This belief shapes everything. It is why the Shuulorrim build so slowly, so carefully—they are not imposing their will on the landscape but collaborating with it, following its suggestions, allowing it to shape them as much as they shape it. It is why they do not fear death—when a Shuulorrim dies, they return to Thurrul, their body feeding the ecology, their voice echoing in the stone forever. It is why they treat the machines with such reverence—not because the machines are sacred, but because the machines are *organs*, as vital to the plateau's life as the Shuulorrim's own hearts.

The Kreth believe something different. They believe the plateau is *haunted*—that something vast once lived here and was killed, or imprisoned, or transformed, and that its remnants linger in the anomalies, in the machines' rhythm, in the strange geometries of the structures. They do not know what it was or what happened to it. But they feel its presence as you do, and they interpret it as a kind of wound, a scar on reality that has never fully healed.

The Thaal-yuum, if they believe anything, do not share it. But the Shuulorrim have noticed that the Thaal-yuum's movements sometimes correlate with the machines' cycles, that their appearances increase when the pulse intensifies, that they seem to be responding to something—or perhaps guiding something, or perhaps simply *being* something—that the surface dwellers cannot perceive.

Perhaps all three beliefs are true. Perhaps none of them are. The plateau does not answer questions. It only continues, breathing, weeping, thrumming in the dark.

## What They Make of You

When you arrive—if you arrive—you are a disruption.

The Shuulorrim will find you by the sound of your breathing, the rhythm of your heart, the clumsy way you move through space that you cannot see. They will approach carefully, singing to announce themselves, and when they reach you they will touch you—not aggressively, but thoroughly, mapping your body with their sensory hands, learning your shape, your temperature, your electrical signature. They will be confused by your eyes, those strange organs that seem to serve no purpose. They will be curious about your voice, so limited in its range. They will ask, through pressure and tone and context, why you have come.

If you cannot answer in ways they understand, they will take you to the Vorruush.

The Vorruush will listen longer. Will ask different questions. Will try sounds and frequencies, searching for resonance, for any hint that you might be more than you appear—that you might carry some piece of Thurrul they do not recognize, some adaptation or gift that explains your presence in this impossible place.

If you can answer—if you can somehow communicate that you mean no harm, that you are lost or curious or seeking something—they will help you. Not out of kindness exactly; kindness is not the right word. Out of *recognition*. You are alive. You are part of the system now, however temporarily. Thurrul does not turn away from its own parts.

The Kreth will be warier. They will follow you at a distance, watching from the edges of your awareness, waiting to see what you do. If you disrupt the hunt, they will drive you away. If you approach their families, they will scatter and regroup elsewhere. But if you prove harmless—if you move through their territory respectfully, if you take only what you need, if you do not linger in the anomaly-zones where they have business you do not understand—they will tolerate you. Eventually they may even offer trade. Information for whatever you carry that they might want.

The Thaal-yuum... you may never encounter the Thaal-yuum. Most surface travelers do not. But if you do—if you are swimming in a deep pool and feel that presence beside you, that pressure that is almost words—do not panic. They are not predators. They are not hostile. They are *curious*, in their alien way, and they have been curious about things like you before.

Listen. Try to understand. Let the water carry your response.

You may learn something. Or you may simply survive the encounter, and that will be enough.

## The Plateau, Populated

So this is what lives on the Breathing Plateau: a civilization that has grown into the machinery until neither can function without the other. A people who sing to maintain reality. Nomads who navigate impossible spaces. Dreamers in the deep water whose purposes are unknown. Millions of blind creatures filling every niche, building an ecology on heat and chemistry and sound. And beneath it all, around it all, *through* it all, a presence that may be mind or may be system or may be something language cannot capture.

They have been here since before memory. They will be here after you leave—if you leave. The plateau continues regardless, its wet breath rising, its stone weeping, its engines turning in the dark.

You are small here. You are temporary. But you are not unwelcome.

Listen. Let your other senses reach.

Perhaps, if you are patient, the darkness will tell you something. Perhaps Thurrul will notice you, in its slow vast way, and decide what to make of you.

The Shuulorrim will still be singing. The Kreth will still be moving. The water will still be carrying the Thaal-yuum's silence to places you cannot go.

This is what lives here.

This has always been what lives here.

The plateau breathes, and its children breathe with it, and the sound goes on forever in the dark.
