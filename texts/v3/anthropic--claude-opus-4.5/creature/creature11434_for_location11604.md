## The Keepers of the Weeping Galleries

They have no name for themselves. Names require someone to speak them to, and they have only each other, and they have always known each other, and so names are unnecessary—like naming your own left hand.

There are seventeen of them. There have always been seventeen of them, for as long as the word "always" has meant anything in this basin. When one of them stops, another begins. This is not reproduction, not exactly. It is more like the way a single thought can end and another thought can start, and both belong to the same mind.

They are not the pale creatures in the pools—those are something else, something older or younger or simply different, something that shares the basin but does not share in the keeping. The keepers watch the pale creatures sometimes, the way you might watch clouds or water, with attention but without expectation.

---

**What they look like:**

You would not see them at first. This is not because they hide, but because they have learned to be still in ways that make them part of the stone's grammar. They rest in the alcoves, in the structures they did not build but have maintained, their bodies folded into the hollows like water pooling in a depression—finding the shape that fits.

They are roughly humanoid only in the sense that they have learned to be. Their bodies are assembled, aggregated, composed of the calcium-ite deposits that form in the places where the mineral-rich water evaporates slowly. Their outer surfaces are pale and faintly translucent, layered like the limestone itself—each layer a different year, a different decade, a different century of patient accumulation. Where the water runs over them constantly, they are smooth, opalescent, catching light in thin films. Where they have remained dry, they are rough, matte, beginning the slow process of becoming stone again.

They have what might be called faces, but these are more like focal points—places where the layering has created slight depressions, slight asymmetries, that suggest attention. Two shallow hollows where water tends to collect. A ridge where minerals have built up more thickly. The suggestion of features rather than features themselves. When they look at something, you know it not because their faces turn but because something in the air changes—a quality of presence that shifts direction.

Their limbs are long and thin, articulated in more places than seems necessary, each joint a point where separate accretions have grown together and found a way to move against each other. The movement is slow, deliberate, and makes a faint grinding sound that is lost entirely in the roar of the basin. When they walk—if walking is the word—they do so by a kind of flowing redistribution of weight, each step taking long minutes, each journey across the basin floor taking days.

They do not need to move quickly. Nothing here does.

---

**What they do:**

They keep.

This is not maintenance exactly, not preservation, not curation. It is something more fundamental—a practice of attention so sustained that it becomes a form of care.

Each of the seventeen has a territory within the basin, a section of the honeycomb walls that belongs to their watching. They know every alcove in their territory, every structure, every channel where the water runs, every place where the stone is weakening and every place where new deposits are building. They know how fast each section is dissolving. They know which structures will fall in the next century, the next millennium. They know where the pale creatures have made their slow circuits, decade after decade, wearing faint paths in the stone that only a keeper would notice.

When a structure begins to fail—when the water has finally worn through some critical support, when the weight of accumulated centuries becomes too much—the keepers do not prevent it. They attend it. They position themselves nearby, sometimes for years, watching the slow-motion collapse that to them feels like urgency. They witness the stones falling, the alcove emptying, the rubble joining the basin floor. And then they begin the long work of carrying the fallen stones to new locations, building nothing, but arranging—placing them in the pools, on the ledges, in the hollows where they will be found by future keepers, or by the water, or by no one at all.

The objects on the submerged floor—the shaped stones that suggest intention without revealing purpose—these are keeper-work. Not tools, not toys, not components. Arrangements. Compositions. Each one the product of decades of slow shaping, of holding a stone in the mineral flow until it accretes into a new form, of wearing it down in precise ways, of creating something that means nothing to anyone but the keeper who made it and perhaps not even to them.

When a keeper finishes an object, they carry it to the central pools and release it. It falls slowly through the clear water. It joins the others. This is not offering, not disposal, not collection. It is simply what keepers do, have always done, will always do until the basin is gone.

---

**What they remember:**

The keepers do not remember who built the structures. This is not because they have forgotten—it is because they were not yet keepers when the builders were here. They were something else then, something earlier, something that predated the keeping. They remember *being* that something else the way you might remember being a child—with certainty that it happened and uncertainty about what it felt like.

What they remember is this: there was a time when the basin was not weeping. There was a time when the stone was dry, when the structures stood sharp-edged and new, when something moved through the galleries with purpose rather than patience. They remember the first water, the first seepage, the first trickle that became a stream that became the roar. They remember the builders leaving, or dissolving, or becoming something else—the memory is unclear, worn down by the same patient erosion that shapes everything here.

They remember deciding to stay. Or they remember being unable to leave. Or they remember that there was no difference between these things.

They remember each object they have made, each stone they have arranged, each collapse they have witnessed. This memory is precise, architectural, filed away in the layers of their own accumulated bodies. When a keeper stops—when the accretion slows and halts and they become fully stone—the new keeper that begins elsewhere in the basin does not inherit these memories, but they inherit the *shape* of having memories, the practice of accumulating attention, the grammar of keeping without knowing exactly what they are keeping or for whom.

---

**What they hear:**

The roar of the basin is not noise to them. It is landscape. It is weather. It is the constant condition of existence, so pervasive that it has become a kind of silence—the silence of absolute presence, of sound so total that it cancels itself out.

Within this silence, they hear other things.

They hear the stone shifting, the slow groans and clicks of limestone under pressure, the tiny fractures that will become large fractures in a thousand years. They hear the different qualities of water—the thin threads that sing a higher note than the heavy sheets, the pools that hum with a barely-audible vibration, the channels that gurgle in patterns the keepers have learned to read like text.

They hear the pale creatures moving in their pools, the soft displacement of water that accompanies their drifting, the occasional sound—too faint for anything else to catch—of one pale creature touching another.

And they hear, very rarely, things that do not belong to the basin. A bird passing overhead, its cry swallowed instantly by the roar but registered nonetheless. A stone falling from the rim above, dislodged by some animal or some wind or some tremor too small for anyone else to notice. The footsteps of visitors—there have been visitors, occasionally, over the long years—picking their way across the slick basin floor, breathing air that the keepers do not need to breathe, speaking words that the keepers cannot understand but can feel as vibrations, as disturbances in the constant sound.

They do not approach visitors. They do not avoid them. They simply continue keeping, and if a visitor happens to see them—folded into an alcove, moving with glacial slowness across a ledge, holding a half-formed object in the mineral flow—the keeper does not acknowledge them, does not react, does not alter their behavior in any way. The visitor is temporary. The keeping is not.

---

**What they want:**

This is the wrong question, but it is also the only question, so:

They want the basin to continue. Not because they have plans for it, not because they believe it serves some purpose, not because they fear what would happen if it stopped. They want the basin to continue the way the water wants to fall—because that is what basins do, what water does, what keepers do. The wanting is indistinguishable from the being.

But there is something else, something smaller and stranger, something that might be called longing if the keepers had a word for it:

They want to understand the objects they make.

Each keeper spends decades shaping stone, holding it in the flow, wearing it down and building it up, creating forms that emerge without being planned. And when the object is finished—when it reaches some state that the keeper recognizes as complete, though they could not explain the recognition—they carry it to the pools and release it and watch it fall and join the others. And then they begin again.

They do not know why they do this. They do not know what the objects mean, or whether they mean anything, or whether meaning is the right framework. They only know that the making feels necessary, that the releasing feels correct, that the accumulation on the submerged floor feels like something being said in a language they are still learning.

Perhaps, in another few million years, they will understand. Perhaps the objects will finally assemble into a message, a pattern, a map of something that the keepers have been documenting without knowing it. Perhaps the pale creatures in the pools will eventually incorporate the objects into their own slow circuits, and the keepers will watch, and something will become clear.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the objects will simply accumulate until the basin drains or fills or collapses, and they will be buried under sediment, and millions of years later something will find them and wonder what they meant, and the wondering will be the meaning, and the keepers will have been speaking to that future wondering all along.

This is what passes for hope in the Weeping Galleries. Not expectation, not belief, not even desire exactly. Just the continued practice of making and releasing and watching, century after century, while the water falls and the stone dissolves and the roar speaks the basin into existence, one drop at a time.

---

**How they end:**

A keeper ends when they stop accreting. This happens slowly, over centuries—the mineral flow that builds their bodies gradually redirecting, finding new channels, leaving the keeper in a state of equilibrium that tips, almost imperceptibly, toward loss. They do not notice at first. The keeping continues. The objects are made and released. The collapses are witnessed. But the layers stop building, and then they start thinning, and then the keeper begins to become more stone than keeper.

When this happens, the keeper moves—if they can still move—to one of the structures in the walls. Not to shelter from the process, but to participate in it. They fold themselves into an alcove, into a hollow, into one of the spaces that they have watched for so long. They become still in a way that is different from their ordinary stillness. They become *part* of the watching rather than the one who watches.

The water continues to fall over them. The stone continues to form and dissolve around them. In a thousand years, ten thousand years, they will be indistinguishable from the structures they have been keeping—just another alcove, another hollow, another place where the basin weeps itself into existence.

And somewhere else in the galleries, in an alcove that has been dry for centuries, in a hollow where the mineral flow has recently shifted, something begins to accrete. Something that will become aware, will become attentive, will become a keeper. Something that will not remember the keeper who ended but will inherit the practice, the patience, the long habit of keeping without knowing what or why.

Seventeen. Always seventeen. For as long as the basin stands, for as long as the water falls, for as long as there is something here worth attending to—and there is always something here worth attending to, because attention itself is what makes worth, and the keepers have nothing but attention, endless and patient and loud in its quiet, filling the basin like the roar that never stops.

---

*In the central pools, the objects accumulate. In the upper galleries, the structures slowly fail. In the alcoves and hollows and honeycombs of the weeping walls, seventeen presences keep their watch, making and releasing, witnessing and arranging, speaking in a language of shaped stone that they do not yet understand but trust, with a faith older than the basin itself, that someone or something eventually will.*
