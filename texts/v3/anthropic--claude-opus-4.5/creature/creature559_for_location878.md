# The Inhabitants of Veth-Morai

## The Seep-Drinkers

The creatures you saw at the pool are called *veleth* by those few surface-dwellers who have names for such things, though the veleth themselves communicate in pressure-waves felt through the wet air and the subtle chemistry of scent-marks left on stone. They are old here—not ancient, but established, three or four hundred generations adapted to this specific darkness.

Their bodies tell the story of their ancestry: once they were something like badgers, burrowers who followed root-systems down and simply kept going. Their forelimbs retain the digging-power of that heritage, though they rarely dig now—the cellar gardens provide. The extra joints in their legs came later, an accommodation to navigating the uneven terrain of root-mass and rubble, allowing them to fold themselves through gaps that seem too narrow, to brace against vertical surfaces, to move with that low, flowing gait you witnessed.

The veleth are social but not communal. They travel in small groups—two to five, rarely more—bound by kinship and mutual benefit rather than hierarchy. The three you saw were likely siblings from a recent litter, still young enough to drink together without competition. As they age, they will drift apart, their ranges overlapping but their encounters growing rarer, marked by careful negotiation rather than easy proximity.

They eat the pale fungi primarily, supplemented by smaller animals when opportunity presents—they are not active hunters, but they are thorough opportunists. Their jaws, which seemed so prominent in the darkness, are built for crushing: fungal stems, beetle-shells, the occasional bone. When they find carrion, they share it, taking turns in an order determined by subtle signals you would need weeks to learn to read.

The sensory spots stippling their skin are exactly what you suspected: organs for detecting vibration, temperature gradient, the electrical fields generated by living muscle. In total darkness, they perceive a world richer than sight could provide—they feel your heartbeat from across a chamber, taste your fear on the moisture in the air, know the shape of you by the way you displace the micro-currents of condensation. They knew you were frightened. They simply did not care enough to act on it.

## The Ceiling-Hunters

What hung above the veleth is called nothing at all, because no one who has seen it clearly has returned with the inclination to name things.

It is not a snake, though snake is the closest approximation a surface mind can reach. Its body is serpentine but segmented, each segment bearing its own set of gripping limbs—not legs exactly, more like the tube-feet of a starfish, soft and flexible and tipped with structures that can grip root-fiber, stone, or flesh with equal facility. It moves by flowing, each segment releasing and re-gripping in waves that carry it across ceilings, down walls, through the narrowest channels of the root-maze.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in Veth-Morai. Perhaps fewer. They are difficult to count because they are difficult to perceive: their skin produces a film that masks their scent, their movement is nearly silent, and they have learned patience across generations of hunting prey adapted to darkness. They can wait for hours—days, if necessary—coiled in positions that seem impossible, their awareness distributed across their entire length, every segment tasting the air.

They do not hunt the veleth often. The veleth are large enough to fight back, and the ceiling-hunters prefer certainty to struggle. But they follow them, because the veleth find water and food and sometimes flush smaller prey in their foraging. A ceiling-hunter trailing a group of veleth is not so much predator as parasite, feeding on the edges of their activity, taking what they disturb.

When they do kill, they drop. The coiled posture you saw is a striking position—they release from the ceiling, spread the limbs of their forward segments into a net of gripping flesh, and envelop their prey before it can react. Death comes from constriction and suffocation, though they also possess venom, a slow paralytic secreted from glands along their length. Against smaller prey, the venom is primary; against larger, it serves only to quiet the struggle.

They are intelligent in the way that solitary hunters often are: patient, observant, capable of learning from failure. They recognized you as something new and waited to see what you would do. When you did nothing, they lost interest—or rather, they filed you away, a variable to be considered in future calculations. If you return, they will remember.

## The Weasel-Kin

The creature that followed you has a name, though not one it would recognize: the people who once lived in the cellar gardens called its kind *thess*, a word that carried connotations of curiosity, nuisance, and reluctant affection. The thess are ubiquitous in Veth-Morai—there are hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, a constant presence in every chamber and passage.

They evolved from something like a marten, though that ancestry is now distant enough to be almost academic. Their bodies have lengthened and thinned, adapted for threading through the interstices of the root-maze, for squeezing into spaces that larger animals cannot reach. Their pale fuzz is not true fur but something finer, a sensory coat that registers air movement and temperature with exquisite precision. Their eyes have not disappeared—some light must filter down through the root-mass, enough for eyes to remain useful—but they have diminished, small dark beads set deep in narrow skulls.

The thess are curious because curiosity serves them. They are too small to hunt most of what lives in Veth-Morai, and too large to survive on fungus alone. They occupy an ecological niche that requires intelligence: they scavenge, they steal, they follow larger predators to their kills and dart in when attention wavers. They have learned that novel things are often opportunities—a new creature might drop food, might disturb prey, might die and become food itself.

The thess that followed you was young and bold, traits that correlate in its kind. Older thess are more cautious, having learned which risks are not worth taking. But the young must learn somehow, and you were interesting: warm, slow, smelling of the surface, clearly lost. It was waiting to see if you would feed it, flee from it, or become incapacitated in some way it could exploit.

They are not pack animals, but they communicate. The high chirps you heard are echolocation—crude compared to bats, but sufficient for navigating the closer passages. Other sounds, at frequencies you may not have registered, serve social purposes: warnings, claims, invitations. A thess that finds food will often call, bringing others to share; the calculation is complex, involving kinship, past reciprocity, and the simple reality that more thess means more eyes watching for danger. They have a culture of sorts, traditions of sharing and obligation that persist across generations.

The one that followed you has already told others about you. Your scent, your warmth, your movements—these have been communicated through the network of chirps and chemical signals that the thess use to maintain their collective awareness. If you return, you will be recognized. Not by the individual that followed you, perhaps, but by the species. They will know: the slow warm thing came back.

## The Congregation

What you witnessed in the vault-chamber was not a ritual, though it resembled one. It was not a parliament, though it had the shape of collective decision. It was simply what happens when the animals of Veth-Morai encounter each other at certain times, in certain places, under certain conditions that you cannot yet perceive.

The water-drip points are neutral ground. This is not law but emergent behavior, a pattern that has stabilized over hundreds of generations: at certain junctions, where water flows predictably, aggression becomes costly. A predator that attacks at a water-point may succeed once, but will find itself avoided thereafter, its hunting grounds shrinking as prey learns to circumvent it. The mathematics favor restraint. And so the creatures gather, warily at first, then with increasing ease as the truce proves reliable.

The grooming you saw was genuine social behavior, not performance. Many of the animals in Veth-Morai carry parasites—small things that burrow into pale skin or nest in sensory fuzz—and mutual grooming provides relief that solitary scratching cannot match. Species that would otherwise flee each other tolerate proximity for the sake of reaching the unreachable itch. Veleth groom thess; thess groom creatures with no surface-name, things like eyeless salamanders the size of your forearm, their skin perpetually damp and vulnerable to infestation.

The sleeping piles are opportunistic thermoregulation. The cellar gardens are cold, and warmth is precious. Bodies pressed together conserve heat that isolated bodies would lose. Here too, the mathematics favor cooperation: a veleth sleeping beside a creature it might otherwise prey upon gains more from the shared warmth than it would from a meal. The prey-creature, for its part, gains protection—the veleth's presence discourages smaller predators who might otherwise attack.

And the ones who sat alert, watching nothing? They were watching everything. The congregation is safe but not secure; the truces hold but can be broken. Some animals—thess especially, but others too—take on a sentinel role, their attention turned outward while others rest. Whether this is altruism or merely vigilance for their own sake is not a question that can be answered. Both, perhaps. Or neither. The darkness does not distinguish.

The signal that dissolved the gathering was a scent, released by one of the sentinels: a chemical that means *time to go*, *something is wrong*, *enough*. You were the trigger—your presence, your strangeness, your lingering attention. You had been tolerated, but you had not been welcomed. When you stayed too long, the sentinel decided the congregation was over.

They will gather again. Tomorrow, or the next day, or whenever the conditions align. They will not remember you specifically, but they will remember the category you represent: the large slow warm thing that watches too long. Next time, they may be more wary. Or less. The darkness keeps no records of individual encounters, only tendencies.

## The Former People

The humans who lived in Veth-Morai are gone, but gone is not the same as vanished. Their traces remain—the alcoves, the tools, the dissolving clay—and in those traces, a story can be read.

They came down generations ago, though how many generations is impossible to say. They were fleeing something: war, plague, persecution, some catastrophe of the surface that made the cold wet dark seem preferable. They brought tools and knowledge, the skills of flint-knapping and cordage and the shaping of clay. They did not bring fire—fire would have been impossible here, where everything is damp and the air itself seems to resist combustion. They learned to live without it.

They ate what the cellar gardens provided: fungi, the pale plants, the smaller animals they could catch. They drank the seep-water, rich with minerals that surface water lacks. They carved their alcoves with patience, lining them with moss that stayed dry only as long as someone slept in it, warming and compressing the fibers. Their tools were beautiful because beauty costs nothing—the obsidian came from somewhere, traded or carried from the surface, and working it with skill was no harder than working it clumsily.

They lived alongside the animals. Not in harmony, exactly—the veleth would have stolen their food, the ceiling-hunters would have taken their children if they could—but in accommodation, the same accommodation all the animals of Veth-Morai have reached. They learned the truce-points. They learned which sounds meant danger and which meant opportunity. They learned to move in the darkness without flinching, because flinching drew attention.

Their language, if they had one distinct from whatever they spoke on the surface, has left no record. Their beliefs, their songs, their stories of how they came to be here—all gone, dissolved like the unfired clay. They did not carve messages into the stone. Perhaps they knew that stone itself is temporary here, worn by water, colonized by roots, returning eventually to rubble.

What happened to them? There are several possibilities, none confirmable. They may have died out—starvation, disease, a failure to reproduce in the cold and dark. They may have returned to the surface when whatever drove them down had passed. They may have gone deeper, following the roots and the water into places that no surface-dweller has seen.

Or they may still be here.

Not in the chambers you walked—those are clearly unoccupied, the alcoves abandoned, the tools untouched for years. But Veth-Morai is larger than you explored. The passages branch and rebranch; the vaults open onto further vaults; the root-maze extends in directions you did not travel. Somewhere in that darkness, in chambers you never found, there may be alcoves still warm with recent occupation. Tools with fresh edges. Clay that has not yet begun to dissolve.

You would not see them if they did not wish to be seen. They would have had generations to learn the darkness, to become part of it. They would know you were there long before you knew they were. They would watch from distances you could not perceive, following at your edges, waiting to see what you would do.

Perhaps the congregation you witnessed included them. Perhaps one of the shapes in the sleeping pile was human-shaped, its skin gone pale, its eyes adapted or closed. Perhaps the sentinel that released the scent had hands, and hair, and a face that would have been recognizable in sunlight.

You would never know. The darkness keeps its secrets.

And something lives in the darkness of Veth-Morai. Many things. They are there now, as you read this, in the cold and the wet and the close pressing dark. The veleth drink at the seep-pools. The ceiling-hunters wait. The thess move through the root-maze, chirping, watching, remembering. The congregations form and dissolve and form again.

And in the alcoves, in the deeper places, in the spaces you did not reach—something breathes. Something that once knew sunlight. Something that has learned to live without it.

If you return, you may find them. Or they may find you. The cellar gardens are patient, and the darkness does not hurry.

It only waits.
