**The Settlers**

She arrived when the mortar was still warm enough to feel against her palm, and she hasn't stopped testing it since. Every morning she presses her fingers to the courtyard wall near the base, where the shadows pool longest, and every morning she notes the infinitesimal change—today harder than yesterday, more certain. She keeps these observations in a book that is itself unsettled: pages added out of order, margins colonized by diagrams that grew beyond their original purpose, the binding held together with cord she keeps meaning to replace.

She is perhaps forty, perhaps older—the kind of age that becomes difficult to read in a face that has spent years attending to things other than mirrors. Her hair she keeps wrapped in cloth the color of undyed linen, practical, forgettable, though strands escape and she doesn't push them back. Her hands are her most distinctive feature: stained at the fingertips with mineral residue she no longer bothers washing away entirely, the gray-white of calcium having become as much a part of her as her own skin's color. She came here to study the setting process, ostensibly. To document how long different mortar mixtures take to cure in this climate, this humidity, this particular quality of shade. But the documentation has become something else. She has begun to notice things that don't fit in the margins of materials science.

She notices, for instance, the boy.

He cannot be more than eleven, and he belongs to no one she has been able to identify. He moves through the settling district like water through the incomplete drainage channels—finding every gap, every unconsidered space, appearing in courtyards that seemed empty and disappearing down alleys that don't yet lead anywhere. He doesn't speak, or hasn't spoken to her, but she has heard him humming to himself in a minor key that has no particular melody. He collects things. She has seen him pocketing fragments of cured mortar that have chipped away, seedpods from the opportunistic plants, the shed skin of a gecko so perfect and translucent it looked like a ghost of itself. What he does with these collections she doesn't know. Where he sleeps she cannot determine. He seems to live in the interstices, in the same gaps where the lamb's ear and clover have established themselves, occupying spaces the builders haven't yet claimed.

She worries about him in a way that feels inappropriate given that she doesn't know his name. She has started leaving small offerings where she knows he passes—a heel of bread wrapped in oiled paper, a clay cup she found cracked but still functional, a stub of pencil worn down to nearly nothing. These disappear, but she never sees him take them. It could be anyone. It could be the settlement's other inhabitants, the ones even more liminal than herself.

There is the old man who tends the cistern, for instance, though *tends* may not be the right word. He sits beside it for hours, adjusting the dampened cloth that covers the clay lining, sometimes singing to it in a language she doesn't recognize. He told her once, in heavily accented speech she had to lean close to understand, that clay has memory. That if you let it dry too fast it remembers the violence of that drying forever, becomes brittle in its bones. He treats the cistern like a child with a fever, patient, attentive, unwilling to rush the healing. She has never seen him anywhere else in the district. She doesn't know where he eats or sleeps or whether he has any other purpose. He may have built the cistern himself; he may have simply claimed it as his responsibility after its builders moved on to other projects. The distinction may not matter here.

And there are the builders, though calling them that implies more cohesion than they possess. They come and go according to logics she hasn't decoded. Some days the district swarms with activity—stone being cut, timber being fitted, arguments about property lines conducted at volumes that send the drab brown birds scattering. Other days nothing. The construction sits suspended, breathing, settling at its own pace. The builders seem to have no foreman, no central plan, no schedule. They are more like the vegetation than like workers: they appear where conditions favor them, establish themselves, do what they came to do, move on. She has tried to interview them about the district's intended shape, its future form, and they look at her as though the question doesn't quite parse. One woman, her arms strong from laying stone, told her: *We build what wants to be built. When it stops wanting, we stop.* Then she laughed, as though this were a joke, though the laugh had an uneasy edge.

The woman with the strong arms is here more often than the others. She has claimed a half-roofed storage space three courtyards over as her own, hung a hammock from the beams, arranged her tools with the care of someone who has learned that organization is a form of prayer. She and the scholar—for that is what the woman with the mineral-stained fingers has started thinking of herself as, though the term fits poorly—have developed a cautious friendship built on shared silence. They sit together some evenings in the borrowed light, passing a bottle of something astringent the builder makes from fermented fruit, watching the gecko hunt, listening to the walls settle. They don't talk about the future. The future here is a contested concept, a string stretched between stakes, an argument waiting to happen.

The builder has a theory about the boy. She thinks he was here before the building started, that he belongs to some earlier settlement that failed or was abandoned or simply dissolved back into the earth the way so many things do. She thinks he stays because he's waiting for something, or because he doesn't know how to leave, or because leaving would require him to become a different kind of creature than he has learned to be. The scholar isn't sure this is right, but she doesn't have a better theory. She has started dreaming about him, dreams in which he opens his hands to show her his collections and they contain things that shouldn't exist together—a baby tooth and a bone button and a word written on air.

There are others. There are always others.

The family who has occupied the stairway-that-ends-in-scaffolding, who have made it into a vertical home: the father climbs the unfinished steps each night carrying his smallest child, the mother has strung lines for drying laundry between the scaffolding poles, the older children have claimed the landing where the stairs give out as their private kingdom and defend it with the ferocity of young things establishing territory. They are waiting for the stairs to be finished, for the upper floor to be built, for the home they were promised by whoever promised it. In the meantime they live in the becoming, adapting to its angles, sleeping on inclines, growing accustomed to the particular way sound echoes in a space that is more vertical than horizontal.

The woman who tends the paper wasps. She is very old, older than the cistern-keeper, so old that her age has become a kind of settled fact rather than a number. She visits the nests each day—there are several now, the wasps having spread through the district's gaps with their hex-comb architecture—and she speaks to them in a clicking language that might be her own invention or might be something the wasps themselves have taught her. She tells anyone who will listen that the wasps are building the true city, the one that will outlast all the human efforts, and when the scholar asks what she means she just smiles with a mouth missing most of its teeth and says: *They know what shape wants to exist. We're just guessing.*

The beetles are no one's responsibility and everyone's fascination. They appeared early in the settling process, their rust-colored shells catching the borrowed light as they pick their way across floors and up walls and through the gaps where mortar hasn't cured. The builder claims they're eating something in the clay, some mineral or organism that doesn't belong, that they're cleaning the district the way certain fish clean reefs. The cistern-keeper claims they're scouts for something larger, something that will come later, something the settlement is being prepared for without anyone's knowledge or consent. The scholar has started cataloging them—their sizes, their movements, their apparent preferences—but the catalog keeps exceeding its bounds, spilling into observations about the boy, about the wasps, about the way the walls seem to respond to the beetles' passage. Everything here connects to everything else. That's the problem with studying a place that hasn't finished becoming itself: you can't isolate variables when all the variables are still in negotiation.

At night the district transforms. The borrowed light becomes starlight, moonlight, the faint glow of oil lamps in windows not yet shuttered. The scholar lies on her bedroll in the courtyard she has claimed and listens to the sounds of settling—the clicks, the pops, the thermal contractions, the water finding its way through unfinished channels. She hears the boy humming somewhere in the dark, his minor key threading through the mineral silence. She hears the builder snoring three courtyards over, a sound that should be annoying but has become comforting. She hears the family on the stairs murmuring to each other, the particular cadence of people who have learned to live in each other's constant presence without losing themselves entirely.

She thinks about permanence. About what it would mean for this place to finish becoming itself. About whether the finishing would be a kind of death—the end of possibility, the foreclosure of all the shapes it might have taken in favor of the single shape it did. She has read about ancient cities that calcified into monuments of themselves, that became so certain of their form they could no longer adapt, could no longer accommodate the opportunistic growth of lamb's ear and clover, could no longer offer gaps for geckos and wasps and boys who belong to no one. She doesn't want that for this place. She doesn't know if what she wants matters.

The toad in the damp corner has been there for weeks now. She has started thinking of it as a neighbor, as a fellow resident of this particular pocket of becoming. It watches her with its patient eyes and she watches it back. They are both waiting to see what this place decides to be. They are both, in their own ways, participating in the decision.

When she sleeps, finally, in the warmth that radiates from walls still learning to be walls, she dreams of mortar that never quite sets, of a district that stays forever in this state of becoming, of herself grown old and mineral-stained and still pressing her fingers to the courtyard wall each morning, still finding it softer than stone should be, still feeling the slow metabolic work of something that has chosen not to finish. In the dream this feels like grace. In the dream the boy has finally shown her his collections and they contain everything, everything, the whole taxonomy of in-between, and he is smiling for the first time, and the wasps are singing in their clicking language, and the walls are breathing, and she understands at last that permanence was never the point.

She wakes to gray light and the sound of water finding its way and the gecko hunting and the boy's humming somewhere close, closer than usual, almost close enough to touch.

She doesn't move. She barely breathes. She lets the moment settle, lets it become whatever it is going to become, lets herself be part of a place that is still deciding.
