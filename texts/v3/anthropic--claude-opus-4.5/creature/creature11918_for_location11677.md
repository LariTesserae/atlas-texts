## The People Who Became Permeable

**Grandmother Vessel** tends the processing station, though "tends" suggests more agency than the relationship actually holds. She is seventy-three years old, and her skin has not been purely her own since she was forty. The symbiotic colonies that mark her as kin-not-food have, over decades, migrated inward—she feels them in her joints now, a secondary warmth when the pit's false-winter comes, a faint itching behind her eyes when the Mother Substrate's bioluminescence shifts spectrum. She no longer knows if her dreams are hers. She no longer considers this a meaningful question.

Her work is maintenance, which here means negotiation. The compression-combustion engine requires fuel at specific intervals, but the Mother Substrate fruits according to her own rhythms, not human schedules. Grandmother Vessel has learned to read the signs—subtle swelling in certain quadrants of the floor, a particular pitch in the Grandfathers' grazing-sounds—and to adjust the engine's demands accordingly. Some days it runs hot and constant. Some days it barely whispers. The canopy above responds to both, though whether this constitutes preference or simply reaction, she cannot say.

She speaks aloud to the engine, to the Mother, to the beetles who sometimes pause on the processing station's walls to observe her work. Her voice has worn into a register that carries undertones—click-harmonics she no longer notices producing, a subsonic thrum that the Grandfathers seem to find soothing. The other pit-dwellers understand her perfectly. Visitors from the surface find her difficult to parse, as though she were speaking through water.

**The Cord-Keeper** has no other name anyone remembers, including himself. He maintains the root-fiber communication network, which means he spends his days inside the walls—climbing through hollow channels where the growth is thin enough to admit a human body, splicing damaged lines with secretions he cultivates in glands that were not part of his original anatomy. The symbiotes gave him these, or perhaps he grew them in response to need. The distinction matters less each year.

He is thin in a way that suggests compression rather than deprivation, his limbs longer than they should be, his joints bending at angles that make surface-visitors uncomfortable. The walls have shaped him, or he has shaped himself to the walls. He can hear the network's full range now—every message that travels the tensioned fibers registers in his bones, in his teeth, in the spaces behind his ears. Privacy is not a concept that survives long in the pit. The Cord-Keeper knows who is sick, who is arguing, who has fallen in love, who is planning to attempt the surface-climb. He does not share this knowledge. He simply *carries* it, a living archive of the community's constant murmur.

When he sleeps—rarely, briefly, wedged into wall-hollows where the beetles have agreed not to garden—he dreams in vibrations. His body twitches in patterns that, if translated, would be messages: status reports, requests for assistance, expressions of contentment or unease. The network runs through him even when he is not consciously tending it. He has become, in some functional sense, a node.

---

**The children** are the most unsettling to outsiders, because children should not move like that.

There are eleven of them currently, ages ranging from perhaps three to perhaps fourteen—aging is imprecise here, where growth follows the pit's rhythms rather than solar cycles. They have names, though they use them rarely, preferring a system of sound-signals that identifies individuals by location, activity, and emotional state rather than fixed identity. The surface-visitors who occasionally descend find this troubling. The pit-dwellers find the surface convention of static names equally strange.

The children's skin is translucent in patches, showing the movement of symbiotic colonies beneath like shadows in fog. They can see further into the infrared than their parents, a gift from generations of dim-light adaptation, and they navigate the pit's geography with a confidence that borders on proprioception—they *know* where the Grandfathers are grazing without looking, *know* when the ceiling-fauna are about to begin a feeding frenzy, *know* which patches of Mother Substrate are about to fruit. Whether this is learned behavior, inherited instinct, or something the symbiotes communicate directly into their nervous systems, no one has determined. No one has tried particularly hard to determine it. The knowledge is useful. Its origin seems beside the point.

They play games that surface-children would not recognize as games: competitions to remain motionless long enough that the small creeping things mistake them for furniture, elaborate chases that traverse all three dimensions of the pit's space, call-and-response singing with the ceiling-fauna that sometimes produces novel harmonics neither species could generate alone. They are not feral—they learn the maintenance of machines, the politics of beetle-offering, the reading of Mother Substrate's moods—but they are not tame either. They are suited, in ways their great-grandparents were not, in ways they themselves do not notice because it is the only way of being they have ever known.

---

**Machinist Oren** came from the surface eighteen years ago, following a lover who did not survive the adaptation. He is the most human-looking of the pit-dwellers, which is not a compliment here—it marks him as still-becoming, not-yet-integrated, a process that may never complete. The symbiotes have not fully taken in him. His skin remains opaque. His joints bend in ordinary directions. He cannot hear the Grandfathers' subsonic communication, must rely on visual signs and the warnings of others to navigate their grazing patterns.

He compensates with technical knowledge the pit-born lack. He understands the theory behind the engines, can diagnose problems the others only intuit, maintains schematics in a notation system the Mother Substrate cannot digest. His quarters are the most architecturally distinct—more metal than growth, sealed against moisture in ways the other dwellings are not, filled with tools that would rust if exposed to the pit's constant exhalation. The other pit-dwellers visit him when machines fail in ways that intuition cannot parse. He visits them when loneliness becomes structural.

He still dreams in surface-colors: the blue of open sky, the hard yellow of sunlight unfiltered by forty meters of canopy. He wakes from these dreams with a grief he cannot explain to people who have never seen such things, and a relief he cannot explain either—relief at returning to the green-gold dim, to the warmth, to the sound that never stops. He is becoming something, even if the symbiotes reject him. The pit works on everyone, given time.

---

**The Listeners** are not precisely a profession, but the pit has produced three of them in living memory, and the community has learned to recognize the type. They are born with ordinary hearing and ordinary cognition, but something in their development goes sideways—the symbiotes colonize their auditory processing in ways that do not kill them but do not leave them unchanged. They hear *everything*. The Grandfathers' deepest communications. The Mother Substrate's chemical speech. The negotiations between beetle-nations that occur at frequencies human architecture was never meant to detect.

The current Listener is a person of perhaps thirty years—gender here is a surface concern, though visitors sometimes impose categories—who sits most days in a structure near the pit's center, eyes closed, body swaying slightly to rhythms only they perceive. They do not translate what they hear; the grammar is too foreign, the concepts without human analogue. But they can detect dissonance. They know when the ecosystem's symphony is building toward crisis, when the ceiling-fauna are preparing to swarm, when the Mother Substrate is experiencing something that might be illness or might be reproduction. They signal warnings in ways the community has learned to heed, a raised hand for minor concern, a specific whistle for immediate danger, a full-body shudder for events that require evacuation.

They do not speak often. When they do, their voice carries all the frequencies at once—human range layered with beetle-click and Grandfather-thrum and something that might be the Mother Substrate's own slow cognition. Other pit-dwellers find conversations with them disorienting but necessary. The Listener anchors the community in the pit's larger life, reminds them that their arrangements with the fauna are contingent, that the tolerance they enjoy is not guaranteed, that the ecosystem is not background but *context*, the medium in which human survival is a temporary and negotiated phenomenon.

---

**The newest arrival** has not yet been given a name the community uses. She descended the spiral ramp six weeks ago, carrying trade-goods from a surface settlement that believes the pit-dwellers are valuable trading partners, or possibly heretics, or possibly both. She intended to stay three days. She is still here.

The pit is working on her. She can feel it—the moisture settling into her lungs, the warmth entering her bones, the symbiotic colonies that have begun to investigate the terrain of her skin. She should leave. The surface settlement expects her return. But each morning she wakes in the guest-quarters—a hybrid structure of corrugated iron and trained vine, uncomfortably warm, alive with small sounds she is learning to parse—and each morning the spiral ramp seems steeper, the light above harsher, the prospect of return more abstract.

She watches the children move through spaces she cannot navigate. She watches Grandmother Vessel negotiate with machinery and organism alike, speaking a language that is becoming less foreign each day. She watches the Grandfathers graze, patient and ancient, and feels something like recognition—not of them, but of the possibility of patience, of ancientness, of becoming a thing that belongs so thoroughly to a place that the boundary between self and environment loses coherence.

The pit does not need her. But it is making room for her anyway.

She is not sure, anymore, if she wants to resist.

---

**What lives in the Pit Gardens of the Vermitect** is not a population but a *process*: human beings becoming less discrete, boundaries softening, the membrane between person and place growing permeable over generations until the question "who lives here?" becomes as difficult to answer as "where does the pit end and the jungle begin?" The Mother Substrate dreams, and the dreams are everyone's. The Grandfathers remember, and the memories seep into human bone. The children play games with the ceiling-fauna, and it is no longer clear who invented the rules.

Sixty, perhaps seventy people. But the number is approximate, because the counting requires definitions this place has learned to dissolve.
