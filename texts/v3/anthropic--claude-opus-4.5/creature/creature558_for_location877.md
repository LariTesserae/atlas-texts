## The Inhabitants of Vorrh-Kaleth

---

### The Witnesses

They work eight-hour shifts in the stabilization chambers, and they are not what you would call fully present even when they go home.

The guild recruits them young—not children, but young enough that their habits of perception haven't calcified. Seventeen, eighteen, nineteen years old, drawn from families who have done this work for generations and from newcomers who show the aptitude during the city's annual assessments. The aptitude is simple to test and impossible to teach: you either see the substrate clearly or you don't. Most people's vision slides off the unfinished matter like water off oiled cloth. Their minds refuse to register it, substitute remembered textures, fill the gap with comfortable assumptions. Witnesses see what's actually there—the flicker, the uncertainty, the hovering between states.

They stand in chambers lined with dark brick, the air cool and damp from the stabilization vents, and they watch. That's all. They watch the raw stone as it moves through the grinding process, as it enters the pressure chambers, as it emerges transformed. Their attention is the final catalyst. Without them, the machinery would produce only a more finely ground uncertainty.

You can recognize Witnesses on the street by their eyes—not the size of them, but the quality of their gaze. They look at things too long. They look at things as though the things might change if they blinked. Many of them develop a habit of counting: steps, breaths, the number of stones in a wall. The counting helps them stay anchored. The work pulls at the edges of their own coherence, and the counting reminds them which version of themselves is the real one.

Senior Witnesses—those who have worked the chambers for twenty years or more—often become slightly translucent around the edges. Not literally. But something about them suggests thinness, provisionality, as though they have given so much attention to uncertain matter that they have little left for their own solidity. They are respected, well-compensated, and frequently lonely. Their spouses learn to call their names when entering a room, to keep touching them casually throughout conversations, to make a practice of insisting that they exist.

The oldest Witness currently working is a woman named Errath Solenne, who has stood in Chamber Eleven for forty-three years. She no longer casts a consistent shadow. Guild physicians monitor her weekly, tracking the slow dissolution that is the cost of her expertise. She says she doesn't mind. She says she has seen so much matter make up its mind to exist that she finds the prospect of her own uncertainty peaceful rather than frightening.

She may be telling the truth. Or she may have forgotten what fear feels like. The other Witnesses aren't sure, and they don't ask.

### The Condensation Readers

Before the moisture fronts arrive, someone has to predict them.

The Condensation Readers live in the upper districts, in narrow towers positioned to catch the first chemical traces of approaching weather. They are trained in a discipline that is part meteorology, part meditation, and part something the guild literature calls "attentional cartography"—the art of mapping what isn't yet there by observing how existing things behave in its proximity.

They watch the goats. This is not mysticism; it's practical observation accumulated over centuries. The goats of Vorrh-Kaleth have evolved a sensitivity to pressure changes that precede condensation events by six to twelve hours. When the herds begin climbing toward the eastern faces, when they cluster in certain configurations that the Readers have learned to decode, the moisture is coming. The Readers send word down the mountain via a system of mirror-flashes and colored flags, and the mills adjust their processes accordingly.

But the goats only tell part of the story. The other part requires human perception applied to the edge of the perceptible.

Condensation Readers spend hours each day in observation alcoves built into their towers' upper stories, open to the air, facing the raw peaks. They breathe slowly. They let their vision soften. They attend to the quality of the ozone smell, to the way sound carries or fails to carry, to subtle shifts in how light behaves at the boundary where stable rock gives way to emerging stone. Over years, they develop an intuition for the mountain's moods—its readiness to produce, its periods of dormancy, the rare and dangerous moments when it seems to be considering something unprecedented.

Most Readers are solitary by nature. The work selects for people who find extended silence comfortable, who prefer the company of goats and weather to the density of the lower districts. Many of them take lovers but few take spouses; the lifestyle doesn't accommodate partnership easily. They eat simply—dried fish, hard cheese, the dense bread that travels well from the valley bakeries. They age slowly or quickly depending on factors no one has successfully correlated with anything measurable.

The current Master Reader is a man in his sixties named Tollun Vask, whose tower is the highest permanently inhabited structure in Vorrh-Kaleth. He has been wrong about a condensation front exactly once in thirty years of service, and that single error killed fourteen workers when an unexpected dry period destabilized a processing batch. He carries the names of those workers written on a cloth that he keeps in his pocket. Every year on the anniversary, he climbs to the edge of the safe zone and reads the names aloud to the mountain.

He says the mountain doesn't care. He says he does it anyway.

### The Cat Factors

Managing the city's feline infrastructure requires a particular combination of administrative precision and profound comfort with being ignored.

The Cat Factors are city employees, technically part of the Department of Coherence Support, though they operate with significant autonomy. Their job is to ensure that cats are present where cats are needed—that the stabilization chambers have adequate observation support, that no district falls below the minimum density required for baseline coherence, that the complex territorial negotiations between feline populations don't disrupt the city's industrial rhythms.

They cannot, of course, command the cats to do anything. No one can. The work is therefore less like animal husbandry and more like diplomacy with a nation whose motives are opaque and whose cooperation is essential.

The Factors maintain detailed maps of territorial boundaries, updated weekly through a combination of direct observation and reports from district wardens. They track births, deaths, and the complicated status shifts that occur when a dominant cat dies or is displaced. They manage the public feeding stations, calibrating the food supply to encourage population distribution that serves the city's needs without overriding the cats' own preferences strongly enough to cause resentment.

The senior Factors develop an unsettling ability to interpret feline behavior. They can watch a cat sitting on a windowsill and know whether that cat is content with its current territory, whether it is contemplating expansion, whether it is engaged in a status negotiation with a rival three streets away. They speak of individual cats the way other bureaucrats speak of political figures: with a mixture of professional respect and personal wariness.

The Chief Factor is a woman named Orellen Marsh, who has held the position for twelve years. She is fifty-four, heavyset, with prematurely gray hair that she keeps short. She lives alone in a modest apartment in the Seventh District and owns no cats of her own. This is deliberate policy among senior Factors: personal ownership creates conflicts of interest, favoritism, the appearance of impropriety. The cats of Vorrh-Kaleth belong to no one and to everyone, and the people who manage them must maintain appropriate professional distance.

Orellen's greatest achievement was resolving the Territorial Crisis of 4073, when a population boom in the Third District threatened to destabilize coherence across the entire eastern quarter. Her solution involved the strategic introduction of a particularly charismatic tom from the valley, the careful manipulation of feeding station locations, and what she describes only as "extended negotiation" with the dominant queens of three adjacent territories. The crisis resolved without violence or significant coherence loss. Orellen received a commendation from the city council and a slight raise.

She says the cats don't know about the commendation. She suspects they wouldn't care if they did.

### The Cartographers of Provisional Streets

Not all maps of Vorrh-Kaleth show the same city.

The Cartographers are a guild, though barely—more a loose association of obsessives who have found each other and discovered that their obsession has practical value. They map the inconstant places. The streets that sometimes lead where they're supposed to and sometimes don't. The buildings whose floor plans shift. The quarters where reality hasn't quite agreed with itself about what exists.

They work in teams of three, always. Solo cartography in unstable zones is prohibited after too many practitioners walked into regions that the city briefly forgot and came back... different. Or didn't come back at all. The team structure provides redundancy: if one member begins to experience coherence drift, the others can anchor them, can insist on the shared version of reality until the episode passes.

Their maps are beautiful—hand-drawn, color-coded, annotated with dense marginalia recording dates of observation, confidence levels, correlation with neighboring stable zones. A complete Cartographer's atlas of Vorrh-Kaleth runs to seventeen volumes and is never finished; by the time the final pages are drawn, the first pages are already outdated.

The guild's headquarters is in the Ninth District, in a building that they chose specifically because it is one of the most architecturally stable structures in the city—a monastery from before the mills, built with stone that had already been settled for centuries when the first Witnesses began their work. The building grounds them. It reminds them that stability is possible, that not everything flickers.

Inside, the atmosphere is one of quiet intensity. Cartographers pore over each other's field notes, comparing observations, arguing about whether a particular intersection has genuinely shifted or whether the previous survey was simply inaccurate. They maintain a wall-sized composite map that attempts to show all known variations simultaneously—a document so dense with overlapping lines and conditional notations that newcomers often find it completely unreadable.

The current Guildmaster is a former Witness named Sollum Keth, who left the stabilization chambers after fifteen years because he found he could no longer stop mapping in his mind. The habit of intense observation had generalized; he couldn't walk down a street without noting how it compared to his memory, without cataloging small changes, without feeling the itch to document. The Cartographers welcomed him. They understood.

He is sixty now, his hair white, his hands steady when he draws and trembling when he doesn't. He has walked every street in Vorrh-Kaleth at least once, and most of them dozens of times. He says the city is a conversation, that the ground and the buildings and the uncertain zones are all talking to each other, negotiating, compromising. He says his job is to listen.

He also says he hasn't been lost in twenty years. The other Cartographers suspect this is not quite true, but they don't press him on it. Everyone in the guild has been lost. Everyone has wandered into a version of the city that didn't include them, that treated them as a foreign element, that took longer than it should have to let them go.

They don't talk about what they saw there. They just map it, as best they can, and move on.

### The Mill-Born

Some people are not born in hospitals or homes. Some people are born in the stabilization chambers, in the processing facilities, in the spaces where uncertain matter is coaxed into coherence.

It shouldn't happen. Pregnant workers are supposed to transfer to administrative roles well before their due date. But shift schedules are demanding, and some women hide their condition longer than they should, and sometimes labor comes early.

The mill-born are different. No one agrees on exactly how.

The most conservative view, held by guild physicians, is that exposure to unfinished matter during the crucial first moments of life produces subtle developmental variations—heightened perception, altered stress responses, a statistical tendency toward certain cognitive patterns. These differences are real but modest, measurable but not dramatic. The mill-born are, in this view, slightly unusual people, nothing more.

Others believe something stranger. That the mill-born have a partial connection to the substrate, that some part of them never fully committed to existing in the same way the rest of humanity exists. That they can sense instability before it manifests, can feel the mountain's moods, can sometimes even influence the stabilization process through their presence alone.

The mill-born themselves offer little clarity. They are a small population—perhaps two hundred living in Vorrh-Kaleth at any given time—and they do not form a community in any organized sense. They range across all social classes, all professions, all districts. Some become Witnesses; the work comes easily to them, though at what cost no one is certain. Others avoid the mills entirely, finding the proximity uncomfortable in ways they struggle to articulate.

There is a woman named Tessaly Orn who was born in Chamber Seven during a pressure spike in 4052. She is thirty-one now, slight and dark-haired, with a habit of standing slightly askew as though she's listening to something no one else can hear. She works as a textile inspector, examining finished cloth for flaws. She is very good at her job. Too good, some say; she spots imperfections that shouldn't be visible to the naked eye, that require magnification for other inspectors to confirm.

She says she doesn't see flaws exactly. She says she sees places where the cloth almost wasn't cloth, where it nearly decided to be something else, where the decision toward being still shows. She says everything is like that, if you know how to look. The cloth. The stone. The people.

She doesn't like to be touched. She says it's not personal. She says other people's coherence feels too loud against her skin, too insistent, too there. She has taken lovers—she's clear about this, matter-of-fact—but only for brief periods, only with people who understood that she would need to leave, would need hours alone afterward to feel like a single person again rather than a blur of overlapping selves.

She is not unhappy. She wants this understood. She is not tragic, not suffering, not waiting to be cured of anything. She is simply what the mills made her, what the mountain made her, what she made herself in the space between uncertain matter and the choice to exist.

She suspects there are more mill-born than anyone counts. She suspects some of them don't know what they are, have never been told, have explained away their differences as personality or neurology or simple strangeness. She doesn't try to find them. She figures they'll find themselves eventually, or they won't.

Either way, she says, they'll be fine. Uncertainty is survivable. She's proof.

### The Salamander Keepers

The salamanders in the moisture trenches are old. Some of them are very old.

The Keepers are the people who attend them, who maintain the trench ecosystems, who monitor the health and behavior of creatures that have been part of Vorrh-Kaleth's landscape longer than the city itself. It is a peculiar profession—part biologist, part historian, part something closer to chaplain. The salamanders cannot speak, of course. But the oldest of them seem to recognize individual humans, seem to respond to voice and presence, seem to carry memories that matter even if they cannot be articulated.

The work involves long hours in the trenches, in hip-waders and waterproof coats, in the perpetual damp chill of the condensation channels. Keepers learn to read the salamanders' movements—the slow crawl that indicates contentment, the rapid lateral shifts that suggest agitation, the distinctive posture of a creature about to hunt. They supplement the natural prey population when necessary, introducing larvae bred in hatcheries that the guild maintains in the lower districts.

But the real work is relationship.

The oldest salamander in the trenches is called the Grandmother, though she is not literally ancestral to the others—or perhaps she is, in ways that salamander genetics no longer quite track. She is enormous by any standard, nearly two meters long, her skin a mottled gray-green that shifts in the dim light of the channels. She has been observed in that trench for over three hundred years, according to guild records. She was there when the first mills were built. She was there when the mountain was shorter, when the upper districts didn't exist, when the city was a cluster of temporary structures around a few processing facilities.

The current Grandmother-Keeper is a man named Vel Torren, who has held the position for forty years. He is seventy-two, weathered by decades of damp and cold, his joints aching with the particular rheumatism that afflicts everyone who works the trenches long enough. He visits the Grandmother every day, sits on the channel's edge, speaks to her in a low voice about whatever comes to mind—city news, weather, his own aches and worries.

She watches him. Her eyes, ancient and dark, track his movements with what he swears is recognition.

He doesn't claim she understands. He doesn't make mystical assertions about cross-species communication. He simply says that she is old, that she has seen everything, that being witnessed by something that patient is valuable whether or not true understanding passes between them.

When he dies—and he knows it will be soon, can feel it in his bones—his apprentice will take over. A young woman named Crelle Ash, who grew up in the Sixth District and fell in love with the salamanders during a school tour and never fell out. She has been learning the Grandmother's moods for eight years now. She has sat with Vel in the channel, has heard his stories, has begun to develop her own sense of what the old creature wants and feels and perhaps remembers.

The Grandmother will outlive them both. The Grandmother will outlive the mills, probably, will outlive the city if the city ever falls. She was here before coherence became an industry. She'll be here after.

Vel finds this comforting. He says it's good to know something will persist.

### The Descent Artists

Every few years, someone climbs too high.

The Descent Artists are the people who bring them back.

The work is dangerous—by definition, since it involves entering zones where coherence becomes negotiable, where a careless moment of attention can leave you flickering between versions of yourself that don't agree on basic facts like your name or the direction down. The Artists train for years before they're allowed into the field. They learn anchoring techniques: counting, recitation, the constant murmur of self-description that keeps their identity from drifting. They learn to recognize the signs of dissolution in others—the glassy stare, the disconnected speech, the way a person starts to move as though they're not quite sure where their limbs are.

And they learn to descend. That's the key skill, the one that gives the profession its name. Climbing up is easy; anyone can do that, and too many people try. Coming back down when your sense of direction has been compromised, when you can't quite remember which version of the path you're on, when the mountain is actively uncertain about whether you're part of it—that requires art.

The Artists work in pairs, connected by rope but also by voice, maintaining a constant verbal tether that gives each something to orient toward. They carry stabilized stone in their pockets—processed material from the mills, anchored and certain, serving as a physical reference point for reality. They wear goggles that filter certain wavelengths, reducing the visual distortions that the raw substrate produces.

Most rescues are straightforward: a hiker who ignored the boundary markers, a teenager on a dare, a researcher who overestimated their own stability. The Artists climb to them, attach a line, guide them down through the uncertain zone, deliver them to the medical station in the Fourteenth District for coherence assessment and stabilization.

But some rescues are not straightforward.

The hardest cases involve people who have been up too long, who have begun to adapt to the instability, whose minds have started to prefer the flux. They don't want to come down. They resist. They argue, sometimes violently, that they've found something better up there, that the descent is a form of death, that the Artists are trying to diminish them rather than save them.

These cases require a different approach. The Artists call it "re-coherence therapy"—a combination of patient conversation, physical contact, and the strategic use of stabilized objects to remind the disoriented person what it feels like to be singular, consistent, real. It doesn't always work. Some people choose to stay, to dissolve, to become whatever the mountain makes of them. The Artists have learned to let them go, when it's clear that the choice is genuine rather than confusion.

The current Master Artist is a woman named Hazel Crenn, who has performed over two hundred descents in her thirty-year career. She is fifty-five, compact and strong, her face marked by the particular weathering that comes from repeated exposure to unstable light. She lost her right hand on a rescue twelve years ago—not to violence, but to a moment of inattention, a flicker of uncertainty about whether the hand existed, resolved by the substrate in the negative. She wears a prosthetic now, wood and brass, carved with stabilization sigils that the guild's craftspeople have developed over centuries.

She says the loss was clarifying. She says knowing exactly where her body ends has made her better at helping others find their boundaries.

She doesn't recommend the experience. But she doesn't regret it either.

### The Valley Merchants

Not everyone in Vorrh-Kaleth is born to the mountain. Some come from the lowlands, drawn by commerce, by curiosity, by the particular opportunities that a city of processed reality offers to those who know how to recognize them.

The Valley Merchants are the traders who move goods between Vorrh-Kaleth and the outside world. They maintain warehouses in the First and Second Districts, manage caravans up the winding mountain roads, negotiate contracts with the mills for textile distribution and with the lowland farms for food supply. They are, in many ways, the city's connection to normalcy—to places where matter has already made up its mind, where streets go where they're supposed to, where the weather is just weather.

They are also, many of them, slightly changed by their time in Vorrh-Kaleth.

It's subtle. A merchant who has worked the mountain routes for twenty years might not notice any difference in themselves. But their families in the valley notice. The way their attention lingers a moment too long on ordinary objects, as though checking that they're real. The slight hesitation before entering a room, as though confirming that the room will still be there when they step through the door. The habit, developed unconsciously, of counting their own breaths during moments of stress.

Most merchants cycle in and out—a season in the city, a season in the lowlands, time to re-anchor before returning. The ones who stay year-round develop more pronounced effects. Their speech patterns shift toward the precise, over-specific diction common among Witnesses and Cartographers. They begin to see the lowlands as somehow less substantial, less vividly present, as though the very stability they once took for granted now reads as a kind of flatness.

The wealthiest valley merchant in Vorrh-Kaleth is a man named Dorek Vane, who has lived in the Third District for thirty-eight years. He came originally to manage a textile export operation for his family's trading house; he stayed because he couldn't imagine leaving. The mountain had gotten into him, he says. The uncertainty. The way nothing here is quite finished, quite settled, quite dead.

He is seventy now, his hair white, his hands liver-spotted but steady. He has not returned to the valley in over a decade. He sends letters to his family—a wife, now long dead, children who have become strangers, grandchildren he has never met—but he does not visit. He says the lowlands feel like a story someone told him once, vivid in memory but lacking the weight of presence.

His business partners worry about him. They think the mountain has taken something essential, has eroded his connection to normal human life. He doesn't disagree, exactly. He just doesn't think what was taken was very important compared to what he's gained.

He can see the substrate now, sometimes, when the light is right. Not as clearly as a Witness, but enough. Enough to know that reality is a collaborative project, that existence is a decision being made continuously by everything that exists, that the solidity of the lowlands is just a more emphatic form of the same negotiation that Vorrh-Kaleth conducts openly.

He finds this comforting. He says knowing how things work is always better than not knowing, even when the knowledge is strange.

### The Children of Vorrh-Kaleth

They grow up knowing things that children elsewhere don't know.

They learn to count their steps when walking in the eastern quarters, to keep up a running tally that anchors them to the version of the street they started on. They learn to call out to friends before rounding corners, giving reality a moment to settle before they arrive. They learn the names of the cats in their neighborhood, understanding that this is not just friendliness but civic responsibility—that knowing a cat's name helps anchor both the cat and the child to a shared version of the city.

They play games that children elsewhere would find strange. "Reality tag," where the one who's "it" has to describe what they see in enough detail that everyone agrees it's real before they can chase. "Condensation racing," where teams predict which surfaces will gather moisture first when a front arrives. "Witness practice," where they stare at a pebble until it seems to settle more firmly into being, competing to see who can make the most convincing case that their observation had an effect.

Most of them are happy. Vorrh-Kaleth is not a grim place, despite its strangeness; the rhythm of the mills, the cool constant air, the density of life both human and animal creates a texture that children find rich and engaging. They know no other home. The flickering edges, the uncertain zones, the cats and goats and salamanders are simply the facts of their existence, no more remarkable to them than trees are to forest children or tides are to those who grow up by the sea.

But some of them feel the pull of the heights.

There are always children who want to climb higher than they should, who are fascinated by the raw peaks, who feel the instability not as a threat but as an invitation. Their parents worry about them, try to channel the fascination into safe directions—maybe the child will become a Condensation Reader, working near the edge but not beyond it; maybe a Descent Artist, engaging with the danger in a structured way.

Sometimes it works. Sometimes the children grow into their fascination, find roles that let them engage with the mountain's strangeness without being consumed by it.

Sometimes it doesn't. Sometimes they climb too high, too young, before they've learned the anchoring techniques that might save them. The Descent Artists bring most of them back. Most of them recover fully, the plasticity of youth working in their favor.

But some are changed, after. Some come down quieter, more watchful, with a look in their eyes that their parents recognize from the oldest Witnesses. The mountain has shown them something. What they do with what they've seen will shape the rest of their lives.

### The Dying

In Vorrh-Kaleth, death is complicated.

The stable dead are buried in the lower districts, in tombs cut into rock that has been settled for generations. The mills ensure that the stone around them remains coherent, that the dead stay where they're put, that no uncertainty creeps into the process of decay.

But not all deaths happen in stable zones. And not all the dying want stability.

There is a hospice in the Fifteenth District, near the boundary markers, where the terminally ill can choose to spend their final days. The building is maintained by a religious order—one of several that have developed in response to Vorrh-Kaleth's particular metaphysics—whose theology holds that dissolution is not death but transformation, that allowing the mountain to reclaim the coherence that was borrowed at birth is a holy act.

The hospice is comfortable: warm rooms, good food, attentive care. The dying are not abandoned or neglected. They are simply offered, if they want it, an alternative to the ordinary ending.

Some accept. They climb, in their final weeks, as high as their failing bodies will take them. They find places where the substrate is young and uncertain, and they sit, and they wait. They feel their own coherence begin to loosen, their memories becoming less fixed, their sense of self softening into something larger. The process is not painful—or so the survivors report, the ones who changed their minds and came back down. It is described as a kind of relaxation, a setting aside of the constant effort required to be a particular person.

Those who stay until the end do not leave bodies. They leave impressions, sometimes—a shape in the rock that looks almost like a sleeping figure, a scent that lingers for years, a quality of attention that visitors to the spot report feeling, as though someone is watching them with mild and distant interest.

The religious order maintains a registry of these sites. They visit them, leave offerings, speak the names of those who chose dissolution. They do not claim that the dissolved are conscious in any way that would make sense to the living. They simply say that they have become part of the mountain, and that the mountain is still growing, still emerging, still deciding what to be.

The dying who choose stability are not judged for it. Many of the order's own members, when their time comes, opt for conventional burial, for the known ending, for the comfort of remaining a discrete thing even in death. The choice, they emphasize, must be free. The mountain does not demand dissolution. It only offers.

### The Visitors

They come from the lowlands, from distant cities, from across seas and mountains—drawn by curiosity, by commerce, by scholarly interest in Vorrh-Kaleth's unique conditions. They arrive expecting strangeness and find it, but also find something they didn't expect: a functioning city, full of ordinary concerns, where people buy bread and argue with neighbors and fall in love and grow old.

The visitors who stay longest are the ones who can hold both truths simultaneously: that Vorrh-Kaleth is profoundly strange, a place where reality itself is an industrial product, and that it is also simply home to the people who live there, no more exotic to them than any hometown is to its natives.

Some visitors never leave. The mountain gets into them, as Dorek Vane would say. They find work, find community, find themselves slowly developing the habits of attention and counting that mark a Vorrh-Kaleth native. Their families back home receive letters that become increasingly difficult to understand—not because the writing is unclear but because the experiences described have no referent in stable geography.

Other visitors leave quickly, disturbed by the flickering edges, by the cats that watch too knowingly, by the feeling that the ground beneath them is still deciding whether to exist. They return to the lowlands and tell stories that no one quite believes, stories that harden over time into the legends and rumors that draw the next wave of visitors.

And so the cycle continues. The mountain grows. The mills process. The Witnesses watch and the Cartographers map and the cats hold reality together with their ancient, inscrutable attention. Vorrh-Kaleth persists—not despite its instability but through it, a city that has learned to thrive in the space between existence and possibility.

The inhabitants go about their lives. They are not heroic, most of them. They are not special in any way that would matter elsewhere. They are simply people who have adapted to an extraordinary circumstance, who have made homes in the uncertain places, who have learned that coherence is a practice rather than a given.

They wake in the morning. They go to work. They love who they love, fear what they fear, hope for what seems possible. They are human, in all the ways that matter.

They are also, in ways they rarely think about, collaborators in the ongoing project of making the world real. Every glance, every moment of attention, every counted breath adds to the weight of existence, helps the mountain become itself, helps the city stay where it belongs.

They don't think of themselves as remarkable. They are simply the people of Vorrh-Kaleth, doing what people do: persisting, adapting, living.

The mountain grows beneath them. The mills hum their endless rhythm. And the city, improbable and provisional and stubbornly present, continues its long negotiation with reality.
