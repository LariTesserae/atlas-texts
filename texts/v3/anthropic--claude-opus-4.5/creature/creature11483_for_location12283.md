## The Keepers of the Notation

They call themselves nothing. The word for what they are exists only in the marks—a symbol that resembles three overlapping circles with a vertical stroke, repeated at the beginning of each keeper's contribution to the walls. It does not translate. It is not a name but a *position*, a relationship to the work.

There are never more than five at any time, and rarely fewer than two. They do not recruit. They do not teach in any formal sense. A keeper simply *is* one, the way a note is part of a chord—either the resonance exists or it doesn't. Most who climb to the plateau feel the headaches, taste the mineral air, hear the cacophony as chaos, and descend within days. A few each generation hear something else. They stay. The others, eventually, make room for them and then make their final descent.

---

The current eldest has been here for what she estimates as forty years, though she long ago stopped tracking time by lowland measures. Her skin has taken on a grayish undertone, not unhealthy but *changed*—the same mineral palette as the ground beneath her feet. Her lungs have adapted so thoroughly that the thick air below the plateau now leaves her gasping. She will die here. This is not a tragedy to her. It is simply accurate.

She is small and has grown smaller, her body paring itself down to what the orchard requires: efficient circulation, minimal heat loss, hands still steady enough to carve. Her eyes have developed a membrane that slides across when the crystalline dust bursts from the leaves—she does not remember growing it, only noticing one day that the glitter no longer stung. Her hearing has shifted in ways she cannot describe to lowlanders but which the other keepers understand implicitly. She can distinguish individual trees by their songs from anywhere on the plateau. She knows when the deep vents are about to shift pressure by a quality in the drone that has no name.

She has filled eleven sections of wall with her notations. She is currently working on a twelfth.

---

The youngest arrived three years ago, a person of indeterminate age whose body had already been shaped by difference before they climbed—joints that bent in odd directions, a spine curved like a question mark, features that the lowland villages found unsettling enough to make leaving easy. They heard about the singing plateau from a trader who heard it from a traveler who heard it from someone who had actually been. The description was garbled, half-mythologized, mostly wrong. It didn't matter. Something in the distorted account resonated.

They climbed for nine days. They vomited for the first week after arrival, their stomach rejecting the mineral-laced water the keepers collect from condensation traps, their lungs burning with every breath. The eldest watched without helping. This was not cruelty. It was simply the first test, the one the plateau itself administers. Either the body learns or it doesn't.

The body learned. The youngest now moves through the undergrowth with a fluidity the others lack, their unusual joints an advantage among the tubular growths and bulbous forms. They have begun making their own marks—hesitant, small, in a corner of the structure where older notations have weathered away. The eldest has examined them without comment. The symbols are wrong, but wrong in interesting directions. This is how the notation evolves. This is how it has always evolved.

---

Between these two, three others occupy their own territories of practice and attention.

One spends most of his time among the trees themselves, climbing their warm trunks with bare feet and mineral-calloused hands, pressing his ear to the translucent tissue where the circulation pulses. He is mapping something—the relationship between internal pressure and song, perhaps, or the way the trees communicate through subsonic vibrations that travel through their root-analogues. His marks are dense with small symbols, variations on a theme, each entry adding another data point to a pattern he has been tracking for nineteen years. He speaks rarely, and when he does, it is often to the trees rather than to the other keepers. They do not find this strange.

Another has made the fauna her focus. She can name every creature on the plateau—not with lowland taxonomy but with the notation's own system, symbols that encode behavior and sound and chemical signature and role in the larger chorus. She has documented seventeen population crashes and recoveries in her time here, watching species she knew individually wink out and then resurge from eggs or spores or methods she still doesn't understand. Her marks spiral across the walls in patterns that mirror the boom-and-bust cycles she studies. She believes the orchard is a single organism. She believes the fauna are its thoughts. She has no way to prove this and has stopped trying.

The fifth keeper tends the structure itself—replacing stones, clearing the crystalline dust, maintaining the condensation traps that provide their only water. He is the most recent to take on this role; the previous keeper of the structure made her final descent eight years ago, her body simply stopping one morning between one breath and the next. He buried her at the plateau's edge, in a place where the mineral ground transitions to ordinary stone. Her notations remain on the walls. Her symbol—the three circles and vertical stroke, with a small diagonal mark that was hers alone—appears at the beginning of two centuries of recorded observation. He adds to her work now, continuing a section she left unfinished. He does not know what it means. He knows it must be continued.

---

They eat what the orchard provides, which is more than lowlanders might expect. The succulent leaves of certain trees, once their crystalline teeth are carefully removed, contain dense nutrition and enough moisture to supplement the condensation traps. Some of the bulbous undergrowth forms produce edible nodules at predictable intervals. The beetles, roasted over heat vents, taste of copper and sustenance. The keepers know which organisms are safe, which are toxic, which are safe only at certain stages of their rapid life cycles. This knowledge is not written down. It passes from keeper to keeper through shared meals, through pointed silences when a newcomer reaches for the wrong growth, through the simple fact of survival.

They do not cook in any elaborate sense. They do not preserve. The orchard provides continuously, and they take continuously, their consumption another thread in the plateau's endless weaving. They are part of the ecosystem now, as much as the beetles and the soft-bodied trunk-clingers and the things that scuttle in the undergrowth. The orchard has not acknowledged them—it acknowledges nothing—but it has incorporated them. Their waste feeds the lesser vegetation. Their dead feed the scavengers. Their presence has become part of the calculation that keeps the turbulent equilibrium turbulent and equilibrated.

---

What are they doing? What is the notation *for*?

The keepers do not agree. This is not a source of conflict; it is simply the nature of the work. Each keeper interprets the accumulated marks through their own attention, their own piece of the chorus.

The eldest believes they are witnessing. Not transcribing, not analyzing, but *witnessing*—that the orchard's song requires ears, that existence at this intensity demands acknowledgment, and that the notation is simply the shape that acknowledgment takes when it must be carved into stone. She does not believe the marks contain information in any extractable sense. She believes they contain *presence*, generations of presence, proof that someone was here and heard and added their hearing to the record.

The tree-listener believes they are translating. Slowly, imperfectly, across centuries, the keepers are building a vocabulary for a language that operates in chemical harmonics and pressure differentials and time scales that no single human lifetime can encompass. His marks are attempts at phonemes, at grammar, at the basic units of meaning that might eventually—in a future he will never see—allow someone to *read* what the orchard is saying.

The fauna-watcher believes they are studying a mind. Her marks are diagnostic, an attempt to track the health and intention of something vast and distributed, something that thinks in population dynamics and nutrient cycles rather than neurons. She is a physician to a patient she cannot examine directly, inferring its state from symptoms, leaving notes for the physicians who will come after.

The structure-tender believes they are praying. Not to the orchard, but *with* it—joining their voice to its voice, their marks to its marks, participating in an act of declaration so large that individual intention becomes irrelevant. He does not need the prayer answered. The prayer is its own answer.

The youngest believes they are playing. The orchard is a game, a puzzle, a vast and ongoing entertainment that has captured generations of players. The marks are moves. The notation is a score sheet. They are winning and losing simultaneously, constantly, and the point is not to finish but to keep playing with enough skill that the game remains interesting.

None of them argue. All of them may be right. The notation accommodates all interpretations because it has never been systematic enough to exclude any.

---

They share the structure but not its space. Each keeper has a section of wall, a corner for sleeping, a path through the orchard that is theirs by long habit. They pass each other in the dim light, nod or don't, speak or don't. Conversation happens in bursts—a question about a particular tree's changed pitch, an observation about the scavengers' new pattern, a shared meal eaten in companionable noise. Then silence, or what passes for silence here, and each returns to their attention.

There are rituals, of a kind. When a new section of wall is begun, the other keepers gather to witness the first marks. When a keeper makes their final descent—either walking down under their own power or being carried to the plateau's edge to die—the others add a small symbol to their section's border, a notation of ending that is also a notation of continuation. When the deep vents shift and the entire orchard's chorus changes key, they all pause in whatever they're doing and simply listen, for hours sometimes, until the new patterns stabilize and the work can resume.

They do not worship the orchard. They do not fear it, exactly. They regard it with something like the attention a musician gives to an instrument—intimate, technical, appreciative of what it can do while understanding that the relationship is not reciprocal. The orchard does not know them. The orchard will never know them. They are audience to a performance that has no performer, transcribers of a text that was never written, keepers of a place that requires no keeping.

And yet they stay. Generation after generation, century after century, someone has always climbed the plateau and heard the chaos as something other than chaos and picked up the cutting tool and added their marks to all the marks before. The work continues because it has always continued. The notation grows because someone is always growing it.

---

In the deepest hours, when the light dims to near-darkness and the orchard's song drops to its lowest registers, the keepers sometimes dream the same dreams. They have never compared these dreams aloud—to do so would feel like a violation, though of what they couldn't say. But each of them knows, in the way that keepers come to know things, that the others dream of roots. Of being rooted. Of drawing nutrition from sources too deep to name and exhaling songs that outlast the singer.

They wake to the endless noise, the endless change, the endless warmth of the plateau's metabolism. They eat what the orchard provides. They make their marks. They grow older and grayer and more mineral, more adapted, more *of* this place.

And the orchard sings on, taking no notice, too busy being itself to attend to the small figures moving through its undergrowth, carving their symbols into walls that will outlast them, adding their witnessing and translating and diagnosing and praying and playing to the great accumulation of human attention that the mountain's breath has gathered to itself across the long, loud, living years.
