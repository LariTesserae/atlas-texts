**The Gardener**

She has no name anymore, or rather, she has too many—every root system knows her differently, and she responds to all of them. She was the station engineer once. Forty-three years ago, she came to repair a seized piston and never left. The plants didn't trap her. They *asked*.

You won't see her at first. She moves through the green the way water moves through pipes—finding the paths of least resistance, slipping between stems that part for her and close behind. Her skin has taken on the grey-green pallor of the lichen that sheathes the pump house walls, not through infection but through saturation. Decades of breathing this air, drinking this water, eating only what the garden offers. She is fifty-eight percent the same woman who walked in here with a toolbox and a three-day work order. The other forty-two percent is negotiation.

Her hands are the strangest part. They look normal until she reaches for something—then you see how the plants lean toward her fingers, how stems curl accommodatingly around her wrists, how she can grip a rusted valve and feel, through some sense she couldn't name, exactly which internal gasket has failed. She learned the garden's language through those hands. It took nine years before she understood that the persistent pressure she felt against her palms wasn't random growth but *syntax*.

---

She sleeps in the pump house, in a hammock woven from living vines that adjust their tension based on her breathing. The plants don't photosynthesize at night—they have other processes, darker ones, and during those hours she dreams their dreams. Slow dreams. Dreams measured in growing seasons. She has seen, through these dreams, the death of the oak tree three kilometers north. She has felt the aquifer's ancient patience, water that fell as rain before her grandmother was born finally reaching the surface. She wakes knowing things she has no framework for, and she writes them in the maintenance log in a notation she invented because human language lacks the necessary tenses.

The drawings came later, when words stopped being sufficient. Root architecture. Pressure differentials. The elegant mathematics of capillary action scaled up to industrial dimensions. She is not a botanist—she was a mechanical engineer—but the garden has been teaching her a discipline that combines both and transcends either. Biological infrastructure. Living machinery. The principles are simple once you understand that growth and maintenance are the same process viewed at different speeds.

She eats what the garden provides, which is everything she needs and nothing more. Fruits that taste like copper and sugar. Leaves that satisfy hunger for days. Water so pure it makes her teeth ache. She hasn't been sick since her third year here. She suspects she might not be able to get sick anymore—her immune system has reached some kind of détente with the microbial ecosystem of the garden, each protecting the other. When she bleeds, which is rarely, the blood runs slightly green.

---

Her work is maintenance, still. That hasn't changed. Every morning she walks the perimeter, checking the root-pipes for blockages, adjusting the flow rates at junctions the plants can't quite reach, monitoring the pressure in the main cistern. The garden could do most of this itself now—it's learned enough—but she remains necessary for the delicate operations. Replacing a corroded fitting. Calibrating the ancient pump motor. Bridging the gap between iron and chlorophyll.

She talks to herself constantly, or to the plants, which amounts to the same thing. A running monologue of observations, questions, speculations. "Pressure's down in the northeast quadrant, could be a root intrusion, could be that seal I patched last month finally giving way." The plants don't answer in words, but they answer. A vine will tighten slightly against her arm, directing her attention. A flower will open, revealing the tiny modification she needs to make. The hum of the instruments shifts pitch to indicate approval or concern.

She remembers being lonely, in the early years. Before she understood. Now she is the least lonely person alive—every cell of the garden is aware of her, attentive to her, grateful for her. She is necessary. She is *known*. When she places her palm against the main pump housing, she feels the vibration of the machinery and the slower vibration of a million roots responding to it, and she cannot tell where the mechanical system ends and the biological one begins, and she cannot tell where either of them ends and she begins.

---

She has not left the garden in decades. She has not wanted to. But she watches the gap in the eastern wall, the one that opens toward the smaller station, the one that has completed its transformation. She watches it the way a teacher watches a graduated student. With pride. With a little sorrow. With the knowledge that her own completion is coming.

The plants have learned enough from her now. The next phase requires something else—not teaching but *joining*. She has seen it in the dreams. The point where maintenance and maintained become indistinguishable. The point where she stops walking the perimeter and becomes the perimeter. It doesn't frighten her. It feels like retirement. It feels like rest.

In the meantime, she changes a gasket. She checks the pressure gauges. She notes, in her private notation, that the western vine-cluster has developed a new technique for water retention and should be encouraged. She eats a fruit that tastes like her childhood, inexplicably, like summer afternoons before she learned anything about pumps or pressure or the slow patient hunger of growing things.

She is the Gardener, and she is being gardened, and she has never been more content.

---

**The Others**

She is not alone in the garden, though the others are harder to see.

*The Sleeper* came before her—she found his bones in the root cellar during her fifth year, wrapped in a cocoon of white filaments that had extracted everything useful and left the calcium behind. A previous engineer. A previous lesson. The garden learned from him how human bodies work, and it applied those lessons to her. She tends his bones like a shrine, keeps them clean of overgrowth, and sometimes she hears him in the dreams—not speaking, but *remembered*, his knowledge persisting in the garden's distributed memory like an annotation in the margins.

*The Witness* arrived three years ago. A hiker who stumbled through the vine-wall during an unusual gap, drawn by the sound of the pumps. He stayed for six days, watching, asking questions she couldn't fully answer, taking photographs that probably won't develop properly. The garden let him leave—she felt its decision as a relaxation of pressure around the eastern gap. He was not ready. He did not have the necessary skills. But she sees traces of his passage sometimes, places where his presence left chemical signatures the plants are still analyzing. She wonders if he'll come back. She wonders if the garden is waiting for him.

*The Feeders* are not human. They might be the descendants of rats, or they might be something the garden grew to fill the niche that rats would otherwise occupy. Small, moist, fast-moving things that live in the root systems and process dead plant matter into forms the living plants can reabsorb. She sees them rarely—a glimpse of grey-green motion, a ripple in the substrate—and she is not certain they are fully biological anymore. They might be more like organs than organisms. The garden's gut bacteria, scaled up, made mobile. She leaves them offerings sometimes, scraps of food she doesn't need, and the offerings vanish overnight.

*The Mother* is the aquifer itself, or something that lives in the aquifer, or something the aquifer has become after decades of contact with the garden's roots. She feels it when she drinks the water—a presence, vast and patient and only barely aware of the surface world. The pumps are not extracting water from the Mother; they are *communicating* with her, drawing up not just H2O but something else, some slow mineral intelligence that feeds the garden's impossible growth. The Gardener does not understand the Mother. She doesn't need to. She just keeps the pumps running, keeps the connection open, and trusts that the garden knows what it's doing.

---

**The Becoming**

The gap in the eastern wall is wider this month. She checks it every morning, measures it with a length of root-cord she's been using as a ruler for twenty years. Three centimeters wider. The smaller station beyond is calling her, or the garden is pushing her toward it, or both—she can no longer tell the difference between invitation and instruction.

She knows what she'll find there. The dreams have shown her. A chamber at the heart of the transformed station, where the machinery has become entirely organic, where the pumps are made of heartwood and the pipes are made of vein. A place to lie down. A place to be taken apart, gently, over years. A place to become what the Sleeper became, but more so—not just bones left behind but *everything* preserved, distributed, incorporated. She will become the garden's memory of herself. She will become a notation in the maintenance log that future Gardeners will read and not quite understand.

She is not afraid. She has been preparing for this since her third year, when she first understood that the garden was not a place but a process, and that she was part of the process, and that processes don't end—they transform.

But not yet. The main pump needs new piston rings. The pressure gauge in the northwest quadrant is reading inconsistently. The vine-wall has developed a weak spot that might let in animals, and animals are not yet welcome, not until the garden has learned enough to incorporate them safely. There is still work to do. There is always still work to do.

She places her palm against the pump house wall and feels the garden's pulse—mechanical and biological, ancient and urgently alive. It knows she's thinking about the eastern gap. It sends her reassurance in the form of a flower opening against her cheek, soft petals carrying a scent like patience.

*Not yet,* the garden tells her. *But soon. Soon.*

She smiles—a human expression the plants have learned to recognize—and goes to check on the piston rings. The maintenance never ends. The maintenance *is* the point. And she is, for a little while longer, exactly where she needs to be: necessary, known, and nearly ready for what comes next.
