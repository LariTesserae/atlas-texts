# The Tenders

They move in crews of three to seven, following routes inherited from the crew before them, who inherited from the crew before that, back through enough generations that the original logic of the paths has been lost. What remains is tradition hardened into something like instinct: you walk the Seventh Radial on days when the amber lights pulse in that particular slow rhythm; you avoid the Hot Straits when the vibration in the catwalk takes on its higher pitch; you never, ever descend to the water-touching platforms during what the crews call a Breathing, when the vast machinery above shifts into some deeper cycle and the air grows too thick to process safely.

They call themselves Tenders, though what exactly they tend is a question that would puzzle them if they thought to ask it. The machines do not need them—the repair-crawlers handle damage, the pump-beasts haul what needs hauling, the float-monitors drift and measure and transmit to receivers that may or may not still function. The Tenders' interventions are small and possibly meaningless: clearing mineral buildup from a section of grating, redirecting a trickle that has begun to pool where pooling seems wrong, documenting observations in notation systems that vary by crew and district and are rarely shared. They do these things because their parents did them, and because the doing structures a life that would otherwise have no structure at all.

They are small people. Not stunted—their proportions are fine—but compact, adapted over uncounted generations to the cramped geometries of maintenance shafts and the narrow clearances between pipe runs. Their skin has taken on a grayish undertone, something between the pale of the lichens and the mineral deposits that coat every surface. Their eyes are large and dark-adjusted, uncomfortable in the rare stretches where multiple maintenance strips converge to create something approaching real brightness. They move through the amber twilight with an ease that looks like ownership but is really just profound familiarity, the way water knows a channel.

Most crews are family, though family here means something loose and accumulative. Children belong to whoever is present when they need something; parentage is acknowledged but not emphasized. A crew might consist of two sisters, their mother's brother, a woman who joined twenty years ago after her own crew died in a platform collapse, and three children of indeterminate relation who are learning the routes. When a crew grows too large, it splits. When one grows too small, it merges with another or simply fades, its routes absorbed by neighbors who extend their circuits to cover the gap.

They speak a language that has borrowed heavily from the infrastructure. Half their nouns are technical terms whose original meanings have drifted: a *conduit* is any passage, a *valve* is any choice, *pressure* is any urgency or emotional weight. They say *the system runs* to mean that things continue as they should; they say *blockage* for any obstacle, physical or social. Their metaphors are all flow and containment, cycling and drainage. When someone dies, they say the person *has dispersed*—returned to the moisture in the air, the mineral film on the columns, the black water below.

They live in the interstices. Some crews have claimed junction housings—those boxy enclosures where multiple catwalks meet, originally built to shelter equipment that has long since failed or been absorbed by the repair-crawlers. They partition these spaces with salvaged grating and hang fabric woven from lichen-fiber to create rooms, privacy, the suggestion of domesticity. Others are nomadic, sleeping in maintenance alcoves along their routes, carrying everything they own in packs that sit low on their backs. Neither approach is considered superior; both are simply ways of being a Tender.

Their relationship with the machine-life is complicated. The pump-beasts ignore them entirely, hauling past on their rail-treads with a single-mindedness that suggests the Tenders are beneath notice—or perhaps invisible to whatever sensors guide them. The float-monitors sometimes follow a Tender for hours, drifting at a fixed distance across the water's surface, before losing interest and returning to their inscrutable measurement patterns. The repair-crawlers are more troubling. They emerge from wall-cavities without warning, and while they have never harmed a Tender directly, they have been known to seal passages while someone is inside them, or to disassemble structures that crews have built, apparently classifying them as damage to be corrected. The Tenders have learned which modifications the crawlers tolerate and which they don't, but the rules are not consistent, and every few years a crew loses a junction housing to mechanical indifference.

What the Tenders believe varies by district and crew and individual, but certain ideas recur. Most hold that the Condenseries are alive in some sense—not conscious, but animate, a vast organism of which they are some small and tolerated part, like bacteria in a gut. Many believe the machines are waiting for something: a signal, a threshold, a completion. A few insist that the water accumulating below has purpose, that it is being saved for an event their ancestors understood but failed to transmit. Others say this is foolishness, that the machines simply run because running is what machines do, and meaning is something the living invent to comfort themselves.

There are those who go into the water.

It is not forbidden—the Tenders have few prohibitions, only cautions—but it is regarded as strange, a calling that sets a person apart. The water-goers build small rafts from salvaged materials and pole themselves across the black surface, navigating by the columns and the stalactite-pillars, mapping regions that the catwalks do not reach. They report that the water is not deep in most places—chest-height, perhaps, over a false floor of accumulated sediment and debris. But in some areas the floor drops away, and their poles find nothing, and they speak of these depths with a reverence that might be religious. A few water-goers have tried to dive, to see what lies below. Most surface quickly, gasping, unable to describe what they felt. Some do not surface at all.

Children here grow up understanding that the world is made. Not made *for* them—the Condenseries are plainly indifferent to human need—but made, constructed, a thing that someone or something once intended. This knowledge does not trouble them the way it might trouble people who remember wilderness. They have no concept of nature as something separate from artifice; to them, the dripping pipes and the amber light and the machine-breathing are simply how existence is organized. They learn the routes and the cautions and the notation systems, and they take their place in crews, and they walk the catwalks until they disperse.

Sometimes, in the quieter districts where systems have failed, a Tender will find evidence of others. Marks on columns that don't match any crew's notation. Structures in junction housings that use unfamiliar techniques. Glimpses, at the far end of a long catwalk, of figures that move differently, that seem taller or shorter or wrong in some way that vanishes before it can be examined. The Tenders have stories about these others—that they are remnants of crews who went too deep, that they are something the machines made, that they were here before the Tenders and will remain after. No one knows. The Condenseries are large enough to hold many populations in isolation, and the Tenders' routes cover only a fraction of the whole.

What they want is simple: to continue. To walk the routes and clear the grating and document the observations. To raise children who will do the same. To live within the breathing of the machines and dispersal into water and mineral, becoming part of the infrastructure that has always contained them. They do not dream of escape or transformation. The Condenseries are not a prison to them; they are a world, the only world, vast and dark and wet and rhythmic, and the Tenders move through them like blood through a body that does not know it is alive.

In the deepest hours, when the amber lights dim to their lowest cycle and the machine-breathing slows to something like rest, the Tenders sometimes gather at the larger junctions and tell stories. The stories are all the same story, really: someone walked a route, something was encountered, a choice was made, the system ran. The details vary but the shape persists, because the shape is the shape of life here, constrained and repetitive and strangely peaceful. They speak the stories into the humid air, and the moisture carries the words away, and the Condenseries continue as they have always continued, and the Tenders continue within them, small and gray and dark-eyed, tending what does not need tending, because tending is what they were made to do.
