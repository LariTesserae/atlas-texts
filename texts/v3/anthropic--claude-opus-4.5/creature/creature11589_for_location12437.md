**The Dwellers**

They are born with their eyes already adjusted.

That is the first thing the midwives note—not the cry, which is thin and quickly hushed, but the way the newborn's gaze tracks the bone-light without flinching, without the searching that would indicate expectation of something brighter. The babies of the plateau do not look *for* light. They look *at* what is lit. This is a distinction that matters.

The people here are not pale, as you might expect. They are warm-skinned, in shades that range from deep bronze to a color like the inside of almond shells, as if their bodies have decided that since no sun will bleach them, they might as well hold onto whatever warmth they can generate. Their hair tends toward dark and heavy, worn long by most, pinned up or braided back for the factory workers and quarry-folk. It holds the pale dust well. After a shift, a worker's hair looks frosted, ancient, though they may only be nineteen.

They are not tall. Generations of walking the same streets, eating the same lichens cultivated in cellar-gardens and the same fungal breads and the same protein paste whose origin no one questions too closely—these generations have produced a people of moderate height and careful build. Not thin, not thick. Durable. Their bones do not break easily. Their joints do not ache with the chill because there is no chill; the plateau maintains a temperature that skin forgets almost immediately. They move through the air as if the air were an extension of themselves.

**The Lamplighters**

They are the closest thing the city has to priests.

There are seventeen of them at any time—never more, never fewer, a number that has held since before the oldest records begin. When one dies, another is chosen, and the choosing is not discussed with outsiders and rarely with each other. It simply happens. A factory worker will set down their tools one day and walk to the Lamplighters' Hall near the city center, and the others will already have set a place for them at the long table, will already have laid out the coat with its many pockets and the long hooked pole and the small journal in which they will record the lamps they tend.

Because the lamps need tending. Not often—a given lamp may burn for years on a single brick of fuel—but eventually. And the Lamplighters walk circuits through the city, patterns so old they have worn grooves into the stone, checking each lamp in turn, noting the color of its flame (steady bone-white is good; a creep toward yellow means the fuel is nearly spent; blue at the edges means something is wrong, though no one can say what, and those lamps are replaced entirely, housing and all). They carry fuel-bricks in their coat pockets. They carry small glass vials of an oil that keeps the housings clear. They carry, each of them, a single deep-black stone the size of a thumbnail, polished smooth, which they will not explain and which no one has ever seen them use.

The Lamplighters do not speak of their work as maintenance. They speak of it as *keeping agreement*. With what, they do not say. The lamps have always burned. The Lamplighters have always tended them. These facts are load-bearing.

**The Quarry Families**

At the edge, the people are different.

Not a different species—the quarry-folk marry into city families sometimes, and city-dwellers occasionally take the long walk out to the rim and stay—but different in the way that a hand is different from a foot. Shaped by their work. Shaped by the edge.

The quarry families have lived at the cliff-face for so long that their genealogies are tangled beyond recovery. They refer to each other by use-names that describe function and location rather than lineage: Secondladder Vess, Eastface Tommon, Sixteenthplatform Kel. A child might be called Newweight until they are old enough to work the winches, at which point they become Westwinch or Chainkeeper or whatever their station requires.

They are leaner than the city-dwellers, with hands that are permanently mapped by the fine pale scars of quarry-work. The mineral that becomes lamp-fuel isite to the touch before it is processed—not sharp, but insistent, pressing back against skin that presses into it, leaving marks that do not fade. A veteran quarry worker's palms look like aerial maps of river deltas, pale lines branching and rebranching across darker skin.

They sleep in barracks carved into the cliff itself, in chambers that look out over the nothing-dark of the drop. Some of them keep their windows unshuttered, preferring to sleep facing the void. They say it reminds them what they're working against. They say the city needs to see itself, and they are the ones who cut the fuel that makes seeing possible.

They say, some of them, late at night in the barracks when the bone-light from the cliff-top lamps barely reaches their windows, that they have heard sounds rising from below. Not echoes of their own work. Something else. Something that might be wind moving through spaces too vast to imagine. Something that might be breathing.

They do not investigate. The job is to quarry, not to question. The fuel comes up. The carts carry it to the city. The lamps burn.

**The Archivists**

The records are kept in a building that has no windows, which is not unusual here, but also no doors on its lower three floors. You enter through the roof, descending a spiral staircase that winds down through the structure's core, and the Archivists say this is for the protection of the documents, though against what threat they do not specify. Fire, perhaps. The bone-light lamps do not spark, do not drip, do not behave like flame in any way that would threaten paper, but perhaps once they did. Perhaps the architecture remembers a danger the people have forgotten.

The Archivists are old, mostly. Not exclusively—there are apprentices, teenagers who show the particular combination of patience and obsessiveness that the work requires—but the senior keepers of the records are men and women who have spent more of their lives in the Archives than out of them. Their skin has taken on a particular quality, not pale exactly but *matte*, as if the years of handling ancient paper have transferred some of its texture to them.

They do not read the records. This is important to understand. The Archivists *keep* the records—they preserve, they organize, they note which documents are stored where—but the content is not their concern. When a citizen wishes to research something, they submit a request, and the Archivists retrieve the relevant materials, and the citizen sits at one of the long tables on the building's fourth floor (the only floor with lamps, the only floor with chairs) and reads for themselves.

What do the records contain? Everything and nothing. Census data going back further than the numbers remain meaningful. Accounts of lamp-fuel production quantities, year by year, century by century. Minutes from council meetings held before the current council structure existed, referencing procedures and concerns that no longer parse. Maps of the city that show buildings which are not there and do not show buildings which are. And, in a locked section that requires three separate Archivists to access, a set of documents in a language that no one alive can read.

The Archivists are aware of these documents. They catalog them like anything else. They do not attempt translation. Whatever the documents say, they say it to no one, and perhaps that is the point.

**The Listener-Keepers**

Not everyone ignores the small six-legged creatures that orient toward sound.

A handful of people—perhaps two dozen in the entire city—have devoted themselves to studying the listeners, or at least to watching them. They call themselves Keepers, though they do not keep the listeners in any meaningful sense. You cannot keep something that does not move, that does not eat, that does not seem aware of your presence. You can only position yourself nearby and wait.

The Keepers map listener territories, noting which individual (they believe the listeners are individuals, though they cannot prove it; the creatures are too similar to distinguish visually) occupies which corner, which shadow, which gap between buildings. They record listener movements when they happen—a single repositioning might be the subject of a year's worth of documentation, analyzed and re-analyzed for meaning that probably is not there.

Some Keepers speak to the listeners. Not because they expect response, but because the listeners orient toward the sound, and there is something in that orientation that feels like attention. The Keepers report that it helps to be listened to, even by something that cannot understand. Especially by something that cannot understand. The listeners do not judge. The listeners do not remember. The listeners simply turn toward the sound and remain turned until the sound stops, and there is peace in that, the Keepers say. There is peace in being witnessed by something that requires nothing from you in return.

**The Children**

They play games, but the games are different here.

Hide-and-seek, in a city of bone-light and deep shadow, becomes something tactical and prolonged. A game can last for days, with the seeking child checking known hiding spots during their walks to and from lessons, with the hidden child relocating at night when the streets are emptiest. The winner is not whoever is found last but whoever maintains the longest unbroken period of hiding—the record, held by a girl named Tessaly some four generations back, stands at eleven days.

There is a game called Listening, which requires two or more children to stand in a public space and remain perfectly still, mimicking the creatures they are named after. The game ends when one of them moves. This can take hours. Parents have learned to check the intersections for statue-still children before assuming they have gone missing.

There is a game with no name that involves finding the oldest thing you can find. A paving stone with a date carved into it. A lamp that has been burning since before your grandparents were born. A listener that a Keeper has documented in the same location for a century. The children show each other these findings like treasures, and perhaps they are. In a city without weather, without decay, age becomes a kind of magic. The oldest thing is the thing most proven, most trusted, most *here*.

The children learn early that the city is not a place that will change for them. They must change for it, or not change at all. Most of them do not find this distressing. It is simply true, like the darkness above, like the bone-light pooling in the streets. The plateau was here before them. The plateau will be here after. They are a brief warmth moving through it, leaving footprints that will outlast their memories.

**The Council**

Someone must make decisions, even here.

The Council is seven people, chosen by methods that have varied across the centuries and currently involve a combination of lottery and refusal—names are drawn, and the drawn may refuse, and the refusing means the next name is drawn, and this continues until seven people have either accepted or been unable to articulate a reason for refusal that the existing Council finds sufficient. The result is a governing body composed of people who did not particularly want to govern but could not explain why they shouldn't.

They meet in a hall near the city center, a building distinguished only by its size—it could hold hundreds, though the Council is seven and the observers rarely number more than a dozen. The acoustics are strange, designed (or evolved, or accidental) such that a voice from the central platform carries clearly to every seat while voices from the seats do not carry to each other. This makes debate difficult and grandstanding easy, which may have been the point.

The Council's duties are mostly arbitration. Disputes over territory—not land ownership, exactly, but use. Who may place a cart where. Which factories have priority access to which quarry output. How the Lamplighters' circuits should be adjusted when a new lamp is proposed (new lamps are proposed perhaps once a generation, and the discussions take years). The Council does not make laws because the city does not have laws in any conventional sense. It has customs. The Council interprets the customs. When the customs are silent, the Council waits for them to speak.

The current Council includes a former factory worker who has not spoken in session for three years, a former Lamplighter who speaks too much, a quarry-family representative who attends via written proxy because she will not leave the edge, and four others of no particular distinction. They are aware that the city does not need them. They serve anyway. Someone must.

**The Ones Who Leave**

It happens, occasionally.

Not often. The plateau is a day's walk across, and its edges fall away into darkness, but there are paths. Narrow stairs carved into the cliff face, older than the quarries, descending into the nothing-below. Some of them end in platforms that lead nowhere. Some of them end in tunnel mouths that lead somewhere, though where is not recorded. Some of them simply end, the final stair giving way to open air and the long fall that returns no echo.

People have taken these stairs. People have not returned. This is not treated as tragedy, exactly, nor as mystery. It is treated as *departure*. The Archivists record the names of those who descend, when the names are known. The families grieve or do not grieve. The listener in the nearest corner turns toward the sound of footsteps on stone, then turns away when the sound fades.

Where do they go? The question is asked less often than you might expect. The plateau is the plateau; the below is the below. Whatever is down there—whatever darkness deeper than darkness, whatever spaces where even the bone-light cannot reach—it is not the city's concern. The fuel comes up. The lamps burn. The living continue living, and the departed have departed, and these two facts do not need to be reconciled.

Some say the factories are connected to the below. That the tunnels beneath the city, never mapped, never fully explored, eventually open onto the same depths that the cliff-stairs lead to. That the humming felt through the walls is the sound of something moving down there, or the sound of the city keeping something down there still. These people are not taken seriously. They are not taken unseriously either. They are simply *noted*, the way everything is noted, the way the dust notes the passage of feet, the way the listeners note the passage of sound.

**The Ordinary Ones**

But most of them—most of them are simply people.

They wake in apartments that have housed their families for generations, in beds whose frames have not been replaced because they have not worn out. They eat breakfast in kitchens that smell of fungal bread and the particular sweetness of cellar-grown lichens. They work in factories or warehouses or shops or do not work at all, because work is not compulsory here, not in any enforced sense; the city produces what the city needs, and enough people find enough purpose in production that the system continues.

They love each other, the way people do. They fall into affections that last decades and into conflicts that last longer. They raise children who are very similar to them and occasionally children who are not, who stare at the bone-light with something that might be longing, who ask questions about the above and the below that have no good answers. They grow old in bodies that do not weather, exactly, but that wear in the same slow way the stone wears: patiently, over timescales that feel like permanence until suddenly they don't.

They gather in halls to share meals that are technically unnecessary—everyone has food at home, everyone has kitchens—but that serve some deeper function, some need for proximity that the wide streets do not satisfy. They talk about small things. The quality of this season's lichen harvest. The lamp at the corner of Fourteenth and Arch, which has been burning yellow for weeks now, which surely the Lamplighters have noticed. The listener near the old factory, which moved last month for the first time in living memory, which must mean something though no one can say what.

They do not wonder, most of them, whether this is enough. The question would require a frame of reference they do not have. Other cities, other skies, other lights that change across the day—these are abstractions, stories, the kind of thing you might encounter in the older Archives if you were the sort of person who researched such things. The plateau is real. The bone-light is real. The darkness above is real and present and patient, and under it, they live.

They have always lived here.

They will, perhaps, always live here.

The city does not ask what they want from it. They do not ask what it wants from them. There is only the continuation, lamp by lamp, step by step, the prints of their feet joining the prints of everyone who came before, the sound of their voices turning the listeners toward them for a moment, just a moment, before the stillness returns.
