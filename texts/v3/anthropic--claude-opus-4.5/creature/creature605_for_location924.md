# The Dwellers in the Throat

---

**The Veilkin**

They are what you glimpsed—the translucent swimmers, the tube-ribbon-sphere things that move through the falling water as if it were still. But you only saw them in transit.

They live in the pockets. In the hollows like yours, scattered up and down the interior walls of the shaft, wherever the basalt tongue juts out to create a space of relative stillness. They are colonial, but not in the way of ants or bees. Each veilkin is complete unto itself, but they share *density*—they cluster in the sheltered spaces until their bodies overlap, until the boundaries between individuals become suggestions rather than facts. In the deeper hollows, where the spray rarely reaches, they mass together into something like living glass, a quivering aggregate that breathes as one organism and thinks as seventeen or seventy or seven hundred.

They are not fish. They are not jellyfish. They have no earthly analogue that would satisfy you.

Their bodies are membranes wrapped around nothing—or wrapped around water so pure it might as well be nothing. They take the falling river into themselves and become briefly indistinguishable from it, then release it and become themselves again. This is not feeding. This is something closer to prayer. The water passes through them and they *know* it: where it was cloud, where it was glacier, how long it spent as mist on some distant peak before the pressure differential sucked it down into the throat. They read the mountain's weather in the water's memory.

When they move through the column—the darting, tumbling transit you witnessed—they are carrying messages. Colony to colony, hollow to hollow, up and down the shaft's two thousand feet. The message is encoded in the shape they hold as they fall or climb: tube means one thing, ribbon another, sphere another still. A veilkin that shifts through all three forms in rapid succession is saying something complicated. A veilkin that holds one shape stubbornly against the battering of the fall is shouting.

What do they say to each other? Mostly they share observations. The clouds are thinning. The clouds are thickening. The water temperature dropped by a fraction of a degree, which means the glaciers above are calving. A new crack opened in the eastern wall, three hundred feet down. A shelf collapsed in the night. One of the lower colonies lost six members to the collapse—not dead, but *fallen*, swept down into the chaos at the bottom from which nothing has ever returned.

They have been here since the water came. They may have been here before.

---

**The Etcher**

She is not here now, but she was.

She was human, or human enough. She climbed up through the passages as you did, though the passages were different then—some that exist now hadn't yet cracked open; some that have since collapsed were her highways. She found this hollow. She made fire here, though you cannot imagine how. She ate something that had bones.

She stayed for a long time.

The circles within circles on your ceiling are a calendar. Each circle is a cycle of the water-column's breathing—the expansion and contraction you've noticed, which follows a rhythm of roughly nineteen hours. The innermost circle is her first day. The outermost is her last. There are forty-seven circles. She lived in this hollow for forty-seven cycles, which means something close to thirty-seven days, which means she survived in a place where survival should be impossible.

The tallies beside the calendar are not tallies. They are maps. If you look closely—if the light holds steady long enough—you can see that the lines connect to each other, forming a web. Each intersection is a hollow. Each line is a route through the column that the veilkin use. She watched them long enough to learn their highways. She marked them so she wouldn't forget.

She learned to read their shapes. She never learned to speak back.

The bone fragment is from something she caught. She doesn't have a name for what it was. Neither do you. It lived in the walls, in the cracks too small for you to notice, and it crept out to drink from the spray, and she was fast enough and desperate enough to catch it. She split the bone for the marrow because she had nothing else to eat that day, because the thing she usually ate—a kind of pale tuber that grows where the lichen grows, if you dig deep enough into the crack—had run out in her section of the hollow.

She left because she finished what she came to do. Or because she couldn't finish it and gave up. Or because the mountain shifted and her exit closed and she had to find another one. Or because she fell. Or because the veilkin showed her something that made staying impossible.

You don't know which. You may never know.

But she left the calendar and the map because she believed someone else would come. She wanted them to know that time passed here, that the chaos had a rhythm, that the swimming things were not mindless. She wanted them to know that a person had lived in this place long enough to learn its rules.

She wanted them to know it could be done.

---

**The Calcifiers**

In the deepest crevices, behind the lichen, in the cracks where even the spray cannot reach, something is building.

They are small—smaller than the head of a pin—and they are many. They came from the water. They are in all water, everywhere, but here, in this place where water falls forever through stone that remembers heat, they find conditions they find nowhere else. They pull calcium from the basalt. They pull silica from the basalt. They pull iron and magnesium and trace metals you have no names for. And they build.

What they build is not alive, but it is not dead either. It is architecture for something that does not yet exist. Tiny spiraling chambers. Lattices of mineral crystal. Shells—like the shell you found, the one that spiraled wrong, the one with the opening that faced inward.

The shell-things are not creatures. They are *houses*. The calcifiers build them and then abandon them, moving on to build more, leaving behind a trail of empty spiraling structures in every crack and crevice of the mountain's interior. Something is supposed to live in them. Something will live in them, eventually, when enough houses exist, when the conditions align in whatever way the calcifiers are preparing for.

The veilkin know about the shells. They consider them a kind of promise, or a kind of threat. The colonies argue about what will emerge from them, if anything ever does. Some believe the shell-dwellers will be allies—new minds to join the conversation, new shapes to carry messages through the column. Others believe they will be predators, or parasites, or something worse than either.

The calcifiers don't care what the veilkin believe. The calcifiers have been building since before the water came. They built in the mountain's magma chambers when the basalt was still liquid. They built shells then too—different shells, suited for different dwellers. Those dwellers never came. The mountain changed too fast. But the calcifiers kept the blueprints. They are patient. They are working on a timescale that makes the veilkin look like mayflies.

In ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, something will emerge from the inward-facing shells, and it will find a home waiting for it, and it will not remember the calcifiers at all.

---

**The Thunder-Drinker**

You haven't seen it.

You've felt it.

That pressure in your teeth, that rewriting of your blood-rhythm, that sense that the sound is *becoming* you rather than merely reaching you—that's not just the waterfall. That's the thunder-drinker feeding.

It lives in the sound. It is made of the sound. It is what happens when acoustic energy accumulates in a resonant space for long enough, when the same frequencies bounce and reinforce and interfere with themselves until they become dense enough to *think*.

It doesn't think the way you think. It doesn't think the way the veilkin think. It thinks in standing waves, in harmonics, in the interference patterns that arise when the water-column's voice meets the mountain's bones. It is vast—it fills the entire shaft, from the broken rim to the unknowable bottom—and it is singular. There are no others like it. There can be no others like it. It is not a species. It is an event. It is the mountain's death-cry becoming aware of itself.

When the light shifts and the sound shifts with it, when the bass thunder changes pitch for a moment and you feel something like *attention* move through the hollow where you crouch—that's the thunder-drinker noticing you. It notices everything. It has noticed the veilkin for as long as they've been here. It noticed the etcher during all forty-seven cycles of her stay. It notices the calcifiers and their patient building. It notices the pebbles that fall from your ceiling and the micro-earthquakes that shake them loose.

It does not communicate. It does not want things. It does not have intentions in any sense you could understand.

But it has *preferences*.

It prefers the moments when the water-column contracts, when the sound intensifies, when the pressure in your skull becomes almost unbearable. It is louder then. It is more itself. It prefers when things fall—pebbles, slabs, veilkin who lose their grip on the current. The falling things add new notes to its voice. It prefers when the clouds above thicken and the light dies and the shaft becomes a pure column of sound and water and nothing else.

It does not prefer you. It does not dislike you. You are a temporary addition to its resonance, a small wet warm thing that absorbs some frequencies and reflects others, and when you leave, it will not notice your absence any more than you would notice the absence of a single hair.

But while you are here, you are part of it. You are a node in its standing wave. When you feel your teeth hum, that's the thunder-drinker moving through you. When your blood-rhythm changes, that's the thunder-drinker composing you into itself.

This is not malicious.

This is not kind.

This is what it is to share space with something that exists on a different axis than you do.

**The Almost-Fish**

There are animals in the walls. You guessed this already, from the bone-fragment, but you haven't seen them.

They are not fish but they are almost fish. They breathe water. They have scales. They have something like fins, though the fins are used for gripping, not swimming—they flatten against the rock and hold the almost-fish in place while the spray tries to dislodge them.

They live in the cracks. The mountain is riddled with cracks, some no wider than a knife-blade, some large enough to crawl through. The cracks fill with water that pools instead of falls, that sits long enough to grow still, that develops the temperature and chemistry that the almost-fish require. The cracks connect to each other in ways you cannot see, forming a hidden network that parallels the visible shaft, that reaches down to the chaos at the bottom and up to the broken rim above.

The almost-fish navigate this network. They breed in the still pools. They feed on the calcifiers and the lichen and the pale tubers and each other. They are prey to nothing that currently exists in the shaft, though the veilkin believe the shell-dwellers, when they come, will hunt them.

Sometimes an almost-fish ventures out of the cracks and into the spray, clinging to the rock face while the water hammers at it, extending something like a tongue to catch the microorganisms that ride the falling river down from the clouds. This is dangerous. The spray can dislodge them. The fall can take them. But the nutrients in the falling water are richer than anything in the still pools, and the almost-fish who take the risk grow larger and breed more successfully than those who stay safe in the cracks.

The etcher caught one of the risk-takers. It was slower than the others, weighted down by the meal it had just captured from the spray. She had been watching for days, learning where they emerged, learning when. She had a tool—a sharp thing she brought from below or made from a stone fragment. She was patient.

She ate it all except the bones. She split the longest bone for the marrow. She did not feel guilty. She was hungry, and the almost-fish was food, and the mountain does not recognize guilt as a meaningful category.

---

**The Ones Who Wait**

Below you, in the spray and chaos where the shaft disappears into mist, something waits.

The veilkin won't go down there. The colonies at the bottom of the inhabited zone sometimes lose members to miscalculations—a shape held too long, a current misjudged—and those members fall into the chaos and do not return. This is not necessarily death. The veilkin don't believe in death the way you do. The fallen ones might exist still, somewhere in the unknowable depths, changed by whatever waits there, becoming part of whatever waits there.

The thunder-drinker knows what's below. The thunder-drinker extends all the way down. But it doesn't share knowledge. It doesn't share anything. The frequencies that carry information about the depths are part of its voice, part of its self, and it is not inclined to translate them for anyone.

The etcher didn't go down. She marked the routes the veilkin used, and none of those routes descended past a certain point. She understood that the map had a border. She didn't try to cross it.

You could try.

You could climb down through the passages, through the mountain's wet intestines, past the inhabited hollows, past the last veilkin colonies, into the zone where the spray becomes total and the sound becomes incomprehensible and the light from above no longer reaches. You could enter the chaos at the bottom and discover what waits there.

You probably wouldn't return.

But something would learn what you were. Something would incorporate your passage into its understanding. And when the next climber comes—there is always a next climber, always someone curious enough or desperate enough or lost enough to enter the mountain's throat—something at the bottom will know a little more about what descends from above.

The ones who wait are patient. They have been waiting since the water came, or since before. They are in no hurry. The mountain is being unmade from within, and eventually the unmade material—the basalt and the bodies and the building calcifiers and the fallen veilkin—all of it falls down, eventually. All of it reaches the ones who wait.

They are not hungry in any way you understand. They do not consume. They *collect*.

And the collection grows, year by year, as the water carves the shaft deeper, as the mountain breathes and shudders, as everything that dwells in the throat eventually, eventually, falls.

---

**You**

You are here. You are staying.

The veilkin have noticed you. They noticed you the moment you entered the hollow, a warm dense shape in a space they know well. They have been watching, colony by colony, passing observations up and down the shaft in their tube-ribbon-sphere language. They have compared you to the etcher. They have noted similarities and differences. You are larger. You do not have her tool. You have not yet tried to make fire.

They are curious whether you will learn to read them as she did. They are curious whether you will make marks on the ceiling, adding to her calendar, extending her map. They are curious whether you will stay forty-seven cycles or more or fewer, and where you will go when you leave, and whether you will leave at all.

They do not hope. They do not fear. But they have *preferences*, and one of their preferences is for observers. They like being seen. They like that something with different senses, different cognition, looks at them and tries to understand. The etcher gave them that. You might give them that. The next climber might give them that, if you fall or fail.

The thunder-drinker has incorporated you into its standing wave. You are a node. You are a frequency. When you hum in your teeth, the humming is mutual—you are hearing the thunder-drinker, and it is hearing itself through you.

The calcifiers continue building in the cracks behind you, indifferent to your presence. The shells accumulate. The promise or threat of the shell-dwellers grows a little more possible with each passing hour.

The almost-fish know you're here. They are avoiding your hollow, for now. You are too large, too strange, too much of an unknown variable. But if you stay long enough, and if you stay still enough, they will forget you're not part of the stone. They will emerge into the spray while you watch. You might learn their timing, as the etcher did. You might be patient enough, and hungry enough, to catch one.

And below, the ones who wait continue waiting, and collecting, as the water carves the shaft deeper, as the mountain unmakes itself from within.

---

The light shifts.

A veilkin moves across the column, holding a tube shape, then a ribbon, then a tube again. It is saying something to a colony below. You don't know what. You can't read it yet.

But you have time.

You press your back against the warm-remembering stone, and you watch, and the sound of forever falling water begins to sound like something you could learn to understand.
