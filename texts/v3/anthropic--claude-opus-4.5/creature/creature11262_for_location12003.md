## The Choirkeepers

They were human once, or something near enough that the distinction has stopped mattering.

Now they are part of the song.

---

There are perhaps thirty of them, though counting is difficult—they move in and out of alcoves, disappear into the terraced levels, sometimes press themselves so completely into wall-tissue that only the rhythm of their breathing distinguishes them from the lung-flowers beside them. They have been here long enough that the green has entered them. Their skin carries a faint chlorophyll undertone, more pronounced in the creases of their palms and the hollows of their throats. Whether this is symbiosis or slow digestion, they do not say. They may not know.

**They do not speak.** Not because they cannot—their mouths still work, their tongues still move—but because speaking would mean competing with the sound, would mean adding their small voices to the overwhelming chorus, and that would be a kind of arrogance they shed long ago. Instead they communicate through touch, through gesture, through a system of body-positioning that has grown as organic as the chamber itself. A hand pressed flat against another's sternum means *I need you to come*. A chin tilted toward the upper dome means *the bell-moths are dying again*. Fingers interlaced and pulled slowly apart means something that has no translation, something that exists only here, something about the relationship between sound and time that outsiders couldn't understand even if it were explained.

---

### How They Live

Their days—if there are days—follow the water.

When the mist begins to gather, the Choirkeepers emerge from whatever tasks occupy them and move toward the central depression. They stand in a loose spiral formation, not touching but aware of each other's positions with a precision that suggests shared proprioception. As the rain falls inward from all directions, they open their mouths.

They are not singing. They are *shaping*.

The sound that fills this place is too vast to control, but it can be guided—slightly, subtly, through the introduction of resonant chambers that happen to be human throats. The Choirkeepers position themselves at acoustic nodes, open their mouths to specific widths, angle their bodies to catch particular frequencies. Together they form a living filter, and the overwhelming noise passes through them slightly changed. Slightly more bearable. Slightly less likely to align into those terrible harmonics that make the eyes water and the teeth ache.

This is their service. This is what they give.

In exchange, the chamber feeds them. Not through any organ or process they could point to—there are no fruits here, no edible structures—but through the air itself, through the green microorganisms that coat their tongues with the flavor of crushed stems. They breathe nutrition. They absorb it through their too-thin skin. They have not eaten solid food in years, most of them. They no longer miss it. They no longer remember what hunger felt like before it was satisfied continuously, involuntarily, by the very act of existing in this space.

---

### What They Were

Some of them remember. Most don't.

**The one they call Resonance** was a musician in a city that had concerts and audiences and the quaint notion that sound could be contained within walls. She came here following rumors of impossible acoustics, thinking she would study the lung-flowers and return with knowledge. That was eleven years ago, or twenty, or three. Time has stopped meaning what it used to mean. Now she spends her hours in the upper reaches, balanced on the bridge of braided vine-cable, adjusting her mouth-shape to counteract the pre-echoes that announce deaths before they happen. Sometimes she catches the future-sound of her own ending and harmonizes with it, curious what will happen when prediction and event finally meet.

**The one called Echo-of-Echo** has no memory of before. He was found by the others as a child—impossible, since no children are born here, since the biology of reproduction seems to have been traded away for the biology of belonging—but there he was, curled in an alcove, already green-tinged, already silent. He is the best of them at reading the procession. He knows which amber bodies will complete the circuit and which will falter, absorbed back into the floor-tissue before their journey ends. He has tried to save them. He has learned that saving is not what they want.

**The Old Listener**—not one of the furred creatures but something that predates the current Choirkeepers—sits always in the same spot, a raised platform near the central depression where the acoustics form a partial shelter. She is more wall than woman now, her lower body fused with the root-mass, her legs transformed into something that might be roots or might be the chamber's attempt at understanding human anatomy. She cannot move. She does not want to. She has become a permanent resonance point, a fixed node in the ever-shifting acoustic landscape, and the others position themselves relative to her, using her body as a reference tone.

---

### Their Relationship with the Fauna

The bell-moths land on them. This is permitted, even welcomed—the translucent wings brush against Choirkeeper skin with a texture like dry leaves, and the silence-where-heads-should-be presses close as if seeking something. The Choirkeepers believe the moths are listening to their bones. They believe the moths can hear the future-sounds that have lodged in human marrow, the pre-echoes that accumulate in anyone who stays here long enough. When a moth lands, it is reading tomorrow's noise from the resonance chambers of today's skeleton.

The listeners avoid them. This is expected—the furred, ear-heavy creatures hunt silence, and the Choirkeepers produce none. They are the opposite of silence. They are shaped by sound so completely that quiet cannot exist in their vicinity. Even their deaths, when deaths occur, are loud events—the chamber notices, the lung-flowers adjust their pitch, the entire space reorganizes its acoustics around the new absence. A Choirkeeper's dying creates a vacuum that the sound rushes to fill.

The procession ignores them, or treats them as geography. The amber bodies crawl over Choirkeeper feet without pausing, continue their eternal circuit without acknowledging the obstacles. Once, Echo-of-Echo tried to block their path entirely, to see what would happen. The procession split around him, reformed behind him, continued as if he had been a stone or a ridge or any other feature of the living floor. He understood then that he had become part of the infrastructure. He was no longer separate enough to constitute an interruption.

---

### The Dreams They Share

Sleep comes rarely and strangely. The sound does not permit unconsciousness in the usual sense—it enters too deeply, vibrates too persistently, keeps the auditory cortex activated even when exhaustion makes continuity impossible. So the Choirkeepers do not sleep so much as *phase*. They enter states of reduced awareness where the distinction between self and sound temporarily dissolves.

In these phases, they share something.

Not thoughts, exactly. Not visions. More like... harmonic memories. One Choirkeeper will phase into a recollection of rain that another experienced, will feel the mist condensing on skin that isn't theirs, will know the precise mouth-shape that someone else held during a particularly difficult alignment of pre-echoes. The sharing is imperfect, fragmented, subject to the same distortions that affect all information in this place. But it binds them. It makes them a choir in truth, not just in function.

Sometimes they phase into memories that belong to no one living. They experience the chamber's founding—though "founding" is wrong, nothing was founded, everything grew—from perspectives that feel human but can't be. They know the taste of air before the lung-flowers developed their current form. They remember a time when the sound was simpler, single-toned, almost bearable. These older memories suggest that the chamber has been inhabited continuously since it first achieved cathedral proportions. The Choirkeepers are only the latest in a succession that might stretch back centuries.

They do not know what happened to their predecessors. They do not ask.

---

### The Thing Beneath

They are aware of it. Of course they are aware of it—the shifts in floor-springiness, the changes in lung-flower pitch, the pale pressure against semi-translucent tissue. They have a relationship with it, though "relationship" implies mutuality and they are not certain mutuality is possible with something so large.

They feed it. Not intentionally, not through any ritual or offering, but through the simple fact of their presence. The sound they shape, the resonances they guide, the acoustic labor they perform—some of it filters down. The thing beneath grows more active when they work well, presses closer to the surface, seems almost to listen. On rare occasions, during particularly complex alignments when multiple Choirkeepers achieve perfect synchronization, the thing rises enough to become briefly visible: a mass of pale tissue, fibrous and vascular, shot through with channels that glow like the water-filled capillaries of the floor. It has no eyes. It has no features anyone could recognize. It is simply *there*, underneath, larger than the chamber it supposedly inhabits.

The Old Listener claims she has touched it. Her fused legs extend down into the root-mass, and she says—through gesture, through the slow language of pressure and position—that she can feel its movement directly now. She says it is dreaming. She says the entire chamber, the lung-flowers, the fauna, the Choirkeepers themselves, are elements of a dream the thing beneath has been having since before there were words for dreaming. She says they are not visitors or inhabitants but *imaginings*, held in existence by an attention that never quite focuses on them directly.

The others are not sure she is right. They are not sure she is wrong. They continue their work regardless.

---

### When Visitors Come

They arrive occasionally—scholars, explorers, the desperate, the lost. The Choirkeepers know they are coming before they arrive. The pre-echoes announce them: footsteps that haven't happened yet, breathing that belongs to futures still making up their minds.

The Choirkeepers do not welcome visitors. They do not turn them away. They continue their work—shaping, positioning, filtering the sound—and allow the newcomers to observe, to wander, to ask questions that will not be answered in words. Some visitors stay a few hours, overwhelmed by the noise, and flee back to wherever they came from. Some stay longer, growing accustomed, growing curious. A few never leave.

There is no initiation. No ritual of joining. The transformation happens or it doesn't, follows laws that may not be laws at all. A visitor wakes one morning—or what feels like morning—and realizes they have stopped wanting to speak. They feel the pull of the water-cycle, the urge to position themselves during the rain. Their skin has begun to take on the green.

The Choirkeepers make room. There is always room. They teach the gestures, the positions, the mouth-shapes that filter and guide. They share what can be shared of the phased memories. They do not explain what is happening because explanation requires words and words require competition with the sound and they have given that up, all of them, in exchange for belonging to something that has no use for language.

---

### What They Have Become

Not a cult. Not a community in any sense the outside world would recognize. Something more like a single organism distributed across multiple bodies, a choir that exists to be sung through rather than to sing.

They do not miss the lives they left. They remember them, some of them, but memory here is a physical phenomenon—it resonates, it echoes, it layers into the present until past and future become equally uncertain. What they remember feels no more real than what they imagine, and both feel less real than the sound that surrounds them always, the sound that has become the medium of their existence, the sound that will continue long after the last of them has been absorbed into the walls.

They are happy. They think they are happy. The concept has changed shape inside them, flattened by the pressure of constant noise into something unrecognizable, but if happiness means the absence of wanting to be elsewhere, then yes. They are happy.

The lung-flowers sing. The bell-moths ride the pressure waves. The procession crawls its endless circuit. The thing beneath dreams. The Choirkeepers open their mouths and shape what cannot be controlled, guide what cannot be directed, serve a purpose that may only exist because they have decided it should.

They will be here when the chamber finally finishes growing, if growing is something that can finish.

They will be here when the song finally reveals what it has been singing toward, if singing can have a toward.

They will be here.

They are here.
