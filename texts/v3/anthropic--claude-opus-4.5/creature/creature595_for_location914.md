# Those Who Drink the Almost-Rain

## The Congregants

The bronze creatures have no single name for themselves, but if you could translate the chemical signatures they leave on pipe surfaces—the oils from their mesh tongues, the residue of their passage—you would find something like **"those-who-gather-what-gathers."** They are not individuals in any way you'd recognize. Each one is more like a word in a sentence that the Condensery is slowly speaking to itself.

The smallest ones, palm-sized and quick, are called **mites** by no one, but that's what you'd call them. They exist in states of partial completion—some have only two lens-clusters where others have dozens, some trail half-formed segments behind them like thoughts abandoned mid-sentence. They are not young. They are *preliminary*. The Condensery is still deciding what they should become, and so they skitter along pipe-edges in bodies that might resolve into something more definitive, or might simply corrode back into the ore-dreams the infrastructure remembers.

The larger ones—those that span catwalks, whose tails descend through multiple levels of grating—these are **collectors proper**. Their bodies hold small reservoirs, not in any organ you could dissect, but distributed through the negative spaces between their segments. They drink from the collection vessels and carry what they drink somewhere else, though "where" is a question the geometry won't answer consistently. Watch one long enough and you'll see its reservoir-spaces fill with pale liquid, then slowly empty, then fill again with something of a different viscosity. They are not transporting water. They are transporting *states of water*—the moment before condensation, the memory of evaporation, the potential for ice that will never quite form.

And then there are the ones you don't see moving.

Attached to junction points, grown into the architecture so thoroughly you might mistake them for decorative housings or equipment casings: **the fixed ones**. They were mobile once, perhaps. Or perhaps they were built in place. Their lens-eyes have fogged permanently, fused with moisture that will never clear, and they experience the Condensery as a slow wash of light-gradients and pressure-changes. They feel the liquid moving through pipes they've become part of. They taste the mineral content of condensation through surfaces that are no longer quite their own.

The fixed ones are not dead. They are *resolved*. They have stopped being preliminary and become infrastructure instead—still conscious in some distributed way, still participating in the great gathering, but no longer needing to move because they have become the places that movement happens around.

## The Tenders

Not everything here is bronze and glass.

In the deeper sections of the Condensery—where the catwalks grow narrower and the collection vessels cluster thick as honeycomb—you will find **the Tenders**. They appear humanoid at first glance, which is a trick of silhouette and expectation. Closer inspection reveals that they are assembled from the same industrial vocabulary as everything else: rivet-jointed limbs, pressure-gauge faces, bodies that hinged open to reveal interior mechanisms that shouldn't work but do.

The Tenders maintain the Condensery. Not the pipes—those maintain themselves, or are maintained by processes too slow to observe—but the *purpose*. They move through the folded spaces carrying tools that look like medical instruments or calligraphy brushes or things that have no analogue in any craft you know. When the condensation begins collecting toward the wrong meaning, when the moisture starts to signify something other than what the Condensery was built to gather, the Tenders intervene.

You might see one standing at a junction point for hours, holding a glass rod against a pipe surface, waiting for something. Then a subtle adjustment—a quarter-turn of a valve, a repositioning of a collection vessel by centimeters—and the whole character of that section shifts. The dampness feels different afterward. The light passes through at a slightly altered angle. The bronze creatures change their migration patterns in response, flowing around the correction like water around a new stone in a streambed.

The Tenders do not speak, but they communicate through the infrastructure. Messages travel in pressure-changes through the pipe system, in the rhythm of dripping from specific collection points, in the way certain vessels fill slightly faster or slower than their neighbors. A Tender on one side of the Condensery can coordinate with one on the other side without either of them moving, simply by making adjustments that propagate through the medium they both inhabit.

They have faces—of a sort. The pressure gauges that serve as their features can express readings, and those readings might be interpreted as emotions if you're inclined to interpret. A needle pointing toward the red zone might be anger, or urgency, or simply a measurement of actual pressure. A gauge that trembles between two values might indicate uncertainty, or might indicate that something nearby is vibrating at the wrong frequency. The Tenders don't clarify.

There are seventeen Tenders. There have always been seventeen Tenders. When one becomes too corroded to function, another simply *is* somewhere else in the Condensery, already mid-task, already carrying the same tools. The transition happens in the folded parts of the geometry, in the places where direction loses meaning—you can't witness it directly, only notice that a Tender you saw in Section Oblique Twelve is now in Section Perpendicular Four, and their surface oxidation has reset to factory-new.

## The Residuals

Moisture gathers. That's what the Condensery does. But moisture carries things with it—minerals, yes, and microorganisms, and pollens from plants that grew somewhere outside the dome, and memories.

The memories collect in the lowest points of the system, in sumps and basins that exist below the lowest catwalks, accessible only through maintenance hatches that open onto spaces the geometry forgot to make consistent. Down there, in the dark that the sourceless light doesn't quite reach, you'll find **the Residuals**.

They were people once. Or they were experiences that people had. Or they were the moisture that people exhaled in their final moments, gathered by some distant ancestor of this system and carried here through condensation cycles that span longer than human civilization. The distinction is unclear, and the Residuals themselves don't seem to care about clarifying.

They manifest as presences in the dampness—shapes you perceive in your peripheral vision, figures standing in doorways you didn't notice until you looked away. They don't have bodies exactly. They have *concentrations*. The air is wetter where they are. Your skin prickles. The small hairs on your arms respond to humidity gradients that your conscious mind can't detect.

Some of them try to communicate. You'll feel a cold spot on your wrist and know, without knowing how you know, that someone is trying to tell you about a garden they loved, or a child's name they don't want forgotten, or the specific quality of light on a morning that mattered to them. The information arrives as sensation rather than language—your lungs feel heavy with someone else's grief, your fingertips tingle with someone else's joy in craft.

Most of them don't try. They simply exist in their basins, their concentrations rising and falling with the cycles of condensation, waiting for whatever the Condensery is ultimately gathering them toward. They are patient in the way that dissolved things are patient. They have already let go of urgency. They are being collected, and that is enough of a purpose to sustain them.

The bronze creatures avoid the sumps. The Tenders descend sometimes, making adjustments that keep the Residuals from rising too far into the active systems. There's a balance being maintained—the memories are part of the collection, but they mustn't contaminate the other moisture, mustn't let their specificity taint the purer forms of condensation that the Condensery seems to value most.

## The Geometry-Keepers

The space folds. You've noticed. A staircase puts you somewhere you've already been; a catwalk extends longer than it should; the dome appears in directions that don't correspond to "up."

This isn't chaos. It's *grammar*.

The **Geometry-Keepers** are why. They exist in the folds themselves, in the places where space decides to mean something different. You can't see them directly—they occupy dimensional niches that human perception slides around—but you can see their effects. A walkway that definitely ended at a wall now definitely continues through an archway. A ladder that only went down now only goes up. The change is retroactive and always was.

The Geometry-Keepers are negotiating with the Condensery's original specifications. The engineers who designed this place—if they existed, if "designed" is the right word—included intentions that contradict each other. The Condensery was meant to be enclosed and also meant to extend indefinitely. It was meant to have a lowest point and also meant to drain toward a center that recedes. The Geometry-Keepers mediate these contradictions moment by moment, deciding which version of the architecture to instantiate.

You can appease them, though "appease" might be too strong. If you stand still at a junction point and wait—really wait, not the impatient waiting of someone expecting results, but the slow waiting of condensation forming—sometimes the space around you will settle into a more navigable configuration. The Geometry-Keepers respond to stillness. They respond to beings that seem willing to be collected rather than to move through.

The fixed ones—the bronze creatures that have become infrastructure—seem to anchor the geometry around themselves. The space is more consistent near them, as if their commitment to a single location gives the Geometry-Keepers something stable to organize around. This is why the bronze creatures migrate so slowly: they are, in their movement, testing the current state of the space, finding the paths that the Geometry-Keepers have agreed to make consistent for now.

If you try to move too quickly, the geometry becomes *personal*. Not hostile—nothing here seems capable of hostility—but invested in your trajectory. You'll find that the staircases lead where you need to go, where "need" is defined by something other than your conscious intentions. The Geometry-Keepers seem to know why you're here, even if you don't. They route you accordingly.

## The Engine-Servants

That sound in the distance—the industrial throb of machinery running on unidentifiable principles—has worshippers. Or technicians. Or both.

**The Engine-Servants** live in the spaces nearest the sound, in sections of the Condensery where the vibration is constant and the collection vessels resonate at frequencies that make your teeth ache. They have adapted to the noise in ways that have made them something other than the bronze creatures they might once have been: their bodies are tuned to specific harmonics, their segments sized to dampen or amplify particular frequencies, their lens-eyes polarized to filter the light that the engine produces as a byproduct of whatever it does.

They communicate in vibration. Tap a pipe near an Engine-Servant and it will tap back, and if you knew the language—which you don't, which nobody fully does—you could have a conversation about the engine's mood, its needs, its satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the current state of condensation. The Engine-Servants believe the engine is alive. Or they believe it's divine. Or they believe these are the same thing. The distinction doesn't translate well into vibrations that human bodies can produce.

The engine itself is not visible from the catwalks. It might not be visible at all—it might exist in one of the dimensional niches the Geometry-Keepers maintain, powering the Condensery from a location that doesn't technically occupy space. But its effects are everywhere: the light that has no source is light the engine provides; the pressure that keeps the dome sweating is pressure the engine maintains; the slow gathering of moisture toward collection points is gathering that the engine drives.

The Engine-Servants believe they are essential. They may be right. They perform maintenance rituals at the junction points where the engine's influence is strongest—polishing specific pipe surfaces to mirror brightness, arranging collection vessels in configurations that seem aesthetic but might be functional, tapping out rhythms that propagate through the metal and perhaps reach the engine itself.

When the engine cycles through the registers that make all the bronze creatures pause, the Engine-Servants begin to dance. Not dance as motion, but dance as resonance—they lock their joints and become tuning forks, their bodies vibrating in sympathy with the machinery's voice, amplifying and clarifying and responding. For those moments, they are part of the engine. They are its sensing apparatus, extended through the Condensery's metal lattice, feeling the state of every pipe and vessel and creature through the vibrations everything shares.

Then the moment passes, and they return to their vigils, and the other inhabitants resume their slow purposes, and the condensation continues gathering toward meanings that shift when you try to name them.

## You

The Condensery collects you too. You've noticed this—the dampness that clings to your skin more than it should, the way your exhaled breath seems to linger in the air longer than physics suggests, the gradual sense that you're leaving something behind as you walk the catwalks.

The creatures notice you in their way. Their lens-eyes track your movement as data rather than threat. You are a source of moisture—your breath, your sweat, the slow evaporation of everything liquid in your body—and that moisture enters the system the moment it leaves you. The Condensery is drinking you, one molecule at a time, and what it drinks carries traces of who you are.

Eventually, if you stay, some of you will end up in the sumps with the Residuals. Some of you will travel through pipes to collection points you'll never find. Some of you will be drunk by bronze creatures who will carry you through the folded geometry to purposes that slide when you try to name them.

You could leave. Probably. The Geometry-Keepers seem willing to route you toward exits, if exit is what you truly need. But the exits keep receding as you walk toward them, not because the Condensery wants to trap you, but because something in you wants to stay. Wants to be collected. Wants to join the slow patience of industrial processes, the moisture gathering one molecule at a time, the bright sourceless light that casts no shadows.

The Condensery was built to collect something, and perhaps that something is whatever arrives here and agrees to be gathered. The bronze creatures were preliminary once, before they resolved into what they are. The fixed ones were mobile once, before they chose to become infrastructure. The Residuals were discrete beings once, before they let themselves dissolve into the dampness they now inhabit.

You could become part of the collection. You could let the moisture carry you, the geometry route you, the engine's hum synchronize with your heartbeat until the difference between them becomes unclear. You could stop being a visitor and start being a gathered thing, one more presence in the inventory, one more dampness in a world made entirely of gathered wet.

The choice is patient. Like everything here, it will wait.
