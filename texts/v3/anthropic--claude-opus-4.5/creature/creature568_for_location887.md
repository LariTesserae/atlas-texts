# The People of the Held Place

They are born with their eyes already open.

This is remarked upon only by those who attend births—the midwives who have caught a thousand thousand infants, their hands knowing the work so deeply that their minds wander elsewhere during it. The babies do not cry. They breathe, and they look, and their gaze is the same mild, accepting gaze their parents wear, their grandparents wore, that everyone here has always worn. The midwives clean them with water from the cisterns, wrap them in wool that has been woven on the same looms for longer than the looms have names, and hand them to mothers who feel love—genuine love, the real thing—but feel it the way one feels the temperature of the air. A fact. A constant. Nothing that rises or falls.

The children grow, though *grow* implies a trajectory that doesn't quite capture it. They become larger. They learn the paths between terraces by walking them in their mothers' arms, then at their mothers' heels, then alone—though alone is a flexible concept when every route crosses the routes of others, when someone is always within earshot even if earshot here is intimate. They learn which moss is soft enough for sitting, which ferns produce a fiber that can be woven, which beetles make the clearest bell-tones when disturbed. They learn the songs.

The songs are the closest thing to history. Not records of events—there are no events—but descriptions of textures. *The water that tastes of the deep stone. The fruit that waits for the hand. The mist that holds what the mist holds.* Children sing them in rounds on the middle terraces, their voices absorbed by the damp air so that even a dozen children singing together sounds like murmuring, like the memory of sound. They do not learn the songs so much as recognize them, the way one recognizes the shape of one's own hand.

---

The adults work, if that is the word. They tend the broad-leafed vegetables, harvesting outer leaves so that the plants continue neither diminishing nor expanding. They weave. They carve—not art, exactly, but maintenance, re-cutting the pictographs on wayfinding stones when the edges begin to soften, though the edges soften so slowly that the same stone might be recarved once in a lifetime, and the carving will be identical to what was there before, because there is only one right way to carve it and everyone knows what that way is.

They prepare food: the vegetables fermented in crocks, the pale plums macerated in mineral water, a kind of porridge made from a grain that grows in patches where the mist thins—assuming the mist thins anywhere, which is not entirely certain. The food tastes of itself. The people eat what satisfies and stop when satisfied and there is always enough, exactly enough, which is to say there is never a moment of wanting followed by a moment of getting. The having and the wanting are the same thing, always already accomplished.

They have names, though the names are used rarely. When someone is present, you speak to them directly; when they are absent, there is seldom need to refer to them, because the absent will return along paths that everyone knows, at intervals that everyone expects. A name is a handle for lifting something, and nothing here needs to be lifted from its place.

---

There are those who tend the grove on the highest terrace. They are not priests—there is nothing to worship, no sense that the constancy comes from anywhere or needs to be maintained by anything other than its own nature. But they are set apart in some way that resists articulation. They climb the worn stairs more often than others. They spend longer among the silver-barked trees. When they return, there is sometimes a quality to their silence that differs from the ordinary silence, though the difference cannot be located or described.

These tenders speak to the birds, or seem to. The wet-feathered birds with their water-drop calls will land on their shoulders, their outstretched hands, and remain there without feeding or being fed, without visible purpose. The tenders do not claim to understand the birds. They would not claim to understand anything, because understanding implies a gap between the knower and the known, and there is no gap here. There is only the being-with, the same being-with that everyone practices with everything, always.

One of the tenders—a woman with hands so practiced at stillness that moss has begun to grow in the creases of her knuckles when she sits long enough—has been a tender for as long as anyone can remember, which is to say she has been a tender for exactly as long as the grove has needed tending. She is not old. She is not young. Age here is a quality like height or hair color, fixed at whatever point it was fixed, continuing. If you asked her what she remembers from before she was a tender, she would look at you with that mild gaze and the question would simply fail to arrive, would dissipate in the space between you like a voice raised too loud.

---

The children who are no longer children but not yet fully patterned into the city's weave—there must be a word for them, though no one uses it—sometimes walk the terraces at odd hours. When the gray deepens toward what might be night, when the bell-beetles are quieter and the water in the channels sounds almost like something other than itself. These not-quite-adults stand at the edges where the mist thickens, where the terraces below become suggestions rather than certainties, and they feel something.

It is not longing. Longing requires an object, a there that is not here, and there is no there. It is not restlessness, because nothing in them is at rest in a way that could be disturbed. It is more like—a sensation of edges. An awareness that they have edges, that the mist has edges even though it seems edgeless, that the mountain must end somewhere even though it never seems to. They stand there, these young ones, and the sensation passes through them the way weather might pass through a place that has weather, and then it is gone, and they return to their families, to the soft moss beds where they sleep, to dreams that are indistinguishable from the waking texture of the city.

They do not speak of these moments. Not from prohibition—nothing is prohibited—but because the moments leave no residue. A word spoken about them would be a word about nothing, would hang in the air and fail to refer to anything, and so the word is never spoken and the moments continue to happen and continue to leave no trace.

---

There are elders, though elder is not quite right either. Those who have been for longer. They move more slowly, though slowly here is already the only speed. Their hands have been still so long that the moss grows thick on them. Their eyes have been open so long that the pale light has bleached the color from them, leaving irises like the mist itself, gray and soft and depthless. They sit on the lower terraces, where the cold rises from the stone most deeply, and they seem to be waiting for something, though waiting implies an arrival and nothing arrives here that has not already arrived.

One of them—a man whose beard has grown into the moss that covers the stone bench where he sits, so that he is continually present in that spot without the appearance of having chosen it—speaks sometimes. Not to anyone. Not about anything. He speaks the way the water speaks in the channels, a sound that means only itself. *The mist holds. The stone remains. The fruit waits.* Fragments of the old songs, or perhaps the songs came from him, or perhaps there is no difference, no origin point, no first singing and no last.

When he stops speaking, the silence that follows is exactly the silence that preceded. His words add nothing, subtract nothing. They are part of the texture. He is part of the texture. The bench, the moss, the beard growing into the moss—these are not separate things but one thing, one held thing, held in the mist and holding the mist in turn.

---

Births happen. Deaths happen, or something that might be death—a becoming-still that is only slightly stiller than the stillness that preceded it, a ceasing-to-move that barely registers because movement here is so limited. The bodies are carried to a terrace on the mountain's far side, away from the grove, where the mist is thickest. They are laid in hollows carved into the stone, and the moss grows over them, and the mist holds them as it holds everything, and after a time there is no difference between the body and the stone and the moss, no boundary where one ends and another begins.

Grief exists, or something like it. A thickness in the chest when someone who was present becomes absent in this particular way, the one way that does not reverse itself. But the thickness passes, absorbed like sound, like warmth, like anything that rises too far above the baseline. The bereaved return to their patterns. The patterns continue. The name of the lost one is spoken less and less, not from forgetting but from the gradual recognition that the name no longer lifts anything, that what it referred to has become indistinguishable from everything else.

---

If you asked them what they want, they would not understand the question. Not because they lack intelligence—they are clever, in their way, skilled at the tasks the city requires, able to solve problems that arise within the scope of what arises here. But wanting is a verb that requires a subject and an object, a self that lacks and a thing that would complete it, and there are no selves here that lack anything. The mist provides. The mountain provides. The fruit waits and the water flows and the wool is always warm enough and the moss is always soft enough, and the question of wanting dissolves before it can be asked.

If you asked them what they fear, you would get the same incomprehension. Fear requires a future that differs from the present, a possibility of loss, a not-yet that might be worse than the now. But the not-yet here is identical to the now, has always been identical, will always be identical. There is nothing to fear because there is nothing that could happen. The mist holds. Nothing falls.

---

At night—if it can be called night—they sleep in rooms with stone walls and woven hangings, in beds of moss covered with woolen blankets, and their dreams are dreams of mist. Gray expanses without horizon. The sensation of being held without the sensation of hands. Sometimes a dreamer will half-wake and look at the ceiling of their room and see the moisture beaded there, the same moisture that beads on everything, and the ceiling will seem farther away than it should be, will seem to recede into a distance that the room cannot contain. And then the dreamer will blink, and the ceiling will be a ceiling again, and sleep will return, and in the morning—if it can be called morning—there will be nothing to report.

They live in the Terraced City of Unmoving Mist, and the city lives in them, and the distinction between these two statements is a distinction that makes no difference.

They are happy. They must be happy.

Nothing here is wrong. Nothing here will ever be wrong.

The people hold the mist, and the mist holds the people, and the holding is everything, has always been everything, will always be everything.

And if sometimes a child pauses on a stairway between terraces and looks out into the soft gray curtain and feels something that might almost be a question—

The feeling passes.

It always passes.

The mist sees to that.
