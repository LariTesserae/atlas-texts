# The Veth

They are born small and stay small—a compact people, narrow-shouldered and dense-boned, as if generations of climbing and clinging had compressed them into more efficient forms. Their skin carries undertones of green and gray, not from pigment but from what lives on them: a fine film of symbiotic algae that colonizes every Veth from infancy, fed by sweat and humidity, photosynthesizing weakly in the rare light that filters through canopy gaps. It does not nourish them significantly. But it marks them. A Veth who has been too long in the dark interior factories grows pale and sickly-looking; a Veth who works the upper platforms, where sunflecks dance, develops a verdant flush across the cheeks and forearms that their poets call *evidence of having been touched*.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. The fingers are long, the joints numerous—not extra knuckles, exactly, but a flexibility that suggests extra knuckles, a capacity to wrap and grip that makes their gestures look like small animals moving. Their palms are heavily calloused from birth, the skin there so thick it is almostite own leather, and the calluses are not smooth but ridged in patterns that vary by family line. A Veth child learns to read these ridges the way lowland children learn letters. A Veth greeting involves pressing palms together and feeling the texture, the subtle identification of lineage and labor encoded in the hand itself.

They smell of resin and char. There is no escaping it. The factories burn constantly, the braziers burn constantly, the sap-lamps burn constantly, and every Veth carries the smoke in their hair and clothes and the very oils of their skin. They do not notice it themselves. Visitors from the lowlands—rare, disoriented, struggling to breathe in the thin air—report that entering Veth Ascendant is like walking into a censer, like being wrapped in incense so thick it becomes weather. The Veth find this amusing. They describe the lowlands as smelling of *nothing*, which they consider far more disturbing.

---

Their children are raised collectively, which is to say, they are raised by the city itself.

A newborn Veth spends its first year strapped to its birth-parent's chest, learning the rhythms of labor before it can walk. The constant noise becomes foundational; Veth children raised in unusual quiet develop anxieties that persist into adulthood, a sense that something essential is missing. By age two, they are placed in the *nursery-platforms*—broad, netted spaces suspended in the mid-canopy, staffed by elders too old for factory work but still sharp-eyed, still quick-handed. Here the children learn climbing before walking, grip before standing. They learn the shape of the city with their bodies.

By age six, they are apprenticed to a trade, but "apprenticed" understates the integration. A child assigned to the shredder-works does not merely learn to operate the shredders; they learn to hear them, to distinguish healthy mechanical rhythm from the subtle hiccup that precedes breakdown. A child assigned to the sap-collectors learns to taste the sap and detect contamination, learns to smell when a collection tube has grown a fungal infection that must be burned out before it spreads. The Veth do not believe in education as separate from labor. They believe in *learning by doing*, a phrase they find so obvious they have no word for it—it is simply how knowledge exists.

The elders say the children are born knowing the city. This is metaphor but also observation. Some deep adaptation has occurred. Veth infants orient toward the constant factory-thrum the way lowland infants orient toward heartbeats. They sleep better when the machinery runs loud. They grow distressed during the rare maintenance-silences, when the generators are down for repair and the city holds its breath.

---

They pray, but not to gods.

The Veth religion—if it can be called that—centers on the Pillar Mothers. Not worship, exactly. Something closer to acknowledgment, to the maintenance of a relationship. The Pillar Mothers are not conscious in any way the Veth can detect, but they are responsive. They grow toward noise and warmth. They extend roots toward ground that has been enriched by factory runoff. They shed branches that shade platforms too aggressively, as if recognizing (without recognition) that the platforms need light. The Veth understand this as a kind of language, immensely slow, written in growth and response.

Their priests—called *listeners*—spend their lives pressed against the Pillar Mothers' bark, learning the grain, feeling the vibrations of internal sap-flow, trying to detect patterns. Most detect nothing. But occasionally, a listener will announce that a branch is preparing to fall, or that a root-system is reaching toward a new water source, and they will be right. These predictions are treated as revelation. The listener who makes them is elevated, given a platform close to the trunk they read, expected to continue reading until they die.

The Veth do not ask the Pillar Mothers for anything. They do not expect intervention. They expect only to be tolerated, to be incorporated, to be part of the growth-pattern the way the symbiotic fungi are part of it. Their highest aspiration is to become, eventually, indistinguishable from the city-forest itself. When a Veth dies, their body is fed to the digesters that fuel the factories; the ash is spread on the root-zones; the components that were once a person enter the Pillar Mothers and become, in some diluted sense, wood. The Veth do not mourn. They describe death as *joining*.

---

The Wound changes them.

Not everyone. Not predictably. But a Veth who spends too long looking at the glassy center, or who works the platforms closest to the edge, or who simply draws the wrong kind of attention from whatever seeps outward—they change. The Veth call it *thinning*. The affected person does not disappear, but they become less specific. Their features blur slightly, not to outside eyes but to memory; people who have known them for years find themselves unable to describe them precisely. Their voice loses distinctiveness. Their palm-ridges, when pressed in greeting, feel like everyone's palm-ridges and no one's.

The thinned are not shunned. They are treated with a gentle sadness, a sense that something has been lost that can never be recovered. They continue to work, to live, to eat and speak and participate. But they participate less somehow. Their opinions carry less weight. Their presence registers less. They are there but diminishing, a process that can take decades, and that ends—everyone knows—in their eventual failure to be present at all. They do not die. They simply stop occurring.

Some Veth seek this out. There is a small community, a handful of platforms closest to the Wound, where those who feel they have already lived enough go to accelerate their thinning. They call themselves *the already-gone*, and they spend their days staring at the impossible center, drinking sap that has been contaminated by proximity, inviting the Seeping to take them. The rest of the city leaves them be. It is understood as a valid choice—perhaps the most Veth choice of all, the ultimate joining, not with the Pillar Mothers but with whatever potential the Wound represents.

---

The engineers are their aristocracy.

Not by blood or by wealth—the Veth have no currency, only labor-credits tracked by the factory councils—but by expertise. The engineers are those who understand the machines deeply enough to repair them, to modify them, to build new ones. They are rare. Mechanical aptitude seems to skip generations, to appear unpredictably in children born to non-engineers. When it appears, it is cultivated ferociously. A child who shows engineering talent is removed from the communal nurseries and placed in a special apprenticeship, shadowing the existing engineers, learning not just the how but the why.

The engineers carry the city's survival in their hands. If the factories fail, the anchoring noise fails. If the anchoring noise fails, the Seeping spreads. Everyone knows this. The engineers are given the best platforms, the richest food, the most potent sap-medicines. They are expected, in return, to work until they cannot work, to train successors obsessively, to document everything in the city's great archives—books of hand-pressed paper, pages covered in diagrams and measurements, stored in a fireproofed vault at the city's heart.

The current Chief Engineer is a woman named Tessaly Grindstone—a title, not a birth-name; the Veth abandon birth-names at adulthood and take names that describe their function. She is old, her algae-film faded to a pale gray-green, her hands so calloused the ridges have worn smooth. She has been Chief Engineer for forty years and has trained eleven successors, eight of whom still live. She sleeps in the generator-room, on a cot beside the main boiler, and she is said to hear the machine's health the way the listeners hear the Pillar Mothers. When she dies, there will be a competition to replace her—a practical test, a demonstration of aptitude—and whoever wins will take the name Grindstone and continue.

---

They do not marry. They *braid*.

Braiding is the Veth partnership-form, and it is explicitly temporary. Two or three or sometimes four Veth will agree to share a platform, to pool their labor-credits, to raise any resulting children together. The commitment lasts until someone wants to leave, and then it ends without ceremony. There is no shame in ending a braid. There is no particular glory in maintaining one. The longest-running braid in current Veth history is sixty-three years old, a triad of elderly leaf-fall processors who have simply never found reason to separate, but this is considered a curiosity, not an ideal.

Children are considered to belong to all their braid-parents equally, and when braids dissolve, children are divided by aptitude and preference rather than biology. A child who shows affinity for one parent's trade goes with that parent. A child who prefers another platform or another branch of the city goes there. The Veth consider the lowland practice of nuclear families, when they hear of it, to be bizarre and stifling—how can a child find their best function if they are bound to only one or two adults?

Sex is uncomplicated. The Veth engage in it casually, frequently, without particular attachment to partnership. The algae-film on their skin produces a faint phosphorescence during arousal—a biological signal that cannot be hidden, which means that Veth desire is always public, always visible. This has led to a culture of frank acceptance. Desire is acknowledged and acted upon or not acted upon without drama. The Veth find the lowland rituals of courtship and coyness genuinely incomprehensible, like rituals for pretending you don't need to eat.

---

The Factory Council governs, but governs is too strong a word.

There are twelve council members, one representing each of the major industries: leaf-processing, sap-collection, fiber-weaving, paper-making, fuel-production, machine-maintenance, food-preparation, medicine, the sap-channel fisheries, the canopy-gardens, the anchor-points, and—always—one representative for the listeners, the priests of the Pillar Mothers. They meet weekly in a chamber hung with bells that ring constantly, a double anchor-point against the Seeping, and they make decisions by consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, the decision is deferred. If it is deferred too many times, the matter is dropped.

This works because the Veth are, by long adaptation, a people who avoid conflict. They have to be. In a city built on branches, where every platform depends on every other platform, where the failure of one factory threatens the anchoring-sound that protects everyone, no one can afford feuds. Disputes are resolved by arbitration. Crimes—what few occur—are punished by temporary exile to the lower platforms, where the Seeping is stronger and the reality-failures more frequent. Most criminals return chastened, eager to reintegrate. Some do not return at all.

---

They have a saying: *The Pillar Mothers do not wait*.

It means many things. It means that growth continues regardless of your schedule, that leaf-fall comes whether you are ready or not, that the factories must run because the alternative is dissolution. It means that the dead do not linger, that grief is not afforded time, that the digesters process bodies within hours of death because the body is fuel and the city is hungry. It means that children must work and elders must retire and no one gets to pause, to step back, to reflect—there is only the constant productive motion, the assertion of reality through labor.

But it also means something gentler. It means that the Pillar Mothers do not wait *for you to be worthy*. They do not wait for you to prove yourself. They grow around you, incorporate you, make you part of the pattern regardless of your virtues or flaws. You do not have to earn your place in Veth Ascendant. You have your place by being born. Your function will become clear. Your labor will contribute. Your ash will feed the roots.

The Veth find this comforting.

They work their industries in the thin cold air, small green-tinged people with flexible hands and smoke-saturated skin. They tend the anchor-fires and process the endless leaf-fall. They raise their children in the city's collective grip and dissolve their partnerships without rancor and grow old listening to machinery or bark or both. They watch the Wound pulse with potential at the city's heart and they do not fear it, exactly—they have lived beside it too long for fear—but they respect it, account for it, build their rituals around it.

And above them and around them and beneath them, the Pillar Mothers grow another ring, extend another branch, drink another drop of condensed cloud-water, and do not wait.
