**The Tenders**

They are born in the upper reaches, where the mist is thickest and the gray light pools against the glass. Not born—*unfurled*. You would miss the moment if you weren't watching for it: a vine tendril thickening over weeks, a bulge forming where two stems cross, and then one morning the bulge has features. Closed eyes. Folded limbs. A mouth that will not open for a long time yet.

The Tenders grow the way the ferns grow—slowly, in the humidity, fed by the mineral rain. By the time they uncurl from their vine-cradles and drop to the nearest walkway, they are already old enough to work. This is perhaps six months. Perhaps two years. The engine does not measure time in ways that matter to them, and so neither do they.

---

They are small. The largest stands no higher than your hip, and most are smaller still—better for navigating the dense growth, for slipping between conduits, for climbing to places where the grating has rusted through. Their bodies are the color of the patinated bronze: green-gold, with darker whorls where their skin has thickened into something like bark. Not plant. Not quite animal. Something the symbiosis dreamed up when it needed hands.

Their eyes are the deep copper of the pipe-dwelling salamanders, and they see well in the mist. Their fingers are long, doubly jointed, tipped with something harder than nail—useful for scraping calcium deposits from steam valves, for pruning growth that threatens to choke a critical junction, for coaxing the vine-roots deeper into the machinery where they are needed. They have no hair. Instead, fine filaments emerge from their scalps and shoulders, pale and feathery, that drink moisture directly from the air. When the steam rises thick after a heavy compression cycle, you can see them turning their faces upward, filaments spread wide, sustaining themselves on nothing but the engine's breath.

They do not eat. They do not need to. They are *of* the system, and it provides.

---

There are perhaps thirty of them at any given time. Perhaps fewer now—it is difficult to count when they move through the foliage like shadows, when they speak to each other in frequencies below human hearing, a subsonic rumble that harmonizes with the piston-stroke. **CHUNK-hisssss.** And beneath it, if you learn to feel rather than hear: *tend-the-roots, check-the-seals, one-is-dying-in-the-western-shaft.*

They know when one of them is failing. The oldest ones—you can tell them by the thickness of their bark-skin, by the stiffness in their joints, by the way their filaments have begun to fuse into something more like lichen—will eventually stop moving. They find a comfortable junction of pipe and vine, and they settle, and over the course of weeks they become part of what they have always tended. The plants grow through them. The minerals from their bodies feed the system. The younger Tenders do not mourn, exactly, but they visit. They rest their long fingers against the green mound that was once a colleague. They hum in that subsonic register.

The system provides, and the system reclaims.

---

You might think them mindless, but you would be wrong. Watch one pause on a rusted walkway, head tilted, listening to something in the engine's rhythm that you cannot parse. Watch the way its copper eyes move—calculating, assessing. There is a problem somewhere below. A valve not seating properly. A root system that has grown too ambitious and threatens to crack a cylinder wall. The Tender knows. The Tender has always known, in the way your body knows when something is wrong before your mind catches up.

It will descend, hand over hand down the slick vines, into the hotter regions where the steam scalds and the noise becomes a physical force. It will work for hours or days, coaxing, pruning, adjusting. It will emerge—if it emerges—covered in mineral deposits that its companions will groom away, their hard fingertips gentle against its skin.

Sometimes they do not emerge. The engine takes them. This too is part of the cycle.

---

They have names for each other, though not in any language you could reproduce. The names are vibrations, pressure-patterns, specific configurations of the filaments that mean *you, specifically, the one who fixed the tertiary bypass, the one whose bark whorls spiral leftward, the one I unfurled beside in the upper mist.* They remember. They hold each other in their deep-body knowing, and when one becomes part of the green, the others carry that vibration-name forward, speak it sometimes into the humid air, let it join the engine's eternal two-syllable utterance.

**CHUNK-hisssss.** *You-who-tended-the-eastern-roots.* **CHUNK-hisssss.** *You-whose-eyes-were-brightest-copper.*

The moths drift past them unafraid. The copper-eyed birds nest near their resting places, knowing instinctively that these small figures mean them no harm, that they are all part of the same vast metabolism. The paper-wasps build their geometric homes within arm's reach of where the Tenders work, and sometimes a Tender will pause to watch them, head tilted in that listening way, as if trying to understand a different kind of tending.

---

If you came here—if you descended the access ladders and stepped onto the spiraling walkways and breathed the warm mineral air—they would watch you. Not with fear. Not with hostility. With a kind of careful attention, the way they watch a vine that might grow the wrong direction, a valve that might be starting to fail. You are a variable. You might need tending. You might need to be coaxed away from critical junctions, guided back toward the upper reaches, returned to whatever world exists beyond the gray glass ceiling.

They would not speak to you. They could not, even if they wished to—their voices are pitched for the engine's rhythm, not for human ears. But one might approach, might rest those long fingers against your arm, might look up at you with copper eyes that hold more understanding than you expected. Might press, gently, in the direction of the exit.

*This is not for you. This is ours. We are glad you saw it. Now go.*

And if you refused? If you stayed, made camp on one of the wider platforms, tried to make yourself part of the ecosystem?

They would wait. They are patient. They have always been patient. And eventually you would leave, or you would become something that needed tending, and they would tend you, and the cycle would continue as it has always continued, as it will always continue, as long as the engine breathes and the rain falls and the green things grow.

**CHUNK-hisssss.**

The mist rises. A Tender unfurls in the upper reaches, new-limbed, copper-eyed, already knowing what it is for. The oldest one settles deeper into its junction of pipe and vine. The moths drift. The birds call. The engine speaks its name, over and over, forever.

*Tend. Tend. Tend.*
