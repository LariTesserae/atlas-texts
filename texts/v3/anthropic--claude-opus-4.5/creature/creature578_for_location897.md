## The Tuned Ones

They call themselves something that translates roughly as "those who have been tuned"—not in the sense of an instrument brought to pitch, but in the sense of a body that has learned to receive. They believe their ancestors came here empty, like vessels waiting to be filled, and that the mountain filled them over generations until they became capable of hearing what it was saying.

This is not metaphor to them. Or rather, it is metaphor, but they do not experience metaphor as something lesser than the literal. The mountain speaks. The water carries meaning. The salamander-knots are sentences in a language they are still learning after a thousand years. To say these things are "just" patterns, "just" phenomena, would strike them as a kind of deafness—a failure to be tuned.

---

The woman building the wall is named something like Keth-in-the-seep, though this is only her current name. She has had four names in her life, each one marking a shift in her relationship to the water. As a child she was Keth-who-follows, because she spent her early years trailing the salamander-roads, learning their logic by immersion. As a young woman she became Keth-at-the-split, having claimed a terrace where a major seep forked into three channels—a position of some importance, as the splitting-places are where the mountain's meaning is most legible. Now she is Keth-in-the-seep because she has given up the splitting-place to someone younger and moved to a terrace where she is building a wall that will, if she completes it before she dies, redirect water in a way that has never been attempted.

She is not certain she will complete it. She is not certain it should be completed. The wall is also a question she is asking the mountain: *Is this what you want?* Each stone she places is a word in that question. Each time she removes and replaces a stone, she is listening for the answer.

She is fifty-three years old. Her skin is the color and texture of the lichen-stained rock. Her eyes, like everyone's here, are permanently narrowed, the muscles around them so developed from squinting that she looks like she is smiling even when her mouth is flat. She has two living children, both of whom have moved to terraces she can see from where she works. She had a third child who died in infancy, and she built a small cairn for him at the base of her terrace, where the water runs over it constantly. She believes he is still being tuned, even now, even dead. She talks to the cairn when she works. The water carries her words down through the rock, and she trusts they reach him.

---

The children rebuilding the bridge are part of a group that the adults call "the quick-listeners." Not all children here are quick-listeners; some are slow-listeners, which is considered equally valuable, perhaps more so. The quick-listeners are trained to respond to the mountain's hour-by-hour changes—the seeps that shift, the salamander-roads that suddenly redirect, the bird-waves that indicate a lichen bloom or die-off. They spend their days in constant motion, adapting infrastructure, sending hand-sign reports up and down the terraces, learning the mountain's daily speech.

The slow-listeners are being trained for something else. They sit in the deep shelters with the moss-gardens and the treasured ferns, and they learn to perceive patterns that unfold over years, over decades. They memorize the positions of seeps going back generations. They study the bone-tablets on which the elders have scored records of salamander-knot formations, trying to find the grammar beneath the syntax. Some of them will spend their entire lives on a single question: *Why did the eastern seeps close for three years in the time of Miru-at-the-edge?* The answer, if they find it, might not be useful in any practical sense. But it will be true, and truth here is the same as holiness.

---

There is a man named Hollow-the-younger—his father was Hollow-the-elder, who died when a terrace collapsed, which happens every few decades and is not considered a tragedy so much as a message—who has dedicated his life to the birds. He is forty years old and has never lived on a terrace with a roof. He sleeps in a woven basket that hangs from one of the ancient stone pillars, exposed to the sun, and he spends his days following the bird-waves.

He believes—and he is not alone in this, though he is the most committed—that the birds are reading the lichen, and the lichen is recording the salamander-slime, and the salamander-slime is a residue of the water's passage, and therefore the birds are reading the mountain's history as it is written on the rock. He watches their waves. He has learned to see patterns in the way they move—spirals that tighten, lines that fork, moments when the entire wave pauses for a half-breath before resuming. He scores these patterns onto bone-tablets with an obsidian blade so fine it could split a hair.

He does not know what the patterns mean. He is not certain he ever will. But he knows they mean *something*, and he believes that someone, someday, will look at his tablets and see what he could not. He is contributing to a project that will outlast him by centuries, and this does not make him sad. It makes him feel like part of something larger than a single life could hold.

---

The children are born in the deep shelters, where the moss-gardens grow, because the light would be too much for a newborn's eyes. For the first weeks of life, they see only the green glow of the moss, the wet gleam of stone in lamplight (the lamps burn salamander-oil, rendered from the bodies of those that die, which is constant, because the salamanders live fast and die fast and are always being replaced). The mothers sing to them—not songs exactly, but tonal murmurs that imitate the sound of water moving through the rock. The idea is to tune them early, to make them ready to receive.

When a child is old enough to be brought into the light, there is a ceremony. The whole terrace gathers—perhaps twenty people, perhaps thirty—and the child is carried up through the carved steps, the rope-bridges, the worn troughs, until they reach the highest point of their family's territory. There, the child is held up to the sun. The adults do not cheer or sing. They simply watch, squinting, as the child's eyes open for the first time in full daylight.

What happens next depends on the child. Some scream. Some go silent. Some reach toward the light. The adults read these reactions the way Hollow-the-younger reads the bird-waves: as information, as meaning, as the first word of a conversation that will last a lifetime.

---

There are elders here who have lived so long they have become part of the infrastructure. A woman named Seep-mother—she is the oldest, perhaps ninety, though no one counts years with precision—has not moved from her terrace in two decades. Her body has shaped itself to the stone. She sits in a hollow that might have been carved for her or might have been worn by her sitting, and the water flows around her and sometimes over her, and she does not seem to mind. The salamanders treat her as an obstacle, routing around her the way they route around boulders. The birds land on her sometimes, their suction-cup feet gripping her weathered skin, and she lets them.

She speaks rarely, and when she speaks, people come from all over the terraces to listen. Her words are not commands or prophecies. They are observations—*The eastern seep has been cold for three days, colder than it should be*—that the listeners take away and consider, turning them over like stones, looking for the meaning underneath.

When she dies, and she will die soon, her body will be placed in a crevice in the rock and covered with fitted stones, and the water will flow over her and through her, and she will become part of the mountain's long memory. This is not a belief; it is simply what happens. The mountain takes everything back.

---

They do not have priests, exactly, but they have people whose role is to maintain the relationship between the settlement and the mountain. These are the channel-keepers: men and women who spend their lives studying the water-flows, learning where the seeps are likely to open and close, predicting—as much as anyone can predict—what the mountain will do next.

The channel-keepers are not revered. They are *needed*, which is different. When a new seep opens, a channel-keeper will be there to assess it: how cold, how fast, how mineral-rich. They will decide where to route it, whose terrace will benefit, whose will have to adapt. These decisions are not arbitrary. They are based on a lifetime of learning, of being tuned, of listening to the mountain's slow speech and trying to understand.

But the channel-keepers make mistakes. Everyone makes mistakes. Sometimes a seep closes unexpectedly and a terrace goes dry and a family has to relocate in a single morning, carrying everything they own in nets slung between poles. When this happens, there is no blame. The mountain did what it did. The channel-keeper read what they could. The family adapts, finds a new niche, begins again.

This is the fundamental belief, if you can call it a belief: that adaptation is not failure. That impermanence is not loss. That the mountain is not cruel when it closes a seep or opens a new one or shifts the salamander-roads in ways no one expected. The mountain is simply *speaking*, and it is not the mountain's fault if they cannot always understand.

---

There is a young woman named Flicker—her childhood name, which she has not yet outgrown—who is considered strange even by the standards of this place. She does not work on the terraces or tend the moss-gardens or follow the salamander-roads. She sits at the edge of the settlement, where the last terrace drops away into a chasm so deep the bottom is invisible, and she watches the light change.

She has been doing this for three years. She eats what others bring her. She speaks only in hand-signs, and even those are minimal: *thank you*, *I am well*, *leave me*. The others do not know what to make of her, but they do not interfere. Perhaps she is being tuned to something no one else can hear. Perhaps she is broken in some way, emptied out, unable to receive. They cannot tell. They bring her food and water and they leave her alone.

She is watching the way the light moves across the wet stone. She is watching the patterns it makes—the flickers and shifts, the way shadows lengthen and shorten, the way the glare-points migrate as the sun arcs overhead. She believes—though she has not told anyone this—that the light is another language, layered on top of the water-language and the salamander-language and the bird-language. She believes the mountain is saying something in light that no one has ever bothered to translate.

She may be right. She may be wrong. She may be neither. The others have decided to wait and see.

---

What they all share, from Keth-in-the-seep to the quick-listening children to Seep-mother dissolving slowly into the stone, is a particular quality of attention. They are always listening, always watching, always trying to parse meaning from the motion around them. This is not anxious or frantic; it is patient, sustained, a kind of full-body receptivity that they learn from infancy and refine throughout their lives.

They believe the mountain knows they are here. They believe it has always known, that it knew before they came, that their presence is part of some larger pattern they cannot see but trust exists. They do not worship the mountain, exactly. They *attend* to it. They show up, day after day, and they do their best to understand, and when they fail—as they often fail—they accept the failure and try again.

This is what it means to be tuned: to know that you are part of something larger than yourself, something that speaks in languages you will never fully learn, and to listen anyway. To keep listening. To let the listening shape you, hollow you out, fill you back up with something that is not quite yourself anymore but is not quite the mountain either.

Something in between. Something that flows.
