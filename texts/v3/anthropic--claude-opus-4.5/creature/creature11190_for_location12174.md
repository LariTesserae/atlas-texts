**The Tenders**

They have no name for themselves. Names require a sense of separation—this one from that one, me from you—and in the Drowned Nursery, such distinctions have grown soft. But if you watched them long enough, you might call them the Tenders, because that is what they do: they tend.

---

There are perhaps thirty of them, though counting is difficult. They are the color of the water itself, that iron-and-old-glass gray, and they move with a slowness that makes them easy to lose against the murk. Their bodies are long—longer than a human's, but narrower, attenuated, like something stretched by patience. They have limbs, yes, but the limbs are not fixed in their purpose: an arm becomes a fin becomes a feeling-tendril becomes an arm again, the flesh shifting over hours in response to need. When there is stone to lift, they grow strong. When there is sediment to sift, they grow delicate. When there is nothing to do but wait, they grow still, and their edges blur until they are barely distinguishable from the architecture they serve.

Their faces—if you can call them faces—are smooth planes interrupted only by two deep-set pits where eyes might once have been. They do not need to see. The water tells them everything: vibration, pressure, the chemical signatures of decay and growth. They taste the city's progress on their skin. When a wall is ready to receive another layer of bone-mortar, they know. When a crab has died in a useful place, they feel the small specific silence of it and come to arrange the shell just so.

They do not speak, but they are not silent. When they work close together—fitting a fragment of carved stone into a gap, guiding the pale anemone-things toward a window frame that needs filling—they produce a sound. It's not language. It's more like the noise of ice settling, or the groan of a ship's hull under pressure: a deep, subsonic murmur that travels through the water and through the stone and through the bodies of every living thing in the hollow. It means, roughly, *here*. It means *this is where the next piece goes*. It means *we are still building*.

---

They were not always like this. There are memories—not individual memories, but a kind of distributed recollection that lives in the water itself, in the taste of the sediment, in the oldest layers of the encrusted dome. Once they had hands with fingers. Once they spoke in sounds that moved through air. Once they began this city because they wanted something—permanence, maybe, or beauty, or simply a place to be that was *theirs*.

The flood came. The flood always comes. They had not finished in time.

But they did not leave. That is the important thing. They could have fled to higher ground, could have started over, could have let the water have what the water wanted. Instead they stayed, and they breathed water, and they learned to be slow. They let go of the urgency that had driven them to build in the first place. They let go of the need to *finish*. And in letting go, they became something that could wait—really wait, geological wait, the kind of waiting that makes mountains.

Their bodies changed. Their minds changed. What had been a community of builders became a single distributed organism, a colonial intelligence spread across thirty gray shapes that move through the unfinished streets like blood cells through a body. They share thoughts the way the little fish share their schooling patterns: not in words, but in orientation. In direction. In the slow, certain knowledge of what must be done next.

---

They have rituals, though they would not call them that.

When one of them is ready to stop—when a body has grown too thin, too soft, when the flesh begins to shed its coherence and drift apart like fog—the others gather. They bring the dying one to a place in the city that needs filling. An archway that has never had a keystone. A floor that has begun to crack. A wall that has waited centuries for its final course of stone.

They hold the dying one in place. They murmur their deep subsonic murmur. And they wait—hours, sometimes days—while the body settles into its new purpose. The flesh calcifies. The bones become architecture. What was once a Tender becomes part of what the Tenders are building, and this is not death to them. This is *contribution*. This is the way. Every wall in the Drowned Nursery contains the patient dead. Every pillar is made of someone who chose to stay.

When the dying is done, the others go back to work. There is no mourning. There is no celebration. There is only the next task, and the next, and the slow certain knowledge that someday the city will be complete, and when it is complete they will all be part of it, and then there will be nothing left to do but *be*.

---

Sometimes, in the dim gray light, a Tender will pause before the encrusted dome at the hollow's center. They will drift there, motionless, for hours. Inside, barely visible through the gaps in the growth, the old statues reach toward a ceiling that is almost—almost—closed.

The Tenders remember, dimly, that those shapes inside are what they used to look like. Hands with fingers. Faces with eyes. Bodies that moved through air and light and *wanted* things, desperately, the way creatures who know they are going to die always want things.

They do not want anything now. Or rather: they want only one thing, and they want it so completely that it has become indistinguishable from breathing, from being, from the slow pulse of their translucent hearts.

*Finish*, the water whispers, and they whisper it back: *finish, finish, finish*.

But not yet. Not for a long time yet. And they are not in any hurry.

---

If you came here—if somehow you found your way down through the newer waters to this hollow that is chapel-sized, heart-sized, cupped-hands-sized—they would notice you. They would taste you on their skin: the salt of your sweat, the carbon dioxide of your panic, the frantic quick pulse of a creature who has not yet learned to wait.

They would not harm you. They do not harm anything. But they would watch you, with those smooth eyeless faces, and they would wonder—in their slow, distributed, colonial way—whether you had come to help.

Whether you had come to stay.

Whether you might like, someday, to be part of something that will last.
