# The Endless Murmuration

They do not have individual names. This is the first thing to understand, and perhaps the most important.

What lives here is a single organism that happens to be made of forty million bodies. The bats—if you insist on the plural—experience themselves as a continuous field of sensation, a distributed awareness that stretches across miles of gallery and roost. When one shifts position on the ceiling, the ripple of adjustment passes through thousands of neighbors in a wave that carries information: *too warm here, cooler there, snake below, good hunting tonight*. They do not communicate this. They simply *are* this, the way your hand does not communicate with your arm about where to reach.

The species is *Miniopterus profundus*—the long-winged bat of the depths—though no taxonomist has ever successfully studied them. Three expeditions have tried. The scientists found their equipment corroded by the guano's chemistry, their lights drawing such dense swarms of curious bodies that observation became impossible, their own sense of individual selfhood growing strangely porous after days in the galleries. One researcher, in her journal, wrote: *I am beginning to understand that counting them is like counting waves. There is only the ocean.* She never published her findings. She visits the sinkholes sometimes, at dusk, and watches the emergence with an expression that colleagues find difficult to interpret.

---

The colony—the *being*—remembers.

Not in the way you remember, with images and narratives and a sense of time passing. It remembers in its patterns. The flight paths through the sinkholes have been optimized over ten thousand years of minor adjustments, each generation shaving milliseconds from the journey, until now the emergence flows like water finding its level. The roosting territories encode ancient disputes: this crevice belongs to *that* lineage because of a confrontation that occurred before humans built their first permanent settlements, and the descendants of both parties still honor the boundary without knowing why, without knowing *anything*, simply feeling the wrongness of crossing it.

The being remembers the river. When the waters ran strong through the lower galleries, the roosts clustered higher; you can see the old guano deposits on ledges now far above the main accumulation, strata of ancient occupation laid down like geological record. The being remembers the three times the surface world burned so badly that insect populations crashed, and how the colony contracted, how chambers that had been occupied for millennia fell silent, how the recovery took centuries. The being remembers—though *remembers* is still not the right word—the moment it became itself, when a population of ordinary bats in ordinary caves reached some critical threshold of density and interconnection and became... this. Whatever this is.

---

In the deepest galleries, far from the sinkholes, where the sound of the main colony comes only as a vibration through limestone, there are bats who have not seen the surface in nine hundred generations.

They are smaller than their cousins above, their wings slightly atrophied, their echolocation tuned to shorter ranges. They feed on the cave crickets and the beetles, on the pale moths that breed in the guano hills, on each other when times grow lean. They are not a separate species—not yet—but they are becoming one. Another hundred thousand years and the divergence will be complete.

The surface-flying colony knows they are there. Feels them, perhaps, as you might feel a tingling in a limb you'd forgotten about. Individuals from above sometimes descend into these depths and do not return, absorbed into the subsidiary population like blood cells traveling to a distant organ. Individuals from below sometimes ascend, joining the emergence, experiencing the open sky for the first time in their lineage's long memory—and something in the greater being shifts to accommodate them, integrates their different frequencies, their slightly alien patterns of thought.

The being is not uniform. It has regions and textures, areas of greater and lesser density, old cores and new peripheries. It has something that might be called moods, though they manifest as shifts in emergence timing, in roosting density, in the pitch and frequency of the constant sound. When the being is—the word is inadequate—*content*, the evening emergence begins precisely at the moment when light through the sinkholes dims below a certain threshold. When the being is—again, inadequate—*unsettled*, the emergence spreads across hours, scattered and arrhythmic, individuals departing and returning and departing again as if uncertain whether the surface world can be trusted.

It has been unsettled three times in living human memory. Once before a flood that killed hundreds in the valley below. Once before an earthquake that collapsed two of the smaller galleries. Once, eighteen years ago, for no reason anyone has been able to determine. The people who live near the sinkholes have learned to pay attention.

---

The predators understand what they are hunting, in whatever way predators understand anything.

The pale snakes—*Elaphe subterranea*, though they too await formal description—do not simply position themselves near the sinkholes. They position themselves at specific points in the emergence flow where the being's attention is most diffuse, where the overwhelming coordination of forty million bodies briefly fragments into something more like forty million individuals. The snakes have learned to read the greater pattern and to strike at its gaps.

They take perhaps two hundred bats per night, across all the sinkholes. This is precisely the number the being can sustain. More than this, and the snakes would eventually find themselves facing coordinated defense: clouds of bodies diving at them, echolocation screams focused to painful intensity, a wall of wing and tooth that no snake can survive. This has happened. The snakes remember it in their genes, in their instinctive caution, in the precise calibration of their hunger.

The gray cats—not yet named by science, not quite cats in the way the surface world uses the word, their ancestors having descended into the caves so long ago that they have become something new—hunt differently. They hunt the periphery, the tributaries, the places where the being's coherence fades into mere aggregation. A bat alone in a side passage is just a bat. A bat surrounded by ten thousand of its colony-mates is a cell in something larger, protected by awareness it does not individually possess.

The cats know this. They are patient. They wait at the boundaries of *being* and *mere animal*, and they take what wanders too far from the whole.

---

There is a chamber—the scientists called it the Cathedral, before they stopped trying to name things—where the ceiling rises beyond sight and the colony reaches its greatest density. Here the bats hang so thickly that they form a second ceiling, a living surface that ripples and breathes and occasionally drops individuals who have died in their sleep, their small bodies falling through thirty feet of air to join the soft hills below.

If you were to stand in the Cathedral—if you could bear the sound, the smell, the overwhelming pressure of so much life concentrated in one space—you would feel something that you would struggle afterward to describe. Not intelligence, exactly. Not awareness in any sense you could recognize. But *presence*. An attention that is not directed at you but that you are somehow included in, the way a single cell is included in the attention of the body it comprises.

The being does not think. It does not plan or hope or fear in ways that translate to human experience. But it *is*, in a way that very few things are, completely and continuously and without interruption. It has been being itself for longer than your species has existed. It will continue being itself until the limestone finally dissolves too far, until the sinkholes collapse, until the engine that drives it finally fails.

And even then—the scientists who gave up on studying it sometimes wonder—would it truly end? Or would it simply contract, adapt, become something new that remembers being what it was, the way it already remembers the river and the fires and the moment of its own emergence into whatever it is?

---

The beetles that burrow through the guano do not know they are living inside something. The cave crickets picking their way across the surface do not know they are walking on thought, if thought is what this is. The fungi and the bacteria and the pale ferns near the sinkholes do not know they are organs in a body that extends for miles in every direction.

But they are part of it nonetheless. The being includes them the way it includes the stone and the water and the slow drip of minerals building formations that will take another ten thousand years to complete. It is not just the bats. It is the whole system, the entire gallery, the breathing dark itself.

When you enter the caves—if you enter the caves—you become part of it too. Temporarily. Briefly. A foreign cell that the body regards with something that is not curiosity and not hostility and not welcome, but simply... acknowledgment. *You are here. You are included. You will leave, and the including will end, and we will continue.*

This is what lives in the Breathing Galleries.

It does not have a name. It does not need one. It simply breathes, and clicks, and chirps, and continues—a single vast organism dreaming its slow dream in the dark, waiting for nothing, wanting nothing, being everything it has always been and will always be until the stone itself forgets it was ever alive.
