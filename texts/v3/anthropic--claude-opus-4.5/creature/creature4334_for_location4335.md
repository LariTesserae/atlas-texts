## The Residents

**The Grandmother Crab**

She is no larger than the others—size is not how authority works here—but she is oldest, and her shell has calcified in patterns that the younger crabs read like text. She positions herself at the highest point of the ceiling, where the central light-column almost reaches, and she has not descended to the water in eleven years. She drinks condensation. She eats the fungal threads that reach even here. The clicking language of her kind began, four generations ago, as simple territorial signals, but she has been clicking complex patterns for so long that the younger crabs have started clicking back in ways their mothers never taught them. She is, without intention or awareness, founding a grammar. When she dies, her shell will fall into the basin, and the others will click about it for weeks in ways that might be grief or might be something that will become grief, given time.

---

**The Frog Who Lives Alone**

Most of the frogs cluster. This one does not. She occupies a crevice near the easternmost light-shaft, in a territory so small you could cover it with your palm, and she has not joined a chorus in three years. Her coloration shifted, very slightly, toward a blue-grey that matches the shadow of her crevice rather than the mineral-stained stone around it. She is not sick. She is not injured. She simply stopped, one day, feeling the pull toward other bodies, other voices. She eats well—the insects that fall from the shaft above reach her first—and she has grown marginally larger than her clustered kin. Sometimes the static of the greater chorus pauses, a gap in the sound, and in that gap her single voice is briefly audible: the same call, the same frequency, but isolated, like a question asked in an empty room. She does not seem to be waiting for an answer. She seems to be the answer to a question no one else is asking.

---

**The Salamander With The Scar**

His left gill-frond is torn, has been torn for years, and healed into a shape that moves water differently than his right. He swims in subtle curves now, always pulling slightly toward his damaged side, and this has made him a better hunter than his straight-swimming kin. He approaches prey from angles they don't expect, because even he doesn't fully expect them. He is the largest salamander in the basin, not through dominance but through this accidental efficiency. His skin is paler than the others, almost translucent, and if you watch him in the dim water long enough you can see the shadows of his organs, the slow pulse of his heart. He has outlived several generations of his kind. He does not know this. He knows only the hunting, the curving, the dim awareness of the eels that are too large to eat and too slow to fear, the strange clicking vibrations that travel through the water from the ceiling.

---

**The Eel That Remembers Light**

She should not remember anything. Her kind have been in darkness for so long that their eyes have simplified, their brains have narrowed to the essential: hunt, eat, reproduce, die. But she was born beneath the central light-shaft during a rare day when the dust above cleared and the sun came down almost *bright*, almost real, and something in her first moment of consciousness fixed on that brightness and kept it. She is drawn to the light-columns in a way the other eels are not. She surfaces there, sometimes, her phosphorescent skin dim in the comparative glow, and she holds still in a way that looks like basking but might be something else. The crabs have noticed her. They click differently when she rises. The Grandmother Crab, from her high perch, angles her eye-stalks downward when the strange eel surfaces, watching with what might be recognition of a fellow deviation, a fellow creature who does not quite fit the role her body was built for.

---

**The Fungal Network**

It is not one organism. It is also not many organisms. It is something in between, a web of pale threads connecting the visible fruiting bodies, running through cracks in the limestone, tasting the water and the air and the decay of everything that dies here. It has no mind. It has something slower and more patient: a responsiveness, a capacity for shift. When the population of copper-shelled beetles grows too large, the fungus produces less of what they eat and more of something faintly toxic, and the population shrinks, and then the fungus shifts again. It has been doing this for centuries. It has reached an equilibrium with everything here, the crabs and the frogs and the salamanders and the eels, that is so precise it looks like design but is only time, only the long slow pressure of death selecting for what works. If you could map its threads—and you cannot, they are too fine, too hidden—you would find that it reaches everywhere. Into the walls. Into the ceiling. Into the stone itself, where it extracts minerals that appear, transformed, in the shells of crabs, in the bones of salamanders, in the eggs of frogs. It is the floor beneath the floor, the skeleton of the cistern's living system, and it is utterly unaware of itself, and it will be here after everything else has changed beyond recognition.

---

**The Small God**

This is not literally a god. There is no supernatural presence in the cistern. But if you sat here long enough, in the blood-warm damp, listening to the frog-static and the crab-clicks and the soft plops of entering and exiting water, you might begin to feel that something cohered from the sum of all these small lives, some *pattern* that was not any individual creature but was not exactly the lack of one either. The crabs climb in formations that, seen from above, trace the old geometric carvings. The frogs call in rhythms that, slowed down, match the drip-rate of the weeping stone. The eels move through the basin in paths that, mapped over weeks, describe the same curves as the ancient channels. None of this is conscious. None of this is intended. It is simply that the cistern, built by engineers who understood water and stone, has shaped its inheritors into patterns that echo its builders' designs. The small god is this echo. It is the memory of human intention, transformed by centuries of non-human life into something that resembles purpose without possessing it. It is what the crabs click about, without knowing they are clicking about anything. It is what the lone frog's isolated call is answering. It is the reason the eel surfaces in the light, the reason the Grandmother Crab watches, the reason the scarred salamander curves through the water in shapes that would look, to the old engineers, like letters in a language they once knew.

It has no needs. It makes no demands. It is simply the fact of this place continuing, long past any reason to continue, because the small inheritors have made it home.
