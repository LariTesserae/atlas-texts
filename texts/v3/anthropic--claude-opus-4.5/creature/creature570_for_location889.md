**The Tender**

She is not young, but she has not yet begun to feel old. Her body knows this work the way hands know weaving—without consultation, without hesitation. She enters the bay at the hour when the sun hits the water at its most forgiving angle, when she can see her way down through twenty feet of green murk to the black dome waiting below.

Her lungs are larger than they should be. Not visibly—she looks like any other woman of her village, broad across the shoulders from net-hauling, skin gone permanently copper from salt and sun. But somewhere in her childhood she began holding her breath longer than the other children, and then longer, and then so long that her mother wept and called her drowned while she sat peaceful on the sandy bottom counting the legs of crabs. The capacity grew with her. Now she can work for the span of two hundred heartbeats before she must surface, and her heartbeat slows when she dives, slows and slows until it is almost nothing, until she is almost stone herself.

She does not remember deciding to build the room.

This troubles her sometimes, when she surfaces gasping into ordinary air, when she walks home through the palm-shadows with salt drying white on her arms. She remembers the building of it—years of it, her hands raw from mortar, her fingernails stained with calcium-paste that would not wash out for months. She remembers choosing each stone from the river three miles inland, carrying them one at a time to the shore, sinking them in net-bags to the seafloor. She remembers the failures: early versions of the lamp-cups that flooded, that would not hold their air-pockets, that snuffed themselves within minutes. She remembers the iteration, the refinement, the years of patient adjustment until she understood what angle, what depth, what proportion of fat to air would let fire live underwater.

But she does not remember *why*.

---

She tells herself stories about it. On different days, she believes different things.

*It is a temple.* This is the most comfortable story. Her people have gods—wave-gods, fish-gods, the great slow turtle who carries the world—and perhaps she is building a place to honor them. But she has never prayed there. She has never brought offerings, never performed rituals, never spoken the names that priestesses speak. She sits on the worn stone shelf and watches the flames and listens to the tones, and if that is prayer, it is a kind she has not been taught.

*It is a hiding place.* This story comes to her on bad days, when the village is loud with argument, when children run screaming through the paths between houses and her head aches and her skin feels like it belongs to someone else. She could go down and stay there. She has thought about it. The warmth, the light, the singing walls—she could curl on the stone shelf and let her slowed heart take her somewhere past breathing, somewhere the noise cannot reach. But she always surfaces. She has never actually hidden there. And from what would she be hiding? Her life is not hard. Her life is not unhappy. She has work and food and a place in the village order. There is nothing to flee.

*It is an anchor.* This is the story she believes most often, though she does not fully understand it. She built the room to hold herself in place. Not physically—her body has never wanted to drift—but in some other way, some way she cannot name. The room is proof that she exists. The room is proof that her hands can shape the world. The room is proof that patience and stubbornness can make fire burn where fire should not burn, can make stone sing, can make a warm bright space in the cold dark water. When she tends the lamps, when she scrapes the ash and pours the fat and trims the wicks, she is not maintaining a building. She is maintaining *herself*. The room is her own heart, externalized, made visible, kept lit.

---

The kelp is food.

Not for her—the taste is bitter, medicinal—but for something else, something she does not fully acknowledge. In the deep water past the bay, past the reef, past the shelf where the seafloor drops away into blackness, there are shapes that rise sometimes to the shallows. She has seen them only in glimpses: a pale curve breaking the surface at dusk, a shadow that moves against the current, a sound at night that might be wind but is not wind. She does not know what they are. She has never seen one clearly. But she knows they eat the red kelp, and she knows that if she grows it for them, if she keeps it trimmed and healthy, they will not come all the way into the bay.

She does not think of this as bargaining. She does not think of it as fear. It is simply maintenance, like the lamps, like the mortar, like the long slow project of keeping something alive that wants to stay alive. The kelp grows. The shapes stay in the deep. The village sleeps without dreaming of pale curves in the water. This is balance. This is the work.

She does not remember who taught her about the kelp. She does not remember learning that the shapes were hungry, that they could be fed, that they would accept this small garden in lieu of other food. The knowledge is simply there, like the knowledge of her own lungs, like the knowledge of fire and fat and air-pockets. Perhaps she was born with it. Perhaps she learned it so long ago that the learning has dissolved into instinct. Perhaps she is wrong about all of it—perhaps the shapes do not exist, perhaps the kelp means nothing, perhaps she is a madwoman tending an underwater room for no reason at all.

She does not know. She tends the kelp anyway.

---

Her name is not important. She has one—her mother gave it to her, and the village uses it, and she answers to it—but it does not describe her. The room describes her. The work describes her. If you asked her who she is, she would not say her name. She would say: *I am the one who keeps the light lit beneath the water.*

She has tried to explain this to others. Once, years ago, she brought a man down with her—a diver from another village, a man with lungs nearly as large as hers, a man she thought might understand. She showed him the flames, the singing walls, the kelp in its alcoves. She sat with him on the stone shelf and watched his face in the amber light. He looked at everything. He touched the mortar with careful fingers. He listened to the tones. And when they surfaced, gasping, clinging to each other in the bright loud air, he asked her: *What is it for?*

She could not answer. She felt something close inside her, a door she had not known was open. She smiled and said *nothing, it is nothing, it is just a thing I built*, and he nodded and did not ask again, and she never brought anyone else down to see.

---

She is fifty-three years old. She has tended the room for thirty-one years. Her joints ache in the mornings now, and her dives are shorter than they were—one hundred eighty heartbeats, sometimes only one hundred sixty, her lungs no longer the impossible bellows they once were. She has begun to think about what will happen when she can no longer reach the bottom.

The flames will go out. This is certain. Without tending, the fat will burn down and the wicks will drown and the light will fail cup by cup until the room is dark. The singing walls will continue for a while—the tides will still move water through the tubes—but silt will accumulate, will clog the openings, will silence the drone note by note. The kelp will grow wild, will escape its alcoves, will fill the room with waving red fronds that no one trims. And eventually—years from now, decades perhaps—the mortar will soften and the stones will shift and the bay will swallow what she built.

She thinks about this and feels nothing. Not grief, not peace, not resignation—simply blankness, a space where feeling should be. The room was never meant to last forever. She knows this. She built it with her hands, and her hands will fail, and the room will fail with them. This is not tragedy. This is sequence. This is time.

But sometimes, in the amber light, on the worn stone shelf, she wonders if she should teach someone. If there is a child in the village with lungs like hers, with patience like hers, with the same strange unremembered knowledge of kelp and shapes and balance. If the work could continue past her body. If the light could stay lit.

She does not know how to find such a child. She does not know how to teach what she cannot explain. She sits in the brightness and listens to the tones and watches the flames hold steady against the water that should drown them, and she thinks: *this is enough. This has always been enough. The room exists. I exist. The light is lit.*

The leather door breathes in. She breathes out.

The work continues.
