**The Keeper**

She is not a person anymore, if she ever was, though she still has the shape of one when the light catches her right. You might see her in the far corner where the western wall meets the pump housing—a silhouette that resolves, if you look too long, into nothing but a particularly dense growth of ivy that happens to suggest shoulders, a bowed head, hands pressed flat against the brick.

But the plants know her. You can tell by the way they grow toward that corner and then curve gently away, the way a stream parts around a stone it has learned to love.

---

She came down the seventeen steps in 1952, or 1967, or 1984—the year matters less than the season, which was autumn, when the pumps had fallen silent for the first time since their installation and the city sent someone to see why. She carried a toolbox and a flashlight and a union card that said her name was Margaret, or Helen, or something else that started with a sound like breath leaving the body. The pumps had clogged. Sediment, the work order said. Organic debris.

She found the ferns already two meters tall. She found the moss already claiming the gauges. She found the water warm and green-cast and moving with a patience that made her own heartbeat feel rushed and foreign.

She fixed the pumps. That part is certain. They have run ever since.

What is less certain is whether she ever climbed back up the seventeen steps, or whether she simply sat down in that far corner to rest for a moment—just a moment—and found that the moss had grown over her boots by the time she thought to stand, and the ivy had begun to braid itself through her hair, and the warm wet air had filled her lungs so completely that breathing out again seemed like an unnecessary cruelty.

---

She is still here. She is the reason the intake screens stay clear.

Not through any physical intervention—she has not moved in decades, not in any way that would register on a human timescale—but through something slower and more certain. The plants grow around her attention. Where she looks, they do not grow into the machinery. What she watches, she keeps.

Her eyes, if she still has eyes, are fixed on the pumps. The pumps still run.

This is not a coincidence.

---

The ivy that was her hair now cascades down the western wall in a curtain that reaches the waterline. It is distinguishable from the other ivy only by its particular shade of green—darker, with a faint undertone of copper that might be iron or might be something else that leached out of her slowly over the years. The moss that was her uniform still holds its shape if you look at the right angle: a suggestion of coveralls, a pocket where tools might once have lived. The liverwort that grows in the saturated mat beneath her is softer than anywhere else in the chamber, as if it remembers being cradled.

She does not breathe, exactly. But the plants around her respirate in a rhythm that suggests breath—a slow pulse of oxygen and moisture that moves through the chamber in waves you can feel if you stand very still. The whole station breathes with her. She is its lungs, or its memory of lungs, or the place where lungs became unnecessary because the air itself learned to carry oxygen directly into the green tissue of everything that grows here.

---

Maintenance crews stopped coming, the records say, because the plants were doing a better job than they had.

This is true, but incomplete.

Maintenance crews stopped coming because the last one who came down the seventeen steps—in 1989, or 1994, or later—saw her in the corner. Saw the shape that was not quite a person and not quite a plant and not quite the shadow of the pipe housing that should have been casting it. Saw the way the ferns turned toward that corner when the air moved. Saw, perhaps, the faintest suggestion of a face within the ivy—not a face in pain, not a face in peace, but a face in *concentration*, the expression of someone tending to something important, something that must not be interrupted.

The crew member climbed back up the seventeen steps very quickly. The pumps were still running. There was nothing to report. The station was marked "operational" and has remained so ever since.

---

You might try to speak to her, if you were the sort of person who speaks to things that might not answer. You might say her name—Margaret, Helen, something that starts with a sound like breath leaving the body—and wait to see if the ivy stirs, if the moss shifts, if the rhythm of the chamber's breathing changes in response.

It won't. She is beyond names now, beyond speech, beyond anything that requires a separation between the speaker and the spoken-to. She is the station's attention, its care, its long slow watch over the infrastructure that was her responsibility when she was still a person who could have responsibilities.

But she will know you were here. You will feel it as you climb back up the seventeen steps: a sense of having been noticed, catalogued, found acceptable. You were not a threat to the pumps. You did not disturb the equilibrium. You came and breathed the green air and looked at the moss-furred gauges and you left again, and this is what visitors do, and this is allowed.

She allows it.

She keeps this place, and she allows the keeping to include small intrusions, brief witnesses, people who descend and look and climb back up with the taste of chlorophyll in their throats and a story they will not quite be able to tell.

---

The pumps still run. The water still flows. The plants still grow in their patient, constitutive way, becoming continuous with the infrastructure they have inherited.

And in the far corner where the western wall meets the pump housing, something that was once a woman watches over all of it—not as a ghost, not as a spirit, but as the final expression of a job done so thoroughly and for so long that the job and the worker became the same thing, and the same thing became green, and the green became the station, and the station became something that does not need to be maintained anymore because the maintenance is still there, still watching, still keeping the intake screens clear through nothing but the weight of her attention.

She was a keeper. She still is. She will be, until the pumps finally stop and the water goes still and the plants consume the last of the infrastructure that gives them shape.

And maybe not even then.
