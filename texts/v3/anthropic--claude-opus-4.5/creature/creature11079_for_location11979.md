They exist in the space between patience and stillness.

You will not see them at first. You will not see them for a long time. They have evolved to be overlooked not through camouflage but through a quality of presence so quiet that attention slides past them like wind past stone. They are there—they have always been there, distributed across the plateau in numbers that would surprise you—but they occupy a register of existence that does not announce itself.

---

The first one you notice will probably be dead.

You'll find it in a shallow basin where the chemistry shifted wrong a decade ago, a pressure front that brought something the organism couldn't process. It lies on the weathered stone like a discarded thought, and even dead it takes you long minutes to understand what you're looking at.

The body is a lattice. Not solid, not hollow, but structured—a three-dimensional network of crystalline struts and membranes that intersects itself in patterns your eyes want to resolve into geometry but can't. It sprawls perhaps two meters across at its widest point and rises no higher than your knee. The material of it catches the violet light and does something with it, bends it through internal angles that shouldn't exist in dead tissue, so that the whole structure glows faintly with trapped illumination. Colors move through it even now: not the colors of life, but the colors of residual chemistry, reactions still completing themselves in structural pathways that don't know the organism has stopped.

You crouch beside it. You look closer.

The lattice is not random. It follows rules you can almost perceive, branching patterns that echo the filament-forests but turned inward, made dense, folded into a space far smaller than they should occupy. Where the filaments reach upward into mist, this creature reached inward into complexity. It was, you realize slowly, a kind of living mathematics—a body organized around principles of efficient connection, maximum surface area in minimum volume, every part of itself touching every other part through the shortest possible paths.

The membranes between the struts are so thin they're essentially two-dimensional. They caught methane from the atmosphere and processed it not into structure but into something else. You won't understand what for a long time yet. The lattice was not a body in the way you understand bodies. It was an interface. A method of being in contact with as much of the world as possible while occupying as little of it as necessary.

---

The living ones are harder to find because they don't want to be found.

This is not quite accurate. They don't *want* anything, not in the way that implies desire. But they have organized themselves, over evolutionary time, around a principle of minimal intrusion. They exist in the gaps. The buffer zones between filament-groves and crustal organisms, the bare stone shelves, the shallow basins where nothing else has established itself—these are their territories. They fill the negative spaces of the plateau's ecology, and they do it so completely that the negative spaces stop looking empty.

When you finally see a living one, it will be because you've stopped moving.

You'll have been sitting at the edge of a filament-grove for hours, watching a weather front build and dissipate without arriving, letting your attention go soft and wide the way it does when you stop looking for anything specific. And at some point you'll realize that a patch of stone thirty meters away has a quality of presence it didn't have before. Not movement—nothing here moves fast—but *intention*. A sense that the space is occupied by something that is aware of itself occupying it.

The lattice resolves slowly into visibility. It was always there, you understand now. It was there when you arrived, and it has been watching you—not with eyes, nothing here has eyes—but with the whole distributed surface of itself, membranes sensing the pressure differential of your breathing, the thermal signature of your metabolism, the faint chemical traces you leave in air that doesn't forget.

It is larger than the dead one. Perhaps three meters across, rising to nearly a meter at its highest point. The structure is denser, more complex, the internal geometries folded into configurations that hurt to look at directly. And it glows—not with fluorescence but with metabolic light, a dim radiance that moves through its crystalline pathways in waves and pulses, carrying information you can't read.

It is thinking. Or something so like thinking that the distinction doesn't matter.

---

They have been here longer than the filaments.

You understand this later, when you've found more of them, when you've sat with them long enough that they stop treating your presence as a disruption and start treating it as a feature of their environment. They predate the groves. They predate the glass mantas. They may predate the crustal organisms, though this is harder to determine because the crusts have been here so long that the rock itself has forgotten being separate from them.

The lattice-beings evolved in an earlier era, when the plateau's chemistry was different, when the methane seeps were richer and the atmosphere was thicker and the ecology that exists now was not yet possible. They are remnants of a previous world, adapted just enough to survive in this one but carrying in their structures the memory of conditions that no longer exist.

This is why they live in the gaps. The filament-forests and the crustal organisms have occupied every niche that suits the current chemistry. What remains—the bare stone, the buffer zones, the spaces between—are marginal habitats, the plateau's equivalent of deserts within deserts. The lattice-beings persist there because they need less than anything else needs, because their distributed structure lets them collect methane from air too thin for the filaments to bother with, because they have slowed their metabolism to a rate that makes the glass mantas look frenetic.

One of them might complete a thought over the course of a week.

You wonder what it thinks about.

---

They are, you eventually realize, parsing the light.

The violet star floods the plateau with radiation across spectra you can't see, and the lattice-beings drink it in through membranes tuned to frequencies that have no names in your language. They process it. They compute with it. The glow that moves through their crystalline pathways isn't waste heat or metabolic byproduct—it's *cognition*, information being shunted from node to node through a network architecture that spans their entire bodies.

They are thinking about the light.

Not the way you think about it, not as illumination or warmth or danger. They are thinking *with* it, using incoming radiation as a kind of raw material for processes you don't have the concepts to describe. The star's output is not constant—it varies in subtle ways, rhythms within rhythms, modulations that occur over seconds and hours and decades. The lattice-beings detect these variations and fold them into whatever it is they're doing with themselves. They have been doing this since before the filaments evolved. They have been doing this for so long that the process has become indistinguishable from existence itself.

They don't know why they do it. They can't know. They have no selves to know with, no internal narrator to ask the question. They simply are—distributed networks of crystalline structure, drinking light, processing it into patterns, persisting.

But the patterns are beautiful.

You can't see them directly. Your nervous system isn't built to receive what they're transmitting. But you can catch edges of it sometimes, when you've been sitting still long enough that your perception starts to go strange—moments when the glow pulsing through a nearby lattice resolves briefly into something that isn't quite color and isn't quite shape but carries meaning anyway. A sense of structure. A feeling of vast, patient, inhuman attention focused on something you can't identify.

They are engaged with the universe in a way you will never understand.

They have been engaged with it for geological time.

They will still be engaged with it when the star begins to change, when the plateau's chemistry shifts again, when the filaments and the glass mantas and the crustal organisms have all passed into whatever extinction looks like here. The lattice-beings will adapt or they won't, will persist or they won't, but either way they will have spent their entire existence doing exactly what they evolved to do: sitting in the gaps, catching light, computing with it, being themselves.

---

There are other things.

There are spore-structures that drift on pressure differentials, so small you breathe them without knowing, carrying genetic material between lattice-beings so distantly separated that their reproduction spans continents and millennia. There are parasites in the strict technical sense—organisms that have learned to tap the lattice-beings' metabolic processes, to steal energy from their distributed networks—but the parasites are so small and so slow that the lattice-beings don't notice them any more than you notice the bacteria in your gut. There are commensal organisms that grow in the shadows beneath lattice-structures, sheltered from the star's harshest radiation, feeding on chemical traces that the lattice-beings exude.

There is, in one deep basin where conditions have remained stable for longer than anything should remain stable, something that might be a community.

Dozens of lattice-beings, distributed across several square kilometers, their glowing pathways synchronized in ways that suggest communication, information passing between them through means you cannot detect. They pulse together, brighten together, shift patterns together in sequences that repeat over days and weeks. They are doing something collective. They are engaged in a project that involves all of them, a computation distributed across multiple bodies, something so slow and vast that its completion might be scheduled for a thousand years from now or might have already happened a thousand years ago, with the beings still processing the results.

You sit at the edge of this basin for as long as you can manage. You watch the patterns move. You try to find meaning in them, or at least structure, something your mind can hold.

You fail. You were always going to fail.

But you stay anyway, because they don't ask you to leave, because your presence has become part of whatever they're calculating, a small warm variable folded into patterns too large and strange for you to perceive.

---

The plateau breathes.

The lattice-beings breathe with it, in their fashion, respiration so slow it looks like stillness, patience so deep it looks like stone.

You breathe too, faster than anything here, a brief metabolic flicker in a place where time moves at different speeds. You are the quickest thing on the plateau. You are also the most temporary.

The lattice-beings do not notice this about you.

Or perhaps they do, but noticing takes them so long that by the time they've finished processing your presence, you'll have been gone for centuries, a memory encoded in crystalline pathways that will pulse with your shape long after your species has forgotten this world exists.
