# The Keth-Avai

They call themselves the Keth-Avai, which means something like "those who are held" or "the carried ones"—the grammar is subtle, implying not passivity but trust, the way an infant is held by a parent, the way a sleeper is held by a hammock. They do not think of themselves as clinging to the Hollow. They think of the Hollow as holding them.

---

Their bodies tell the story of generations lived in vertical emptiness.

They are small. An adult stands perhaps a meter and a third, and this is not accident but necessity—the Skeins have weight limits, the platforms flex and sway, and mass is a luxury the Hollow does not forgive. Their bones are thin-walled tubes, not unlike the Pipe-Stems themselves, strong in proportion to their weight but liable to shatter from impacts that a heavier-boned creature might survive. They do not fall often. But when they fall, they break.

Their hands are their glory. Four-fingered, each finger jointed in three places and tipped with a hard curved nail that can hook into fiber, into bark, into the rough surface of basalt where it shows through the growth. The thumb opposes, but so does the smallest finger, giving them a grip that can close from both sides at once. They climb the way other beings walk: without thought, without effort, their fingers finding purchase on surfaces that look smooth to outside eyes. A child of three can traverse a Skein faster than an adult can run on flat ground—though of course no Keth-Avai has ever seen flat ground, would not know what to do with it, might find it as terrifying as the void below.

Their feet are similar, prehensile, capable of gripping. They rarely wear foot-coverings. When they sit—on platforms, on the great swaying nets they weave between Pipe-Stems—they often grip with their feet while their hands remain free for work. They can sleep this way, feet locked around a Skein, body swaying in the wind, as secure as a stone.

Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, pupils that expand to fill nearly the entire visible surface, capable of gathering the dim gray light and making use of it. In what passes for full daylight in the Hollow—the brightest hours, when reflected light from the distant sky actually penetrates—they squint and shade their faces, uncomfortable with brightness. They see better in the dark than any surface creature. They see well enough to navigate by Mat-glow and the faint phosphorescence of certain Pipe-Stem species, well enough to hunt at the edges of true night.

Their ears are large, mobile, cupped like leaves to catch sound. This matters more than vision. A Keth-Avai child learns the language of the Hollow before she learns to speak: which humming means which wind direction, which creak means a Skein under strain, which distant rumble means a Pipe-Stem breaking somewhere in the dark. They navigate by ear. They recognize places by their sound—every junction of Skeins has its own harmonic signature, every settlement its own acoustic fingerprint, and the Keth-Avai carry mental maps made of tones rather than images.

---

They are born into the Hollow and they do not leave it.

A mother gives birth in a nest-basket, a woven enclosure suspended from a Skein-junction and lined with dried Mat-fiber for softness and insulation. The child emerges already gripping, fingers curled, toes flexed, the reflex so strong that newborns can hang from a rope within hours of birth. This is not precociousness. This is survival. The first lesson is hold on. The second lesson is hold on. There is no third lesson until the first two are mastered.

Children learn the Skeins the way surface children learn meadows and streets—as a landscape, as a territory, as the shape of home. They learn which routes are safe, which platforms are sturdy, which Pipe-Stems sing in tones that mean good harvesting. They learn the boundaries: how far they are allowed to go, which directions lead to friendly settlements, which lead to stretches of wild growth where no one has built, where the predators are bolder. They learn, above all, to be quiet when quietness matters, to move when movement is safe, to read the wind and the sound and the behavior of the Skein-runners to know what dangers might be near.

They grow slowly. Puberty comes late, at fifteen or sixteen, and full adulthood not until the early twenties. They live long if they live at all—seventy years, eighty, sometimes more—but infant mortality is brutal. The Hollow does not forgive mistakes. A child who has not learned to grip, who releases at the wrong moment, who panics in a wind-shift and loses her hold—that child falls. The parents watch. The parents cannot follow. The sound of the body striking lower growth, or vanishing into the darkness below without any sound at all, becomes part of the Hollow's voice, absorbed into the great humming, indistinguishable from all the other sounds of growth and death and change.

The Keth-Avai do not have a word for "ground." They have seventeen words for different qualities of rope.

---

A settlement is a web.

Picture a junction where dozens of Skeins converge, a knot in the three-dimensional cat's-cradle of the Hollow. The Keth-Avai have built outward from this knot, weaving new connections, strengthening natural ones, creating a sphere of structured space perhaps two hundred meters across. At the center is the fire-platform—the most precious thing, the most carefully tended, a broad surface of layered Pipe-Stem sections covered with ash and ringed with woven windbreaks to protect the flames. Around it, at varying distances, hang the nest-baskets: family dwellings, each one a capsule of woven fiber large enough for four or five bodies to sleep curled together against the cold.

Beyond the dwellings are the work-platforms: flat surfaces where Mat-fiber is processed into cordage, where Pipe-Stem pith is prepared into the bland cakes that form the staple diet, where tools are knapped from volcanic glass carried up from lower reaches. Beyond these are the gardens—cultivated Mats, selected over generations for their nutritional content, grown in great hanging curtains that the gardeners tend by climbing directly onto their surfaces, distributing fertilizer gathered from the waste-pits, trimming away dead growth, harvesting mature sections.

And beyond the gardens, at the edges of the settlement's sphere, are the watch-points: small platforms, barely large enough for a single person, positioned at the ends of long Skeins where sentries can listen to the approaches. The Keth-Avai know their neighbors. They trade with nearby settlements, intermarry, share information about weather patterns and Pipe-Stem blooms and the movements of Skein-runner herds. But they also guard their boundaries. Resources are limited. Territorial disputes are settled carefully, through negotiation when possible, through violence when necessary.

The violence is quiet. It happens at night, at the edges, between small groups who know each other's names. It is not war—there are no armies in the Hollow, no possibility of massed forces, the infrastructure cannot support such things—but it is killing, and the bodies fall the same way all bodies fall, and the survivors remember.

---

They worship the Hollow itself.

Not as a god, exactly—the Keth-Avai do not have a concept of divine beings, of supernatural entities that exist outside the world. They worship the Hollow the way a fish might worship water if a fish could worship: as the medium of existence, as the thing that holds and feeds and kills, as the great fact of life to which all other facts are subordinate.

Their religious practice is sonic. Prayer is humming. Ritual is the production of specific tones at specific times, harmonics that blend with the voice of the Hollow, that become part of its chord. A coming-of-age ceremony involves learning to produce, with the throat alone, a note that matches one of the Pipe-Stems near the settlement—a process that can take months of practice, of listening, of adjustment, until the young person's voice and the Hollow's voice are briefly indistinguishable. A funeral involves cutting a small section from a Pipe-Stem and tuning it, carefully, to the pitch of the deceased's voice, then installing it permanently in the settlement's structure, so that the dead person's note continues to sound in the wind forever.

Long-established settlements are surrounded by these memorial pipes. The sound of an old community is layered, complex, a chord that contains centuries of voices. The Keth-Avai can hear their ancestors in the wind. They sing with the dead every day.

---

The Skein-runners are more than prey.

The Keth-Avai hunt them, yes—the meat is dense and nutritious, the hide useful for waterproofing, the bones workable into tools. But they also follow them, learn from them, treat them as a kind of oracle. The herds know things. They know which Skeins are weakening, which Pipe-Stems are about to break, where the moisture is gathering, where the wind is about to shift. A herd that suddenly changes direction, that abandons a route it has used for months, is telling you something. Wise hunters listen.

Some Keth-Avai have developed a closer relationship. They call it thread-bonding, and it involves following a single herd for years, learning its patterns, making oneself known to the animals without ever hunting them. A thread-bonded person can move among the herd without causing panic, can travel with them during migration, can even—this is rare, this is the stuff of stories—ride on the back of a large individual, letting the animal's grip and balance substitute for her own.

Thread-bonded Keth-Avai are the long-range travelers, the messengers, the traders who cross the vast distances between settlements. They are respected and slightly feared. They have seen more of the Hollow than anyone else. They have been to the far walls, to the distant communities, to the edges of known space where the Skeins grow wild and strange and the Pipe-Stems are so large that their humming drops below the range of hearing. They bring back stories. They bring back songs.

---

There are things below.

The Keth-Avai do not speak of them directly. They have euphemisms, circumlocutions, ways of referring to the predators of the deep without naming them. The deep-dwellers. The weight-bringers. Those who rise.

What is known: they are large. The sounds they make when they climb—because they do climb, slowly, following the scent of fire-smoke and settlement-noise—suggest bodies massed in tons, gripping surfaces with appendages that leave marks visible for years afterward. They hunt at night, in the darkest hours, when even Keth-Avai eyes cannot penetrate the gloom. They take whole platforms sometimes. They are patient. They are, the Keth-Avai believe, intelligent—intelligent enough to learn patrol patterns, to avoid sentries, to wait until a settlement is sleeping before beginning their ascent.

The Keth-Avai have defenses. The watching-posts are part of it: listeners trained to recognize the specific sounds of a large body moving through the structure, the creak of Skeins under unusual weight, the silence that falls when Skein-runners sense something wrong. When an alarm is raised, the settlement mobilizes. Fires are stoked to brightness, creating a sphere of light that the deep-dwellers seem to avoid. Long poles with obsidian tips are deployed, not to kill—nothing so small could kill what comes from below—but to make contact, to let the creature know that this settlement is awake, is defended, is not worth the effort when easier prey might be found elsewhere.

Sometimes it works. Sometimes the creature descends again, fading back into the darkness, seeking something less troublesome.

Sometimes it does not work. The stories about those nights are not told to children.

---

They have mathematics, the Keth-Avai.

Not written—they have no writing, no flat surfaces stable enough to preserve marks for long—but memorized, passed down in chanted formulas that encode geometric relationships, load-bearing calculations, the complex logistics of three-dimensional navigation. A master weaver can look at a junction of Skeins and calculate, in her head, how much weight it will support, how the stress will distribute, where reinforcement is needed. A master navigator can hear the chord of an unfamiliar location and determine, from the pitches alone, approximately how far from the walls it lies, how high in the caldera, which wind patterns dominate.

This is not magic. This is survival. The Hollow is a physical system, and the Keth-Avai have learned to read its physics the way a sailor reads currents and winds. They do not understand the underlying principles—they do not know about acoustics, about resonance, about the relationship between tube length and pitch—but they have accumulated generations of empirical knowledge, codified into formulas and proverbs and songs that encode truth without explaining it.

A child learns that a Pipe-Stem humming in a certain range can be trusted to hold for another season. She does not learn why this is true. She does not need to. The knowledge saves her life regardless.

---

They tell stories about the Hollow's birth.

Long ago, they say, there was only stone. Hot stone, liquid stone, stone that flowed like water and glowed like ember. The stone shaped itself into a great bowl, a cup to hold the sky, and then it cooled and hardened and grew still. But the stillness was not complete—something moved in the deep places, something breathed, and its breath rose through cracks in the cooling rock and carried seeds.

The seeds took root. The plants grew. And with the plants came the first Keth-Avai, climbing upward from somewhere below, emerging from the steam and the darkness into the new-growing world.

Where did they come from? The stories do not say. The stories imply that the question is not meaningful—that the Keth-Avai have always been part of the Hollow, that they grew with it, from it, out of it. They are not visitors to this place. They are not colonizers or refugees. They are native. They belong.

The stories also say that the Hollow is not finished growing. That the Pipe-Stems will continue to extend, the Skeins to spread, the Mats to thicken, until someday—generations from now, centuries, ages—the center will finally be reached. The great stems from the far walls will meet in the middle of the void, will weave together, will form a single continuous structure from one side of the caldera to the other.

When that happens, the Keth-Avai say, the Hollow will finally be complete. And something new will begin.

They do not speculate about what that something might be. The Hollow holds them, has always held them, will continue to hold them. That is enough.

---

At night, in the settlements, when the fires have been banked to coals and the families have retreated to their nest-baskets and the sentries have taken their positions at the watching-posts, the Keth-Avai sing.

Not loudly. Not in unison. Each voice finds its own note, its own place in the chord, adding to the Hollow's humming rather than competing with it. The songs are wordless, or nearly so—long vowels, sustained tones, the kind of music that requires nothing but breath and attention and time. A settlement singing together sounds like a new instrument has joined the orchestra, a warm organic presence amid the cold vegetable voices of the Pipe-Stems.

They sing for comfort. They sing for communion. They sing because the alternative is silence, and silence in the Hollow means something has gone wrong—a fire extinguished, a structure failed, a predator near. They sing to prove that they are still here, still alive, still held.

The songs carry. Sound travels far in the Hollow, carried on the same winds that make the Pipe-Stems hum. A singer in one settlement, if conditions are right, might be heard in another settlement days of travel away—not as distinct words or even distinct melody, but as a presence, a warmth in the chord, a reminder that the great void is not empty, that others are out there in the darkness, listening.

You might hear them now, if you listen. Beneath the humming, beneath the wind, beneath the creak of Skeins and the distant calls of Skein-runners. Small voices, human voices, making themselves part of the only world they have ever known.

They are the Keth-Avai. They are held by the Hollow. And they hold on.
