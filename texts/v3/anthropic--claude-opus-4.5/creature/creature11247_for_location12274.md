**The Custodian**

He has no name that he remembers, though he must have had one once—must have signed it on paperwork, heard it called across rooms. Now he is simply the one who comes down the thirty-seven rungs, and that identity has proven sufficient.

He is old in the way that certain objects become old: not decrepit, but settled into a final form. His joints ache in the cold, and the cold here is constant, so the ache has become less a sensation than a fact about his body, like the length of his fingers or the way his left eye waters. He wears the same coveralls each visit—municipal issue, decades obsolete, the reflective strips long since faded to a dull gray that matches the tideline on the bricks. He carries a torch he rarely switches on, and a notebook he rarely writes in, and a small glass jar that once held preserves and now holds nothing, or holds possibility, or holds whatever he decides to collect and then decides not to.

He is not supposed to be here. His clearance expired when the department reorganized, when the drainage cathedral was redesignated from "active maintenance zone" to "monitoring only" to, finally, nothing at all—dropped from the schedules, from the maps, from institutional memory. But he kept the key. And the lock still recognizes it.

---

He comes every nine days. Not weekly—he tried weekly once and found it too regular, too much like the schedules he used to keep when schedules mattered. Nine days allows for drift, for the visits to fall on different days of the week, different hours. Sometimes he descends at dawn, when the phosphorescent panels seem almost warm against the darkness he's left behind. Sometimes he comes at midnight, when the city above has quieted enough that he can almost hear the membrane creatures pulsing.

He does not know what he is looking for. That is the point, perhaps, or has become the point through repetition. He walks the perimeter slowly, one hand on the rusted railing, his footsteps ringing dull against the grating. He pauses at each of the seven pipe mouths and listens. He watches the water for movement and notes what he sees, though his notes have become less language and more symbol over the years—a system of marks only he can read, cataloging patterns only he is tracking.

The eels know him. He believes this, though he could not defend it as science. When he first started coming, after his wife died and the silence of his apartment became unbearable, they would vanish at his approach—shadows dissolving into deeper shadow, the water smoothing over their absence. Now they surface while he watches. Not approaching, not performing, simply continuing their lives in his presence as if he has become part of the architecture, another support pillar, another thing that has always been here.

---

He spoke to the membrane creatures once. Spoke *at* them, rather—stood at the point where the walkway passes closest to the water and addressed them in a low voice, the way he used to address his wife when she was sleeping and he could not. He told them about the city above, about weather and traffic and the particular quality of autumn light in the park where he no longer walks. They did not respond. Their cilia continued their slow waving, their drift continued its purposeless course. But he felt, afterward, that something had been exchanged—that the act of speaking into the silence had changed the silence, had made it a shared silence rather than an empty one.

He has not spoken to them since. Once was enough. Once established the relationship; repetition would cheapen it.

---

The crabs he feeds. This is his one intervention, his single disruption of the ecosystem's closed loop. He brings scraps—bread gone stale, vegetable trimmings, once a piece of fish that he worried was too on-the-nose but that the crabs accepted without apparent irony. He drops the offerings at the tideline and watches from a distance as the pale bodies emerge from crevices, as the fighting begins, as hierarchies establish and re-establish themselves over morsels he chose arbitrarily from his own kitchen's waste.

He tells himself this is observation. He tells himself he is studying their behavior, gathering data that might someday matter to someone. But the truth is simpler: he likes being needed. He likes that something in the world—even something small and blind and unaware of him as anything other than a source of food—depends on his return.

---

There is a place on the walkway, near the pipe that breathes cold, where the grating has corroded thin. He discovered it three years ago, when his foot broke through and he caught himself on the railing, heart pounding, staring down at the black water a meter below. He should have reported it. He should have stopped coming, or at least avoided that section.

Instead, he visits it each time. He stands at the edge of the weakened section and tests his weight against it, feeling the metal flex, listening to the groan of rust against rust. He does not want to fall. He is not trying to fall. But he wants to know that he could—that the boundary between himself and the water is thin and growing thinner, that his presence here is contingent, negotiated, sustained only by structures that are themselves failing.

This is not despair. He was despairing in the months after his wife died, in the apartment that still smelled of her, surrounded by objects that had been theirs and were now only his. This is something else. This is acceptance, maybe. This is learning to live at the edge of things, to find comfort in thresholds.

---

He knows about the choral humming. He has heard it four times—always from the center of the chamber, always when he has been still for a long while, always fading the moment he tries to focus on it. He does not know what it is. He does not try to know. Some things, he has decided, are meant to remain at the edge of perception, glimpsed but never grasped.

He hums back, sometimes. Low, tuneless, barely more than a vibration in his throat. The dome catches it and carries it outward, lets it mingle with the drip and the echo and the subsonic memory of the city above. If anything answers, he cannot distinguish the answer from the echoes of his own voice.

This, too, feels like enough.

---

He will die someday—sooner than later, probably, given his age and his joints and the way his breath catches on the climb back up. He has thought about what will happen then. The key will be found among his effects, or it won't. Someone will wonder where he went every nine days, or no one will. The lock will continue to recognize the key, or the lock will rust shut, or the entrance will be sealed in some future renovation and the cathedral will become truly forgotten, truly alone.

The eels will not notice his absence. The membrane creatures will continue their drift. The crabs will wait at the tideline for food that no longer comes, and then they will stop waiting, and then they will forget they ever waited.

This does not trouble him. He has made his peace with irrelevance—has found, in fact, a kind of freedom in it. He matters here only as much as a drip matters, as much as a ripple matters. He is part of the pattern for now, and then he will not be, and the pattern will close over the space where he was.

---

Tonight, he sits on the walkway with his legs dangling over the edge, his boots almost touching the water. The phosphorescent light turns his hands green, turns the water green, turns everything into a single continuous surface of dim luminescence. An eel passes beneath him, close enough that he could touch it if he reached down. He does not reach. The eel does not pause.

Somewhere in the pipes, metal settles. Somewhere above, the city continues its bright, loud, forgetting life. Here, in the space between, an old man watches the water and waits for nothing in particular and finds, in the waiting, something very close to peace.

He will climb the thirty-seven rungs eventually. He will return to his apartment and eat his dinner and sleep his thin sleep and wake to another nine days of the world above. But for now, he stays. The drip continues. The dome waits. And he waits with it, patient, patient, asking nothing, expecting nothing, simply present in a place that did not need him and does not need him and will not need him, and is, for that very reason, the only place he has ever felt at home.
