## The Woven People of Emmelwound

They are shorter than you expect, and wider—bodies built low to the ground, with hips and shoulders that know how to shift weight across surfaces that never stop moving. Their feet are broad, the toes long and prehensile, gripping rope and reed-weave with the same unconscious certainty that their hands grip tools. You notice this first because you see so many feet: bare, calloused, splayed for balance, often stained green-black from the substrate or marked with the pale crescents of old leech scars.

Their skin holds the color of the water beneath them—brown with undertones of bronze and olive, though among the elders it has taken on a grayer cast, as if the perpetual moisture were slowly leeching something away. Many bear raised patterns on their arms and shoulders, not tattoos but deliberate scarification that has healed into whorls and parallel lines. The youngest children don't have these yet. You see an adolescent girl on a platform below you, sitting very still while an older woman works at her upper arm with a curved tool, and the girl's face is set in an expression of determined endurance that suggests this is an important thing, a threshold being crossed.

Their hair, almost universally, is kept short or bound tight against the skull—anything else would become a matted catastrophe in this humidity within days. Some shave elaborate patterns into what remains; others coat the stubble in a reddish clay that dries to a protective shell. The few who wear their hair longer have locked it into ropes thick as fingers, often threaded with small carved beads or the iridescent shells of beetles.

---

They dress in layers that seem contradictory until you watch them move through a day. In the early hours, when mist rises thickest, they wrap themselves in woven fiber cloaks that shed water and hold warmth. As the day heats, these come off layer by layer, folded and stored in niches carved into the platform supports. By midday, most wear only short wrapped skirts and, for those who want them, bands of cloth across the chest. The fabric is coarse but supple, dyed in colors that the forest provides: muddy yellows, deep rust-reds, the blue-black of certain crushed berries that stains hands for days.

But there is another layer beneath—one of grease and paste, rubbed into the skin each morning from communal pots that sit near every family platform. The smell is part of Emmelwound's signature: animal fat rendered with herbs that repel the biting things, mixed with something fungal that seems to prevent the skin from going soft and rotten in the endless wet. Children squirm away from its application; adults submit to it the way they submit to breath.

---

The family structures are visible in the platform arrangements, if you watch long enough to read them. The smallest platforms—barely room for two adults and a few children to sleep pressed close—house what seem to be new couples, young and recently established. These perch at the edges of larger clusters, connected by short bridges to bigger platforms where extended families congregate. Three generations often live within shouting distance, and shouting is constant—not anger, usually, but the simple necessity of communication in a place this loud. Voices here have developed a particular carrying quality, a way of cutting through the frog-song.

The old stay near the center, where the platforms are oldest and most stable, where the best light-wells have been cultivated for longest, where the paths are so established that even failing eyes can navigate them. You watch an ancient man make his way across a bridge, his hand trailing along the rope rail, his feet finding each plank from memory. A child runs past him at full speed, and he doesn't flinch, doesn't break stride—he knows exactly where that small body will be, how the bridge will sway, what space will remain for him.

Children belong to the cluster as much as to their parents. They flow between platforms like water, appearing at any family's cooking fire when hungry, sleeping where exhaustion takes them, raised by whoever is present. A woman on a nearby platform is nursing an infant; two older children play at her feet, and you realize slowly that none of the three are hers. She is simply the one with milk and time and attention, and that is enough.

---

The eels are more important than you first understood. They are not just food, not just a resource managed—they are something closer to partners. The eel-tenders are specialists, mostly older women with a particular patience, who spend their days at the gaps in the lower platforms where the dark water surfaces. They know individual eels, or at least speak as if they do, crooning to them in a register below normal speech, clicking their tongues in patterns you can't decode. The eels surface for them, allow themselves to be touched, sometimes fed by hand.

Not all eels are harvested. Some have lived in the waters beneath Emmelwound for longer than any human resident, grown massive and pale and strange. These are named. You hear the names in passing, spoken with a respect that borders on reverence: *Siltwallow*, *Old Thread*, *the White Daughter*. A child who pesters one of these ancient eels, even accidentally, receives a swift reprimand that carries real fear in it. What the eels might do is never specified, but the fear is enough.

When an eel *is* taken—the younger ones, the ones without names—the taking is done with ritual efficiency. The eel-tender sings as she draws it up, a low repetitive song that might be apology or blessing or simply distraction. The killing stroke is quick: a curved blade behind the head. The body is processed entirely, every piece used, and small portions are returned to the water. For the other eels, people say. But you suspect it is also for something older, something the people here half-believe and half-perform, a transaction that keeps the waters generous.

---

Conflict is constant and contained. You witness three arguments in your first hour, each following its own grammar. The dispute about fishing rights escalates to the point where both parties are red-faced, veins standing out, voices cracking—and then an older man appears on a connecting bridge, says four words you can't catch, and both disputants deflate into sullen muttering. Resolved, or at least deferred.

There are no formal leaders that you can identify, but there are *heavy* people—those whose opinions pull others into orbit. A woman with a severe face and burn scars across one arm seems to organize work parties by simply beginning work; others join without being asked. A laughing man with a missing hand holds court on a central platform, and people bring him disputes the way they might bring firewood, something to be processed. He listens, he jokes, he renders judgments that seem to satisfy no one entirely but that everyone accepts.

The worst conflicts—the ones that cannot be contained—result in leaving. A young man on the settlement's edge is packing a woven bag with grim focus, his jaw tight, while his mother weeps on the platform behind him and his father refuses to look at him. You don't learn what he did. You learn only that by nightfall his platform will be absorbed into the neighbor's, the bridge to it cut and rewoven elsewhere, and his name will become one of the many that the old remember and the young do not.

---

The children are everywhere, half-feral, brilliant in their adaptation. They race across bridges that adults cross with care. They leap between platforms, catching themselves on ropes, swinging out over drops that would kill them if they missed. Some of them spend more time in the canopy than on the platforms, emerging with birds' eggs and strange fruits and the occasional bite-wound from a territorial lemur.

But they are also working, constantly, in ways that don't look like work. A girl of perhaps eight sits at the edge of a light-well, weaving a new section of floor-mat from split reeds, her fingers moving in patterns she doesn't have to watch. A younger boy follows an eel-tender, learning the songs, the clicks, the patience. Two children stand in a cistern-plant up to their waists, harvesting water-beetle larvae into baskets, making a game of who can catch the most.

The transition to adulthood comes in pieces. The scarification is one piece. The first solo platform-repair is another. For the eel-tenders, there is a night spent alone at the water's edge, singing to things that may or may not surface. For the bridge-weavers, a new span built without supervision, tested by the whole community walking it in single file. You see a young man standing at the far end of a bridge he has just completed, watching person after person cross, his hands clenched so tight the knuckles have gone pale.

---

The religion, if it is religion, lives in the doing. There are no temples. There are no priests. But there are moments when the whole settlement seems to pause, to synchronize—usually at dawn, usually at the first great swell of the frog-chorus, when the light comes gray-gold through the mist and every person who is awake falls silent for a count of perhaps twenty breaths. What they are doing in that silence, you cannot say. Listening, perhaps. Acknowledging. Being part of the sound by not adding to it.

There are also the ancestors, who are not gone. The dead are given to the water, weighted with stones, and the eels take them. This is spoken of openly, without horror—the eels carry the dead into the deep channels, into the places that connect to other water, and something of the person goes with them. When an eel surfaces that has recently fed, there is a moment of attention, a wondering about whose grandmother it might be carrying, and then the moment passes and work resumes.

The very old sometimes go to the water while still living. A great-grandmother, a man whose sickness will not release him, a woman who has simply decided it is time—they are helped to the edge, lowered in gently, and they do not swim. The eel-tenders sing. The named eels come. The person goes under, and what happens beneath the surface is not watched, but it is listened to, and it is accepted.

---

They will try to keep you, if you stay too long. Not through force—through incorporation. You will be offered food, and the food will be good, strange, the smoked eel-flesh and the sour tubers and the beetles roasted until they crunch. You will be offered space on a platform, just for tonight, and the night will be close and warm and full of unfamiliar breath. You will wake to find someone has rubbed the grease-paste on your exposed skin while you slept, because the biting things were gathering.

By the third day, someone will ask about your skills. What can you mend? What can you carry? Can you learn the songs? A child will start calling you by a name that is not yours, a name in their clicking, rising language, and others will pick it up, and you will start to answer to it. A platform-cluster will begin referring to your sleeping spot as if it were permanent. The boundaries of Emmelwound are porous, and you can feel yourself beginning to seep through.

Some have come here and never left. You see them occasionally: faces that don't quite match the others, bodies that never fully learned the low-centered walk, hands that still fumble with the weaving. They are Emmelwound now, as much as anyone is. They have names. Their children have the scarification. Their dead have gone to the eels.

The frogs call. The bridges sway. The rain begins again, and everyone adjusts, pulling cloaks from niches, angling bodies against the wet, continuing whatever they were doing. The hum beneath it all continues. And you realize that what you are watching is not a settlement but an organism, a single vast creature made of wood and rope and water and flesh, breathing and feeding and reproducing and maintaining itself through the coordinated labor of its countless cells.

The cells look up at you, curious and unsurprised. They make room on the platform. They offer you something to eat.
