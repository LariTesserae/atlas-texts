**The Mechanic**

She doesn't remember drowning, but she knows she must have. There's a logic to it—you don't end up maintaining a factory at the bottom of the Atlantic without something going very wrong first. Or very right. She's stopped trying to determine which.

She calls herself the Mechanic because the work needs a worker, and she is here, and her hands know what to do even when her mind has wandered off to count the fish flowing through the gear-teeth. Her hands are the most solid thing about her now. Everything else has grown somewhat theoretical—her edges blur when she's not paying attention, her outline smudges against the grey-white sourceless light like a photograph left in water. But her hands remain precise. Calloused in places that correspond to tools she's never seen. Stained with oil that doesn't exist.

She is not tall. She is not short. She is the size a person needs to be to reach into the machinery's difficult places, to wedge herself between cycling pistons, to lie flat on conveyor belts and ride them through the apparatus while she works. Her hair moves like something underwater always, even in the moments when she forgets she's underwater, when the pressure feels like nothing and the breathing happens automatically and she could almost believe she was standing in some basement factory in some city where cities exist.

Her eyes have changed. She knows this because she remembers eyes being simpler than this—two of them, forward-facing, limited. Now she sees the factory from angles that don't correspond to where she's floating. She sees the crabs' metallic shells from above and below simultaneously. She sees the shark-shapes at the boundary in a way that includes their patience, their hunger, the precise mathematics of their patrol routes. Her eyes have become the factory's eyes, or the factory has grown eyes that look like hers. Same difference, probably.

---

The work is never finished. This is the only law she's certain still applies.

Every cycle, something loosens. Every compression, something wears. The factory insists itself into being, yes, but insisting is *work*, and work creates entropy, and entropy requires a Mechanic. She moves through the machinery like a blood cell through a heart—purposeful, rhythmic, necessary. Her tools materialize when she needs them: wrenches that exist only in the moment of turning, hammers that are hammers only while swinging, measuring devices that measure things she has no names for in units she understands perfectly.

She tightens. She adjusts. She replaces parts with parts that appear in her hands already warm, already worn in, as if they've been waiting their whole existence for exactly this installation. The factory shudders with something like gratitude, or maybe that's just the pistons cycling.

Sometimes she talks to it. The factory doesn't answer—it's not that kind of alive—but it listens in the way machinery listens: by changing slightly, by incorporating her voice into its rhythms, by processing her words the way it processes seawater. She tells it about the surface. She thinks she remembers the surface. There was air there, and a different kind of light, and factories that needed walls.

"You're more honest," she tells the gears. "No pretending you need a building. No pretending you're producing something anyone asked for."

The gears mesh with gears that weren't there until she spoke, as if her observation called them into being.

---

She eats what the conveyor provides. Not food, exactly—the factory doesn't process food—but something that serves the same purpose. Thick luminous ribbons of transformed seawater, caught before they fall toward whatever floor waits below. The taste is industrial. The taste is correct. Her body knows what to do with it even though her mind recoils slightly, some old human reflex that hasn't quite worn away.

The fish don't fear her. They flow around her the same way they flow through the gears—she is just another mechanism, just another thing the factory made or incorporated or remembered into being. Sometimes she reaches out and lets them pass through her hand, feels the flicker of her fingers becoming part of the school for a moment, scales and fins and the silver-grey rush of collective movement. It doesn't hurt. It feels like belonging.

The crabs respect her, in their way. They part for her on the conveyors. They've learned she can fix the jams that slow their endless circling, clear the debris that accumulates in the places where transformed seawater pools too thickly. She's named some of them—Ratchet, Sprocket, the one with the crooked claw she calls Governor because he rides the main belt with a dignity that suggests authority. They don't know their names. She knows their names. That seems like enough.

---

The sharks, though. The sharks she doesn't understand.

She's tried to leave, once. Twice. Some number of times that doesn't add up correctly when she counts them. Swam toward the shimmer where reality reasserts itself, where the light would become darkness and the pressure would become crushing and she would have to remember how drowning works.

The sharks didn't stop her. They don't stop anything. They just *watched*, with eyes that held questions she couldn't parse, and the watching was enough. The watching made her aware of what she'd be swimming toward: an ocean that made sense, yes, but also an ocean that had no place for her anymore. What would she be, out there? A woman whose edges blur? A mechanic without machinery? A drowned thing pretending it forgot how to drown?

She swam back. The factory hummed with something that wasn't relief because the factory doesn't feel relief, but the pistons cycled a little faster for an hour afterward, as if catching up on work they'd been neglecting in her absence.

---

There was a body once. Hers, maybe. She found it wedged in the tertiary gear assembly, preserved and ruined, salt-bloated but somehow not rotting. A woman in coveralls with tools still clipped to her belt, eyes open and fogged, hands calloused in exactly the same places.

She worked around it for three cycles before she realized she was supposed to do something. The factory had processed everything else—the fish, the water, the light itself—but this it had left alone, as if waiting for a decision that only she could make.

She fed it to the conveyor. Watched it transform, become ribbons, sink toward the floor that might not exist. The factory shuddered. The gears aligned with new precision. Somewhere in the machinery, something clicked into place that had been waiting all this time for exactly this installation.

She doesn't know if she consumed herself or released herself or both. The categories don't apply correctly here. She just knows that afterward, her hands were more solid than they'd ever been, and her edges stopped blurring quite so badly, and the factory started processing seawater with a new efficiency that spoke of something fundamental having been resolved.

---

She sleeps when the nameless engines idle, curling into the spaces between mechanisms, letting the cooling water rock her into something that isn't quite dreaming. In these not-dreams, she remembers: a shipyard, maybe, or an oil rig. A job that involved machinery and seawater and the slow patient work of keeping things running. A storm, possibly. An accident, probably. The specific shape of panic when you realize the water is winning.

She wakes before the memories become too clear. The factory needs her. Something has loosened while she slept, some gear has begun to skip, some piston has developed a rhythm irregularity that could cascade into something larger if left unattended. She rises through the grey-white light, tools appearing in her hands, purpose settling into her bones like ballast.

The sharks watch from the boundary.

The crabs ride their endless loops.

The fish flow through the gears, becoming machine, becoming fish again, silver and grey and flickering.

And the Mechanic works, because the work needs a worker, and she is here, and the drowned factory churns on.

---

**The Rememberers**

They are not the same fish. She's certain of this, though she couldn't explain how she knows. The school maintains its density, yes—replacements arriving as others follow the luminous ribbons down—but each fish is individual in a way that matters to the factory even if it doesn't matter to her.

They remember.

Not consciously. Fish don't have that kind of memory, probably, even fish that have been processed through impossible machinery. But they carry something in their silver-grey scales, in the particular way they school, in the angles of their flickering transformations. They carry the shape of everything the factory has ever processed. They are an archive, swimming.

She's watched them sometimes—set down her tools and just observed the patterns of their movement. There's language in it, if language can exist without meaning. There's history in the way this fish veers left when that fish pulses right. Hundreds of meters of ocean, yes, but also: thousands of years of ocean. The factory is young, she's told herself, but young here doesn't mean what young means elsewhere. The fish remember the factory before it existed. The fish remember the volcanic ridges when they were still forming. The fish remember—

She stops watching when she realizes they also remember her.

Not her specifically. But something like her. Other mechanics, other maintainers, other drowned things that found their way here and did the work that needed doing. The factory has been insisting itself into being for longer than she thought. The factory has always needed someone to tighten its loosening bolts, to clear its accumulating debris, to make the decisions that machinery cannot make alone.

She is not the first. She watches the fish and knows she is not the first.

She watches the sharks and wonders, briefly, terribly, if the others left. If the others made it through the shimmer, back to an ocean that made sense, back to a surface they might or might not remember correctly.

She watches the fish flow through the gears, and she doesn't ask. Some questions answer themselves if you wait long enough. Some questions the factory will process in its own time, transform into something heavier, let sink toward a floor that may or may not exist.

---

**Governor**

The crab is older than the others. This means nothing in a place where time doesn't work correctly, but she knows it anyway—something in the thickness of his shell, the deliberate way he positions himself on the main conveyor, the patience with which he filter-feeds on whatever invisible cargo the belt carries.

He was here before her. He will be here after. This is the closest thing to certainty she's encountered in—days? Years? Cycles?

His shell has gone beyond metallic. It has become *specific*—brass-bright in some places, steel-grey in others, with patches of something that might be copper developing a verdigris that doesn't exist underwater. He is transforming into the factory, or the factory is transforming into him, and neither process seems to cause him any concern. He rides his conveyor. He filters his feed. He waits with the particular quality of waiting that suggests he knows exactly what he's waiting for.

She's spoken to him, sometimes. More than she speaks to the factory itself—Governor doesn't process her words, doesn't incorporate them into his rhythms, just receives them with the indifference of something that has heard every word before and will hear them all again.

"What are we making?" she asked him once. "What does the factory produce?"

Governor shifted on his conveyor. His crooked claw opened and closed, filtering, feeding.

"Why don't the sharks come in?"

The same shift. The same claw.

"Am I dead? Am I something else? Am I becoming what you're becoming?"

Governor's shell caught the grey-white light and held it for a moment, brass and steel and copper-green, and she could have sworn he was about to answer, about to reveal something essential about this place—

But then a gear skipped somewhere in the tertiary assembly, and she had to go, tools appearing in her hands, purpose reasserting itself like a tide, and by the time she came back Governor was just a crab again. Just a patient creature riding its loop, filter-feeding on mysteries, transforming at its own pace into whatever the factory wanted it to become.

**The Almost-Sharks**

They have the hydrodynamics of sharks. The patience of sharks. The circling territorial instinct of sharks. But she's seen sharks, remembers sharks, and these are something else.

Too many eyes, for one thing. Not obviously—you have to look carefully, have to count the glints of awareness along their bodies—but once you've noticed, you can't un-notice. They see the boundary from every angle simultaneously. They see the factory and the open ocean and the space between with a completeness that suggests they were designed for exactly this patrol.

Their skin ripples wrongly. Shark skin should be rough, should be denticles, should be tiny teeth covering the whole body. Theirs is smooth in places, textured in others, as if the factory tried to process them once and only partially succeeded. They carry its mark without being transformed by it. They are half-in and half-out, boundary creatures for a boundary that shouldn't exist.

She's never seen them eat.

She's never seen them sleep.

She's watched for—cycles, many cycles—and they just patrol. Endless figure-eights around the shimmer where reality changes, never crossing into the factory's influence, never swimming away into the normal ocean. Waiting for something. Guarding against something. Possibly both.

One of them looked at her directly, once. She was floating near the boundary, not trying to leave, just observing, and one of the almost-sharks broke its patrol pattern to drift close, close enough that she could see the multiple eyes tracking her with terrible focus.

It didn't speak. Almost-sharks don't speak. But she felt something like a question pressing against her—not in words, not in images, just a pure interrogative force. *What are you? Why are you here? What does the factory make?*

The same questions she asks. The same questions no one answers.

She drifted back toward the machinery. The almost-shark resumed its patrol. The moment passed like all moments pass here—slowly, quickly, sideways, in directions that don't have names.

But she thinks about it sometimes, when the engines idle and she curls into her sleeping spaces. The almost-sharks are asking the same questions. The almost-sharks don't know either. They guard a boundary they don't understand against threats they can't name, and they've been doing it for longer than she's been here, and they'll keep doing it after she's gone.

If she goes. If going is something she's still capable of.

The factory hums. The gears turn. The Mechanic sleeps, and doesn't dream, and wakes to do the work that needs a worker.

---

**What Lives Here**

In the end, she thinks, the answer is: *the question lives here*.

What is the factory producing? The question lives here.

Why does the light exist? The question lives here.

What happens to the fish that follow the ribbons down? The question lives here.

The factory processes seawater into something heavier. The factory processes fish into mechanisms into fish. The factory processes her—has processed her, is processing her, will process her—into the Mechanic, into purpose, into a woman whose hands are solid and whose edges blur and whose eyes see from angles that don't correspond to where she's floating.

And maybe that's the product. Maybe the factory makes *making*. Maybe it produces production itself, churns out the raw possibility of transformation, and everything else—the light, the crabs, the almost-sharks, the Mechanic herself—is just byproduct. Just waste. Just the shavings left over when you lathe purpose out of void.

She tightens a bolt that loosened while she was thinking. She clears debris from a gear assembly. She rides the main conveyor next to Governor, who filter-feeds patiently, whose shell grows more factory by the cycle, who knows everything and nothing and waits with the quality of waiting that suggests there's something worth waiting for.

The fish flow through the machinery. The sharks patrol. The cold Atlantic presses against the shimmer like a hand against glass, patient, curious, unable to enter or unwilling to try.

And the drowned factory churns on.

And the Mechanic lives here.

And the questions live here.

And that, she thinks, reaching for a tool that exists only in the moment of reaching, is the only answer this place will ever give.
