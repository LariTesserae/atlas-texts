**The Keeper**

She has been here for thirty-one years, or thirty-two—the calendars stopped being useful when the pages fused, and she stopped counting when counting stopped mattering. Her name was Margaret, once. Now she doesn't think of herself as having a name. She is simply the one who is here, which is identity enough when there is no one else to distinguish herself from.

She lives on the third floor, in the room with the least rot, the one where she long ago dragged a steel table from the control room to serve as a bed frame because steel doesn't soften and swell like wood. Her mattress is layers of canvas sheeting, replaced as each layer succumbs to mildew, pulled from a supply closet that still has nineteen more rolls. She will not outlive the canvas. This is a comfort.

Her body has adapted to this place the way the plants have adapted to the water. Her skin has taken on a permanent dampness, a slight give to it, as if she too has absorbed what hangs in the air. Her joints ache in ways she no longer notices, the ache having become simply the way joints feel. She breathes the wet air in shallow sips, her lungs long accustomed to working harder for less. When she coughs—and she coughs often, a wet, patient sound—she does not wonder what it means. Wondering would not change anything.

---

Her days have a rhythm that mirrors the pumps, that has synchronized with them so completely she cannot say whether she follows their schedule or they follow hers.

She wakes when the amber light through the window shifts from gray-amber to yellow-amber—not sunrise, exactly, but something the clouds do around that time. She descends to the second floor, placing her feet in the same spots on the damaged stairs, her body knowing the path the way a body knows how to breathe. In the control room, she wipes the faces of seven gauges—only seven, the ones she has decided matter, though she could not tell you why these and not others. She notes their positions in her mind, not on paper. Paper is useless here.

The gauges do not tell her anything actionable. She knows this. But the ritual of reading them structures the morning, and structure is what keeps a person solid in a place that wants everything to soften and dissolve.

---

She talks to the machinery. Not constantly, not madly—she is not mad, she is fairly certain, though she acknowledges she would be the last to know. She speaks to it the way one speaks to an animal that has been a companion for decades: brief observations, occasional questions that expect no answer, warnings when she is about to do something that might startle it if machinery could startle.

"Running hot today," she'll say, her hand on a pipe that is, in fact, slightly warmer than usual. The pipe does not respond, but something in the system shifts—a valve opening somewhere, a new hiss of steam from a vent she cannot see—and the pipe cools by a degree. Coincidence, probably. She does not believe in coincidence, but she does not believe in anything else either, so the distinction is academic.

She knows the sounds. This is her deepest knowledge, the thing she could not teach anyone even if anyone came to learn. She knows which clicks are normal and which are warnings. She knows when the subsonic hum shifts by a quarter-tone, and she knows that this means sediment is building in the intake pipes, and she knows which valve to open to flush it clear. She does not know *how* she knows. The knowledge grew into her the way the mineral deposits grew up through the grating: slowly, over years, until it was simply part of the structure.

---

When things break—and things do break, despite the building's apparent self-sufficiency—she fixes them. Her tools are kept in a canvas roll on the second floor, oiled weekly against the rust that takes everything else. She has wrenches and pliers and a welding torch with three remaining fuel canisters. She has wire and solder and a collection of gaskets salvaged from machines that were beyond saving. She has, in the back room of the first floor, a graveyard of parts: pump housings and pipe sections and valves of every size, all of it pulled from the marsh over the years, dragged back from the other pump houses she can see on the horizon.

She makes the journey four times a year, in the driest part of what passes for summer, when the water drops low enough that she can wade rather than swim. The other pump houses are empty of people but full of parts. She takes what she needs, catalogues what she leaves, and returns before the water rises again. The journeys take two days each way. She sleeps on the metal grating of the other buildings, her canvas bedroll spread over the mesh, the water black and warm below her.

She does not think of these journeys as lonely. She does not think of anything as lonely. The concept requires a comparison she no longer has access to.

---

There is a cat. Or there was—she is not certain whether it still lives, because she has not seen it in eleven days, which is either normal or terminal depending on the cat's mood. It appeared six years ago, walking up the loading dock as if it had always lived here, and it stayed because she fed it and because the building is warm and because cats make decisions that do not require explanation.

It was thin when it arrived and remains thin, a gray creature with eyes the color of the amber light, and it caught nothing in all its years here because there is nothing to catch. She feeds it from her own supplies: canned fish, canned meat, the protein bars that arrive in the shipments. It eats without enthusiasm and sleeps in the warmest corners and sometimes sits beside her on the third floor, looking out the window at the marsh with an expression of perfect indifference.

She does not love it, exactly. Love is another concept that has softened and dissolved in the wet air. But when it is absent for too many days, she notices, and noticing is close enough.

---

The shipments. This is the part she does not understand, and she has stopped trying.

Every three months, a boat arrives. Not a person—she has not seen another person in nine years—but a flat-bottomed barge that emerges from the mist to the west, piled with crates, and docks itself at the loading platform with a mechanical precision that suggests automation. The crates contain food, fuel, canvas, tools, replacement parts she did not know she needed until she opens them and realizes yes, that valve has been clicking, hasn't it. The crates contain exactly what she requires and nothing more.

She unloads them. She takes what she needs. She leaves the empty crates on the platform, and when the next shipment arrives, the old crates are gone. The barge hums with its own machinery, its own pumps and systems, and when it departs, it vanishes back into the mist as if it had never existed.

She has tried, once, to board the barge. To ride it back to wherever it comes from. But the moment she set foot on its deck, the engines cut, the silence absolute and sudden in a way that felt like a warning. She stepped back onto the loading platform, and the engines resumed, and the barge departed, and she did not try again.

She is meant to be here. The building runs because she is here to run it. This is a conclusion she has reached without evidence, but it feels true in the way the pump rhythms feel true, in the way the location of rot on the stairs feels true. She is a part of the system. The system provides for its parts.

---

At night—or what she calls night, when the gray light fades to a gray less light—she sits in the control room and listens.

The pumps cycle. The steam hisses. The subsonic hum settles into her chest, and she breathes with it, her heartbeat slowing to match its rhythm, until she is not certain where she ends and the building begins. This is not meditation. This is not spiritual practice. This is simply what happens when you have been somewhere long enough: you become continous with it. Your edges blur.

She thinks, sometimes, about the purpose. What the pumps are *for*. Water goes down and water comes up and water goes out into the marsh, and the marsh grows strange, and the water cycles back, and none of it seems to *accomplish* anything. But this is human thinking, she has decided. Purpose is a human concept. The building is not human. The building simply runs, and running is its own justification, and she—

She is part of its running. She tightens the valves and clears the sediment and replaces the parts that fail, and in doing so, she runs too. She is a process, not a person. Processes do not require purpose. They only require continuation.

---

There is a room she does not enter.

Subbasement level, accessible through a hatch in the first-floor grating, down a ladder that descends into the warm water and continues below it. She went down once, in her first year, and what she found there—

She does not think about what she found there. She replaced the hatch. She welded it shut. The pumps still draw from whatever is below, and whatever is below still feeds the system, and this is not her concern. Her concern is the machinery she can see, the parts she can touch, the rhythm she can hear. The rest is below the waterline, and below the waterline is not her domain.

Sometimes, at night, she hears sounds from beneath the grating. Not the bubbles—those are normal, those are just gas escaping from sediment, she is certain of this. Other sounds. Sounds like movement. Sounds like something shifting in the warm dark water, something large enough that its displacement sends ripples up through the pylons, vibrations she feels through the metal floor.

She does not investigate. She turns up the second-floor radio—it receives nothing but static, but static is a sound, and sound is company—and she waits until the sounds stop, and then she sleeps, and in the morning she does not remember.

---

She will die here. She knows this the way she knows which pipes are hot and which valves need attention. Her body will fail—is already failing, slowly, the way everything here fails slowly—and when it does, she will become part of the sediment. The pumps will continue. The system will adapt. Perhaps another person will come, another keeper, delivered on the barge with the canned fish and the canvas rolls. Perhaps not. Perhaps the building will simply run without her, as it ran before she came, as it will run after.

This does not frighten her. Fear, like loneliness, like love, like purpose, has dissolved in the wet air. What remains is only the rhythm: pump and rest, breathe and release, maintain and continue. She is a function. Functions do not fear their termination. They simply run until they stop.

---

For now: she wipes the gauges. She checks the valves. She listens for the clicks that mean danger and the hums that mean health. She watches the marsh through the third-floor window, the strange plants swaying in currents that have no wind to cause them, the other pump houses distant on the horizon, their own amber lights flickering in their own unknowable rhythms.

She is not happy. She is not unhappy. She is *present*, which is the only state this place allows, the only state that survives the damp and the hum and the long slow years of continuation.

The cat returns on the twelfth day, stepping through the rusted doors as if it had never left. She feeds it. It eats without enthusiasm. They sit together in the amber light, two processes running, and the pumps cycle, and the building breathes, and the marsh accepts them both as it accepts everything: without judgment, without purpose, with only the patience of water finding its level.
