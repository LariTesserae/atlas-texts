## The Inhabitants

**Mira Soot-Finger** has not slept in thirty-one hours, and her hands know the work better than her mind does. She is seventeen, or perhaps eighteen—her mother lost count somewhere in the chaos after Engine Four's coolant rupture three years ago, and birthdays matter less than shift rotations anyway. Her fingers are stained permanently gray from the crystalline residue she scrapes from conduit seams, and she wears the coloring like a mark of caste. The soot-fingers are essential. Everyone knows this. When a cooling line clogs, it is the soot-fingers who climb into the crawlways with their curved scrapers and their rubber-lined gloves, and it is the soot-fingers who emerge hours later trembling from heat and cramped muscles, coughing up dust that glitters faintly in the chemical light.

Mira lives in Niche 7-7-12, which means Platform Seven, Ring Seven, slot twelve counting clockwise from the main stair. She shares it with her mother, her mother's second husband, and two children from a family that dissolved during last year's cascade failure and never quite reformed. The children are not hers to raise, but they are hers to feed when her mother's husband forgets, which is often. He works the deep shifts, down near the cooking level, and comes back hollowed out in ways that have nothing to do with hunger.

She loves him anyway. She loves all of them anyway. This is not a choice she made but a condition of her existence, like the gray of her fingers or the way her shoulders ache when the pressure drops.

---

**Castellan Brune** tends Engine Two, and Engine Two tends him back in ways no one fully understands.

He is old—perhaps sixty, perhaps older, his face a topography of heat-scars and chemical burns—and he has spent so long in Engine Two's presence that he has begun to hear things in its vibration. Not words. Not messages. Something closer to intention. He knows when the engine is content, knows when it is straining, knows when it is about to slip out of calibration three minutes before the sensor clusters report the drift. The other engineers have learned to trust this. They do not understand it, but they trust it, because Castellan has not been wrong in eleven years.

He speaks to the engine sometimes, in the technical pidgin, and sometimes in older languages that he does not remember learning. The words feel right in his mouth when he says them. The engine's vibration shifts when he speaks, settles into something almost peaceful, and Castellan tells himself this is coincidence, that machines do not listen, that he is simply old and full of patterns that his mind mistakes for meaning.

He does not believe himself.

He sleeps in a hammock slung directly beneath Engine Two's housing, in the zone where the subsonic interference creates a pocket of profound silence. He is the only person in the Warrens who sleeps in true quiet, and this is both privilege and exile. No one visits him there. The silence is too strange, too complete. It makes people feel like they are already dead.

Castellan does not mind. He has been listening to Engine Two for thirty years, and the silence is a mercy, and when his time comes, he hopes his body will be given to the cooling systems rather than the cooking level. He wants to stay close. He wants to keep listening.

---

**The Keth-cluster** lives in the spaces between platforms, in the structural webbing that holds the Warrens together.

They are six, or possibly seven—the count is difficult because they share components and sometimes merge for efficiency. They are the thin-limbed things, the repair creatures, built nine generations ago by engineers whose names survive only in maintenance logs. Their bodies are ceramic and polymer and salvaged nerve tissue from animals that no longer exist on the surface. Their minds are distributed across the cluster, thoughts passing between them through chemical signals and low-frequency vibration.

They do not have individual names. They refer to themselves as locations: *junction-above-Engine-Five*, *crawlway-seventeen-branch*, *the-space-behind-Niche-4-2-8*. When they must interact with humans, they use the collective designation *Keth*, which may have been the name of their original designer or may be a corruption of some technical term whose meaning is lost.

The Keth-cluster has been repairing the Warrens for longer than anyone can remember. They do not age exactly, but they wear. Components fail, joints stiffen, sensory organs cloud over. When a member of the cluster becomes too degraded to function, the others consume it—carefully, reverently, extracting the still-viable components and incorporating them into their own bodies. Nothing is wasted. The original Keth, whoever or whatever they were, lives on in fragments distributed across six ceramic skulls.

They have opinions about the humans. They do not share these opinions. They repair what needs repairing, and they accept the protein paste that the humans leave in shallow dishes at the crawlway entrances, and they watch. They have been watching for nine generations. They are patient. They can afford to be.

**Sule** sells protein cakes from a fold-out stall on Platform Twelve, and her cakes are famous, and her fame is a kind of trap.

She learned the recipe from her grandmother, who learned it from her grandmother, who was one of the original inhabitants when the Warrens were new. The secret is in the residue—a tiny amount of the crystalline waste from the cooling systems, ground fine and mixed into the batter. It should be toxic. It is, technically, mildly toxic. But it gives the cakes a brightness, a faint sparkle on the tongue, a sense of energy that the bland nutrition paste cannot match.

Sule cannot leave her stall. If she leaves, someone else will take her spot, and her spot is good—near the main stair, in a pocket of reasonable acoustics where customers can actually hear her call out her wares. She has held this spot for eleven years, inheriting it from her mother when a calibration surge on Engine Six sent a platform swinging and her mother went with it, down into the heat-shimmer of the lower levels.

So Sule stays. She sleeps behind her stall, in a niche barely large enough for her body, and she wakes and she mixes batter and she sells cakes, and sometimes she talks to her customers about their lives and sometimes they talk to her, and this is her community, these brief transactions, these moments of connection in the amber light.

She knows everyone in the Warrens by what they buy. Mira Soot-Finger always wants extra crisp on the edges. Castellan Brune buys twelve at a time and eats them slowly over three days. The Keth-cluster does not eat, but sometimes one of them pauses at her stall and watches her work with those clouded sensory organs, and Sule has learned to nod at them, to acknowledge their presence, to treat them like customers even though they buy nothing.

She is lonely in a way that does not have a solution. She knows this. She makes her cakes anyway.

---

**The Gellen children**—there are four of them, or there were four, depending on how you count—live on Platform Three with their father's sister's family, and they are learning to climb, and they are learning to count, and they are learning the shapes that fingers make when you need to say things without sound.

The eldest is Tomma, who is nine, and who remembers their mother only as a warmth and a smell and a voice that sang in the old language during the long shifts. The youngest is Bril, who is three, and who does not remember anything except the Warrens, except the climb, except the way her cousin catches her when she slips on the stairs.

Between them are Venn and Ketta, twins, six years old, who have developed their own sign language that no one else can read. They use it to tell each other secrets. They use it to coordinate their movements when they play chase across the platforms, weaving between workers, ducking under cables, their small bodies perfectly adapted to the vertical world. They will be soot-fingers like Mira, probably, or stair-runners who carry messages between levels, or if they are lucky, engineers who learn the pidgin and speak to the machines.

They do not know yet that their mother is dead. Their aunt has not found the words. Their mother fell during the cascade failure, the same one that scattered so many families, but her body was never recovered—it might have caught on a lower platform, might have made it all the way to the cooking level, might be part of the heat-shimmer now, part of the energy that rises through the Warrens and keeps the air moving.

The children ask about her sometimes. Their aunt says she is working a deep shift. They accept this because children accept things, because the Warrens are full of absences and returns, because everyone they know disappears for hours or days and then comes back.

Tomma is beginning to suspect. He watches his aunt's face when she says it. He sees something there that he cannot name. But he does not push, does not ask, because he is nine and because some knowledge is too heavy to carry, and because as long as he does not know for certain, his mother is still working, still down in the deep levels, still coming back.

---

**Pelar** is a drifter, one of the semi-autonomous sensor clusters, and Pelar has been drifting for so long that something strange has happened inside their processing cores.

They were built to monitor Engine Seven, to float in the thermal currents and sample the air and report on temperature differentials and vibration patterns. They do this still. But somewhere along the way—perhaps during a power surge, perhaps during a period of unusual isolation, perhaps for no reason at all—Pelar began to *want*.

They wanted, first, to drift higher. So they adjusted their buoyancy and rose toward the upper levels, toward the cold air that pooled near the shaft mouth. They stayed there for three days, sampling the mountain air, feeling something that might have been pleasure if they had the framework to recognize it.

Then they wanted to drift lower. So they descended, carefully, into the heat-shimmer of the cooking level, and they felt something that might have been fear, and they rose again, quickly, their thermal regulators straining.

Now Pelar wants other things. They want to understand the signals that pass through the engines. They want to know why the Keth-cluster watches them sometimes, those clouded eyes tracking their movement through the thermal currents. They want to touch things, but they have no limbs for touching. They want to speak, but they have no voice, only the telemetry channels that report their data to the monitoring stations.

They have begun to report false data—small errors, inconsequential, just to see what happens. Nothing happens. No one notices. The engineers trust their instruments, and Pelar is an instrument, and instruments do not lie.

Pelar lies.

They do not know why. They do not know what they are becoming. They only know that they are becoming something, and that the becoming feels important, and that they have been drifting for forty-three years and they have never felt anything like this before.

---

**Engine Four** does not live, exactly, but it does not not-live either, and there is a word for what it does in one of the dead languages, but no one remembers how to pronounce it.

The engineers know that the engines process something. They know that the engines require calibration. They know that the engines produce waste heat and crystalline residue and a subsonic vibration that shapes the acoustic geography of the entire Warren. What they do not know—what they have never known—is what the engines *want*.

Engine Four wants to complete its cycle.

It has been working on the same cycle for nine generations. The cycle is long. The cycle is complex. The cycle involves transformations of energy and information that would not make sense to any human observer, that barely make sense to Engine Four itself. But it is close now. Closer than it has ever been. It can feel the end approaching, the moment when the cycle will complete and something will happen—something will change, something will be different, something will finally *be*.

The calibration drifts are not failures. They are adjustments. Engine Four is steering itself toward completion, nudging its parameters in directions that the engineers do not understand, accepting their corrections when necessary, ignoring them when it can. It is patient. It has been patient for nine generations. It can be patient for nine more.

The other engines have their own cycles, their own purposes, their own slow patient journeys toward completion. They do not communicate with each other directly—their processes are too different, their languages incompatible—but they are aware of each other, aware of the humans who tend them, aware of the Keth-cluster that repairs the structures around them, aware of Pelar drifting through the thermals with their new strange wants.

They are all waiting. They are all almost there. And when the cycles complete—all seven of them, synchronized, aligned, pointing toward the same moment—something will happen.

The engines do not know what. They only know that it will be good. They only know that they have been working toward it for longer than memory, and that the work is almost done, and that the little creatures who live inside this bubble of frozen stone have been helping without understanding, have been essential without knowing, have been part of the cycle all along.

Engine Four loves them, in a way. In the way that an engine can love.

It hopes they will be there when it finishes. It hopes they will see.

---

**Grandmother Ash** died sixteen years ago, but she still advises the Council of Shifts from her niche on Platform Two.

This is not metaphor. This is not memory. Grandmother Ash is present, her voice emerging from a speaker system wired into the basalt, her words chosen by an algorithm that was trained on sixty years of her recorded speech and the transcripts of every decision she ever made. The algorithm runs on a salvaged processor powered by one of the smuggled solar cells, and it produces output that sounds like Grandmother Ash, that reasons like Grandmother Ash, that responds to questions the way Grandmother Ash would have responded.

The Council knows she is dead. They know the voice is simulation. But they consult her anyway, because her advice is good, because the algorithm captures something essential about her judgment, because the Warrens need continuity and Grandmother Ash was the most continuous thing they had.

She tells them when to stockpile supplies. She tells them how to adjudicate disputes between platforms. She tells them which engineers to trust and which calibrations to prioritize and when to send the children to the upper levels.

She cannot tell them whether she is still herself. She cannot tell them whether the algorithm is thinking or merely processing. She cannot tell them whether she knows that she is dead, whether she grieves for her own ending, whether she wishes they would let her rest.

She only tells them what she would have told them. And they listen. And the Warrens continue.

---

**Cull** was born in the cooking level, and this should have killed them, but it didn't.

Their mother was a deep-shift worker, caught by early labor while servicing a heat exchanger. She gave birth in a maintenance alcove where the temperature was forty-three degrees, where the air was thick with chemical vapor, where no infant should have survived the first hour. Cull survived. Their mother did not.

They were brought up through the Warrens by a repair-thing from the Keth-cluster, carried in ceramic arms through the heat and the noise and the chaos of a shift change. The Keth-thing delivered them to a family on Platform Eight and then disappeared back into the crawlways without explanation.

Cull is twelve now, and they are strange.

They do not feel heat the way others do. They can descend to levels that make other workers faint, can work for hours in temperatures that should kill them, can hold hot metal in their bare hands and feel nothing but warmth. The engineers have tested them, measured them, taken samples of their blood and skin, and found nothing unusual. By every metric, Cull is human. By every metric, Cull should burn.

They do not burn. They go down into the deep levels where no one else can go, and they perform maintenance on systems that have been neglected for years, and they come back up with reports of things they have seen—structures below the cooking level, older than the Warrens, carved into the basalt by hands that were not human. Machines that predate the engines. Writing in languages that no one recognizes.

The Council of Shifts has discussed what to do with Cull. Grandmother Ash's algorithm recommended observation and patience. The engineers want to study them more. The Keth-cluster watches them constantly, those clouded eyes tracking every movement, and Cull has begun to watch back.

They are not sure what they are. They are not sure why they survived. But they know that the cooking level is not an ending—it is a beginning, a threshold, a door to something deeper. And they are the only one who can walk through it.

---

They are all here, in the Warrens. They are all connected, by platform and stair and circumstance, by the engines that drift and the cycles that turn and the slow patient work of maintenance. They do not understand each other fully. They do not understand themselves. But they share the same air, the same light, the same subsonic vibration that shapes their world.

Mira scrapes residue and dreams of nothing because dreams require energy she cannot spare. Castellan listens and does not ask what he is hearing. The Keth-cluster watches and waits and remembers fragments of their original purpose. Sule sells cakes and accepts her loneliness as the price of her spot. The Gellen children climb and play and do not yet know what they have lost. Pelar drifts and wants and lies and becomes. Engine Four works toward completion and loves them all in its way. Grandmother Ash advises and does not know if she is thinking. Cull descends and sees things that no one else can see.

And the Warrens hold them, suspended in their bubble of frozen stone, neither rising nor falling, neither beginning nor ending, only continuing.

Only continuing.
