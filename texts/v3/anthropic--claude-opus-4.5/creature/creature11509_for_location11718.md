# The Keepers of the Swell

**Maret** is here, though you won't see her until she moves.

She's been lying in the moss for six hours—sunk into it, really, her body having pressed a Maret-shaped depression into the saturated cushion. Only her face stays above the surface, tilted toward the sky, eyes closed against the violent light. She's wearing what everyone who works here wears: a suit of layered wool felt, hand-felted dense enough to insulate against the cold water that seeps through everything, loose enough to let her move when she needs to. It was grey once. Now it's the color of the moss, stained and colonized, small fronds actually beginning to root in the fabric at her shoulders and hips. She doesn't mind. She likes the blurring.

She's listening.

Not to the obvious sounds—the grinding, the water-roar, the wind. Those are too big to hear, really; they become the condition of hearing anything else. She's listening *through* them, the way you might listen through radio static for a faint transmission. Every few hours, if she stays still enough, she catches something: a specific frequency in the underground grinding that tells her which section of substrate is shifting. A change in the pitch of water running through a particular channel. Information the monitoring station used to gather automatically, before the instruments fused and swelled and stopped. Now Maret gathers it with her body, with her ears pressed to the moss, with the patience that this place demands.

She has been here for eleven years. She looks older than she is.

---

**The Gelbek brothers** maintain the pylons—what can be maintained.

There are three of them. There were four. The fourth, Enn, is buried somewhere in the basin, under moss that has long since swallowed the marker they placed. He fell from a pylon in the third year, during a wind event that exceeded anything in the logbooks. The brothers don't speak of him often, but they speak *to* him sometimes, when they're alone on the towers, shouting into the roar. They believe he can hear them. They believe the moss absorbed him the way it absorbs everything, and that he's distributed now across the basin floor, still listening, still part of the work.

The remaining three—**Yonn**, **Daved**, and **Pell**—take shifts. One climbs while two anchor the ropes below. They move slowly, testing each handhold, each footrest, because the rust is always worse than it looks and the moss hides the weakness until you're already trusting it with your weight. Their hands are extraordinary: callused beyond any normal toughness, the skin cracked and healed and cracked again until it's become something closer to bark than flesh. They can feel structural integrity through their palms. They know, before any instrument could tell them, when a bolt is about to give.

They don't repair the lines anymore—the original power grid is beyond saving. Instead, they're *repurposing*. Slowly, over years, they've been stringing new cables between the pylons: not for electricity but for communication. Simple flag-lines, pulley systems, a network of signals that can be seen across the basin when the light is right. Two tugs means *shift change*. Three means *bring water*. A sustained back-and-forth flutter means *come now, something is wrong*.

Daved, the eldest, has started teaching the system to the children.

---

There are **children**.

You wouldn't expect it, in a place this raw and demanding. But there are seven of them, ages four through fifteen, and they're the reason any of this continues.

They live in a structure you haven't seen yet—it's on the basin's western edge, built *into* the moss rather than on top of it. The builders (long gone now, or absorbed) excavated a hollow in one of the thicker cushions, then reinforced it with bent iron salvaged from the monitoring station, then let the moss grow back over and around until the whole thing became a kind of living dome. Inside, it's warm. The moss insulates; the children's bodies heat the enclosed space; moisture condenses on the inner walls and runs down to collection channels they've dug into the floor. They have clean water, always. They have warmth, mostly. They have very little else.

The oldest, **Kire**, remembers the lowlands. She was six when her family came here, fleeing something she's never fully explained—not to the other children, not to Maret, not even to herself in the privacy of her own thoughts. She knows only that down there was worse. Up here is hard, but it's not *wrong*. She has taken on the role of teacher, though she dislikes the word. She shows the younger ones how to read the moss: which patches are safe to walk on, which will swallow you to the hip; which colors indicate fresh growth, which mean something is dying underneath; how to find the small hard nodules of calcium deposit that the moss accumulates, which can be ground into a powder that treats the rashes everyone gets from constant wet exposure.

The youngest, **Bem**, is four and has never seen a tree. She thinks the pylons are what trees used to be, before the moss came. No one has corrected her. She may, in some sense, be right.

---

**The Machinist** has no other name anyone uses.

She's old—sixty, maybe seventy, her age hard to read under the weathering this place inflicts on skin. She lives in the pumping station, in a back room she's cleared and dried by sheer force of will, running a small stove that burns peat cut from the drier eastern margins of the basin. The stove is against every principle of moss-keeping. It damages the local ecosystem. It releases carbon that the moss would otherwise slowly sequester. The Machinist does not care. She has decided that she will be warm, and dry, and alive to maintain the machines, and that this is worth the cost.

Because the machines *matter*.

Not for their original purpose—no one remembers exactly what that was, and the logbooks that might have explained are pulped beyond reading. But the pumping station has become something else: a *regulator*. The Machinist has spent decades learning its rhythms, adjusting its flows, using it to prevent the catastrophic drainage events that would strip the moss from whole sections of basin floor. She's not draining the plateau; she's *balancing* it. When too much water accumulates in the eastern pools, threatening to burst through and carve a new channel, she opens certain valves and redirects the pressure. When the grinding underneath grows too intense in one sector, she can—sometimes—relieve it by pumping water into specific underground fissures, lubricating the stone, easing the violence of the earth's settling.

She does this mostly by feel. The dials still work, some of them, but she rarely looks at them. She puts her hand on the pipes and knows.

She is teaching no one. When she dies, the station will continue running without guidance, doing whatever it does, until it stops. She has made peace with this. The plateau was being born before humans came; it will continue after they leave.

---

**The salamanders** are not what you thought.

There are more of them than that single flicker suggested—dozens, maybe hundreds, living in the deeper pools and the water-channels that run beneath the moss surface. They're large, larger than any salamander has a right to be: the biggest are nearly a meter long, their bodies pale and soft, their eyes adapted to the dim green light that filters through the moss-ceiling above their pools. They've been here longer than the pylons. Longer than the monitoring station. Possibly longer than the mountains themselves, their ancestors having colonized the warm seeps that existed before the uplift, surviving the catastrophic changes by retreating deeper, adapting, waiting.

The children know about them. The adults pretend not to.

This is a matter of some tension in the community. Maret believes the salamanders are essential—that they're part of the water system, their movements through the channels helping to prevent stagnation, their bodies cycling nutrients through the pools. She's argued that they should be studied, protected, integrated into the community's understanding of how the basin works. The Machinist thinks this is sentimental nonsense. The Gelbek brothers are afraid of them, though they won't admit it. Yonn saw one once, fully surfaced, its pale body catching the light, and he has not been able to shake the image: something *ancient*, something that was here before any of them and will be here after, watching from the deep water with eyes that don't need the violent sun.

Kire has been feeding them.

She discovered, two years ago, that they'll come to the surface at night, when the light dims and the roar seems somehow quieter. She brings them scraps from the peat-cutting: roots, grubs, the small invertebrates that live in the drier margins. She doesn't know if they need the food. She thinks, maybe, they just like the company. She has named the largest one *Enn*, though she's never told the Gelbek brothers this.

---

And then there's **the one who walks the perimeter**.

No one knows his name. He showed up three years ago, during the spring melt, when the water was at its highest and the roar was so loud that conversation was impossible for weeks. He walked out of the eastern pass, alone, carrying nothing but a staff cut from some lowland wood that no longer exists in his possession—the moss ate it within months. He didn't speak. He still doesn't, not in words. He lives on the move, circling the basin's edge in a slow continuous patrol, sleeping in the moss when he needs to, eating what he can find, which isn't much.

Maret thinks he's mad. The Machinist thinks he's a surveyor, sent by some lowland authority to assess the plateau for future exploitation, driven to silence by what he found. Daved thinks he's holy: a pilgrim, walking a circuit of devotion, witnessing the birth of the landscape as a form of prayer.

The children think he's a ghost. Specifically, Enn's ghost—the fourth Gelbek brother, returned in another form to continue watching over the pylons.

The truth is probably simpler. He's someone who found this place, and couldn't leave, and couldn't stay still either. The roar won't let him rest. The light won't let him close his eyes. So he walks, and walks, and walks, wearing a slow path through the moss that heals behind him almost as fast as he travels it, leaving no permanent trace, becoming part of the basin's own restlessness, its constant motion, its refusal to settle into any final shape.

He has been seen, at night, standing at the edge of the deepest pool, looking down. Kire thinks he's watching the salamanders. She thinks they're watching him back.

---

These are the keepers of the Roaring Nursery: the listeners and the climbers, the children and the machinist, the ancient amphibians and the silent walker. They did not build this place. They cannot control it. They can only be present while it becomes itself, doing the small slow work of survival at the edge of something that doesn't need them, that was here before them, that will continue long after.

The light doesn't let them look away either.

So they stay. They listen. They climb. They walk the perimeter, circle after circle, while the plateau roars beneath them and the moss grows over everything, patient and green and absolutely certain of its own time.
