**The Keeper of Mechanisms**

She has no name she would recognize as such, but the pigeons know her by the pattern of her movements, and the carp have learned that her shadow passing overhead means food will follow.

She came down here eleven years ago, following the water. Before that she kept a room in a building that no longer stands above the surface, and before that she did something with numbers in an office, and before that she was a child somewhere dry. These memories have become like the sediment on the floor below—present, foundational, invisible.

Her home is the control booth at the walkway's midpoint, a steel-framed box with windows on three sides that once let an engineer monitor the pump gauges. The gauges are dark now, their needles frozen at meaningless positions, but she has made the space her own. A sleeping mat of layered cloth. Containers for catching condensation. A small collection of objects pulled from the water over the years: a brass doorknob, a ceramic cup with half its handle missing, a child's shoe she has never been able to throw back.

She is perhaps sixty, though time moves strangely here. Her hair she keeps short with a knife, purely practical—long hair tangles, catches, becomes a problem when she leans over the water. Her hands are permanently wrinkled from immersion, the skin of her palms pale and soft no matter how long she stays dry. She wears layers she has scavenged: a wool sweater that once belonged to someone who worked on boats, canvas pants she has patched eleven times with eleven different materials, rubber boots that leak at the left toe but remain better than bare feet on the rusted walkways.

What does she do? She *tends*.

Every morning—though morning is a fiction she maintains by watching the light through the grates—she walks the full circuit of the walkway, checking. The willow sapling she monitors for signs of rot where its trunk meets the crack; twice she has packed the gap with clay she carries down from a deposit she found in the western tunnel. The ferns in their emergency housings she cups water for when the condensation runs thin. The pigeon nests she leaves alone unless a bird is clearly dying, in which case she will wring its neck quickly and drop the body to the carp, because a slow death spreads disease through a flock and she has seen what happens when disease takes hold in an enclosed space.

The carp she feeds when she can. Bread that has gone too stale to eat, or insects she catches along the walkway, or scraps of whatever she has scavenged from the upper tunnels. The large bronze one—the twenty-pounder that patrols the hall's center with proprietary calm—she calls Old Parliament, because of the way the other fish defer to it, parting around its bulk. She does not love Old Parliament. She is not sentimental about the fish. But she recognizes that its presence keeps a certain order, and she approves of order.

She talks to herself constantly, a low muttering narration of her activities that has become indistinguishable from thought. "Check the eastern intake, check the eastern intake, sounds wrong today, grinding underneath the grinding." Her voice arrives at the far end of the hall seconds later, reflected and strange, and sometimes she answers it as though it were a different person, because in a sense it is.

The pumps she cannot fix. She knows this. They are beyond her, beyond anyone now, their joints frozen with rust that would take machinery to break and knowledge to repair that exists nowhere in the flooded world. But she maintains them anyway. She scrapes the worst of the corrosion from the brass nameplates—*Metropolitan Water Authority, 1908*—and polishes them with rags until the letters gleam. She oils the visible joints with fish fat, knowing it does nothing, performing the gesture anyway. She believes, in a way she could not articulate, that the pumps deserve witness. That someone should remember they once worked, that the people who built them meant them to work forever.

Sometimes—rarely—others come.

A man arrived two years ago, following the tunnel system from somewhere east. He stayed for three weeks, sleeping on the walkway, trying to convince her to leave with him. "There's a community," he said. "Forty people, maybe fifty. Dry buildings. They're growing food." She listened politely. She fed him from her stores. When he left, she felt only relief, and something that might have been sorrow for the version of herself that could have wanted what he offered.

Before him, a woman with a child passed through, staying only two nights. The child was fascinated by the carp, leaned too far over the railing trying to touch them. The keeper caught him by the back of his shirt and did not let go until they were well away from the edge. The mother looked at her with something like fear, or recognition, and they left the next morning through the southern tunnel without saying goodbye.

She prefers the animals. The animals do not ask her to be anything other than what she is: a presence that moves along the walkway, that drops food into water, that makes sounds that are not threatening. The mallards have grown so accustomed to her that they will paddle within arm's reach while she sits with her feet dangling above the surface. She does not touch them. That would be a violation of the terms they have established.

At night—another fiction, maintained—she lies in the control booth and listens to the water. The plunk of drips. The gurgle of slow currents. The deep resonant groan when the municipal pumps three blocks away cycle on. She has learned to read these sounds like weather. A change in the dripping pattern means rain above, water finding its way down through new channels. An increase in the current's gurgle means something has shifted in the blocked intake valves, and she will need to investigate in the morning. The groan of the distant pumps she simply endures, a reminder that somewhere the old systems still strain against the flood, still try to impose order on water that will not be ordered.

She dreams about the floor. Twelve feet below the surface, invisible, tile laid in patterns she has never seen. Sometimes in the dreams she can breathe underwater, and she swims down through the weak-tea murk, past Old Parliament and the schools of silver minnows, until her feet touch the accumulated sediment, and she walks through the hall as it was meant to be walked, dry and echoing and full of purpose. She wakes from these dreams with her face wet, and she cannot tell if it is condensation or something else.

The heron came once. She saw it standing at the walkway's edge, utterly still, watching the water with an intensity she recognized. They regarded each other for perhaps five minutes—she on her morning circuit, the heron frozen in its hunting posture—and then it lifted on enormous wings and was gone through one of the ceiling grates, up toward whatever remained of the sky. She has not seen it since. She does not expect to. But she leaves a section of walkway clear now, near the best fishing spot, just in case.

What does she believe? She is not sure. The question seems to belong to the dry world, to the time before. But if pressed, if someone arrived who could press her, she might say this: that the cistern house is a heart. That water moves through it the way blood moves through chambers. That the fish and the birds and the ferns and the tilting willow are the life that a heart sustains, and that she—she is nothing so grand as its keeper. She is merely the part of the heart that notices itself beating.

She will die here. This is not tragedy; it is simply fact, like the rust on the pump joints, like the light that filters down through the grates. When she goes, Old Parliament will continue its patrols, and the pigeons will coo their endless undersong, and the willow will grow another inch toward the light. The brass nameplates will tarnish again. The control booth will fill slowly with dust and then with nests and then with the patient accumulation of years.

And the water will move through, slowly, following pressure gradients too subtle to see, and the whole chamber will go on breathing without her, as it breathed before she came, as it will breathe long after the last built thing has finally fallen into the flood.
