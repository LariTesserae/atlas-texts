**The Tenders**

They were human once, or their ancestors were, in the era when the machines below still answered to hands and voices. Now they are something that continues.

You see them first as interruptions in the mist—places where the knee-high vapor curls differently, where it rises rather than drifts. They stand so still that your eye slides past them twice, three times, before catching on the wrongness of their silhouettes. Too patient for people. Too vertical for the landscape's slow undulations.

When they move, they move like the plateau breathes: gradually, without beginning or end, a drift from one position to another that seems less like walking and more like the adjustment of something rooted. Their feet—if they are feet—don't leave the weeping prints that yours do. The moss doesn't mark their passage. They have been here long enough that the ground no longer considers them foreign bodies.

Their skin has taken on the plateau's palette. Not green exactly—nothing here is simply green—but the grey-brown-viridian of moss compressed past memory, shot through with darker threads that might be veins or might be something the ground has given them in exchange for what they've given it. Their eyes, when they turn toward you, are the color of the sky's permanent bruise, and they do not blink. Perhaps they have forgotten. Perhaps the mist keeps their eyes wet enough that blinking became unnecessary, and what is unnecessary here eventually becomes impossible.

They don't speak. Not anymore. But they *transmit*.

---

You feel it before you understand it: a pressure behind your forehead, a sense of information arriving through channels you didn't know you had. Images, impressions, the ghosts of concepts. The Tenders are showing you what they tend.

They remember the machines. Not as the plateau remembers them—as food, as slow incorporation—but as *purpose*. The vast grinding processes below were built for something, and the Tenders' ancestors were built to oversee that something, and though the something itself has been digested into meaninglessness, the *tending* remains. It has become its own reason. They walk the plateau reading vibrations the way their predecessors read gauges. They know where the fissures will open before the silk-tearing begins. They know which undulations are safe to traverse and which are the plateau dreaming of the peaks it ate.

They tend the fog-waders too, though it looks nothing like tending. A Tender will stand beneath one of those impossible stilt-birds for hours, days, both of them motionless, both of them waiting. Something passes between them. The fog-wader's mechanical clicking takes on new rhythms. When it walks again, it walks with direction. The Tenders are giving it something like memory, teaching it paths through the maze of the plateau's moods.

And the herds—the moss-colored, milk-eyed oxen—follow the Tenders without commands. They simply know. When a Tender drifts toward safe ground, the herd drifts behind, and when the grinding below shifts toward something dangerous, the Tender is already moving, already guiding, its bruise-colored eyes reading futures in the mist.

---

There are young ones.

You don't see them at first because they are still learning stillness. They move too quickly, their silhouettes too recognizably child-shaped, their skin still showing pink beneath the creeping plateau colors. They follow the older Tenders at distances that would seem careless if you didn't understand: they are learning to read the ground. Their feet still leave weeping prints. The moss still considers them visitors.

But you watch one crouch beside a fissure as it begins to seal, and you see her press her palm flat against the knitting moss, and you see her eyes go distant, receiving something the ground is telling her. When she stands, she drifts. Just slightly. Just the beginning of that patient, rootless movement that will eventually become the only movement she knows.

The older Tenders do not teach. They do not need to. They simply *are*, and the young ones watch, and the plateau watches, and slowly the children become what they are watching: intermediaries between the living ground and everything that lives upon it.

Some of them tend the factories that walk.

You see a Tender far out in the deep mist, at the edge of where shapes stop resolving. It stands against one of those vast ambulatory silhouettes, hand pressed flat against something that might be metal or might be moss or might be the place where those categories dissolve. The factory-shape has stopped moving. The grinding below it has shifted into that temporary harmonic, that almost-music. The Tender is listening to something or transmitting something or both simultaneously, and the factory-shape is *answering*, its bulk tremoring with a frequency you feel in your teeth.

They are negotiating. Or worshipping. Or simply maintaining, the way their ancestors maintained, the way their descendants will maintain, tending machines that have become landscape that has become something beyond either.

---

One of them is approaching you now.

It moves across the moss with that impossible driftless drift, its trajectory an arc that suggests it has known you were here since before you arrived, that it has been adjusting its path for hours to bring it to this moment. Up close, you can see the threads more clearly: dark lines beneath the moss-colored skin that pulse faintly, that might be carrying nutrients or information or both. Its hands are large and very still. Its breath does not fog.

It stands before you, bruise-eyed, silent, and you feel the pressure behind your forehead intensify. Images: your footprints filling with water. The weeping ground. The place where you end and the plateau begins, which is less clear than you thought. A question that isn't a question, a concept arriving in a language older than words:

*What are you tending?*

You don't know how to answer. The Tender does not seem to expect an answer, only to have posed the question, only to have planted it where it might root and grow and eventually become something the plateau can use.

It drifts away. The mist closes behind it. Somewhere in the distance, the fog-waders click their gear-throats, and the herds adjust their paths, and the factories walk their slow walks toward purposes that have become their own reasons.

The water seeps up around your boots.

You are beginning to understand that standing still here is not the same as waiting. Standing still here is the first step toward becoming something that tends.
