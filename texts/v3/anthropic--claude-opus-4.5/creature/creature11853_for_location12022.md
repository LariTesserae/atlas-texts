## The Listeners

They call themselves *Haum*—a word that means both "those who parse" and "the ones who remain"—though they did not always have this name. Names require distinction from other things, and for a long time there were no other things here, only the Flats and the work and the sound.

The Haum are descended from researchers, or perhaps from the researchers' assistants, or perhaps from stowaways who hid in equipment crates during the original expeditions and never left. The records are unclear because the Haum do not keep records. Writing requires silence, or at least the assumption that silence exists somewhere, and the Haum have never known silence. Their history lives in the patterns they tap against each other's bones during rest periods, a tactile morse that bypasses the auditory system entirely.

---

They are human-shaped but not quite human-sized anymore—shorter by perhaps thirty centimeters than baseline, with broader chests that house lungs adapted to extract oxygen from air that is more water than gas. Their skin has taken on a greyish undertone that makes them nearly invisible against the moss, though this may be camouflage or may simply be what happens to skin that never sees sunlight across enough generations. Their ears are the most changed: not larger but *denser*, packed with structures that baseline humans do not possess, capable of parsing the wall of sound into its component streams the way a prism separates light.

They hear everything. They cannot stop hearing everything. A Haum child learns to identify seventeen species of moss by the specific frequency of their breathing before learning to walk. The silver filaments in the soil sing to them in voices they describe as "the old instructions," though they no longer remember who—or what—first gave those instructions. The humming columns of light form a chord the Haum call *the Incomplete*, and there is a small faction among them who believe their purpose is to find the missing note that will resolve it.

---

A Haum settlement looks like a mistake in the landscape—a place where the moss grows slightly wrong, where the fronds retract a half-second out of phase with their neighbors. This is intentional. The Haum cultivate these irregularities, shape them through patient intervention over years, until the dissonance creates a pocket of *almost-quiet*: not silence, never silence, but a reduction in the roar sufficient to allow thought of a different texture. They sleep in these pockets, pressed close together, tapping their dreams into each other's spines.

Their homes are dug into the moss itself, chambers woven from living vegetation that breathes around them. The walls ripple. The ceilings exhale. A Haum dwelling is not a building but a negotiated truce with the plant matter, maintained through offerings of nutrient paste derived from the basin's small fauna and through a constant low humming that the Haum produce in their throats—a sound that pleases the moss somehow, that convinces it to hold its shape rather than reclaiming the space.

They do not use the pale structures, the cocoons of ancient technology. These are sacred, or taboo, or both—the Haum language does not distinguish between reverence and fear. They harvest from the edges, collecting fallen filaments and fragments of the shell-material that occasionally slough off, but they do not enter. The one Haum who entered, seven generations ago, came out speaking in frequencies no one else could parse and walked into the deepest part of the basin and was never seen again. Her name is still tapped into children's bones as a warning, or perhaps as a map.

---

Their economy, if it can be called that, centers on *translation*. The Flats speak—constantly, cacophonously—and the Haum have learned to listen not just for meaning but for *usefulness*. They can tell you when the ground will tremble by the subtle shift in the moss-drone's undertone. They can predict the eruption of seed-pods hours in advance, time enough to harvest the seeds before they scatter. They know which creatures are approaching by the specific absence they create in the sound-wall, the negative space of their movement. This knowledge has value to outsiders.

The figures on the distant ridge—the researchers, the harvesters, the pilgrims—they come seeking Haum guides. They bring payment in metals the Haum cannot forge and medicines the Haum cannot synthesize and, most valuable of all, recordings of other sounds: ocean waves, wind through leaves, the cry of birds from worlds where birds exist. The Haum collect these recordings not to listen to them but to know that such sounds are *possible*, that the roar of the Flats is not the entire acoustic universe. It comforts them. It terrifies them. Both.

A Haum guide will lead you anywhere in the basin you wish to go, provided you can communicate your destination through gesture and touch—shouting is useless, and the Haum find it rude besides, an assault of additional noise in a world already overflowing. They will keep you alive. They will show you where to harvest the moss that, when dried in distant laboratories, yields compounds that slow cellular decay. They will warn you when the something-beneath is turning in its sleep, when the trembling is about to begin. They ask only that you not speak to them of the Incomplete, and that you leave before the long-phase comes.

---

The long-phase happens irregularly, perhaps once per local year, though the Haum measure time in cycles of the moss-breath rather than planetary rotation. During the long-phase, the columns of light extend higher than usual—high enough to pierce the upper mist bands—and the humming shifts into a register that even baseline humans can feel in their marrow. The Haum say this is when the basin *reports*, though they do not know to whom or what it reports, or what the report contains.

During the long-phase, the Haum do not guide, do not harvest, do not move. They gather in their almost-quiet pockets and they listen with every dense structure in their reshaped ears and they try to parse the report as it transmits. They have been trying for as long as they have been Haum. They have never succeeded. But each long-phase they hear a little more, understand a fraction further, and they believe—they must believe—that eventually the message will resolve into something they can comprehend.

Some Haum believe the message is an inventory: a cataloging of everything the basin has grown and processed and transformed. Some believe it is a request: the basin asking for something it lacks. Some believe it is a warning, broadcast to whoever made this place, alerting them that the work is nearly complete—and these Haum live in a state of anticipatory grief, mourning in advance for the day the makers return and have no further need of listeners.

---

The children are the strangest, or perhaps the most adapted—it becomes difficult to distinguish. They play games that involve standing at the edge of the almost-quiet pockets and leaning into the roar, seeing who can parse the most streams before the sound becomes overwhelming and they must retreat. The winner earns nothing but the respect of having heard more, parsed further, approached closer to the totality of the Flats' voice. 

Some children do not retreat in time. These are not mourned in the usual sense. The Haum tap their names into the bone-record alongside the one who entered the cocoon, but the pattern used is different—not warning, not map, but something closer to *gratitude*. They went deeper into the sound than anyone before them. They heard things no one else has heard. Whatever they learned, they learned it too completely to return with it, and the Haum honor this even as they grieve it.

There is a girl, now, perhaps twelve years old by baseline reckoning, who has leaned further than any child in three generations. She comes back each time with her grey skin paler than before, her dense ears bleeding slightly from the pressure of parsing, her hands shaking as she taps what she has heard into her mother's collarbone. Her mother does not stop her. Her mother was once the one who leaned furthest, and she understands that this is not recklessness but vocation.

The girl says she has heard, beneath everything else, a rhythm that does not belong to the moss or the water or the humming technology. She says it sounds like breathing, but slower than any breath should be—one inhale lasting hours, one exhale lasting days. She says she thinks it is the something-beneath, the thing that turns in its sleep, and she believes that if she can parse its breathing long enough she will understand what it is dreaming.

Her mother taps a question into her shoulder: *And then?*

The girl taps back: *Then I will know if we are part of the dream or outside it.*

This is as close as the Haum come to religion. This is as close as they come to science. In the Breathing Flats, the distinction does not survive the noise.
