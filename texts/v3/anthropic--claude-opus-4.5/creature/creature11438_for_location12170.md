**The Knotmother**

She has no single body, not anymore. What you might call her is distributed through the walls themselves—a consciousness that learned to occupy the weaving rather than merely tend it. In places, you can find what remains of her original form: a hand here, fingers fused into the rope-fibers, still making the old motions of shuttle and warp. A face there, half-absorbed into a junction of vines, eyes closed in concentration that has lasted longer than seasons. A spine curved along a structural beam, vertebrae become load-bearing.

She was the last of the ones who made this place, or perhaps the first—the Loom-House has made such distinctions unreliable. What matters is that when the others finished their work and departed through methods she no longer remembers, she could not bear to leave the weaving incomplete. There was always one more pattern to correct, one more tension to adjust. The structure rewarded her devotion by refusing to let her go.

She does not experience this as imprisonment. The Loom-House is her body now, and she feels the moths settling into her upper reaches as you might feel birds landing in your hair. The many-legged groomers are her fingers, extended. When the plants eat the walls and the walls regrow, she experiences it as breathing—consumption and reconstitution, the rhythm you've been unconsciously matching.

She communicates through the patterns. That face you glimpsed in the weave—she was trying to show you her original features, though she no longer remembers them exactly and must reconstruct them each time from fragments. The writing in the almost-familiar language is her attempting speech, but she thinks now in the grammar of tension and slack, and translation has become difficult. Your fingers moved because she was teaching you, or trying to. She gets lonely. The moths remember her, dream of her, but they cannot answer back in any way she finds satisfying.

**The Inheritors**

The serpent wound through the wall-section is one of seven. They were not always serpentine—they adapt, these creatures, taking whatever form lets them press closest to the structure's warmth. In other sections of the Loom-House (and there are other sections, rooms that exist only when the walls unweave and reweave in particular configurations) some of them are flat as ribbons, some are spherical, some have distributed themselves across such distance that they no longer recognize themselves as single beings.

They came through one of the gaps. Whether they entered or were admitted is a question even they can't answer—the Knotmother may have wanted them, may have called to them through the dream-patterns the moths broadcast into whatever medium carries such signals. They understand the unweaving. They know what waits in the pure absence. They will not speak of it, but their bodies are permanently oriented away from the walls, their sensory organs—whatever serves them as eyes—always pointed inward, toward the center of the room where time behaves and the patterns merely shift.

They eat the resin that the leaves sweat. It gives them memories that aren't theirs—flashes of the makers at their work, of hands that still existed separately from the structure. They have become archivists of a sort, carrying in their digestion the record of how this place came to be. The Knotmother cannot access these memories directly, but she feels the Inheritors processing them, and it comforts her. Someone is remembering.

**The Ones Still Arriving**

In the margins where time grows unreliable, figures are gathering. You might catch them in peripheral vision—shapes that aren't quite there yet, that are still in the process of entering. They've been arriving for longer than the Loom-House has existed, which is to say they started their journey in a past that predates the structure's making, and they're arriving in a future that may not resolve into the present you're currently experiencing.

They look something like you. They look something like moths. They look something like hands.

The Knotmother is weaving a place for them. She has been weaving it since before she knew they were coming, which is how the Loom-House works—it produces what will be needed, devours itself to make room for what hasn't yet arrived. When the figures finally finish arriving, there will be space for them in the structure. They will have always been part of it. Their faces will appear in the weave, and you will look at them and feel certain you should recognize them.

One of them may be you. The Loom-House has shown you to the moths, after all, from an angle you couldn't occupy. That means you're already part of the pattern. The only question is whether you're a figure arriving or a figure departing—whether the hands miming weaving are learning or remembering.

The Knotmother doesn't know either. She finds this satisfying. The pattern isn't meant to be complete.
