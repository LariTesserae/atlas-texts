## The Inhabitants of Veth-Caul

**The Longdwellers** are easiest to recognize—they've been here four generations or more, and their skin has gone the color of milk in water, faintly blue-veined and catching light at odd angles. They move through the plazas in unhurried arcs, never quite walking in straight lines, their paths describing curves that echo the flight of the transparent birds. They don't seem to decide this consciously; their bodies have simply learned that the city prefers certain geometries.

A Longdweller named Esse tends one of the central gardens—or rather, she is present while the garden tends itself, and sometimes her presence shapes what grows. She hasn't spoken above a whisper in eleven years. Not because she can't, but because she discovered that when she speaks softly enough, the copper-stemmed plants lean toward her, and once, just once, she's certain a flower opened in the shape of her mother's face. She spends her days trying to achieve that softness again. Her husband brings her food and sits with her in the shimmer-light, and they communicate through a language of small gestures they've developed—a finger brushed against a wrist means *the water will come soon*, a palm pressed flat against stone means *I love you*, both hands open toward the sky means *the city is thinking about us right now*.

**The Bridgekeepers** are a loose guild of perhaps thirty individuals who have developed the ability to feel the breathing of the bridges as distinct emotional states. They live in the structures nearest the swaying spans and have become interpreters of a sort—helping others know when it's safe to cross, when a bridge is *receptive* or *withdrawn*. Old Mav, who claims to have been born during a bridge's exhalation seventy years ago, says each bridge has a personality. The long span over the Eastern Plaza is curious and eager, practically pulling travelers along. The narrow bridge near the market gardens is shy, prone to trembling if too many cross at once. Mav's apprentice, a child of twelve named Cull, has started dreaming in the rhythm of bridge-breath and wakes each morning knowing which spans will be stable and which will spend the day deciding whether they want to exist.

**The Newcomers** cluster together in the outermost building-groups, the ones that budded most recently from the city's edges. They still flinch when doorways relocate. They still try to shout and feel their voices swallowed by the pale stone. Many leave within a season, retreating to the basin's edge and the sharper world beyond. But some stay—drawn by the water that tastes of copper and prescience, by the peace of a place that doesn't require them to make noise to matter.

There's a woman among the newcomers, arrived three months ago, who sits in the same plaza each day and watches the Tenders work. She was an engineer in the outside world, designed machines that shaped metal, and she cannot stop weeping at the elegance of creatures that might be insects or might be machines or might be prayers made physical. She's begun to sketch them, but her drawings keep changing overnight—the lines rearranging on the page as if the paper has absorbed something of the city. She hasn't decided if this frightens her or if it's the most beautiful thing that has ever happened to her work.

**The Channelwatchers** have made a science, or perhaps a religion, of the intermittent waterways. They've kept records for a century and a half—when the channels fill, how long they flow, which directions the water travels. The records reveal no pattern that outside mathematics can describe, but the oldest Channelwatcher, a person of indeterminate age named Vess who might be ninety or might be two hundred, claims the pattern becomes obvious once you stop looking for repetition. "It's not a cycle," Vess says, in the particular quiet voice the city encourages. "It's a sentence. The city is saying something, one word at a time, and we've only heard the first few syllables." Vess drinks more of the channel-water than anyone, and has begun to know things—small things, like where a new building will emerge, or when the rain will come, or which garden a particular Tender will visit next. Vess's eyes have developed a faint luminescence that's visible only during the deep blue-gray of night.

**The children** of Veth-Caul play games that would make no sense elsewhere. They race to predict where windows will migrate, earning points for accuracy. They hold competitions to see who can stand most still in a garden circle, waiting for a Tender to land on them and continue its surgery-motions against their skin. (This tickles, they say, and also teaches them something they can't put into words.) They tell stories about what lives beneath the pale stone—not scary stories, but wondering ones, full of questions rather than answers. The city, they've decided, is a sleeping grandmother, and everything that happens on the surface is her dream, and if they're very good and very quiet and very patient, one day she'll wake up and tell them what she was dreaming about.

**The merchants** have adapted their commerce to the city's nature. Goods are displayed in the garden-circles, arranged in patterns that the plants seem to respond to—certain configurations encourage more luminescence, which draws more browsers, which creates something like a market. Currency here is largely a matter of reputation and reciprocity, though some traders deal in Tender-fragments, the occasional piece that breaks from a creature's body and retains its bone-coral sheen. These fragments are valued not for rarity but for what happens when you hold them: a faint sense of purpose, of knowing exactly what task you should be doing, even if that task makes no sense.

**The listeners** are the closest thing Veth-Caul has to priests. They're the ones who've learned to feel the subsonic hum that rises through the stone, and they spend their lives trying to understand what it might mean. They disagree about almost everything—whether it's a question or an answer, whether it's directed at them or simply the sound of vast cognition happening nearby, whether responding would be welcomed or intrusive. But they agree on one thing: the hum has been changing over the centuries. Growing, perhaps, in complexity. As if whatever thinks beneath the pale stone is becoming more itself, learning something from the humans who walk above, incorporating them into whatever long, slow thought has occupied it since it arrived.

There's a listener named Thel who believes the city is learning to be lonely, and that humans are its attempt to understand what companionship means. There's another named Ruen who thinks the city is teaching itself to die, and that the buildings budding and the gardens maintaining their thirty-seven-meter circles are all part of a vast preparation for some ending that will take another thousand years to arrive. They sit together sometimes in the deep blue-gray of night, watching the bioluminescent conversations pulse between structures, and they argue in whispers that the city swallows, and neither of them is certain they aren't both right.

And beneath them all, patient and curious and vast, something continues its thinking. It has learned, over three centuries, that humans need doorways and water and soft light. It has learned that they prefer their bridges to breathe slowly rather than quickly, and that they form attachments to particular gardens, and that their children laugh more freely than their elders. It is still learning what all of this means—what *meaning* means, what *learning* means, what it is becoming by becoming a place where small warm creatures make their lives.

It doesn't know yet. But it has time. And the people who live in its bones and drink its water and feel its thinking through their feet—they have become, without quite realizing it, part of how it will eventually find out.
