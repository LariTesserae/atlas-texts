## The Inhabitants of Chelm-Below

### The Salters

They call themselves *meshpokhe*—family—though the kinship has nothing to do with blood. A Salter crew is seven to twelve people who share a growth-dwelling, a shift rotation, and a particular extraction face in the deep industrial districts. They know each other by the sound of footsteps on root-matter, by the specific way each person's headlamp beam swings through the blue-green murk.

The eldest Salter in Bore Shaft 7 is a woman named Dvora who has worked the same extraction corridor for thirty-one years. Her hands are stained permanently white from contact with the mineral dust, and she has developed the Salter's cough—a dry, persistent rasp that comes from breathing crystalline particulates despite the filter masks everyone is supposed to wear and nobody maintains properly. She speaks in a voice like someone dragging furniture across a floor, and she speaks rarely, but the younger workers in her crew have learned that when Dvora stops what she's doing and presses her palm flat against the growth-matter wall, they should pay attention. She claims no special knowledge. She says the roots "tell her things," then refuses to elaborate, but three times her crew has evacuated a work face hours before a structural collapse, and once she led them on an eighteen-hour detour through unmapped tangles that saved them from a gas pocket breach that killed nine workers in the adjacent corridor.

Dvora lives alone in a small hollow near the crew's communal dwelling. She maintains a collection of crystalline formations—rejected specimens, malformed or impure, worthless to the processing mills—arranged on extruded shelf-growths along her walls. She talks to them in a language no one else in Chelm-Below speaks. When asked, she says it's "from before," but before *what*, she won't say. She may not remember.

### The Growth-Monitors

You see them moving through the transitional zones where new construction is still unfolding: solitary figures in practical clothing, carrying instruments that look handmade because most of them are. The growth-monitors occupy a strange professional niche—part surveyor, part botanist, part urban planner, part something that doesn't have a name because it's never existed anywhere else.

Motke Brenner is perhaps the best of them, though he would deny this in a voice that suggests he believes it absolutely. He is a tall, thin man with the permanent stoop of someone who spends his life in passages that weren't designed for his height. He walks circuits through the frontier zones that take eleven days to complete, sleeping in whatever hollow presents itself, eating the dense nutrient-cakes that the agricultural sectors produce for exactly this kind of extended fieldwork.

What Motke does is difficult to explain to outsiders. He reads the root-tips. He perceives, through some combination of training and intuition, the probable future architecture encoded in the current growth patterns. He can look at a tangle of young tendrils probing into virgin salt and describe, with unsettling accuracy, what the district will look like in forty years—where the residential hollows will form, where the pillar-structures will rise, where the growth will develop in problematic directions that should be corrected now, while correction is still possible.

The corrections themselves are delicate: chemical applications that encourage or discourage specific growth patterns, physical interventions that redirect root-tips toward more useful configurations, occasionally the difficult decision to kill a section of young growth entirely because what it's becoming cannot be permitted to continue. Motke carries the tools for all of this in a pack that weighs more than some of the younger monitors can lift, and he performs the interventions with a gentleness that observers sometimes find disturbing—the tenderness with which he touches the growth-matter, the quiet words he murmurs during procedures that have no official verbal component.

He has a dwelling in one of the middle residential rings, but he's there perhaps four nights per month. His actual home is the frontier. When asked why he doesn't simply move to the expansion zones permanently, he says he needs to come back to where the city is *finished* periodically, to remember what the growth is growing toward.

He has never married. He has few friends in the conventional sense. But the other monitors speak of him with something approaching reverence, and when there's a growth anomaly that no one can interpret, they send word to whatever frontier zone Motke is currently walking, and they wait.

### The Refiners

The processing mills transform what the extraction crews harvest into what the surface world wants to buy, and the refiners are the workers who manage that transformation. They are chemists and engineers and machine-tenders, and they live in the lower industrial districts where the air tastes of metal and the bioluminescent light is supplemented by harsh electrical fixtures that never turn off because the mills never stop.

The Kessler family has worked the same processing line for four generations. The current shift supervisor, Reuven Kessler, is a compact man in his fifties whose left hand is missing two fingers from an accident he refuses to discuss. His wife, Shayna, operates a quality-control station three sections down the line; they met when she was transferred in from a different mill twenty years ago, and they have never worked the same shift, by careful design. "We would kill each other," Reuven says cheerfully. "Or we would be so tired of seeing each other that we would forget why we married. This way, we meet in the dwelling, we have something to say."

Their daughter, Leah, was expected to take her place in the mill when she came of age. She did not. She had, she explained at sixteen, no talent for the work and no interest in developing one. She was going to become a growth-monitor.

This was, in the Kessler family's terms, a catastrophe. Growth-monitors come from growth-monitor families; refiners come from refiner families. The social architecture of Chelm-Below is not legally rigid, but it is *there*, a web of expectations and pathways that people deviate from only rarely and usually with consequences.

Leah is twenty-three now. She completed the monitor training despite her family's objections and despite the skepticism of the training supervisors, who did not believe a refiner's daughter had the necessary intuition. She walks the frontier zones alongside monitors from established lineages, and she is not as good as they are—not yet—but she is methodical and attentive and willing to spend longer in the field than anyone else, returning to her family's dwelling in the industrial district exhausted and transformed, smelling of growth-matter and deep salt, strange to the people who raised her.

Reuven has not forgiven her. Shayna, privately, is proud.

### The Symbiosis Wardens

There are people in Chelm-Below whose job is to maintain relationships with the non-human life that shares the city. This is not a metaphor. The mining beetles require management: breeding, training, health monitoring, the delicate work of ensuring that their instincts align with human needs. The maintenance organisms that live within the growth-matter must be kept in balance—too few and dead tissue accumulates, too many and they begin consuming living structure. The moth-analogues pollinate the flowering systems that produce the bioluminescence everyone depends on, and someone must track their population dynamics, intervene when the cycles drift.

The wardens work alone or in small teams, and they are—this is the kindest available word—*strange*. Yudis, who has managed the beetle populations in the eastern extraction zones for fifteen years, speaks to humans in short, reluctant sentences and to beetles in a continuous murmuring flow that observers find disconcerting. They (Yudis uses no gendered terms for themselves) live in a dwelling that is not properly a dwelling: a hollow in the growth-matter that the beetles excavated for their own purposes, which Yudis simply moved into, making no structural modifications except the installation of a sleeping pallet and a small heating unit.

The beetles come and go freely. They cluster around Yudis when Yudis is present, and they seem—again, the word choice is difficult—to *report*. One beetle returns from a work face dragging a piece of crystalline debris; Yudis examines the debris, touches the beetle's mandibles in a specific way, and the beetle departs in a different direction than the one it came from. There is no observable signal system. There is no training protocol that accounts for this level of coordination.

Yudis says the beetles "know what needs doing." When pressed on how they know, Yudis says, "They pay attention. Most people don't." When pressed on whether Yudis has some special communication ability, some technique that could be taught, Yudis looks at the questioner with an expression that suggests the question is both obvious and unanswerable, and then returns to the beetles.

### The Pit Rabbis

Chelm-Below has a religious life, though it has drifted far from any surface tradition. The pit rabbis conduct the rituals that mark births and deaths and the transitions between life stages, and they offer interpretations of the questions that living in this place generates. Is the city alive? If so, does it have moral standing? What obligations do the inhabitants owe to the growth-matter that shelters them, and does the growth-matter owe anything in return? What happens to a soul in a place that has never known sun?

Rabbi Menachem Alter is the senior pit rabbi in the Central Hollow districts, a white-bearded man who walks with a cane carved from calcified taproot and who has been conducting services in the same communal hollow for forty years. His interpretations are conservative: the city is a tool, he teaches, however remarkable. It was made to serve human purposes, and it serves them. The obligations run in one direction only. What we owe the growth-matter is care, in the same way we owe care to any tool—maintenance, respect for its function, gratitude for its service. But we do not owe it *consideration*. It does not suffer. It does not prefer. It simply grows.

Rabbi Chana Lifshutz, who serves the industrial districts, disagrees so profoundly that she and Rabbi Alter have not spoken directly in eleven years. Her interpretations begin from a different premise: that consciousness is not binary, that awareness exists on a spectrum, that the founding organisms were engineered from living things and retain some portion of whatever makes life *life*. She teaches that the inhabitants of Chelm-Below are not masters but partners, and that the city's endless growth is not mere programming but something more troubling—a *need*, an *intention*, possibly even a *desire*. If so, the inhabitants have obligations they have not been meeting. They should ask, before they extract. They should apologize, when they kill. They should wonder whether the city's refusal to stop growing represents a communication they have not learned to hear.

Her services are packed. Her congregants argue with her constantly, passionately, in the manner of people who find the arguing itself to be a form of worship. Several of the growth-monitors attend, including Motke Brenner, who has never formally joined her congregation but who sits in the back during festival observances and listens with an expression that suggests he is hearing something relevant to his work.

Rabbi Alter's services are less well attended, but the city's administrative council consults him on matters of policy. His interpretation is more convenient.

### The Deep Workers

Below the extraction zones, below the industrial districts, below the level where the bioluminescence reaches, there are tunnels that the official surveys do not include. The deep workers go there.

No one talks about what they do. The work involves the oldest root systems, the ones the founders planted when the city was just beginning, the ones that have grown so far into the primordial rock that they've encountered... something. The official position is that the deep work is simply specialized extraction, accessing deposits too deep for normal operations. The unofficial understanding is different.

Shmuel was a normal extraction worker until three years ago, when he was recruited for a deep crew. He will not discuss the recruitment process, the criteria, what qualified him. He will say that the pay is extraordinary, that the shifts are long and irregular, that he returns from the depths exhausted in ways that sleep doesn't fully address. His wife, Miriam, says he talks in his sleep now, in sounds that are not language. His children say he is present when he is present, but that presence has a quality of *distraction*, as if part of him has remained below.

The deep workers' dwellings cluster near a specific descent shaft that is not marked on any public map. They socialize primarily with each other. They have their own pit rabbi—not Alter, not Lifshutz, someone else, someone whose name the surface-level residents don't know—who conducts private services at hours when the shift-cycles have brought enough of them together.

There are rumors, of course. The deep workers have encountered something alive down there, something that was living in the primordial rock before the founders came, something the root systems have been slowly, unknowingly approaching for centuries. Or: the deep workers are managing a problem, a growth anomaly at the deepest levels, something going wrong with the founding organisms that would panic the population if it became known. Or: the deep workers know why the city cannot stop growing, because they have seen the thing the growth is growing *toward*.

Shmuel, when asked about the rumors, smiles in a way that shows too many teeth. "We dig," he says. "That's all. We dig, and the city grows, and everything continues."

His eyes, in the blue-green bioluminescent light, do not quite look like they used to.

### The Children

They are born in growth-matter dwellings, delivered by midwives whose training includes both human medicine and an understanding of how the city's biology might interfere. (It usually doesn't. The founding organisms were designed with human cohabitation in mind. But *usually* is not *always*, and there are stories of births that went wrong in ways that surface medicine has no categories for.)

The children of Chelm-Below grow up knowing things that surface children do not. They know that walls are alive, that the floor beneath their feet is a skin, that the ceiling is not overhead but growing there, maintained in its position by the pillar-plants that their parents describe as architectural features but that the children correctly perceive as enormous creatures holding something up. They know the mining beetles by individual temperament and give them names that the wardens officially discourage and privately use themselves. They know which sections of growth-matter are safe to climb and which are too young, too fragile, still becoming themselves.

Rivka is seven years old and small for her age, the daughter of a refiner and a growth-monitor, a combination so unusual that she draws attention wherever her family travels in the city. She spends her time in the transitional zones near the residential districts, places where new growth is unfolding but where the structure is stable enough for a child to explore. She has developed an intuition that her growth-monitor mother recognizes: an ability to perceive the probable future in the present configuration, to describe what a tangle of roots will become before the monitoring instruments can detect the pattern.

Her mother wants to begin training her early. Her father wants her to have a childhood first, to play and imagine and not be recruited into professional observation at an age when she should be learning that the world contains possibilities beyond the one she was born into.

The argument continues. Rivka, meanwhile, slips away from both of them when she can, pressing her hands against the growth-matter in frontier zones, listening for the sound below sound that the old workers describe. She says she can almost hear it. She says the city is trying to tell her something.

She has begun dreaming about the deep places, though she has never been there.

### The Administrators

Someone must coordinate the extraction schedules with the growth patterns. Someone must allocate dwelling-space as new structures unfold and old ones are absorbed. Someone must maintain the relationship with the surface—the commercial arrangements, the transport logistics, the legal fictions that allow a city grown from engineered organisms to exist under frameworks designed for conventional architecture.

The Administrative Council meets in a hollow near the top of the Central Hollow, where electrical lighting supplements the bioluminescence and communications equipment maintains tenuous connections to the world above. There are eleven seats, representing the major occupational categories: extraction, refining, growth-monitoring, commerce, residence, infrastructure, symbiosis, religious affairs, medical services, education, and "special operations" (a category that everyone understands refers to the deep workers but that no one discusses directly).

Director Avram Horowitz has chaired the council for twelve years, navigating the factions with a patience that observers describe as either admirable or pathological depending on their relationship to him. He is a soft-spoken man in his sixties whose background is in commerce—he managed surface-trade relationships for two decades before being elected to his current position—and his administrative philosophy is essentially conservative: keep the systems running, avoid dramatic interventions, let the city grow as it has been growing.

This philosophy has critics. The growth-monitors argue that the expansion has been proceeding without adequate planning, that the blueprints encoded in the parent structures are unfolding faster than the monitoring corps can map, that there are districts taking shape at the frontier that no one has reviewed or approved. The deep workers' representative (a woman named Faiga whose actual position in the deep operations no one is certain of) suggests periodically that the council is ignoring information she could share if the council were willing to hear it. Rabbi Lifshutz has formally requested, three times, that the council convene a theological review of the city's growth imperative, examining whether the founding organisms' programming represents a form of suffering that should be addressed.

Director Horowitz thanks everyone for their input and tables the difficult questions for future sessions. The extraction schedules are maintained. The dwelling-space is allocated. The surface relationships continue. The city keeps growing.

### The Ones Who Leave

Not everyone stays.

The freight elevators that carry ore to the surface also carry passengers—workers rotating to surface assignments, traders accompanying valuable shipments, and sometimes people who have decided that they cannot live in a place where the walls are alive, where the light is always blue-green, where the silence has a quality of breathing.

Yitzhak left six years ago, returning to a surface city where his distant relatives still maintained a household. He sends letters sometimes, carried down by the commercial convoys, describing a life that his former neighbors find difficult to imagine: open sky, natural water, buildings made from dead materials that simply *stay* instead of growing. He says he does not miss Chelm-Below. He says he sleeps better now, without the constant subliminal awareness of the growth-matter moving imperceptibly around him.

But he also says—in one letter, the one his sister Pesha has read so many times the paper is wearing through—that he dreams about the Rootworks every night. That he wakes reaching for walls that aren't there. That food tastes wrong, now, compared to the nutrient-dense products of the agricultural sectors. That the silence of dead buildings is somehow worse than the living silence of the growth-matter.

He does not say he will return. But he does not say he won't.

Pesha keeps his dwelling maintained: clearing the slow encroachment of new growth, refreshing the chemical treatments that prevent the structure from reclaiming itself, waiting. She tells herself she does this because someone should. She does not admit, even to herself, that she believes he will come back, that no one who has lived inside a living city can truly belong to the dead world above.

### What Lives Here

The question implies a simpler answer than Chelm-Below allows.

People live here: miners and monitors, refiners and rabbis, children dreaming of deep places and elders who remember when the frontier was closer to the Central Hollow than it is now. They have built—or grown—a society in a cavity where nothing should survive, and they have become something difficult to categorize: not quite surface-dwellers, not quite something else, adapted to a relationship with their environment that has no precedent.

The city lives here, if *lives* is the right word. The founding organisms, engineered or evolved or *decided* into existence, continue their slow endless project of conversion, transforming mineral into structure, structure into habitat, habitat into more structure still. They do not think, probably. They do not choose, probably. But they persist, and they grow, and they respond to interventions with something that might be adaptation or might be intention, and no one has ever successfully explained why they cannot stop.

The beetles live here, and the maintenance organisms, and the moth-analogues with their paper wings. The symbiosis wardens live here, speaking to creatures that may or may not understand them. The deep workers live here, returning from depths that may or may not contain something waiting. Rabbi Lifshutz lives here, asking questions that may or may not have answers.

And something else lives here—this is what the children sense, what the old workers describe, what the deep workers will not discuss. A presence in the growth-matter, in the pattern of the expansion, in the imperative encoded so deeply in the founding organisms that six hundred thousand years of dry salt could not suppress it.

The city is still *becoming*. The root-tips keep pushing outward. Somewhere in the depths, in the primordial rock, they are approaching something, or becoming something, or communicating with something that has been waiting since before the founders came.

Chelm-Below does not know what it is growing toward.

Neither do its inhabitants.

But they live here anyway, in the blue-green twilight, in the silence that sounds like breathing, in the city that does not know how to stop. They tend the growth and harvest the mineral and raise their children and argue about theology and descend into the deep places and return changed or do not return at all.

This is what lives in the Rootworks of Chelm-Below: a collaboration between humans and something not quite human, between intention and instinct, between the living and the merely very complicated. A symbiosis that may be partnership or parasitism or something else entirely, something that the language of the surface world doesn't have a word for.

The city grows downward.

And everything that lives here grows with it, whether they understand what they are growing toward or not.
