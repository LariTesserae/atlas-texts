**The Tenders**

They move among the beetles like afterthoughts—figures so still, so slow, that your eye slides past them twice before catching on the wrongness of their shape.

There are perhaps thirty of them visible from where you stand, though you suspect more occupy the deeper sheds, the places where the steam thickens to opacity. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense: two legs, two arms, a central mass that serves as torso. But their proportions belong to something that learned bipedalism from a great distance, through fog, and approximated it with imperfect devotion.

Their limbs are too long. Not grotesquely so—just enough that their hands hang at knee-level when they stand, just enough that their gait covers ground in unsettling increments. Their skin—if it is skin—has the texture of wet chalk, pale grey-white, and it does not move the way flesh moves. When they bend, there are no creases. When they straighten, no stretch marks bloom and fade. They are *wearing* their bodies the way the larger beetles wear their iron brackets: as equipment, as interface.

They have faces, technically. The features are present: eyes, nose, mouth, arranged in the expected configuration. But the features do not *do* anything. The eyes are open but do not track. The mouths are closed but show no evidence of breath. You watch one Tender for a full minute as it stands beside a sorting chute, and in that time, it blinks once—a slow, deliberate lowering and raising of lids that takes nearly four seconds to complete.

---

They are not supervising. That implies hierarchy, judgment, the possibility of correction. The Tenders do not correct. They *tend*—in the oldest sense of the word, the sense that means simply to be present with, to attend, to exist alongside.

One of them stands at the junction of two beetle-rivers, where a worn groove in the basalt causes occasional pile-ups. Every few minutes, when the smaller beetles begin to stack against each other in confused spirals, the Tender reaches down with one long arm and—gently, impossibly gently—redirects the flow. Its fingers are bifurcated at the tips, splitting into smaller manipulators that can isolate a single rice-grain beetle from the mass without disturbing its neighbors. The motion is tender in the other sense too. Careful. Almost reverent.

Another stands at a furnace-beetle's flank, one hand pressed flat against the creature's rust-brown shell. The furnace-beetle has been still for as long as you've been watching—not broken, not dormant, simply *between tasks*, waiting for slag that has not yet arrived. The Tender waits with it. Its hand does not move. It is not checking for damage or monitoring temperature. It is simply *there*, accompanying the beetle through its pause.

---

You approach one.

It does not react until you are within arm's reach, and then the reaction is minimal: a slight turning of the head, a focusing of those static eyes on a point approximately where your chest would be. Up close, you see that its skin is not uniform. There are seams—faint lines where the pale surface meets itself imperfectly, like a paper sculpture assembled from multiple sheets. At the seams, something darker shows through. Not flesh. Not machinery. Something with the color and texture of compressed ash.

It does not speak. You are not certain it has the anatomy for speech. But it tilts its head—that slow, four-second motion—and you understand that it is *waiting*. Not for instructions. Not for information. Simply waiting to see if you will do something that requires tending.

You do not. You step back.

The Tender returns its gaze to the middle distance and resumes its stillness.

---

Later, deeper in the complex, you find where they rest.

It is not a dormitory. There are no beds, no personal effects, no signs of habitation. It is simply a shed like any other, distinguished only by its emptiness—no conveyors, no chutes, no beetle-rivers flowing through. The Tenders stand in loose rows, perhaps two dozen of them, motionless, their arms at their sides, their bifurcated fingers slightly curled.

They are not sleeping. They are not powered down. Their eyes are open. Their bodies hold the same tension as the Tenders outside. But they are *not working*, and you realize this is the closest they come to rest: standing in the dark, in the company of their own kind, waiting for the signal—the pressure plate, the chute-blockage, the beetle-loss—that will call them back to their posts.

One of them, near the back, has something wrong with it. Its left arm hangs at an angle that suggests damage, a joint that no longer articulates correctly. The arm is wrapped in what looks like a bandage, but when you look closer, you see it is beetle-silk—the secretion of specialized weaver-beetles, applied in careful layers, hardening into a cast. Another Tender tended to this one. The thought is vertiginous.

They care for the beetles. They care for each other. They care for the buildings and the steam-vents and the worn grooves in the basalt. They have been caring for all of these things since before the condensation first formed, and they will continue caring until there is nothing left to care for.

---

You understand, watching them, that they are not masters here. They are not servants either. They are something the beetles needed—something this place needed—and so they emerged, or were shaped, or were always present in potential until the moment the work required a gentler hand than chitin could provide.

They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They do not die. 

They tend.

And in the tending, they are complete.
