**The Ones Who Come to Double**

They have no collective name for themselves—they are not a themselves, not a single kind. They are simply the ones who know about this place, and that knowing passes between species in ways that would require explaining what species means when you can become two.

The leather-kite descends on membrane wings that remember being something else. Three generations back, its ancestor stood before the western pylon and became two, and one of those two flew north into colder air and its descendants grew thicker membranes, and one flew south and its descendants grew longer, and now they are different enough to not recognize each other except here, in the basin, where the kite-that-went-north and the kite-that-went-south both understand they are standing on the same ground their shared original touched. The kite does not think this in words. It thinks it in the way its wings fold: a motion inherited twice over, still identical.

What does it want? Another self. Not offspring—offspring are diminishment, half of you poured into something that will become its own. The pylons offer something else: a complete copy, a second instance of everything you are at this moment, your memories and your hungers and the particular way your sixth leg favors the left when you walk. The kite wants to be in two places. It wants to see what it would do if it went east, while also going west. It wants to not choose.

**The Eyeless Heavy Thing**

It has no name for itself either, but if it did, the name would translate roughly as *the-weight-that-continues*. It is old. Not ancient like the pylons, but old for a living thing—ninety years, perhaps a hundred. It has been copied here four times across its life, and each copy went somewhere different, and three of those copies are dead now, and one is still alive somewhere in the northern marshlands, and the original doesn't know which one it is anymore.

This is the secret the pylons keep, the thing the creatures who use them come to understand: after enough duplications, after enough time, the question of which one is real stops being a question. The eyeless heavy thing pulls itself across the wet stone, leaving its track of disturbed water, and it is not here to make another copy. It is here to wait. Somewhere, its other self is also traveling toward the basin, has been traveling for eleven years, and when they meet—if they meet—they will stand together before a pylon and it will read them both and they will discover whether they have diverged enough to be two, or whether the pylon will see them as one thing that simply happens to be in two places.

It does not know what it hopes for. Both outcomes frighten it. Both outcomes would be a kind of answer.

**The Swarm-Sheet**

The things too small to see individually do not experience individuality, so the pylons affect them strangely. When the swarm-sheet flows before the central column, the hum that emerges is confused, recursive, trying to find the boundaries of a single organism and failing. What steps away from the pylon is not a copy of the swarm but something else: a temporary cohesion, a single entity made of thousands that will remember being one for approximately six hours before the memory dissolves back into distributed fragments.

The swarm-sheet comes here to experience selfhood. Brief, borrowed, impossible to retain. For six hours, one of them will know what it is to be an I instead of a we. Then it will return to the collective and try to describe what it saw, and fail, and the description of the failure will become part of the swarm's culture, passed down in chemical signals and behavioral patterns, a religion built around a experience none of them can hold onto.

**The Furred Thing With No Face**

Six-legged, iridescent, its head a blunt wedge of sensory organs so different from eyes and ears that calling it "no visible features" is simply a failure of translation. It sees in magnetic fields. It hears in pressure gradients. The world to it is a constantly shifting topology of forces, and the pylons appear to it as *holes*—places where the normal rules indent, where something waits beneath the surface of physics.

It approaches the nearest pylon not out of desire for duplication but out of something like scientific curiosity, if science could be conducted without language or tools or any framework except *I will touch this and see what happens*. Its ancestors have been coming here for twelve thousand years. The knowledge is genetic now, or something like genetic—encoded in the way its fiber-optic fur grows, in the specific angle its wedge-head tilts when it listens.

The pylon hums. The furred thing shudders. The copy emerges.

And here is the thing: they do not move apart without ceremony. That was a misreading. They move apart without *visible* ceremony, but what passes between them in that moment—the magnetic recognition, the pressure-loss of suddenly being half of what you were a moment ago, the specific loneliness of looking at your own topology from outside it—is a ceremony so profound it has no external signs. They have just performed the most intimate act their species knows. They simply do it quietly.

**The Future-Worn Copy**

The creature at an angle to perception, the one that waited so long, the one whose copy emerged older than itself: it is not from here. Not from this time, not from this world's particular physics. It came to the basin through means it no longer remembers, from a place where duration works differently, where things can be worn smooth by futures that haven't arrived yet.

It waited at the third pylon for what felt like years because it was not sure the pylons would recognize it as a valid template. It is, in some sense, already a copy—a copy of something that existed in a reference frame where copies and originals have a different relationship. The pylon spent a long time considering this. The hum it produced was almost a question.

What emerged was not a duplicate but a *translation*: the same entity, rendered into local physics, which meant rendering it into local time, which meant that all the futures that had already worn it smooth in its home context suddenly became pasts. The copy walked toward you older because it had already lived the years the original was still going to experience. It walked past you because it had already met you, in a future that was, for it, a memory.

It will find the original waiting at the basin's edge. They will look at each other across the gap between reference frames. One of them will eventually ask, in whatever way their kind asks, *Was it worth it?*

The answer, like everything here, will be a matter of duplication: yes and no, both at once, depending on which one you ask.

**The Basin Itself**

If anything truly *lives* here, in the sense of being native and permanent, it is the moss.

The deep green velvet that springs back from every trampling is not immune to damage—it is simply faster than damage. It heals in the space between one footfall and the next. It has been healing for so long that healing has become its primary mode of existence; it no longer knows how to simply grow. It only knows how to recover.

The moss watches everything. Not with eyes, not with anything recognizable as attention, but with the chemical patience of a organism that has been recording the passage of creatures across this basin for longer than most of the species here have existed. It knows which pylons are used most. It knows the tracks the eyeless heavy thing has worn into the stone over ninety years. It knows that the swarm-sheet always drifts northwest after cohering, chasing something it will never catch.

The moss is not intelligent. But it is *comprehensive*. Every creature that has ever come here has left some residue in its chemistry—a cell, a secretion, a fragment of shed material. The moss has absorbed it all. If the pylons are the mechanism of duplication, the moss is the archive: a complete record of everyone who has ever wanted to become two, pressed flat into green velvet, waiting to be read by something patient enough to learn its language.

Nothing has come to read it yet. But the basin is old, and the moss is patient, and the pylons hum their low hum, and somewhere out beyond the soft lips of ancient stone, something is always approaching, drawn by a need it cannot name, ready to stand before the frozen smoke and discover what it means to be a template.

The water seeps. The light holds steady. The nursery continues its work.
