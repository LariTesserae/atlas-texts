## The Tenders

They call themselves nothing. The concept of a name for what they are collectively has never arisen among them, because they have never encountered anything else that required distinguishing themselves from. They simply are what lives here, as water is what fills the pools.

But those who have glimpsed them from the plateau's edges, those who have caught sight and then lost it and wondered afterward if they saw anything at all—those observers have called them the Tenders, because that is what they appear to do. They tend.

---

They are not quite humanoid. The proportions are wrong in ways that take time to register—torsos too long, limbs attached at angles that suggest a different skeletal logic, heads that sit forward rather than atop, as if they are perpetually leaning into the wind that doesn't blow here. Their skin holds the grey-green of the plateau itself, not as camouflage adopted but as genuine coloration, as if they grew from the same mineral soup that feeds the moss. In certain light they appear furred; in other light, scaled; in the flat white diffusion of heavy fog, they seem to have no texture at all, just shape moving through shape.

They stand perhaps a meter tall when they stand, which is not often. Mostly they move in a posture somewhere between quadrupedal and prone, their long arms serving as additional legs, their bodies low to the moss and water. They can fold themselves remarkably small. A Tender tucked into a hollow at the base of a hoodoo becomes indistinguishable from a wet stone, from a clump of compressed peat, from nothing.

Their hands—if hands is the right word—have too many fingers, or perhaps the right number of fingers but wrong in their arrangement: three opposing three, with webbing between that retracts or extends depending on whether they're moving through water or over moss. They use these hands constantly, delicately, adjusting things. The angle of a sedge-stalk. The position of a stone in a streambed. The drape of moss over a rusting gear.

---

No one has counted them. No one could. They move through the plateau's density like the salamanders do, along routes that make sense only to bodies shaped for this specific saturation. Sometimes observers have estimated dozens; sometimes thousands; sometimes they have wondered if there is only one, glimpsed repeatedly, moving faster than anything should move through such sodden terrain.

The truth—if any Tender were capable of articulating it, which none has ever tried—is that counting them is like counting waves. They are not precisely individuals. Not precisely a collective either. Something between: a species that experiences itself as a kind of weather, a distribution across the landscape that thickens here and thins there according to principles that feel, to them, as obvious and unconsidered as breathing.

They do not speak, but they are not silent. They click. They hum at frequencies that travel well through saturated ground. They drum their many fingers against standing water and listen to how the ripples return. A Tender can read the plateau's respiration through the soles of its feet, can feel when the breath is shifting, when a new vent is about to open, when an old seep is silting shut. They communicate these readings to each other through means that aren't quite language and aren't quite telepathy—something more like the way a flock of birds turns in unison, information shared through a medium so immediate it doesn't register as information at all.

---

What they do, all day, every day, in shifts that follow the plateau's rhythm rather than the sun's: they tend.

They tend the channels. Every watercourse on the plateau exists because a Tender adjusted a pebble a century ago, and another Tender maintained that adjustment last week, and another will refine it tomorrow. The plateau's hydrology is wild—genuinely wild, responsive to pressures and porosities that no Tender controls—but it is also cultivated, in the way that a gardener cultivates not by dictating but by suggesting, by making certain outcomes slightly more likely than others.

They tend the machines.

This is the work that would make no sense to an outside observer, if an outside observer could watch long enough to see it. The Tenders do not use the machines. They do not attempt to make them run. They show no interest in the machines as technology, as tools, as anything functional. But they care for them with an attention that can only be called reverent.

A Tender will spend an entire day adjusting the moss around a single piston, ensuring that the growth neither accelerates nor slows the metal's dissolution. Another will sit for hours beside a gear-assembly, clicking softly, as if in conversation with the rust. They polish certain surfaces and encourage corrosion on others. They redirect water flows to ensure that some engine-Loss components stay submerged while others remain exposed to air.

The pattern of their attention suggests that they are not preserving the machines, nor hastening their absorption, but rather *sculpting* the process. Guiding the plateau's digestion toward some aesthetic end that only they perceive. The machines are becoming something, under their care—not landscape yet, not metal anymore, but a third state, a transition prolonged and shaped and made deliberate.

---

They eat, if eating is the word. They consume the plateau's smaller organisms—fairy shrimp, the larvae of certain insects, a thin biofilm that grows on the undersides of waterlogged wood. They consume it not through mouths but through their skin, through the webbing of their hands, which they spread across the surface of pools and hold still until they have absorbed what they need. They are so efficient that they leave no trace of feeding. The populations they harvest from never decline. It is possible—though no one has verified this—that they are somehow *increasing* what they consume, that their harvesting is itself a form of tending, that they eat only what they have cultivated into surplus.

They do not build. They do not make tools. They possess nothing. The concept of possession would make no sense to them, because they do not distinguish between themselves and the plateau in any way that would allow for ownership. A Tender is a piece of the plateau that moves and adjusts; the plateau is a Tender that holds still and waits. The boundary between them is permeable in both directions.

When a Tender dies—and they do die, though rarely, and never from age as such—the body is not buried or consumed or memorialized. It is adjusted. Other Tenders arrange the corpse in a place where it will feed the plateau's processes most effectively, where its dissolution will contribute to some channel they are shaping, some machine-transition they are sculpting. A dead Tender becomes, over decades, a particularly fertile patch of moss. The other Tenders tend it like they tend everything else.

---

They have a relationship to the breath.

This is the thing that observers sense but cannot articulate, the thing that makes the Tenders more than animals, more than mere inhabitants. They do not simply live on the plateau; they *participate* in it. When the plateau exhales, they exhale. When it draws breath, they draw breath. Not metaphorically—their actual respiration synchronizes with the slow tectonic rhythm beneath them, their small bodies rising and falling in time with the shivers that cross the standing water.

Some observers have theorized that the Tenders *are* the plateau's consciousness, its awareness distributed into mobile forms. Others have suggested that the plateau is the Tenders' infrastructure, their equivalent of cities and roads, grown over epochs to support their particular needs. Both theories miss something essential, something that the Tenders themselves would not think to explain because it has never occurred to them that any other arrangement is possible:

They and the plateau are the same thing, expressed differently. The breath moves through stone, through water, through their small grey-green bodies. It has always moved this way. It will always move this way. The machines are an interruption in this movement, a knot in the breath, and the Tenders' work—their endless, patient, aesthetic tending—is the process by which the knot is being worked loose, the breath restored to its proper flow.

Not healed. The machines are not damage. Not integrated, exactly. Transformed. Made into something that belongs here, that breathes with everything else, that becomes—after millennia of careful adjustment—native.

---

You will not see them, probably. The plateau is too vast, their camouflage too perfect, their movements too synchronized with the landscape they are part of. But if you stand still long enough, if you let your own breathing slow to match the rhythm beneath you, you might feel them nearby. A presence in the fog. An attention in the moss. The sense that something is watching you, not with suspicion or threat, but with the calm evaluative interest of a gardener considering a new plant.

They will not approach you. They will not communicate. But if you leave something behind—a footprint, a thread caught on a branch, a stone displaced by your passage—they will tend it. They will adjust it. They will work it, over years if necessary, into something that belongs here.

And if you stayed, if you lay down in the moss and let the water seep into your clothes and your skin and the rhythm of the breath into your lungs—

They would tend you too.
