# The Shapers of What Will Be

They do not remember being born. This is not because birth was traumatic or shrouded—it simply wasn't. They emerged from the work itself, or perhaps the work emerged from them; the distinction matters less than you'd think. The first Shaper woke with a chisel in her hand and stone-dust in her throat and the absolute certainty of where the first cut should fall. The others followed, not born but *accreted*, gathering into being the way the condensation gathers on the walls—slowly, inevitably, pulled together by forces that need no name.

They call themselves nothing. Names require mouths shaped for speaking, and while they have mouths, the architecture of their throats is wrong for language. Or perhaps right for a different language—one made of impact and echo, the crack of bronze on stone, the particular ring of a well-struck chisel versus a poorly angled one. When a Shaper dies (and they do die, though rarely—crushed by falling blocks, worn down by centuries of labor until their too-many fingers can no longer grip), the others mark it with a specific rhythm: seventeen strikes in descending pitch, then silence, then the resumption of work. This is grief. This is enough.

---

Their bodies are not beautiful by any standard that values symmetry. The arms are wrong—too long, yes, but also hinged in extra places, capable of angles that make human joints ache in sympathy. The additional fingers (seven on most hands, though some have eight, and the eldest have nine) are not uniformly sized; the outer ones are thick and blunt for power, the inner ones tapered and precise for detail work, and there's one on each hand—always the fourth from the outside—that seems to exist solely for sensing, for pressing against stone and reading something in its grain that no eye could catch.

Their skin is the color of the stone they work, not through camouflage but through accumulation. The calcium-ite andite andite dust settles into their pores, builds up in the creases of their joints, fills the whorls of their fingerprints until they are, in a sense, becoming what they build. The eldest Shapers are nearly geological, their movements slower but more certain, their strikes carrying the weight of rock against rock. When they finally stop—when the work wears through them completely—they don't fall. They simply become part of the terrace where they stood, a shape that the others will eventually carve around, incorporating the outline of a body into a buttress, a cornerstone, a load-bearing truth.

---

They do not eat. Or rather, they eat *work*—the friction-heat of labor enters them through their palms, the phosphorescent glow of fracturing stone feeds something behind their eyes. They drink the condensation that their own breath creates, licking it from walls they've just finished smoothing, tasting their own effort returned to them as sustenance. This is why they work in such numbers despite the vastness of the Yards: not because more hands finish faster, but because more breath makes more water makes more life. They are an ecosystem that runs on construction. Stop the hammers and they would slowly, over generations, dry out and still.

But they do not stop.

---

The knowing is the strangest thing about them, and they do not find it strange at all. Each Shaper wakes—*accretes*—with the complete structure already present in their mind, not as blueprint but as memory. They remember the finished Hollowing Yards the way you remember your childhood home: not with precision, not with measurements, but with the *felt sense* of how the light will fall through windows that haven't been cut, how footsteps will echo in halls that haven't been roofed, where the water will pool and where it will run. They are building from memory, except the memory is of something that hasn't happened yet.

This is why they need no plans. This is why they never speak. What would they say? *Remember the fountain in the eastern cistern, how the water will sound against those particular stones?* They all remember. They all know. The variations between them—the slight differences in how each one recalls the angle of a specific arch—these are what they resolve through the rhythm of hammers, call and response, a negotiation conducted in percussion until consensus emerges and the stone is struck.

Sometimes the consensus is wrong. Sometimes a block is placed and the *feel* of it is off, the memory disrupted, and they must unmake what they've made—carefully, preserving the stone for reuse, because nothing here is wasted. These moments are marked by a different rhythm: three quick strikes, a pause, three more, repeating until the error is acknowledged by all. Then the slow work of undoing, which is harder than doing, which requires them to hold two memories at once—the wrong one they've created and the right one they're returning to.

---

The pale scuttling things—the fauna that have colonized the unfinished spaces—the Shapers do not name them either, but they know them. They know them the way they know the structure: by memory, by feel, by the role these creatures will play in what the Yards become. The things that drink from pre-cisterns, that nest in walls-to-be-sealed, they are not pests. They are *early*. They have arrived ahead of schedule, before the spaces meant for them are ready, and the Shapers work around them with something that might be tenderness if tenderness could be expressed in the angle of a chisel-strike.

The creatures know this, somehow. They do not flee the workers. They watch from their half-finished burrows with eyes that catch the phosphorescent glow, and when a Shaper's hand passes near, they sometimes press against it—a brief warmth, a quick pulse, some communication that neither species could explain but both understand. *We are waiting for what you will make. We know it already. We are practicing our lives in the spaces you haven't finished.*

---

There are hierarchies, if you watch long enough to see them. The eldest Shapers, the ones whose skin is nearly stone, they work the deepest terraces, cutting into bedrock that has never seen light of any kind. Their strikes are slower but absolute—they do not make errors, or if they do, the errors are too small to matter, variations beneath the threshold of memory. The younger ones, newly accreted, skin still holding the faint flush of whatever warmth they gathered into being from, they work the upper levels, the detail work, the finishing. Between them, the vast middle generations shape the bones of the place: the load-bearing walls, the major archways, the channels that will carry water from the breath-cisterns to fountains that will sing in chambers not yet enclosed.

They move between levels freely, but they do not *look* at each other. Eye contact is not part of their social grammar. Instead, they track each other through vibration—the specific signature of each Shaper's hammer-strike, as distinct to them as faces are to humans. They know who is working three terraces down and two hundred meters east. They know when someone tires, when someone's rhythm falters, when someone is approaching the stillness that will eventually take them all. The information travels through the stone itself, carried by the very blows that shape it.

---

What are they building?

They know, and they do not know. They remember the finished structure as sensation, as space, as the way air will move through it and sound will carry and water will fall. But they do not remember its *name*. They do not remember its *purpose*. These are not things that fit in the kind of memory they carry—these are abstractions, categories, the work of minds that stand outside what they make and label it from a distance.

The Shapers cannot stand outside. They are inside the memory, inside the structure, inside the becoming. They are the Hollowing Yards in the same way that your cells are you: not the whole, not aware of the whole, but constitutive of it, unable to separate self from function. When the last stone is placed—and they remember that too, the feel of it, the specific weight and angle and the silence that will follow the final strike—they do not know what will happen to them. Perhaps they will become the residents of what they've built. Perhaps they will still, geological at last, transformed entirely into the architecture of their own memory. Perhaps something else will accrete in the finished spaces, something that needs what they've made, something that will live here the way the pale scuttling things are already practicing to live.

They do not wonder about this. Wondering is for minds that can stand outside.

The hammers continue. The rhythm calls and responds. The walls rise around them, around you, around the pale things watching from their half-made homes. Somewhere deep below, water collects from breath, and somewhere far above, the last of the daylight fails to reach.

They are building the memory of something that doesn't exist yet. They are building it until it does.
