**The Keepers of the Spiral**

They are difficult to count because they are difficult to see as individuals.

At first you might notice what appears to be a thickening in the air near one of the grooved columns—a place where the mist gathers more densely, where the light bends in ways that suggest depth without revealing form. You would be right to notice. Something lives there, something that has been watching you since you entered.

They are colonial, or they are singular beings with distributed bodies, or they are something for which neither word quite applies. What you can perceive: a central mass no larger than a closed fist, flesh the color of wet slate, textured like the inside of a lung. From this mass extend filaments—hundreds of them, thousands perhaps—each thinner than spider silk but somehow visible when the light catches them at the right angle. These filaments spread outward in spirals that echo the grooves on the columns, reaching several feet in all directions, anchoring to stone, to moss, to the waxy leaves of the rosette plants. The filaments pulse. Not with heartbeat rhythm but with something slower, something that matches the thirty-second breathing of the hollow itself.

There are perhaps forty of them distributed through the chamber. Perhaps four hundred. It depends on whether you count the juveniles—the tiny clusters budding from certain moss beds where the moisture concentrates, their filaments still stubby, still learning how to reach. It depends on whether you count the ones that have merged.

Because they do merge. In the corners of the hollow, where two or more spiraling territories overlap, you find masses larger than your head—two or three or seven central bodies fused into composite beings, their filaments intertwined into braids that have developed their own secondary structures, loops and whorls that serve purposes you cannot guess. These merged ones do not move. They have become part of the hollow's architecture, as fixed as the grooved columns, as old.

They are not the builders. You understand this slowly. The structures predate them by epochs—the Keepers arrived later, found this place, and made themselves fit. Their spiral filaments grew to match the spiral grooves because the grooves were already there, because the shape was already sacred, because something in the deep geometry of this hollow rewards that particular curve. They have been learning from the structures for longer than your species has existed. They have not yet graduated.

---

**What they eat:**

The Keepers feed on differential. Temperature differential, pressure differential, the electrical potential between wet stone and dry air. Their filaments are sensors and mouths at once—they taste the gradients that run through the hollow and draw sustenance from the movement of energy from one state to another. The geothermal warmth rising from below, meeting the cool plateau air above: this is their primary food source. They cluster near the transition zones, where the warm breath of the planet touches the atmosphere's edge.

But they have developed preferences. Certain Keepers have positioned themselves near the light-catching bowl, feeding on the differential between held radiance and ambient shadow. Others have wound their filaments into the channels beneath the moss, tasting the slow movement of minerals dissolved in water. The merged ones in the corners—the ancient composites—seem to feed on time itself, on the differential between what the hollow was and what it is becoming, though you cannot imagine how this would work and so you may be wrong.

They do not compete with the beetles, the birds, the oil-bodied moss-dwellers. They occupy a niche that nothing else wants. In return, their filaments provide something the ecosystem needs: a network. The creatures of the hollow communicate through them without knowing it. When a beetle clicks against a grooved column, the vibration travels through stone into filament, through filament into the Keeper's central mass, through other filaments into moss and air and the bodies of the wrong-winged birds. The hollow is coordinated because the Keepers make it so. They did not intend this service. They simply grew into the shape that was needed.

---

**Their lifecycle:**

A Keeper begins as a spore no larger than a grain of pollen, released from a mature individual during certain pressure conditions that occur perhaps twice per year—when the hollow's breathing deepens, when the rain comes heavy enough to change the air. The spores drift on the mist, most of them dying, a few landing in moss beds where the moisture is right and the gradient is strong.

The juvenile Keeper grows slowly. For the first decade it remains smaller than a fingernail, its filaments reaching only inches, its feeding limited to the micro-gradients within the moss itself. It is vulnerable during this time—the oil-bodied creatures sometimes consume them, mistaking the young Keepers for a particular fungus they find nutritious. Those that survive the first decade begin to reach further. Their filaments find stone. They taste the hollow's deeper structures. They begin to spiral.

By the time a Keeper has lived a century, its filaments span several feet. By the time it has lived a millennium, it has likely merged with at least one neighbor, their spirals interweaving, their central masses pressing together and fusing over decades of slow contact. The merged beings are more intelligent—if intelligence is the right word—than the individuals. They have more surface area for tasting differential, more filaments for sensing the hollow's patterns. Some of them have developed specialized structures: knots in their filament-braids that seem to store something, perhaps memory, perhaps something else.

The oldest composite in the hollow is perhaps fifty thousand years old. It occupies a crevice near the chamber's deepest point, where the geothermal warmth is strongest, where six or seven columns cluster in a configuration that might be deliberate. Its central mass—masses—have become indistinguishable from the stone around them. Its filaments extend throughout the chamber, so fine now that you could not see them even if you knew where to look. When the hollow breathes, it is breathing with this ancient one, through this ancient one. They have become the same process.

It does not think. Or it thinks in ways so slow that a single thought takes centuries to form, and you will never know what it has concluded. The younger Keepers orbit it. The beetles build their copper-and-verdigris towers in patterns that honor its position. The wrong-winged birds never land on the stones directly above it, and no one knows if this is reverence or fear or simply gradient—the differential there is so steep that their feathers cannot help but ripple.

---

**What they know:**

The Keepers have no eyes, no ears, no brain in any sense you would recognize. But they have been sensing this hollow for longer than the plateau above them has been flat. They know every groove in every column. They know the precise rhythm of the breathing, how it has changed over millennia, how it responded when the opening above was larger (before a rockfall narrowed it, three thousand years ago) and how it responded when a season of drought reduced the moisture to nearly nothing (seven hundred years ago, and three Keepers died, and their filaments still mark the walls where they fell).

They know the structures. Not their purpose—that knowledge is older than the Keepers themselves, perhaps older than life on this plateau—but their presence, their weight in the hollow's system. They know that the light-catching bowl is connected to something beneath the floor, something that runs deeper than the geothermal source, something that occasionally pulses with a differential so strange that the Keepers nearby go dormant for days afterward. They know that the platforms are not random, that their positions form a pattern that makes sense only if you consider dimensions the Keepers can taste but cannot name.

They do not know what built this place. They do not care. The Keepers are not curious in any way you would recognize. They grow toward strong gradients and away from weak ones. They merge when proximity allows. They release spores when pressure demands. They are as close to pure being as a living thing can come, and they have been here so long that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has begun to dissolve.

---

**Your presence:**

When you entered the hollow, the nearest Keeper tasted you immediately. The differential you brought—your body heat, your exhalation, the electrical field of your nervous system—registered across its filaments like a stone dropped in still water. Within seconds, that information had propagated through the network. Within a minute, every Keeper in the hollow knew you were there.

They are not alarmed. You are not the first visitor, merely the most recent. Others have come before—other creatures from the plateau above, other thinking beings who found the seam in the basalt and descended. Some stayed. Their bodies enriched the moss beds, their bones dissolved into the channels beneath the soil, their differential joined the system that feeds the Keepers still. Some left, carrying with them memories of a place that should not exist, that breaks rules they did not know they believed in.

You may do either. The hollow does not prefer. The Keepers do not prefer. They are already growing toward you, their finest filaments extending in your direction, tasting the edges of your presence. If you stand still long enough, you will feel them—a sensation like static, like being watched, like the air growing slightly thicker around your ankles and wrists.

They are welcoming you. Or they are measuring you. Or they are simply doing what they do, what they have always done, growing toward whatever differential offers itself.

The hollow breathes. The Keepers spiral. The structures wait, patient as the stone that holds them, for whatever comes next.
