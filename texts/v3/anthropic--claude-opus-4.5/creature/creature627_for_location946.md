**The Tenders of the Hatchery Cisterns**

They are born above, but they become something else down here.

The keepers arrive as apprentices at fourteen or fifteen, blinking in the amber gaslight, lungs laboring against air that feels like breathing through wet cloth. Their skin is still the color of the surface world—ruddy, freckled, marked by sun. Within a year, that changes. The pigment fades to something between parchment and mushroom-flesh, a pallor that reads as illness topside but marks belonging in the vaults. Their eyes don't change color, but the pupils learn to open wider, and they develop a flinch in full daylight that never quite leaves them, even after decades.

Marek has been head keeper for eleven years. Before him it was his aunt, and before her a man named Castellan whose portrait—rendered in oils that have bloomed with moisture damage—hangs in the main junction chamber. Marek is perhaps fifty, though the unchanging climate makes age difficult to read. His hands are the hands of his profession: fingers slightly webbed from years of immersion, nails thick and ridged, palms mapped with pale scars from the freshwater crabs that sometimes mistake a reaching hand for competition. He speaks rarely and quietly, not from temperament but from practice—sound carries strangely in the vaults, and the mother-fish grows agitated at loud voices.

His second is a woman called Drei, though that's not a name so much as a designation. The keepers number themselves in the old language, and she is the third of her cohort to survive the first five years. The others—Ein and Zwei—succumbed to the lung-wet, a fungal infection that colonizes those whose bodies never adapt to the constant moisture. Drei breathes with a faint whistle that she's learned to suppress when approaching the more sensitive pools. She keeps a small notebook wrapped in waxed cloth, and in it she records the lineages of the salamanders in a cramped shorthand that only she can read.

The apprentices sleep in the upper vaults, where the heat is merely oppressive rather than stupefying. They share long dormitories carved into the limestone—narrow alcoves fitted with rope beds that must be replaced every season as the fibers rot. The older keepers have private chambers deeper in, closer to their charges, and some of them have stopped sleeping on beds altogether. Marek drowses in a hammock of woven reed that hangs above the salamander pools, waking every few hours to check the temperature by touch, the gills by count, the water by taste. He can tell from a sip whether the mineral content has shifted, whether the feed is adequate, whether something is beginning to die.

There are twenty-three keepers currently, not counting the apprentices. This is fewer than a generation ago; the city above has developed other enthusiasms, other projects, and the budget for the cisterns has contracted. But the work itself cannot contract—the eels still need culling, the channels still need clearing, the mother-fish still needs feeding—and so each keeper has absorbed the duties of those who were not replaced. They are stretched thin as the pale ferns that grow in the margins, and like those ferns, they have learned to subsist on less light than should be possible.

**Those Who Are Not Quite Keepers**

The workers carving the eastern expansion are a different breed. They come down for shifts of eight or ten hours and then return to the surface, their lungs not yet colonized by the humid air, their skin still holding its original color. They are stonecutters and miners by trade, hired through the guild, and they regard the keepers with a mixture of pity and superstitious unease. The keepers, for their part, tolerate the noise of the pickaxes the way one tolerates a seasonal storm—something that will pass, something necessary, something that nonetheless sets the teeth on edge.

The foreman of the expansion crew is a heavyset man named Bosc who has learned just enough of the keepers' hand-signals to communicate when the work must stop. He knows the signs for *too warm*, *hatching*, *mother moving*, and *silence now*. He does not know the sign for *one of ours has died*, which requires a complex sequence involving both hands and a slight bow of the head, because the keepers have not shown it to him. They do not expect him to need it. They do not expect him to stay.

There are others who pass through: the municipal inspector who descends twice yearly to count the stock and certify the operation, always accompanied by a boy carrying a lantern and a look of barely suppressed terror; the engineer who maintains the pump system, an ancient man named Tull who lost his hearing decades ago and communicates through written notes on waterproof slate; the occasional scholar who wishes to study the mother-fish or document the adaptation of the salamanders, permitted to observe from approved distances, never permitted to touch.

And there are the ferrymen who bring supplies—sacks of feed, casks of salt, replacement glass for the lamp mantles—down through the main shaft in a hand-cranked lift that groans with rust and age. They are surface people who have made their peace with the brief submersion into heat and dark, and they unload quickly, efficiently, without meeting the keepers' eyes. It is not hostility. It is the discomfort of seeing someone too far along a path you chose not to walk.

**The Mother-Fish and Those Who Attend Her**

She has her own keepers—two of them, always, working in rotation. Currently they are Loren and Vess, though Vess has begun coughing in a way that worries the others, and there is quiet discussion of who will replace her if the lung-wet takes hold.

Loren has tended the mother-fish for nineteen years. He was not selected for the role; he simply never left the deep pool after his first assignment there, and eventually the others stopped trying to rotate him out. He knows her moods by the displacement of water. He knows her health by the color of what she leaves behind. He has touched her once, years ago, when she surfaced fully for the first and only time in his tenure—a vast pale shape breaching into the gaslight, her eye fixing on him with something he refuses to call recognition. His hand found her flank before he understood he was reaching. The skin was cool despite the heated water, and it felt less like fish-scale than like wet stone.

She ate from his hand for a week afterward. Then she stopped, returning to her usual pattern of taking food only from the long-handled nets they lower into the deep. Loren does not know what he did to lose the privilege. He has spent nineteen years not knowing.

Vess came to the mother-fish pool after her partner drowned in the eel channels—a rare accident, a foot caught in a grate, the water only ankle-deep but enough when you cannot free yourself. She does not speak of him. She watches the deep pool with the attention of someone who has learned that even shallow water holds deaths. The mother-fish seems to sense something in her; she surfaces more often when Vess is on duty, her vast shape drifting just below the reach of the light, as if observing the observer.

What species the mother-fish once was, no one can say with certainty. The records from her introduction list her as a *Polyodon spatula*—a paddlefish, native to rivers thousands of miles away. But that was forty years and countless meals ago, and she has grown beyond any paddlefish on record. Her snout has shortened, or perhaps thickened; her eyes have clouded and then cleared into something with too many reflective layers; her body has taken on the paleness of the salamanders who share her subterranean world. She is perhaps eight feet long now, perhaps longer—no one has measured her in decades, and she does not fully surface for measurement.

The keepers have a term for creatures that change down here, that adapt beyond their origins: *vault-touched*. The salamanders are vault-touched, their gills more elaborate than their surface cousins, their sensitivity to vibration acute enough that they scatter before a footstep lands. The freshwater crabs are vault-touched, their shells incorporating the calcium deposits that encrust the pipes, growing armor that gleams like wet bone. Even the eels—though raised from stock introduced each generation—grow differently in the warm seep-water, their lateral lines more pronounced, their behavior more coordinated than any surface school.

The mother-fish is the most vault-touched of all. And sometimes, watching her rise, Loren wonders if the term might apply to him as well.

**The Children of the Cisterns**

Not all keepers were born above.

It is not spoken of in official records, but it is known: sometimes keepers partner with other keepers, and sometimes there are children, and sometimes those children are raised in the vaults from birth. They are few—the damp is hard on infant lungs, and many do not survive—but those who live become something the surface has no word for.

There is a girl named Kess who has never seen the sun. She is perhaps eleven, small for her age, her skin so pale it seems to glow in the gaslight. Her eyes have developed a reflective quality that makes them shine like the mother-fish's when light catches them directly. She cannot read—no one thought to teach her, and the books brought down have all long since swelled with moisture and become illegible—but she can identify every salamander in the western pools by the pattern of its gill-fringes. She can tell when the eels are ready for culling by the way they move. She can hear the pickaxes of the eastern expansion from chambers where the sound should not carry.

Kess does not have an official role. She is not apprenticed, not numbered in the old language, not recorded in any municipal register. She exists in the margins of the hatchery, moving through the chambers like the pale ferns—persistent, uncounted, drawing sustenance from sources the surface world does not acknowledge.

There are two others like her: a boy called Null, because he was born too small to be expected to survive; and an older child of indeterminate gender who answers to Stillwater and who has not spoken aloud in three years, though their hands move fluidly through the keeper-signs. Null helps with the crab-tending, his small fingers able to reach into crevices the adults cannot access. Stillwater assists Drei with the salamander records, copying the shorthand into new notebooks when the old ones grow too damp, their memory apparently perfect for the patterns if not for spoken words.

Marek knows they are there. He does not report them. The municipal inspector knows they are there—has seen them moving in the far chambers—and he does not report them either. There is an understanding, unspoken and unexamined, that the cisterns make their own children now, and that some things the surface need not govern.

**The Ancestors in the Walls**

In the oldest section, where the rough-cut stone predates the city's founding records, there are carvings.

They are not decorative. They are not commemorative. They seem, if anything, instructional: diagrams of pool arrangements, of water-flow patterns, of creatures that do not quite match any the current keepers raise. One panel, nearly effaced by mineral deposits, appears to show the mother-fish—or something like her—attended by figures whose proportions are not quite human. Their limbs are too long. Their eyes are rendered as simple circles, lacking pupils.

The keepers do not worship the carvings. They do not worship anything; the damp has long since rotted any capacity for the numinous out of their daily existence. But they acknowledge the carvings in small ways. Drei touches the salamander panel each time she passes it, a gesture so habitual she no longer notices she does it. Loren has been known to address the mother-fish carving when he is alone, speaking to it in a low murmur, telling it of her health, her moods, her occasional rises. The apprentices learn quickly not to deface the carved walls, not because they are told it is forbidden, but because the older keepers simply stop teaching anyone who shows disrespect.

There were others here before. That much is clear. Others who raised things in the warm seep-water, who carved instructions into stone for those who would come after, who understood something about the deep pools that the current keepers are still learning. Whether they died out, or moved on, or simply became something that no longer needed to carve—this is not known. The carvings do not say. The carvings show what to do, not who did it.

Sometimes, in the deepest chambers, the keepers find tools that are not their tools. A ceramic scraper of unfamiliar design. A net woven from fibers that do not rot in the moisture. A feeding implement shaped for a hand with one too many joints. These are collected and stored in a locked cabinet in Marek's quarters, and they are not shown to the municipal inspector. They are not shown to anyone.

Marek has counted forty-seven such objects in his tenure. His aunt collected thirty-two. Castellan's portrait hangs beside a cabinet that holds his collection, now too corroded to examine. The cabinet is never opened.

**What Moves Between the Pools**

Not everything in the cisterns is there because the city put it there.

The crabs came first—or rather, were first noticed—seventeen years ago, emerging from a crack in the foundation that has since been widened into a proper channel. They breed in the mineral-rich water without any need for stocking or management. The keepers learned to harvest them, learned their behavior, learned which cracks they come from and which deeper chambers they return to when disturbed. The crabs are vault-touched, certainly, but they are also wild in a way the eels and salamanders are not.

There are smaller things too. Pale shrimp no longer than a fingernail, appearing in the overflow channels. Flatworms that cling to the undersides of the ceramic tiles, their bodies translucent enough to show the brickwork through them. Beetles with no eyes and antennae longer than their bodies, clustering near the fern-growth, vanishing when the gaslights pass too close. None of these were introduced. None of these are in the records. They simply are, as if the warm water and the darkness called them into being, or as if they were always here and merely waited for the pools to be built before revealing themselves.

And then there is the thing that is not the mother-fish.

Loren has seen it twice. Drei has seen it once. Marek has never seen it but believes the others absolutely, because he has heard it moving in the eastern channels where the new expansion opens into older stone.

It is large—larger than the mother-fish, which should not be possible in the shallow pools and narrow channels. It moves without rippling the water, which should not be possible for anything with mass. It is pale, but not the pale of vault-touched creatures; it is the pale of things that have never seen light of any kind, not even gaslight, not even the faint bioluminescence of certain deep pools. Its eyes—if it has eyes—have not been seen.

The keepers do not speak of it. They do not report it. They do not know what it eats, or where it rests, or how it came to be here. But when the expansion crews break through into a new seam of porous rock, when the groundwater begins to seep into a freshly carved basin, the keepers wait three days before introducing any stock. They wait, and they watch, and they do not explain why.

Sometimes the new basins are empty after three days. Sometimes they are not.

**The Hours Before Dawn**

Above, the city has a name. The streets have names. The people go about their business with the certainty of those who have never felt the ground breathe beneath them.

Below, the keepers work their rotations, and the apprentices learn the hand-signs, and the mother-fish rises and descends in patterns that might be random and might be meaningful and might be something in between. The eels slide over one another in their thousands. The salamanders fan their gills in water that tastes of iron and old stone. The crabs cluster and disperse according to rhythms no one has fully mapped.

In the eastern expansion, the pickaxes fall silent as the crew rotates out. The raw limestone glows pale in the gaslight, not yet darkened, not yet colonized by the life that will come. The seep-water gathers in the rough-cut basins. In three days, the keepers will come to inspect.

Kess moves through the older chambers, checking temperatures by touch, counting the salamanders by gill-pattern, listening for the distant sound of something large shifting in the deep pools. She does not know the name of the city above. She does not know that cities have names. She knows only the vaults, the water, the warmth, the endless becoming.

When she sleeps, she dreams of the carvings—of hands with too many joints, of eyes that are only circles, of instructions written in a language that was old when the stone was young.

She is learning to read them.
