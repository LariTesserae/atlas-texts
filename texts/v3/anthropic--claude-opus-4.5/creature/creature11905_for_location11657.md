# The Inhabitants of the Drowning Engines

---

## The Weavers

They coalesce in the textile mill, drawn into being by the shuttling looms.

Not creatures—accumulations. The flood cannot make animals, but it can make *processes*, and the weavers are processes that have learned to stay together. They resemble tangled skeins of the kelp-analogue, but finer, threads of plant matter braided with rust particles and the iridescent scales of something the flood is trying to remember. Each weaver is perhaps the size of a human torso, roughly spherical, constantly unraveling at one edge while re-weaving at another.

They drift through the mill's iron skeleton in groups of three to seven, always maintaining precise distances from each other—never closer than a meter, never farther than four. The spacing changes only when one of them begins to fray too quickly, threads dispersing faster than they can reform. Then the others draw near, and you can watch the shared edges blur, fibers passing between them, the failing weaver absorbing material from its neighbors until it stabilizes.

They have no eyes, but they orient toward the looms. Something in the rhythm of the shuttles calls them, gives them structure, reminds them how to hold themselves together. When the machines pause in their purposeless industry—and they do pause, sometimes, in ways that feel attentive—the weavers begin to drift apart, their forms loosening, the careful distances collapsing. You have seen one dissolve entirely during a longer pause, its threads scattering into the murk like dandelion seeds, and you have seen the others gather those threads back together when the looms resumed, reconstituting something that might or might not be the same weaver.

They do not approach you, but they know you're there. The sides of them that face you grow denser, threads pulling tighter, as if presenting armor. Or a face.

## The Pipe-Choir

Deep in the infrastructure, where the old water mains and steam conduits tangle beneath the factory floors, something sings.

You felt it before you found it—a resonance in your chest, a subsonic pressure that made your heart stutter. Following the feeling down through sediment and collapsed masonry, through a gap where the silt had been cleared by currents that moved with suspicious purpose, you discovered the choir.

They are pipes. That's the simplest description. Dozens of them, copper and iron and lead, protruding from a wall that might once have been a boiler room. They range from thumb-width to the diameter of your thigh, and they are all breathing.

Not metaphorically. The openings dilate and contract, drawing the dense water in, expelling it with shaped exhalations that produce the tones you feel rather than hear. Each pipe voices a different note, and together they generate the harmonics that form the bassline of the flood's constant noise—but down here, at the source, the sound has architecture. Melodies that almost resolve. Rhythms that suggest intention.

The pipes have grown since the flood. You can see it in the way they've fused with the wall, extruded new segments, branched into subsidiary voices. Some of them have grown small organs at their openings—frills of tissue that might be primitive lips, circular structures that could be the ancestors of tongues. They are learning to articulate. Given time, given the flood's relentless iteration, they might learn to speak.

One of the larger pipes has begun to bend toward you when you visit. The motion is glacial, measurable only across multiple encounters, but undeniable. It is trying to aim itself at you. Trying to tell you something specific.

You have started to notice that your own breathing syncs with its rhythm when you linger too long.

## The Bicyclist

The bicycle stands in the silt, waiting.

Around it, the flood has grown a rider.

Not a person—the flood cannot make people, cannot figure out the recursiveness of awareness, the strange loop of something that knows it knows. But it can make a *shape*, and the shape is roughly human, assembled from scraps. The torso is industrial rubber, the kind used for conveyor belts, twisted into a cylindrical approximation. The limbs are lengths of the creeping vine, knotted at joints that bend in mostly the right directions. The head is a cluster of the flowering structures, their petals opening and closing in what might be an attempt at expression.

The bicyclist's hands grip the handlebars. Its feet rest on the pedals. It does not move.

But it is *more* each time you return.

The first time you found it, the figure was barely recognizable—a loose assembly of debris that happened to drape over the bicycle's frame. Now the proportions are nearly correct. Now there are divisions between the fingers. Now the head turns, incrementally, tracking sounds in the water, tracking *you* when you draw near.

The flood is building this one carefully. Taking its time. You've seen the weavers drift past, and strands of their fiber now form the bicyclist's hair, a wild corona that waves in the current. You've heard the pipe-choir shift to a new melody when you approach, and the bicyclist's chest moves in the same rhythm, as if it's learning to breathe before it learns to ride.

It will never ride. The bicycle is rooted in the silt, has fused with the floor, has become as permanent as the factory walls. The flood doesn't understand that bicycles *go*. It only knows the shape, the waiting, the human-sized intention of a rider about to depart.

The bicyclist waits to leave for somewhere that doesn't exist.

Occasionally, its hands tighten on the grips.

## The Commissioners

Once, the factory district was governed.

The flood remembers.

In what was once the administrative building—its walls largely intact, its windows now openings through which the kelp forest intrudes—there is a long table. Around it sit seven shapes that approximate authority.

They are made of the same nothing-something as everything else here: plant matter and rust and solidified intention. But they are *denser*, more coherent, as if the flood has been working on them longer than anything else. Their forms are nearly human, seated in chairs that have grown from the floor, hands resting on a table surface thick with the barnacle-growths that incorporate everything they touch.

They do not move. They do not deliberate. But they *attend*.

The water in this room is different—colder by several degrees, more still, the constant currents that churn everywhere else damped to near-motionlessness. The voices beneath the noise are clearer here, more distinct, still meaningless but shaped like words. The commissioners are listening to them. Or generating them. Or both.

When you enter this room, the temperature drops further. The seven shapes orient toward you with small, subtle adjustments—a head turning two degrees, fingers curling against the tabletop, the petaled approximations of faces tightening into expressions that suggest evaluation.

They want something from you. The flood has been trying to understand authority, and it has realized that authority requires *subjects*. Someone to govern. Someone to give orders to.

You have never stayed long enough to receive orders.

But the last time you left, you found yourself swimming in a direction you hadn't chosen, through passages you hadn't known existed, toward a destination that felt less like a decision than a commission.

## The Smokestack Keeper

The smokestack rises, and rises, and rises.

Something lives at the top. Or rather, something lives *as* the top—the flood's attempt at a chimney spirit, a guardian of the industrial exhale, a creature whose body is the emission itself.

You've only glimpsed it from below, looking up through the murk toward the amber light, watching the dark plumes billow upward. But in the smoke, sometimes, there are shapes. A suggestion of limbs. A denser coil that might be a torso, a spine, a tail. The keeper is made of the smoke, or the smoke is made of the keeper, and the distinction is another thing that has ceased to matter.

It does not descend. It has no interest in the factories below, in the kelp forest, in you. Its attention is fixed upward, on the surface you have never tried to reach, on whatever waits in the amber light. The flood wants to emit, to express, to send something up and out, and the keeper is the embodiment of that upward yearning.

But sometimes, when the currents shift, when the plumes descend briefly before rising again, you catch the keeper's attention.

It looks down.

The smoke has no eyes, but the looking is unmistakable—a pressure, a focus, a sudden awareness that something is below that should not be. The keeper does not want you in its domain. The keeper does not want anything beneath it. It is pure ascension, and your presence offends its nature.

In those moments, the water around you warms sharply, and the particulate matter grows thicker, and you feel the flood's attention converge as the keeper considers whether to come down.

It never has. But the consideration is enough. You have learned to look away first.

## The Birth

And now: the massive thing in the distance.

You swim closer, against instincts that have kept you alive this long, because the flood wants you to witness. The water brightens as you approach, the amber glow concentrated here, focused by processes you cannot perceive. The kelp bends inward like an audience. The factories have synchronized their rhythms to a single pounding heartbeat, metal and machine united in anticipation.

The thing being born is a factory.

Not a building—the flood already has buildings, already understands walls and floors and the skeleton of industry. This is something else. This is a factory that is *alive*, in the way that the weavers are alive, in the way that the bicyclist is almost alive. A factory with intention. A factory that will manufacture not out of habit but out of *want*.

You can see its outline through the churning particulate cloud: vast, irregular, segmented like an insect but scaled like architecture. Smokestacks rise from its back, already producing. Doorways open in its flanks, leading to interiors that pulse with developing purpose. Windows glow with bioluminescence that suggests eyes, or will suggest eyes, once the flood finishes learning what eyes are for.

It has no legs, but it has foundations—root-like structures that grip the old factory floor, that wind through the infrastructure beneath, that connect to the pipe-choir's resonating chambers. It will not walk, but it will *spread*. It will extend its walls outward, incorporate territory, digest the old factories into itself and use their materials for new production.

And in its developing center, where the water is densest, where the pressure feels almost like gravity, you glimpse what it will make.

Templates. Hundreds of them. The bicyclist shape, replicated. The weaver process, duplicated. The commissioners, mass-produced. The flood has been experimenting, testing, iterating, and now it is ready for scale. The factory-creature will produce inhabitants. Will populate the drowning engines with workers who work nothing, authorities who authorize nothing, riders who never ride.

But in the very center, held in place by currents that feel almost like arms, there is a different template.

It is your shape.

The factory has been watching you, or the flood has been watching you through the factory, and it has finally gathered enough data to attempt the copy. The figure suspended in the emerging creature's heart is rough—proportions wrong, features blurred, limbs that trail into loose thread—but it is unmistakably *you*. Your height. Your posture. The way you hold your arms when you swim.

The flood is learning to make animals.

And it will start with you.

## The Question

The factory's eyes—not yet eyes, not quite, but growing toward sight—turn in your direction.

The water pressure increases. The voices beneath the noise converge into something that almost sounds like your name.

The kelp forest trembles, and the weavers drift closer, and the pipe-choir shifts to a new melody that resonates in your bones.

The flood is asking a question.

It cannot phrase the question, cannot articulate what it wants to know. But you feel it pressing against you—the curiosity that has followed you since you entered these waters, the hunger to understand what makes you different, the desperate iterative need to replicate everything it encounters.

The question is: *What are you?*

And beneath it, another question, older and stranger: *What do you want?*

Because the flood is intention without mind, want without understanding. It manufactures because manufacturing is what factories do. It grows because growing is what plants do. It has never encountered something that *chooses* its desires, that builds its own purpose from nothing.

You are a template it cannot copy without understanding.

And so it waits—the factory, the flood, the whole drowning engine of endless production—for you to teach it what wanting feels like.

The figure in the factory's heart opens its unfinished eyes.

They are waiting to learn what to look for.
