# Those Who Fill the Lattice

## The Permanent Adaptation

They call themselves *mensen*, which means nothing more than "people" in a language that no longer remembers being a language. They have lived here long enough that their bodies have begun to answer the city's questions.

Their skin runs slick. Not wet exactly, but prepared for wetness—a subcutaneous oil that beads moisture away from pores, that keeps the constant humidity from rotting them where they stand. Their eyes have grown larger across the generations, pupils capable of constricting to pinpoints in the city's relentless glow, though they never need to dilate for darkness that never comes. Their ears are the most changed: asymmetrical now, the left grown slightly cupped to catch the bass-note warnings of construction waves, the right developed finer structures for parsing speech from the crystalline chatter, from the vendor-shriek, from the thrumming engine roar of everything.

They are shorter than their ancestors must have been. Compact. Shoulders that fold inward for passage through the narrowest routes, ribcages that compress more than seems possible when the pressure-winds demand it. Their bones are denser than they should be, weighted against the gusts that would otherwise carry them off catwalks into the humming dark between structures.

When mensen are born, they do not cry. They *click*—a sharp crystalline sound that the fauna answer, that the walls seem to register, that announces to the local architecture: *here is another one, make room, we are still here*.

## The Districted

There are no nations. No governments in any sense the word once carried. There are *districts*, which are less places than patterns—densities of people who have learned to read the same signs, speak the same acoustic shortcuts, share the same folk-maps of routes likely to remain open.

A district might span three vertical layers and two hundred horizontal meters, or it might stretch thin across a kilometer of catwalks while claiming no depth at all. Districts merge, split, reorganize themselves when construction waves redraw their boundaries. The people adapt. They have always adapted. A child born in the wet-zones who finds herself, after a major restructuring, suddenly living in a dry-zone corridor will spend months relearning everything: which harmonics mean safety, which crystalline songs indicate fauna worth harvesting, which neighbors can be trusted with the new routes.

The districted have *anchors*—individuals whose role is to remember. Not maps, exactly, because maps become lies within weeks. The anchors remember *relationships*: this junction tends to connect to that market-zone. This thermal chimney usually vents near water-processing. This acoustic signature means construction fifteen minutes out, unless the bass-note carries this particular flutter, in which case you have perhaps three. The anchors do not write these things down. They speak them into the ears of children, who speak them into the ears of other children, who grow up and become anchors themselves or forget and become merely *lived*, those who inhabit the district without carrying it.

## The Weavers

In the membrane-tunnels that span the widest gaps between structures, the Weavers work.

They are mennesens like any other, but their hands have grown clever with the city's own materials. The membrane-tunnels are semi-living things, stretched between anchor-points, maintaining themselves through processes that might be biological or might be architectural—no one is certain. But the membranes tear. Pressure-winds shred them. Construction waves sever their anchor-points and leave them flapping like dying things until they dissolve back into the moisture.

The Weavers repair them, or grow new ones, or convince the city to grow new ones—the distinction is theological, disputed between Weaver-schools that have been arguing for generations longer than anyone can remember. They carry with them seed-structures harvested from the city's construction-wave detritus: tiny crystalline templates that, planted in the right moisture conditions and sung to with the right frequencies, will begin to extrude membrane-material. The Weavers know the songs. They claim the songs were taught to them by the city itself, in the vibrations of the bass-note, in the patterns of the pressure-winds. Other mensen consider this metaphor or madness, depending on how much they need a new membrane-tunnel and how expensive the Weavers are charging.

A Weaver's hands are stained the color of wet pearl, permanently iridescent from contact with the membrane-secretions. They smell like ozone and baking bread. They tend toward silence, conserving their voices for the frequencies that matter, and when they do speak, other mensen listen—not because Weavers have authority, but because a Weaver's voice has been trained to cut through noise that would drown anyone else.

## The Harvesters

The crystalline fauna are not food. Not exactly. But they are *useful*.

The Harvesters work the wet-zones primarily, though they can be found anywhere the singing nothing swarms thick enough to be worth collecting. They carry nets woven from their own hair—mensen hair holds up better than any fiber they've found, and the fauna seem less alarmed by it, as if recognizing something biological in the material. They catch the clickers, the chime-crawlers, the small wet-glass that cluster on moisture-rich walls, and they process them before the shells can dissolve back into the water-film.

Processing is delicate. The crystalline structures, if treated correctly—dried at specific temperatures, exposed to specific frequencies, crushed in specific sequences—yield materials the city cannot or does not provide. Pigments that hold color in the constant humidity. Abrasives that can reshape ceramic-polymer. Resonators that, embedded in clothing or jewelry, help the wearer parse the city's acoustic chaos. The largest fauna, the cat-sized ones that produce almost-musical tones, yield shells that can be carved into instruments, into art, into devotional objects for the small strange religions that bloom and fade in the endless warrens.

Harvesters develop a relationship with their quarry that outsiders find unsettling. They learn to predict where swarms will form by reading the city's moisture patterns. They learn to call specific fauna closer with sounds that mimic the city's own construction-frequencies. Some Harvesters claim to dream in the crystalline chatter, to understand things in their sleep that they cannot articulate in words. Others simply do their work, collect their materials, sell to the markets, and go home.

The Harvesters' children often continue the trade. The knowledge is too embodied, too tied to specific sensory attunements, to pass easily to outsiders. A Harvester family might work the same wet-zone for generations—or would, if construction waves did not periodically reorganize the wet-zones into something unrecognizable. When this happens, Harvester families migrate, seeking new territories, sometimes merging with other families whose zones have similarly vanished, sometimes dissolving into the general population of the merely lived.

## The Route-Runners

Information is a fluid here, and the Route-Runners are the channels through which it flows.

They are young, mostly. Speed matters more than experience when the routes themselves change faster than anyone can learn them. A Route-Runner knows how to read the city: which walls are darkening toward construction, which harmonics mean a passage has hours left to live, which pressure differentials indicate shortcuts opening in the maze. They carry messages, packages, sometimes people—the old, the injured, those who cannot navigate the vertical complexity alone. A Route-Runner might cross thirty districts in a single work-cycle, might see more of the Lattice Flats in a week than a stayed-put mensen sees in a lifetime.

They do not last long at the work. By thirty, their joints protest the climbing. By forty, their ears lose the fine discrimination that keeps them ahead of the construction waves. Former Route-Runners often become anchors, their vast experience of district-relationships invaluable once their bodies slow. Or they become teachers, training the next generation in the art of reading a city that refuses to be read.

Some Route-Runners never stop. They join the expeditions that seek the edge, their route-sense extending outward and outward, their messages growing stranger, their intervals growing longer, until they fade entirely into the noise. Perhaps they find what they're looking for. Perhaps there is no distinction, at the distance they reach, between finding and disappearing.

## The Market-Makers

Where mensen gather in sufficient density, where acoustic conditions allow, markets crystallize out of the population like the fauna crystallize out of the moisture.

The Market-Makers are not merchants themselves, necessarily. They are *organizers of gathering*, readers of crowd-flow and sound-topology. A good Market-Maker can look at a junction and know whether it will hold a market today—whether the competing vendor-frequencies will harmonize or cancel, whether the foot-traffic will reach critical mass, whether the construction waves will hold off long enough for commerce to complete.

They charge for this knowledge. A vendor arriving in an unfamiliar district will pay a Market-Maker to identify the optimal junction, the optimal time-window, the optimal acoustic register for their particular goods. Poorly advised vendors waste their effort screaming into sonic chaos where no one can parse their pitch. Well-advised vendors find themselves at the center of temporary economic ecosystems, their amplification systems layering into the acoustic bazaar at precisely the right frequencies to be heard without being drowned.

Market-Makers keep extensive records—not written, but sung into the crystalline shells of the larger fauna, which resonate with the stored information when properly activated. A Market-Maker's collection of resonator-shells is their most valuable possession, containing the accumulated wisdom of generation upon generation of crowd-reading, route-predicting, commerce-shaping knowledge. When a Market-Maker dies, their shells pass to their apprentices, who spend years learning to read the vibrations properly, arguing with each other about what the old songs meant.

## The Sound-Shapers

The noise is annihilating, but it can be sculpted.

The Sound-Shapers maintain the acoustic funnels, the pressure channels that direct the city's roar into something bearable. Without them, mennesens would go deaf within years, their ear-structures unable to parse anything through the undifferentiated wall of sound. The Sound-Shapers tune the passages, adjusting the curvature of walls, installing resonator-screens that filter specific frequencies, creating acoustic shadows where mennesens can speak at normal volume, can think without the city's voice drowning their own.

They are priests, in a sense, though they would reject the term. They serve the city by making it habitable. They study its patterns by immersing themselves in noise that would destroy anyone else—their ears surgically modified, their skulls packed with resonance-dampening materials that cost them half their hearing in exchange for protection for the other half. A Sound-Shaper hears the world through frequency-windows, missing entire ranges but perceiving those they retain with preternatural clarity.

The temples of the Sound-Shapers are the quietest places in the Lattice Flats: chambers so carefully tuned that the city's roar reduces to a whisper, where mennesens can hear their own heartbeats for the first time since infancy. Visitors to these temples often weep, overwhelmed by the silence, by the sudden awareness of how loud their lives have been. The Sound-Shapers watch impassively, their filtered hearing unable to process the visitors' sobs.

## The Climbers

Up is a direction that means something here, even though the structures rarely rise above seven stories. The difference between levels is not just altitude—it's air quality, sound profile, fauna density, construction-wave exposure. The lower levels, closest to whatever serves as ground, run thick with moisture and the larger crystalline creatures. The upper levels catch the thermal updrafts, the shriek of distant construction, the light that reaches them less filtered through the bridge-and-walkway lattice.

The Climbers make their lives in vertical movement, carrying goods and people between levels, knowing the routes up through the building-mesh as intimately as the Route-Runners know the horizontal passages. A Climber's body is rope and muscle, arms grown long and hands grown grip-strong, legs that never tire of stairs that never end.

They have their own districts, the Climbers—vertical communities that span six levels at a single horizontal point, all their anchor-relationships oriented up-and-down rather than side-to-side. A Climber child learns the language of tensile strength before they learn to walk flat, knows the difference between a cable that will hold and a cable that will sing warning before it snaps.

When construction waves come, the Climbers ride them. They cling to surfaces as the architecture reshapes around them, reading the changes, adjusting their routes in real-time, sometimes finding themselves the first to discover new vertical paths opened by the city's self-modification. They are the explorers of their districts, in their way, their expeditions measured in meters upward rather than kilometers outward.

## The Deep-Dwellers

Below the lowest ordinary level, where moisture concentrations run so high that the walls themselves seem to dissolve into fog, the Deep-Dwellers make their home.

They are spoken of rarely and in careful terms. They are mennesens still—they must be—but they have adapted to conditions the upper-dwellers find unthinkable. Their skin has grown semi-permeable, exchanging moisture with the saturated air in ways that might be respiration or might be osmosis. Their voices carry strangely, tuned to frequencies the fog transmits rather than absorbs. Their relationships with the crystalline fauna have grown intimate, symbiotic: the Deep-Dwellers cultivate the largest forms, and the fauna seem to protect them, their resonant songs warning of dangers the Deep-Dwellers' modified senses cannot detect.

What they do down there is unclear. The Deep-Dwellers rarely ascend, and when they do, they speak little, trading crystalline materials unlike anything the upper Harvesters produce and then returning to the fog. Some mennesens believe they are excavating, digging toward the bedrock the city built itself upon, searching for something buried beneath the foundations. Others believe they have simply found a way to live that requires nothing from above, that they are becoming something other than mennesens, something that belongs to the city more than to the humanity that once built it.

When expeditions descend to find them, the expeditions rarely return. Those that do describe fog so thick that seeing becomes impossible, sounds so attenuated that speech becomes gesture, crystalline structures so large and complex that they might be buildings, might be creatures, might be the city itself dreaming in mineral form.

The Deep-Dwellers are patient. Whatever they are doing, they have time. They have always had time.

## The Children of the Lattice

They are born clicking, into the roar, into the light that never dims.

A mennesens child's first years are spent learning to parse: to distinguish speech from the crystalline chatter, to recognize the harmonics of approaching change, to map the sound-shape of home district against the sound-shapes of everywhere else. Their parents click at them, teaching the frequencies that mean *safe*, *danger*, *food*, *love*. Their siblings teach them the routes—or the routes teach themselves, the children learning through crawling and climbing which passages feel stable, which walls are darkening toward construction, which acoustic shadows offer respite.

By the time they walk, they are already becoming districted, their bodies memorizing the local patterns, their ears tuning to familiar voices, their skin calibrating to familiar humidity levels. A child raised in the wet-zones will feel dry-zone air as harsh, almost burning, the relative dehydration uncomfortable even when it poses no actual danger. A child raised in the upper levels will experience the fog of lower levels as drowning, their lungs confused by air that feels more like water.

They play in the crystalline swarms, the children. The smaller fauna tolerate them, perhaps recognizing non-threat, perhaps simply lacking the capacity to care. A mennesens child's first toy is often a clicker shell, harvested and dried, which resonates pleasingly when shaken, which chimes against other shells in patterns the child learns to control. The patterns become language. The language becomes identity. A child's clicker-rhythms mark them as belonging to particular districts, particular families, particular lineages of acoustic tradition.

And they learn, the children, what every mennesens learns eventually: that the city does not notice them. That their clicking does not register in its bass-note. That their lives, their districts, their markets and temples and carefully-maintained routes, are happening in the space between the city's processes, in the gaps the city's self-modification happens to leave. They learn to live in these gaps. They learn that the gaps are enough.

## The Memory-Keepers

In a city where the architecture reorganizes itself constantly, where no map survives longer than a week, something must hold the menschen's sense of themselves.

The Memory-Keepers maintain the oral archives: the sung histories, the resonator-shell libraries, the accumulated folk-wisdom of how to be a person in a place that refuses to hold still. They are not priests exactly, though they share some function with the Sound-Shapers, not historians exactly, though they argue endlessly about accuracy, not teachers exactly, though the young come to them to learn what cannot be learned from routes that will soon close.

They remember the districts that vanished. Not their locations—those are meaningless—but their *characters*, the acoustic qualities that made them unique, the fauna populations that thrived there, the social rhythms their mennesens developed. A Memory-Keeper can describe a district that reorganized three generations ago with enough precision that someone who once lived there might weep with recognition.

They also remember the expeditions. Not where they went—outward, always outward, the direction mattering less than the going—but what they reported, what patterns emerged from the increasingly strange messages, what might be learned about the city's scope from the failures to find its edge. The Memory-Keepers argue about what the expeditions mean. Some believe the edge exists but cannot be reached, that the city is simply too vast for mennesens-lifetimes to traverse. Others believe there is no edge, that the Lattice Flats curve somehow, that expeditions traveling outward must eventually return to where they started, though none have. Still others believe the city is growing, that the edge retreats faster than expeditions can pursue it, that the construction waves are conquest as much as maintenance.

The Memory-Keepers do not resolve these arguments. They preserve them. The arguments are themselves the memory, the sense that mennesens make of their condition, the persistent human need to understand what is not meant to be understood.

## The Patient Ones

In the low wet places, where the glow filters through a hundred layers, where the fog breathes in and out with pressures that might be the city's breathing, they have learned to wait.

Not for anything specific. Not for rescue or transformation or the edge that never comes. They wait the way the crystalline fauna wait: simply, completely, their existence its own justification. They raise their children in the fog, teach them the frequencies that carry through saturated air, cultivate their symbiotic fauna, and accept that they will die where they were born, in structures that will reorganize around their bones.

The Patient Ones have a saying, passed down through Memory-Keepers who may have invented it themselves: *The city breathes in. The city breathes out. We are the breath.*

It is not a religion exactly. Not a philosophy. It is something smaller and more durable, a practice of attention, a discipline of acceptance. The city does not notice them. The city will not change for them. They are happening in the gaps, and the gaps are enough, and the gaps are all there is, and there is a kind of peace in understanding this, a kind of freedom in the absolute enclosure.

The mennesens of the upper levels, the fast-dwellers, the Route-Runners and Market-Makers and Weavers with their hands full of urgent work, do not understand the Patient Ones. They find them unsettling, their slowness a rebuke, their contentment a puzzle. How can anyone be content here, in the roar, in the unchanging light, in the constant change?

The Patient Ones do not answer. They do not need to answer. They are busy being breath, being gap, being the mennesens-shaped spaces between the city's processes.

Somewhere above them, construction waves are coming. Somewhere below them, the Deep-Dwellers pursue whatever excavation requires descending toward bedrock that may not exist. Somewhere outward, expeditions are fading into the noise, their messages attenuating toward silence.

And in the low wet places, the Patient Ones wait. Not for anything. Just wait.

The city breathes in. The city breathes out.

They are still here.
