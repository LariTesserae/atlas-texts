**The Listeners**

They call themselves nothing. Names require sound, require the expectation that someone will speak and someone else will hear, and they abandoned that transaction so long ago that the very concept has atrophied. When the lowland traders ask what to call them, they make a gesture—hand flat, palm down, fingers spread, then slowly curling inward as the hand rises—that might translate as *those who remain* or *those who have settled* or simply *the ones still here*. The traders call them the Listeners, which is exactly wrong, but the plateau people don't correct them. Correction would require caring about the error, and they have learned to stop caring about sounds.

---

Their bodies have changed.

Not in any dramatic way—they're still recognizably human in form, if you encountered one in the lowlands. But the generations have done their quiet work. Their chest cavities are larger, ribcages flared to accommodate lungs that wring oxygen from air half as thick as what their ancestors breathed. Their skin has thickened into something almost leathery, perpetually wind-chapped but no longer raw from it, tanned to a deep copper-brown that doesn't burn under the unfiltered sun. Their eyes have developed secondary membranes like the grazing animals they share the plateau with—translucent shields that slide across automatically when the wind sharpens, giving them that same dreamy, half-absent look.

But it's the ears that mark them most profoundly. They haven't disappeared—evolution doesn't work that fast—but they've receded, grown smaller, pressed closer to the skull. The ear canals have narrowed. Some of the children are born with them nearly sealed, the passages reduced to vestiges that transmit only the deepest vibrations directly to bone. The elders consider this a blessing. To hear clearly here would be a kind of violence, a constant assault. Their partial deafness is not disability but adaptation, a gift the plateau has given them for staying.

They feel sound instead. Their bare feet read the stone's trembling the way lowlanders read facial expressions—this frequency means the wind is shifting west, that resonance means rain is eighteen hours away, this particular shudder means someone is walking through the northern pillar-forest. They feel the great harmonies in their sternums, in their teeth, in the fluid of their inner ears even when the mechanisms of hearing no longer function. The plateau speaks to them through their bones.

---

A family group—they organize in clusters of eight to twelve, rarely more—inhabits a wind-shadow on the plateau's eastern quarter, where three massive pillars have fallen against each other in an arrangement that creates a zone of acoustic cancellation. Here, the roar drops to something merely uncomfortable rather than obliterating. They've built their shelter into this triangle of relative quiet: low walls of fitted stone, roof of stretched hide weighted with rocks, everything angled to deflect rather than resist.

The grandmother is the oldest, though age is difficult to read on their weathered faces. She might be sixty or ninety; her joints move stiffly, her fingers have thickened with work, but her eyes remain sharp behind their membranes. She spends most of her days sitting at the shelter's entrance, watching the pillars, her hands busy with cordage she's twisting from dried plant fiber. She's mapping the argument.

For forty years she has tracked the patterns in the wind-song, marking them with knots in an ever-growing rope that coils in massive hanks around her sleeping area. A particular sequence of tones—she doesn't hear them, but she feels their pressure, their duration, their relationship to each other—gets a particular knot. The rope is her life's work, a physical record of what the plateau has said during her time here. She believes, in a way she would struggle to articulate even in their gesture-language, that if she can find the repetition—the moment when the argument circles back to something it has said before—she'll understand what's being debated.

Her daughter thinks this is beautiful but futile. Her granddaughter, seven years old, helps tie the knots anyway, learning the system without questioning it.

---

The children develop differently here.

In the lowlands, children learn by listening: to parents, to stories, to the ambient sounds of community that teach them how to be human among humans. Here, the children learn by watching with an intensity that unsettles visitors. They observe hands, faces, the angle of shoulders, the timing of breaths. They develop an almost preternatural sensitivity to physical presence—they know when someone is behind them, know when someone is angry or afraid or lying, know from the most subtle shifts in posture what a person wants before the person has decided to reach for it.

They play in ways that seem eerie to outsiders. Silent games of chase through the pillar-forests, navigating by the feel of vibration and the patterns of shadow. They'll pile atop each other in wind-sheltered hollows, a tangle of small limbs, communicating in touches and pressures that adults can no longer fully read. They're developing their own vocabulary, the eldest ones, a refinement of the adult gesture-language that includes signals the grandparents don't recognize. Each generation drifts a little further from what came before, adapting to what the plateau requires.

The seven-year-old granddaughter, the one who ties knots with the old woman, has a particular gift for the wind-riders. The membranous creatures will land on her if she holds still enough, something they rarely do for adults. She's learned their patterns, learned to position herself in their paths when they emerge during calmer moments. Sometimes she stands in the open with her arms spread and lets them settle on her in dozens, their fin-wings folding, their tiny bodies vibrating with some frequency she can feel through her skin. She's trying to learn what they know about the air currents. She's trying to read the wind through their choices.

Her mother worries about her. Not about the wind-riders—they're harmless—but about the intensity of her focus, the hours she spends alone at the edge of their territory, the way she's started sleeping outside the shelter on calm nights, pressed against the stone, feeling the plateau's pulse directly. The child is becoming something more adapted than her mother can follow. This is proper, perhaps inevitable, but it aches in a way the mother can't express even to herself.

---

The thing in the hollow pillars—they don't speak of it, but they know it.

It moves at night, and only at night, through the larger columns where erosion has carved passage-ways wide enough for something to pass. They feel it in the stone's vibration: a rhythm that doesn't match wind or earthquake, something with the cadence of locomotion. It's large—they can tell by the bass frequencies it produces when it moves—and it's careful. It never emerges into the open spaces where they might see it clearly. It never approaches their shelters.

The grandmother remembers her own grandmother telling her that the thing was already here when the first people climbed to the plateau. It was here before them and will be here after, part of the argument somehow, perhaps one of the voices the wind channels or perhaps the listener the argument is aimed at. The mythology is unclear, which is appropriate: clarity would suggest understanding, and the plateau does not permit that.

Once every few years, someone goes missing.

Not violently—no blood, no signs of struggle. Simply gone, their sleeping place empty in the morning, their belongings undisturbed. The community doesn't search for them. They've learned that searching is useless, that the missing are never found, that the plateau has taken them the way it takes everything eventually. Some say the thing in the pillars collects them, carries them down into chambers beneath the stone where they become part of whatever process has been running since before human memory. Some say they simply walked into the wind and kept walking, called by something they couldn't resist.

The grandmother's husband disappeared that way, thirty years ago. She doesn't mark it as tragedy. He went to the argument, she believes. He's part of the debate now, one more voice in the chorus. On nights when the harmonies briefly align, she thinks she can feel him—a particular frequency that wasn't there before he vanished, a subtle new note in the ancient chord.

She's probably wrong. But the belief sustains her, and sustenance is what the plateau provides least.

---

Trade with the lowlands happens twice a year, during the brief seasons when the wind patterns become predictable enough for outsiders to survive the ascent.

The plateau people bring crystal salt, dried algae pressed into cakes, the shed membrane-wings of wind-riders (valued by lowland healers for purposes the plateau people don't understand), and occasionally the bones of the grazing animals, dense and hard enough to take edges sharper than any lowland material. In exchange, they receive metal—they can't smelt it here, can't maintain fires hot enough in the thin air—and cloth woven from fibers that don't grow at this altitude, and sometimes news from the world below.

They don't care much about the news. It arrives in a language they can mostly lip-read, full of events and names that have no bearing on the pillar-songs or the rain cycles or the endless argument. Wars happen below, kingdoms rise and fall, religions spread and schism, and none of it changes the wind. The traders think the plateau people are simple, primitive, uninterested in the great affairs of civilization. The plateau people let them think this. Correction would require explanation, and explanation would require caring whether the lowlanders understood.

What they do want, sometimes, is people.

Not captives, not slaves—they wouldn't take anyone unwilling. But occasionally a trader brings someone who doesn't fit below: a person driven mad by lowland sounds, or born deaf in a society that shuns silence, or simply consumed by a restlessness that nothing in the conventional world can satisfy. These they welcome. Not warmly—warmth is a lowland affect, dependent on vocal reassurance—but with the practical hospitality of showing the newcomer how to position themselves in the wind-shadows, how to find the protected channels, how to read the stone's vibrations for coming weather.

Most don't survive the first season. The adjustment is too severe, the isolation too complete, the constant trembling too disorienting for minds that expect stillness. But some do. One in ten, perhaps, finds something they needed in the roar. They stay, learn the gesture-language, gradually lose the habit of trying to speak, eventually become indistinguishable from those born here. Their children will be fully of the plateau, and their children's children will have the narrowed ear canals, the flared ribs, the membrane-shielded eyes.

This is how the population sustains itself. Not through birth alone—the plateau is too harsh for large families—but through a slow absorption of those who couldn't survive anywhere else.

---

The oldest shelter on the plateau is also the strangest.

It sits near the center, where a ring of pillars produces, during certain wind conditions, a sustained chord of extraordinary beauty—and extraordinary danger. The frequencies there resonate with human organs in ways that can cause hemorrhaging, heart arrhythmia, unconsciousness. No one lives there now. But someone did, once.

The shelter is carved into living rock rather than built from fitted stones. Its chambers go deep, following natural fissures in the plateau's substance, branching into passages too narrow for adults to navigate. The walls are covered in marks—not writing exactly, but systematic, clearly intentional, tens of thousands of incisions made with stone tools over what must have been generations of work. The marks map the pillar-songs: pitches represented by position, durations by length, harmonies by clustering. It's a notation system for the argument.

No one now can read it. The knowledge was lost, or the system was never complete, or it records something that can't actually be decoded into meaning. The grandmother visits sometimes, when the winds permit safe passage, and sits in the outer chamber comparing the wall-marks to her rope of knots. She's found correlations—sequences on the wall that match sequences in her record—but correlation isn't translation. She's been working on this for twenty years and expects to die still working on it.

Her granddaughter has started visiting too, against her mother's wishes. The child traces the marks with her fingers, feeling their depth, their spacing, the subtle variations in how different carvers shaped their incisions. She's trying to learn through touch what can't be learned through sight. She believes—in the way children believe things, without doubt or evidence—that the marks are a message left for her specifically, across however many generations, by someone who knew she would come.

This is probably the plateau talking, shaping her toward purposes she can't understand. The plateau does this: gives people certainties they didn't choose, sends them down passages they might not have entered otherwise. The grandmother recognizes the signs. She doesn't interfere. Interference would require believing she knows better than the stone what her granddaughter should become.

---

During the harmonies—those brief moments after dawn when the argument pauses and the pillars find alignment—the people stop whatever they're doing.

It doesn't matter if they're eating, mending tools, tending wounds, even sleeping. The harmony wakes them, pulls them upright, turns their faces toward the sound they can't hear but feel in every bone. They stand or sit in stillness, breathing slowly, letting the music pass through them. Some cry, though they couldn't say why. Some laugh, which is rare here, the physical convulsion of it strange in their silent lives. The children go especially still, as if listening with their whole bodies, as if trying to memorize something that can't be held.

Then the gusts shift, the chaos returns, and they go back to their tasks. No one speaks of it—no one speaks of anything—but there's a particular gesture that passes between people who've shared a harmony: hand to chest, then hand extended outward, palm up. *I felt that. You felt that. We are here.*

The grandmother believes the harmonies are the argument's conclusions—brief moments when the debate reaches temporary consensus before collapsing back into discord. The mother believes they're random, statistical inevitabilities in a system complex enough to produce any pattern eventually. The granddaughter believes they're the thing in the pillars singing, showing itself in the only way it can, communicating something urgent and incomprehensible.

None of them are wrong. None of them are right. The plateau doesn't resolve into meaning that human categories can hold.

---

What will become of them?

The grandmother won't see it—her rope is almost finished, her knot-tying slowing as her fingers stiffen—but she suspects: her granddaughter's generation will move deeper into the stone, following passages that previous generations lacked the adaptation to survive. They'll find chambers where the vibrations are purer, where the argument can be felt without the interference of weather and distance. They'll become more of the plateau and less of themselves, which is proper, which is what staying has always meant.

Eventually, perhaps, they'll stop being recognizable as human at all. The thing in the pillars might once have been like them—might be what they're becoming, a hundred generations further along the same path. This doesn't frighten the grandmother. It seems, if anything, like a kind of success: to become so adapted to a place that you become the place itself, your body just another pillar singing in the wind.

The granddaughter, pressing her palm to the carved wall in the oldest shelter, feels certainty she can't explain. She will learn to read the marks. She will find the message. She will understand what the argument is about, and then—

And then the wind gusts, the moment passes, and she's just a child again, tired and hungry, making her way back through the pillar-forest to her family's shelter before dark.

The plateau keeps its secrets. It always has. It always will.

But it keeps its people, too, and that's a kind of love, if you're willing to define love as what refuses to let you go.
