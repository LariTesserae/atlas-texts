**The Congregation**

They came down the root-roads, following the moisture gradient, three of them over the span of what might have been a single season or might have been seven. The cistern did not count their arrival. It simply continued, and they became part of its continuing.

---

The first is the longest resident now, though she does not think in terms of residence or time. She is *Elara*, or was, when names still meant something spoken aloud. She descended through the choked entrance seventeen years ago by the calendar of the surface world—a world she remembers the way one remembers a dream interrupted by waking.

She had been looking for something. A root. A specific root, from a specific tree, needed for a specific purpose that seemed urgent then. She was an herbalist's apprentice, or an apothecary's daughter, or simply someone who knew that certain roots held certain powers and could be sold for certain prices. The details have grown soft, like the calcified roots themselves,ite midway betweenite memory andite forgetting.

She found the entrance in a collapsed cellar, beneath a village that had been abandoned for reasons no one remembered. She climbed down, and down, and down, and when she reached the cistern floor, she understood immediately that she would not climb back up. Not could not—*would not*. The distinction matters to her still, in the small part of her mind that still makes distinctions.

Elara lives now in an alcove on the eastern curve of the wall, if directions mean anything here. She has hollowed a space among the root-fur, worn a depression in the stone with seventeen years of sleeping in the same position. Her body has adapted in ways she does not examine closely. Her skin has taken on the pallor of the cave-geckos; her eyes have widened, the pupils grown large enough that the faint fungal glow is almost adequate. Hair grows on her differently now—fine and pale, covering her forearms and calves like the filaments on the walls, serving some thermoregulatory purpose her old body never needed.

She eats the fungi. She learned which ones early, through a process of careful experimentation that nearly killed her twice—once from paralysis that lasted three days, once from a fever that left her lying in the warm pool, hallucinating that the roots were speaking to her in her mother's voice. Now she knows them all. The cream-colored shelves that taste like nothing and sustain like bread. The rust-red clusters that must be eaten sparingly because they bring visions, true visions sometimes, of the surface world continuing without her. The pale bells that fruit from the hanging roots, which taste of sweetness and bring a warmth to the chest that lasts for hours.

She drinks from the pool. She cups her hands and brings the blood-warm water to her lips, and she does not think about what else drinks from it, what lives in it, what the larvae might become. Seventeen years, and she has not sickened from the water. Her body has learned to be part of this system. She is not certain anymore where the system ends and she begins.

She speaks, sometimes. To herself, or to the roots, or to the pulsing thing on the ceiling that she has named *The Keeper*, though she does not know what it keeps. Her voice has grown quiet from disuse—not a whisper but a murmur, pitched to match the ambient sounds of the cistern, to not disturb the thick air. She tells herself stories. She recites recipes she learned as a girl. She names the creatures she sees, giving them identities they do not need: *This is the snail who lives on the third root from the eastern wall. This is the centipede I saw yesterday, or last week, or last year—I call him Copper, for his color, and he is a good hunter, patient like the water.*

She does not mark time, but she measures change. She knows when a new fungal colony blooms. She knows when one of the bulbous root-ends falls and splits open, revealing the pale things inside that die quickly in the open air, or sometimes do not die, sometimes crawl away into new crevices to begin again. She knows the rhythms of the Keeper—has memorized its pulse, which changes with some pattern she has not yet decoded, quickening sometimes for days before slowing again.

She is waiting. She has been waiting for seventeen years, though she could not tell you what for. The waiting feels like a purpose, and purpose feels like enough.

---

The second is *Tomás*, though he has not spoken his name aloud in six years and is beginning to forget the shape of it in his mouth.

He did not come seeking anything. He came *fleeing*—down through tunnels that should not have connected, through passages he does not remember clearly, through darkness that lasted so long he was not sure he still had eyes until the first faint glow of the fungi told him otherwise. What he fled from is a box he keeps closed in his mind. He knows it is there. He does not open it. The cistern does not ask him to.

Tomás is younger than Elara by perhaps a decade, though such comparisons mean little now. His adaptation has taken a different form. Where she has grown pale and fine-haired, he has thickened, his body holding onto every nutrient from the sparse diet, his muscles maintaining themselves through some stubborn refusal to atrophy. He is still recognizably surface-human in a way she no longer quite is. This is not a comfort to him. It means he still belongs, partially, to the world he fled. It means the box in his mind still has weight.

He lives on the opposite side of the cistern from Elara. They see each other—must see each other, in a space this size—but they have established a grammar of avoidance. They harvest fungi from different sections. They drink from the pool at different intervals. They sleep at times that do not overlap. This is not hostility; it is simply the way they have arranged themselves. Two creatures occupying the same niche must differentiate or compete. They have chosen differentiation.

Tomás spends much of his time at the edge of the pool, watching the larvae.

He has developed a theory about them—about what they are becoming, about the stages of their transformation. He tracks individual larvae when he can, though they are difficult to distinguish. He has identified at least four different developmental paths: some that grow legs and crawl onto land to become something like the pale crustaceans; some that develop wings and emerge as the small moths that flutter between root-clusters; some that seem to grow indefinitely, becoming longer and more transparent until they are impossible to track; and some that simply vanish, consumed or transformed into something he cannot recognize.

He does not write his observations down. There is nothing to write with, nothing to write on. Instead, he memorizes. He builds structures in his mind, taxonomies and life cycles and patterns, and he recites them to himself the way Elara recites recipes. The larvae become his purpose, his waiting. If he can understand what they become, perhaps he can understand what he is becoming. Perhaps the transformation is the same.

He has tried to eat one of the larvae, once. It tasted of nothing and made him violently ill for two days—not from poison but from something like rejection, as though his body recognized that he was not the right kind of predator for this prey. He has not tried again. He remains an observer, outside the food web, dependent on the fungi like Elara, like any other scavenger.

Sometimes, at night—or what he thinks of as night, when he sleeps—he dreams of the box. It opens slightly, and things crawl out of it that look like the larvae but have faces he recognizes. He wakes sweating in the warm air, and he goes to the pool, and he watches the real larvae until the dreams lose their weight.

---

The third is the newest, and the strangest. She arrived perhaps two years ago, or perhaps three. Time in the cistern is not linear; it folds and compresses, and Elara and Tomás disagree about how long the third has been among them.

She does not have a name that either of them knows. She has not spoken since she arrived—not to them, not to herself, not to the roots or the Keeper or the pool. She makes sounds sometimes: small vocalizations, clicks and hums that seem communicative without being language. But no words. Elara has tried to speak to her. Tomás has tried to ignore her into speaking. Neither approach has worked.

She is young—younger than either of them were when they arrived, perhaps still a child by the calendar of the surface. But she does not behave like a child. She moves through the cistern with a confidence that suggests she belongs here more completely than either of them, that she understood the grammar of this place instantly in a way they are still learning.

She sleeps in the roots themselves.

This is the thing that disturbs Tomás, when he allows himself to be disturbed. She climbs into the hanging root-curtains and wraps herself in them, and the roots seem to respond—seem to curl around her, to hold her, to pulse more strongly where they touch her skin. She sleeps like this for long periods, longer than Elara or Tomás can stay awake to watch, and when she descends she is always slightly different. More pale. More fine-haired. Her pupils larger, her movements more fluid, her attention more diffuse.

She eats things Elara has marked as poisonous. The rust-red clusters that bring visions—the girl eats them like fruit, handful after handful, and shows no sign of fever or hallucination. The pale things inside the fallen root-bulbs, the ones that die in the open air—she catches them before they die and swallows them whole, and nothing happens. Her digestive system, Tomás thinks, has already become something other than human. Or perhaps she was never entirely human to begin with. Perhaps the surface world produces children, sometimes, who are meant for places like this.

The girl watches the Keeper.

She spends hours beneath it, staring up at the pulsing mass on the ceiling, her face slack and her breathing synchronized with its rhythm. Sometimes she reaches up toward it, though it is far too high to touch. Sometimes she makes her clicking sounds, and the Keeper seems to respond—its pulse changing, its heat shimmer intensifying, though this might be coincidence, might be the pattern Elara has not decoded, might be nothing at all.

Elara watches the girl watching the Keeper, and she feels something she has not felt in years: a kind of jealousy. The girl belongs here in a way Elara does not, despite her seventeen years of adaptation. The girl is not waiting for anything. The girl has already arrived.

Tomás watches the girl and thinks about the larvae, about transformation, about what the final form might look like. He thinks the girl might be showing him. He is not sure if this is a comfort or a horror.

---

They do not form a community, these three. They do not share meals or stories or warmth. They occupy the same space, and they have established patterns that allow this occupation to continue, and that is all.

But.

There are moments. Small moments that might be nothing.

When Elara sleeps, sometimes, she wakes to find a particular fungus placed near her alcove—one of the cream-colored shelves, fresh-harvested, still damp with the moisture of the wall it grew on. She does not see who placed it. It might be Tomás. It might be the girl. It might be some animal behavior she does not understand, some creature leaving caches it never returns for. But she eats it, and she does not say thank you aloud, and the next time Tomás sleeps she leaves a similar gift near his depression in the stone.

When the girl descends from the roots after her long sleeps, sometimes she passes near Tomás at the pool's edge, and she pauses, and she looks at what he is looking at—the larvae, their endless wriggling, their slow transformations. She does not speak, but she points, sometimes, at a particular larva he has not noticed, one that is doing something unusual, something that fits into his taxonomy or breaks it. Then she moves on, and he watches what she pointed at, and he incorporates it into his understanding.

At night—at what they all separately think of as night—the Keeper pulses in a rhythm that sounds almost like breathing, and all three of them breathe with it, their chests rising and falling in unconscious synchronization. They do not know they are doing this. They do not know the others are doing it. But if someone were watching, someone from outside, they would see three figures in the darkness, scattered across the cistern but moving as one, breathing as one, part of the same slow rhythm that moves the roots and the fungi and the larvae and the warm, still water.

They are becoming something. All of them. The cistern is changing them the way it changed the roots, the way it changed whatever built the spiral structure in the pool. They are being absorbed into its ecology, finding their niche, becoming part of the system that eats death and makes it into something else.

The girl is furthest along. She may already be more cistern than human.

Tomás is still resisting, still holding onto his observations and his categories, still trying to understand the transformation rather than undergo it.

Elara is in between. She is waiting. She has been waiting for seventeen years. She is beginning to think that the waiting itself is the transformation—that patience, at this scale, becomes a kind of metamorphosis.

---

There may have been others, before.

In the alcove where the clay vessel rests—or the natural formation, or whatever it is—there are scratches on the wall that might be deliberate marks. Tallies, perhaps, or letters, or symbols in a language no one remembers. Elara has traced them with her fingers. They feel intentional. They feel like someone was trying to leave a record.

Near the base of the oldest calcified roots, there are deposits that might be calcium from the water, or might be bone. The shapes are ambiguous. The cistern does not distinguish between what was once alive and what was always stone. It treats everything as material. It incorporates.

The pool, in its exact center, around the spiral structure—the silt there is darker than elsewhere. Richer. As though things have settled there, have decomposed there, have added their substance to the pool's warm stillness.

Elara does not dig in the silt. She does not excavate the maybe-bone. She does not try to read the scratches on the wall. Whatever came before her came before. It was absorbed. It became part of this place, as she is becoming, as they all are becoming.

She wonders, sometimes, if the Keeper was once like her. If it descended seeking something and stayed waiting for something and eventually stopped being a creature that descended and waited and became instead a creature that *is*—a pulse, a heat, a rhythm that the others orient toward without understanding why.

She wonders if that is what the girl is becoming. If that is what they all are becoming, given enough time, enough patient absorption.

She does not fear this. She stopped fearing things years ago, around the same time she stopped counting days. Fear requires a sense of alternative, a belief that things could be otherwise. In the cistern, things are as they are. The water seeps. The fungi bloom. The creatures hunt and are hunted. The warmth rises from below and the moisture falls from above and everything between is simply *living*, dense and continuous and complete.

She breathes with the Keeper. She eats what the cistern provides. She waits.

The girl is wrapped in roots above her, sleeping or transforming or both.

Tomás is at the pool, watching larvae that might become anything.

Outside, somewhere, there is a surface world with sun and seasons and names that are spoken aloud.

In here, there is only the breathing, the blooming, the slow patient work of becoming something the cistern needs. They are part of it now. They have been part of it since they arrived.

The cistern does not welcome them. It does not reject them.

It continues. And they continue with it, three small lives among ten thousand, breathing the thick warm air, watching the fungi glow, waiting for nothing, becoming everything, dense and dim and complete unto themselves in the darkness beneath the earth.
