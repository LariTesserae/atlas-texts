## Who Lives in the Ascent

---

### The Shift-Walkers

They sleep in six-hour rotations, never more, their bodies trained to the mountain's rhythm before they consciously understood there was a rhythm to learn. The heat-workers are recognizable by their skin—not burned, exactly, but *finished*, like leather that has been cured. Their pores have narrowed over years of exposure until they barely sweat anymore, their bodies having learned to retain moisture through some adaptation the lowland doctors find troubling and inexplicable when they bother to visit.

Mira Senn has worked the emergence front for nine years, since she was sixteen and lied about her age to escape a drowned village in the eastern basins. She no longer remembers what humidity felt like before it burned. Her hands are mapped with white mineral scars where the crystalline deposits have worked into small cuts and healed over, leaving traces of the mountain in her flesh. She says she can feel when the growth is about to accelerate—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like copper and ozone—and the other workers have learned to trust this. When Mira calls for anchor relocation, they relocate, even if the instruments show nothing. The instruments, they have learned, measure what has already happened. Mira measures what is about to.

She speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice is barely above a whisper, roughened by years of breathing mineral vapor. But she sings while she works—tuneless humming that seems to synchronize, accidentally or otherwise, with the subsonic pulse of the mountain's growth. Other workers have noticed that the stone seems to *settle* differently around her. The basalt columns nearest her work station emerge with fewer fractures, cleaner geometries. No one mentions this. It feels like the kind of thing that shouldn't be spoken aloud.

### The Anchor Engineers

Yusuf Kade lives in the spaces between—the gaps where the city meets new stone, where iron must be bolted into formations still warm from their emergence. He is fifty-three years old, though he looks older, and he has been doing this work since the Ascent was a research station with seven personnel and a single steam lift. He remembers when they didn't understand that the mountain grew at all, when they thought they were simply building on unstable volcanic terrain. He was there when they realized the instability was *generative*.

Yusuf carries tools that he has modified so extensively they no longer resemble their original designs—drills that operate on frequencies he determined through years of trial and error, anchors shaped to geometries that mirror the basalt's own crystalline structure. He speaks to the mountain while he works, not in words but in pressure and vibration, in the rhythm of his drilling, in pauses that seem to ask permission. The other engineers think he is eccentric, possibly senile. They also notice that his anchors hold longer than anyone else's, that the formations around his work seem to *accommodate* the intrusions rather than stress-fracturing around them.

He has never married, has no children. When asked about his life before the Ascent, he becomes vague, not evasive but genuinely uncertain, as though the memories have been replaced by something more immediate. He can describe the exact mineral composition of seventeen different emergence zones but cannot remember his mother's face. This does not seem to trouble him. He says the mountain remembers enough for both of them.

In the deep machinery spaces, where the others make offerings and move carefully, Yusuf works without ritual. He simply goes where he needs to go, does what he needs to do. Whatever lives in those spaces—if anything does—leaves him alone. He has a theory about this that he does not share: that the mountain knows its own, and he has been its own for a very long time.

### The Condensation Keepers

Water is life, and the life of the Ascent depends on those who understand its movement.

The Condensation Keepers are not a formal guild, but they have become something like one—a network of pipe-runners, cistern-monitors, and humidity-readers who have developed their own terminology, their own hierarchies, their own quiet rituals around the work of keeping thousands of people hydrated in a place that should kill them with thirst. They are mostly young, mostly quick, able to navigate the lattice of catwalks and pipe-scaffolding with a speed that makes the older workers dizzy to watch.

Tomas Lin is seventeen and already a senior keeper, recognizable by the blue-gray mineral deposits that have formed along his hairline from years of leaning into pipe joints to check flow rates. He can taste the difference between condensation from different thermal zones, can tell by the mineral content whether water has passed through sections of pipe that are failing, whether a cistern has been contaminated by the wrong kind of precipitation. His tongue is his primary instrument; the chemical sensors are backup for when his body fails.

He leads a crew of eight runners who cover the mid-level infrastructure, ensuring that the hammock-quarters and the foundry workers' rest stations never run dry. They move in near-silence, communicating in hand signals and, when necessary, in a clicking language they have developed that carries further than whispers without the strange muffling effect that swallows normal speech. The clicking annoys some of the older residents, who find it eerie, reminiscent of the arthropods that navigate the same pipe-spaces. Tomas has noticed that the arthropods seem to avoid his crew, giving them wider berth than they give others. He wonders sometimes if the clicking has marked them as something other, something neither fully human nor fully pest, and whether this is protection or warning.

His younger sister Wen works the storm-catchment systems, deploying the collection arrays when the humidity sensors trigger, and she is the one who first noticed that the storms have been arriving *early* lately—not by much, perhaps twenty minutes, but consistently, as though something in the atmospheric system has shifted. She reported this to the survey office and received no response. She reported it to Tomas, who has been tracking the discrepancy in a hand-bound journal made from processed mineral-fiber paper. They have not yet determined what it means. They have determined that it is accelerating.

### The Algae Tenders

In the basement levels, where the heat is unbearable for unprotected flesh, the illuminated tanks glow with cultivated life.

The algae tenders work in shifts even shorter than the emergence-front crews—fifteen minutes in the cultivation chambers, then forty-five minutes in the cooling stations above, cycling endlessly through their work periods. They wear suits that circulate water across their skin, powered by the same geothermal energy that feeds the tanks, and they move with the careful deliberation of people who know that haste leads to heat exhaustion.

Elder Vasquez—she insists on the title, though no one is certain whether it denotes religious authority, age, or simply her tenure in the position—has tended the primary cultivation chambers for twenty-two years. She was a marine biologist once, in a coastal research station that no longer exists, and she came to the Ascent because it was the only place that would fund her work on extremophile adaptation. She has since lost interest in publication, in credentials, in anything that exists outside the basement glow.

Her algae strains have evolved under her care, or perhaps she has bred them deliberately—she is vague about the distinction—into something that photosynthesizes under the specific spectrum of the gas-lamp illumination, that thrives in mineral-saturated water, that produces a nutritional yield far exceeding what the original strains should have been capable of. The city could not feed itself without her work. She does not seem to regard this as a source of power or leverage; she seems barely aware of it. Her attention is entirely on the tanks, on the microscopic dynamics of growth and consumption, on the patterns she sees in the bubbles that rise through the green-lit water.

She believes the algae are *responding* to the mountain. She has charts showing correlation between growth rates in her tanks and the rate of basalt emergence—not causation, she is careful to say, but synchronization, as though the life she cultivates is breathing in time with the life being born in stone. She has shared this theory with no one except her most trusted assistants, two young women named Priya and Cass who have begun to adopt her mannerisms, her deliberate speech, her tendency to stare into the tanks for hours without moving.

In the deepest tank, the one they do not show visitors, something new is growing. It might still be algae. It might be something that algae is becoming. It glows faintly even when the lamps are off, and Elder Vasquez has begun keeping a cot beside it, unwilling to be away from it for longer than her cooling rotations require.

### The Mineral Assayers

The foundries require precision, and precision requires those who can read the stone.

The assayers work in the processing facilities where extracted mineral is sorted, graded, and prepared for the machines that turn it into components. They are trained in spectroscopy, crystallography, the mathematics of atomic structure—but the most valuable among them have developed something beyond training, an intuition for the mountain's output that borders on the diagnostic.

Harun Osei can identify seventeen distinct mineral configurations by touch alone, running his fingers across raw extraction samples and sorting them into categories that the machines will later confirm. His hands are ruined by this work, the nerves damaged, the skin perpetually cracked and bleeding, but he refuses the protective gloves that would preserve them because the gloves interfere with his sensitivity. He says the minerals *want* to be understood, that they yield their secrets to those who approach them correctly, and that his damaged hands are simply the price of correct approach.

He is not wrong, exactly. His sorting accuracy exceeds ninety-eight percent. The machines exist primarily to verify what he has already determined.

Harun has begun to notice something troubling in recent months: the mineral configurations are *changing*. Not in ways that compromise their industrial value—if anything, the newer extractions are purer, more thermodynamically perfect—but in ways that suggest *intention*. The crystal structures are becoming more complex, more ordered, exhibiting symmetries that approach biological rather than geological organization. He has requested a meeting with the survey office to discuss his findings. The meeting has been postponed four times. He is beginning to suspect that the survey office already knows, and that their knowledge is why the meeting keeps being postponed.

He has started keeping samples—unauthorized, hidden in his quarters—of the most unusual configurations. He does not know what he will do with them. He knows only that someone should be paying attention, and that if the official channels will not pay attention, then he will have to pay attention himself.

### The Lift Operators

Vertical movement is the Ascent's lifeblood, and the steam-driven lifts that connect the settlement's levels require constant operation and maintenance.

The operators work in enclosed cabins at the heart of the lift mechanisms, surrounded by pressure gauges, steam valves, and the particular smell of superheated water and mineral lubricant. They know their machines intimately—every rattle, every vibration, every variation in the rhythm of the pistons. They can tell by sound when a bearing is beginning to fail, when a steam line needs clearing, when the geothermal pressure is fluctuating in ways that require compensation.

Devi Chakraborty has operated Lift Seven—the primary connection between the mid-levels and the Pinnacle—for eleven years. She no longer thinks of the lift as a machine she operates but as a partner she collaborates with, and she has decorated her cabin with offerings that she leaves for it: small crystals from the mineral deposits, droplets of purified water, fragments of the blue-gray stone moss that she harvests carefully from the older formations. She does not believe the lift is alive, exactly. She believes it has developed something like preferences, and that honoring those preferences leads to smoother operation.

Her lift has not experienced a significant mechanical failure in nine years. The maintenance crews find this suspicious; lifts fail constantly in the Ascent's harsh conditions. They have inspected Lift Seven repeatedly and found nothing unusual, nothing to explain its reliability. Devi suggests they ask the lift directly, and they assume she is joking.

She is not joking.

She has begun to hear something in the rhythm of the pistons—not words, but patterns, repetitions that seem too regular to be mechanical variation. She has started transcribing these patterns in a notation system she has invented, filling notebooks with symbols that she does not understand but feels compelled to record. She has noticed that the patterns change when the mountain's growth accelerates, and she has begun to suspect that Lift Seven is not simply a machine that happens to still function but a machine that is *listening* to something, and that she, in turn, is listening to it.

### The Record-Keepers

Every day, the Ascent's administrative core produces documentation: extraction yields, growth measurements, population counts, resource allocation, maintenance schedules. Someone has to manage this information, and the record-keepers have done so since the settlement's earliest days.

They work in chambers carved into the older formations—stone cool enough to be survivable without protective equipment, lit by gas lamps and insulated from the surrounding heat by layers of processed mineral composite. Their archives contain everything: handwritten logs from the first survey teams, engineering schematics for infrastructure that no longer exists, personnel records for workers who have died or departed. They are the memory of the Ascent, and they take this responsibility seriously.

Chief Archivist Nalini Mather is seventy-one years old and has never left the settlement since arriving forty-three years ago as a junior clerk. She knows more about the Ascent's history than anyone alive, and she has begun to notice patterns in that history that disturb her.

The mountain's growth rate has not been constant. The official records show steady expansion, three centimeters per day, but Nalini has access to the original survey logs, and those logs show fluctuation—periods of acceleration and deceleration that correspond to events in the settlement's history. The growth slowed when the first major industrial accident killed seventeen workers. It accelerated when the foundries achieved their first successful extraction of the rare configurations. It fluctuated wildly during a period of labor unrest that ended in negotiated resolution but nearly ended in violence.

She does not know what to make of this correlation. She does not know whether the mountain is responding to human activity, whether human activity is responding to the mountain, or whether both are responding to something else entirely. She has compiled her findings into a report that sits in a locked drawer in her office, awaiting a recipient she has not yet identified—someone who will take it seriously, who will not dismiss her as an old woman seeing patterns in noise.

She is beginning to suspect that the patterns are not noise. She is beginning to suspect that the mountain has been paying attention from the beginning, and that the documentation she has spent her life maintaining is not a record of human activity but a record of *communication*—one side of a conversation that has been ongoing for eighteen years, conducted in the language of growth and extraction, of industrial rhythm and human presence.

### The Children of the Ascent

They have never known another world.

There are perhaps three hundred of them now, born in the settlement, raised in the heat and the mineral vapor and the subsonic pulse that their parents still notice but that they have never been without. They are different in ways that are beginning to become visible—their skin pores narrowed from birth rather than through adaptation, their tolerance for heat exceeding their parents', their comfort with the muffled acoustics of the Ascent so complete that normal sound levels, when they encounter them, seem painful and overwhelming.

Oma is eight years old and has never touched soil. She knows it exists—the lowlands, the basins, the places where things grow in organic matter—but these places are abstractions to her, as unreal as the stories the older workers tell about rain that falls for hours instead of minutes, about temperatures that require warm clothing, about silence that is actual silence rather than the ever-present pressure of the mountain's growth.

She has begun to hear the humming.

Not the stone moss, which some of the adults claim to almost-hear. Something else—a frequency that seems to emanate from the mountain itself, that vibrates in her bones before it reaches her ears. She mentioned it once to her mother, a condensation keeper on the upper levels, and her mother dismissed it as imagination. But Oma has found others who hear it too: a boy named Fen who works as a runner-in-training, a girl named Suki whose parents tend the algae tanks. They meet sometimes in the gaps between the city and the newest stone, in the spaces where the anchor engineers make offerings, and they listen together.

They do not know what they are hearing. They know only that it seems to *respond* to their attention—that when they listen, the frequency shifts, becomes more complex, seems almost to organize itself into patterns that approach meaning without quite achieving it. They have begun to experiment with responding: humming back, tapping rhythms on the warm stone, arranging themselves in configurations that mirror the crystalline geometries around them.

They do not tell the adults what they are doing. They are not certain the adults would understand. They are not certain they understand themselves. But they are beginning to suspect that the mountain has been waiting for someone who could hear it clearly, and that they—the first generation born in the Ascent, their bodies shaped by its conditions from the moment of conception—might be the first who can.

### The Surveyors

They map what does not stay still.

The survey teams operate at the Ascent's edges, where new growth meets established infrastructure, where the geometry of the mountain is still being determined. They carry instruments that measure angle, elevation, mineral composition, thermal output—and they carry notebooks where they record the things the instruments cannot capture: the feeling of the stone beneath their feet, the quality of the heat shimmer, the behavior of the seeds that drift on the thermal columns.

Lead Surveyor Constance Wren has been mapping the Ascent for fifteen years and has come to the conclusion that the mountain is *designing* itself.

Not randomly, not through the blind processes of geology, but with something approaching intention—the columns emerge in patterns that optimize structural stability, that create natural channels for heat dissipation, that seem almost to *anticipate* where the city will need to expand. She has observed the surveyors on the new emergence fifteen kilometers away, and she has noticed that the patterns there are different: not optimized for human habitation but for something else, something she cannot identify, something that might not have emerged yet.

She keeps two sets of records: the official surveys, which document geography and mineral content and support the city's expansion planning, and her private surveys, which document the patterns she is beginning to see—the geometry that repeats across scales, the configurations that echo biological organization, the sense that she is not mapping a mountain but mapping an *organism* in the early stages of its development.

She has requested a long-term assignment to the new emergence, where she can observe the mountain's growth from its earliest stages. The request has been approved; she will depart in three weeks with a team of six. She tells herself this is scientific curiosity, the desire to understand. She does not admit, even to herself, that it is also something like pilgrimage—the need to be present at the beginning of something, to witness the birth that the first surveyors named eighteen years ago.

### The Visitors

They do not belong, and they know it.

A few arrive each month from the lowlands: government inspectors checking extraction quotas, merchants negotiating supply contracts, academics who have heard rumors of the Ascent's strangeness and want to study it. They stay for days at most, overwhelmed by the heat, unsettled by the subsonic pressure, disturbed by the silence that is not quite silence.

Dr. Emery Sohn is a geologist from a university that no longer funds his research, here on a personal grant cobbled together from savings and borrowed money. He arrived four days ago intending to collect samples, to document the lithic parturition, to write the paper that will restore his academic reputation. He has instead spent most of his time in his quarters, fighting heat exhaustion and a creeping unease that he cannot articulate.

The Ascent is *wrong*, he has concluded. Not the mountain—the mountain is fascinating, genuinely new, a phenomenon that rewrites his understanding of volcanic processes. But the city, the people who live here, the way they have adapted to conditions that should be uninhabitable—this troubles him in ways that his training has not prepared him for. They are too comfortable. They speak too quietly. They look at him with eyes that have adjusted to the haze and the heat-shimmer in ways that make their expressions difficult to read.

He has noticed that they move in rhythms—not consciously, not obviously, but there is a synchronization to their activity that suggests coordination at a level below awareness. The foundry shifts, the lift schedules, the water distribution patterns—they mesh together too smoothly, too precisely, as though choreographed by something that understands the whole system better than any individual participant could.

He is leaving tomorrow, a week earlier than planned. He will not write the paper he intended to write. He will write, instead, a private memorandum to colleagues he trusts, warning them that the Ascent is not a mining settlement that happens to exist on a strange geological formation but something else entirely—a system, an organism, a process of transformation whose inputs are human beings and whose outputs he does not want to contemplate.

He suspects he is being paranoid. He suspects he is not.

### The Deep Workers

Someone has to go where the mountain is newest.

They are a small crew, perhaps twenty workers, who operate in the emergence zones where fresh basalt is still cooling, where the geometries are still forming, where the heat is survivable only with full protective equipment and strictly limited exposure. They drill anchor points into stone that will not exist for another month at the current growth rate, preparing the way for the city's expansion before the expansion is possible.

Foreman Josiah Tent has led the deep crew for seven years. He lost his left hand three years ago to a thermal surge and replaced it with a prosthetic made from the mountain's own minerals—a decision the lowland doctors found medically inexplicable but that has, inexplicably, worked. The prosthetic integrates with his nervous system in ways that the engineers cannot explain; he can feel temperature, pressure, texture through it as clearly as through his remaining flesh hand. More clearly, sometimes.

He has begun to feel other things through it too. The mountain's pulse, which everyone feels in their bones, he feels through the prosthetic with crystalline clarity—not just presence but *information*, patterns that his brain is beginning to interpret as something like meaning. He has not shared this with the medical staff, who would insist on removing the prosthetic and replacing it with something properly engineered. He has not shared it with his crew, who would think him unstable. He has shared it only with the mountain itself, in moments of solitude at the emergence front, when he presses his mineral hand against the newest stone and feels the pulse answer.

He does not know what is happening to him. He suspects it is the same thing that is happening to the Ascent—a transformation, a becoming, a process that began eighteen years ago and has only accelerated since. He suspects that his prosthetic is not a replacement for his lost hand but a *graft*, a joining of human and mountain that the conditions of the Ascent have made possible.

He suspects he is becoming something other than what he was. He is not certain whether to be afraid.

### What Drifts

In the thermal columns, the seeds continue to rise.

They have no name, no classification, no place in the taxonomy of living things. They might not be living at all—they might be mineral, might be byproduct, might be something for which no existing category is adequate. But they drift upward on currents of superheated air, catching the light in patterns that suggest structure, and they disperse into the haze above the Pinnacle where no human has yet ventured to follow.

Those who have tried to study them report consistent frustration: samples cannot be collected, observations cannot be replicated, measurements vary wildly between attempts. The seeds seem to exist in a state of indeterminacy, their properties depending on whether and how they are observed. Some of the researchers have concluded they are an artifact of heat-distorted perception, not real at all. Others have concluded they are too real, more real than the instruments designed to measure them.

The children who hear the humming have noticed that the seeds respond to their attention. When the children gather in the machinery spaces to listen, the thermal columns above them produce more seeds, denser clouds of drifting potential, shapes that almost resolve into patterns before dispersing. The children do not know what the seeds are becoming. They suspect they will find out.

### The Mountain's Question

Eighteen years of growth. Eleven kilometers of new stone. A city of several thousand people living in rhythm with something they do not understand.

The geologists called it birth. They did not ask what was being born, or why, or what it would become when it finished being born. They measured growth rates and mineral content and thermal output, and they did not notice that the mountain was paying attention back—that its patterns were not random, that its rhythms were not mechanical, that its emergence was not geology but *development*.

The Ascent exists because humans needed what the mountain produces. But perhaps the mountain needed what humans provide: attention, response, the feedback loop of observation and adaptation that transforms random growth into intentional becoming. Perhaps the city is not a settlement but a *organ*, a sensory apparatus through which the mountain learns to perceive itself.

The workers continue their shifts. The children continue to listen. The visitors continue to feel unsettled by something they cannot name.

And somewhere in the deepest formations, where the stone is still forming, where the crystals are organizing themselves into configurations that approach biological complexity, the mountain continues the process that began eighteen years ago—the slow, patient work of becoming something that has never existed before.

The heat presses down. The condensation drips. The foundries turn.

The birth continues.
