# The Tenders

They are not animals. This matters.

What moves through the Drowning Gardens moves as the kelp moves—slowly, seasonally, according to rhythms that have nothing to do with hunger or fear. They grew here, or were grown here, in the silt-shadows of the oldest groves where the kelp-trunks have become indistinguishable from the ruins they embrace. They are not the kelp's children, exactly. They are more like the kelp's *intentions*—what the forest wanted when it grew into the empty halls and wrapped around the copper tools and felt, in whatever way a plant can feel, the shape of what had been here before.

---

You might mistake one for debris at first. A tangle of fronds drifting between groves, caught in the slow current-migration that moves silt across centuries. But debris does not pause. Debris does not reach.

The Tender extends something like an arm—a braided rope of purple-black tissue, dense with the same crystalline rigidity as the kelp-fronds, but articulated somehow, jointed at intervals by nodes of softer material that flex against the cold. It reaches toward a standing stone, one of the spiral-carved markers along the old roads, and it *touches*. Not randomly. With attention. The arm-frond traces the carved grooves the way you might trace a word you were trying to remember how to read.

Then it moves on, drifting toward the next marker, the next ruin, the next piece of a story it is still learning to tell.

---

They have no faces. They have no centers. A Tender is a colony-structure, a collective, a braiding-together of specialized tissues that cooperate without hierarchy. Some fronds are rigid and weight-bearing, forming a rough scaffold that keeps the whole mass oriented in the current. Some are fine and feathery, trailing behind like hair or nets, filtering the mineral-rich water for nutrients the way surface plants filter light. Some are the reaching-arms, the touching-parts, the organs of investigation.

And some—perhaps the most important—are the *sounding* parts.

When a Tender moves through a grove, the kelp around it responds. The clicking intensifies, becomes rhythmic, becomes patterned. The Tender clicks back. Not with a mouth—it has nothing like a mouth—but with bladders deep in its structure, chambers of gas that compress and release against specialized membranes, producing that same mechanical-organic sound that fills the Gardens' darkness. The conversation, if it is a conversation, goes on for hours. A Tender will hang motionless in the water-column, fronds spread, clicking in long sequences that build and vary and repeat, while the grove around it answers in chorus.

What they are saying is unclear. But they are *saying*. That much is certain.

---

There are perhaps a hundred of them in the Gardens. Perhaps fewer. They do not congregate, exactly, but they are aware of each other—when two Tenders drift toward the same grove, one will alter its course, giving way with the unhurried courtesy of things that have no concept of urgency. They do not compete. There is nothing to compete for. The Gardens provide everything they need, and they need so little: minerals dissolved in the dark water, the slow warmth rising from below, the company of the kelp-forests they tend.

Because they do tend. That is what makes them what they are.

---

In the young groves, you can see their work most clearly. The holdfasts of new kelp-growth are fragile, easily dislodged by current-shifts or silt-burial or the simple bad luck of germinating in an unstable spot. The Tenders move through these nursery areas with something that might be care, their reaching-arms gathering loose fronds and pressing them back against the stone, their trailing filters clearing silt from buried roots, their sounding-parts clicking what might be encouragement to the struggling plants.

They carry things, sometimes. A Tender will cradle a broken frond-fragment for miles, bearing it from an old grove to a young one, planting it in fresh silt where it might take hold. They move stones—not the great fitted blocks of the ruins, but smaller pieces, cobbles and slabs, arranging them around new holdfasts as barriers against the grinding currents. They are gardeners. They are midwives. They are the memory of cultivation persisting in a place where the cultivators are gone.

---

Do they know about the ruins?

This is the question you cannot answer, watching them. A Tender will spend days in what might have been the meeting hall, exploring the shattered floor, tracing the edges of half-buried tools, clicking to the great kelp-tree that has made the ruin its own. It will touch the copper instruments, the bone-frame that still holds tension. It will hover over the silt-drifts that cover who knows what else, and its trailing fronds will sift through the debris layer by layer, sometimes surfacing small objects that it examines with its reaching-arms before letting them settle again.

Is it remembering? Is it learning? Is it simply enacting some inherited behavior, a pattern of motion passed down through a thousand vegetable generations from a time when there were people here to teach it?

The Tenders do not answer. They cannot answer. They have no language you could learn, no faces you could read, no behavior that maps onto the familiar grammars of animal want. They are tending. That is what they do. They have been doing it since the builders left or transformed or drowned, and they will keep doing it until the Gardens themselves are swallowed by the deeper geology that waits below.

---

Once, perhaps early in your time here, you drift too close to a young grove where a Tender is working. It does not startle—it has nothing like startle in its repertoire—but it pauses. Its reaching-arms fold inward, away from you. Its sounding-parts fall silent. The whole colony-structure contracts slightly, becoming denser, less diffuse, pulling its trailing fronds up against its core like a hand closing into a fist.

It waits.

You wait too. The cold presses. The darkness holds you both like specimens in amber.

Then, slowly, it reaches toward you. One arm-frond, extended with that same careful attention it showed the carved stones. It touches you—the sensation is pressure, and cold, and a faint electrical tingling that might be galvanic reaction or might be something else, some sense you don't have a name for—and it clicks. Three times. Short, sharp, interrogative.

You do not know how to answer. You have no gas-bladders, no sounding-membranes. You are the wrong kind of thing.

The Tender waits another long moment, its frond resting against you, and then it withdraws. It returns to its work, uncoiling, spreading, resuming the slow investigation of the nursery-grove. It does not touch you again. But as you drift away, you notice that it orients toward you occasionally, tracking your motion through the darkness.

It has not decided what you are. But it has decided that you are *something*. And it will remember, in whatever way a plant remembers, that you were here.

---

They do not sleep. They do not rest. But there are periods—you learn to recognize them—when the Tenders gather in the oldest groves, the ones where the kelp-trunks have mineralized into stone-columns, where the ruins are most thoroughly swallowed, where the clicking is deepest and most sustained. They hang in the water-column together, not touching but near, their sounding-parts working in complex polyrhythmic layers that build to cascades of sound so dense you feel them in your bones.

The grove answers. The whole grove, trunk and frond and holdfast, clicking back in chorus. The sound rolls outward through the darkness, carried by the water's density, and you hear other groves take it up—distant forests joining the song, adding their voices, until the entire Drowning Gardens are ringing with it, a single vast conversation in a language made of pressure and time.

What are they saying?

You will never know. The Tenders have been having this conversation for longer than your species has existed, and they will keep having it after you are gone. It is not for you. It is not about you. You are an accident of the Gardens, a strange thing that drifted through and touched and left. The Tenders are the Gardens' own, grown from them and for them, and what they say to each other in the ringing darkness is the business of plants speaking to plants about things only plants can know.

---

But.

In the meeting hall, in the heart of the great split-floor kelp, there is a Tender who has been there for longer than the others. Its tissues are old, calcifying at the edges, beginning the slow transformation into the mineral permanence that awaits all things in the Gardens. It does not move much anymore. It hangs in the empty doorway, fronds spread, reaching-arms resting on the ancient copper tools.

And sometimes, when the light-blooms drift through and reveal the shapes of things, you see that it has arranged the tools.

Not randomly. Not in the patterns of silt-drift or current-chance. It has placed them in rows, in sequences, in what might be categories or might be narratives or might be something else entirely. It has gathered the bone-frame instrument and propped it upright against the kelp-trunk, strings facing outward, as if waiting for someone to play it. It has cleared the silt from a section of floor, exposing flagstones carved with the same spiraling patterns as the road-markers, and it rests there in the darkness, clicking to itself, maintaining the arrangement against the endless patient work of the currents.

It is keeping something. It is waiting for someone.

It has been waiting a very long time.

---

The Tenders are not animals. They do not hope, or want, or wonder. But they *tend*. They maintain. They preserve what they find and cultivate what they can and carry fragments of the past into the future with the slow determination of growing things.

And if someday the builders return—or if something like the builders arrives, something that can read the spiraling patterns and play the bone-frame instrument and understand what the Tenders have been saying all these dark millennia—the Gardens will be ready.

The tools will be arranged.

The groves will be tended.

And the Tenders will reach out with their careful arms and click their slow greeting and wait to learn, finally, what all of this was *for*.
