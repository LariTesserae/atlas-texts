## The Tenders of Verdemoor

They do not think of themselves as pale. Paleness implies a deviation from some norm, and there is no other way to be here—no sun-touched standard against which to measure. Their skin is simply *Verdemoor skin*: the color of mushroom flesh, of steam, of the fog that fills the lower districts. The children grow up assuming all people look this way, and the first time they ride the cable-car to the outside markets, many of them weep. Not from wonder at the sun, but from a kind of wrongness they can't articulate—the sky too open, the light too sharp, their own shadows startling them like accusations.

They come back. They always come back.

---

**The Graft-Mothers** work the espaliered orchards, and their hands tell their trade: fingers stained permanent black-green from the binding resins, nails thick and split from decades of wire-work, palms mapped with pale scars where the grafting knives slipped in the damp. They speak to the trees. Not superstitiously—they would be offended by the suggestion—but practically, a running murmur of assessment and intention. *Yes, this branch. No, not yet. You're reaching too far, I'll have to cut you back.* The trees, trained horizontally across their copper frameworks, seem to listen. Certainly they grow in directions that the Graft-Mothers prefer, though whether this is responsiveness or simply the accumulated wisdom of generations knowing exactly where to make each cut, no one can say.

The oldest Graft-Mother currently living is called Samma Thread—the surnames here are occupational, inherited through apprenticeship rather than blood—and she no longer walks the orchard platforms herself. Her joints have swollen in the constant damp, her fingers curved into permanent hooks. But the younger Mothers carry her on a chair-litter to difficult cases: trees that won't take a graft, branches that grow malformed despite every intervention. She looks at them for a long time, breathing the wet air, and then she tells the Mothers what the tree needs. She hasn't been wrong in thirty years.

When she dies, they will bury her in the mulch-pits below the orchards, where all organic matter eventually goes. Her body will feed the root systems she tended. The trees will taste her, finally, after all those years of conversation.

---

**The Drum-Walkers** tend the great rotating cultivation engines, and their gait marks them anywhere in the structure. They have learned to move on surfaces that are always slightly in motion, their weight shifting in micro-adjustments so constant that they've become unconscious. On stable ground—the fixed platforms, the carved-stone chambers—they look drunk, over-correcting for movement that isn't there. Most of them stop visiting the stable areas. It's easier to stay where the world moves the way their bodies expect.

They work in teams of three, always: one inside the drum, one outside, one on the observation platform monitoring the rotation speed and nutrient flow. The one inside moves through a slow-motion rain of soil and roots, checking for disease, for pest damage (though there are no pests—but the old procedures persist, because you check anyway, because the one time you don't will be the time something finds a way in). They wear masks against the soil-dust. Their lungs are still the weakest part of them, still prone to the calcium deposits that come from breathing mineral-rich air for decades.

The drums never stop. If a drum stops, the plants inside collapse under their own weight within hours, root systems that evolved for constant gentle stress suddenly confronted with the full pull of gravity. So the Drum-Walkers work in shifts that ignore day and night, sleeping in hammocks strung between the great axle-housings, waking to the same hum they fell asleep to. Some of them claim they can tell the time by the pitch of the rotation—that the drums sing slightly differently as the temperature changes through the day-cycle, expansion and contraction altering the harmonics. Others say this is nonsense. The argument has continued for six generations.

---

Below, in the districts where the mist thickens to something almost liquid, live **the Tenders of the Deep Green**.

These are the workers who cultivate the thermal gardens near the Original Works, where the hot springs keep the air blood-warm and humid enough to grow things that have no business surviving this far north. Orchids. Spice plants that originated in jungles half a world away. Medicinal herbs so delicate that a draft can kill them—and there are no drafts here, no movement of air except the valley's slow exhalation, which the Deep Green Tenders have learned to work around, scheduling their tasks to the forty-second rhythm, opening and closing greenhouse panels in precise synchronization with the breath.

Their skin *has* taken on a greenish cast. The stories are true. It's chlorophyll, or something like it—a symbiotic algae that colonizes the outer dermis, feeding on the same light spectra as the plants they tend, contributing some small amount of energy to its hosts in exchange for a warm wet habitat. The effect is subtle at first, a faint verdancy in the shadows of the throat and inner arms. After twenty years in the Deep Green, it spreads. After forty, a Tender's skin will glow faintly in complete darkness, a soft luminescence that marks them as surely as the Graft-Mothers' hands or the Drum-Walkers' gait.

They do not consider this a disfigurement. Why would they? It is useful. It is beautiful. It is *Verdemoor*, written on the body, impossible to leave behind.

The Deep Green Tenders rarely speak above a whisper. The plants are sensitive to vibration. So much of their communication happens in gesture, in glance, in the positioning of tools left on workbenches to convey information to the next shift. A new Tender takes years to become fluent, and during that apprenticeship they feel half-blind, missing meanings that flash between their colleagues too fast to follow. But eventually it comes. Eventually the silence becomes a language richer than speech, capable of expressing subtleties that words would only flatten.

They touch each other often. A hand on a shoulder, fingers brushing across a wrist, foreheads pressed together in greeting or farewell. The plants lean toward warmth, toward contact, toward the carbon dioxide of exhalation. So do the Tenders. So has everyone who lives long enough in Verdemoor, though they don't notice it until they meet outsiders, who flinch.

---

**The Ledger-Keepers** occupy the only truly dry spaces in the valley: climate-controlled chambers in the upper structure where the archives are stored. Temperature-regulated. Humidity-controlled. The paper must survive.

They are the memory of Verdemoor, and they know things that no one else bothers to remember: the sequence of grafts that produced the current orchard cultivars, traced back seven generations; the engineering specifications of drums that were decommissioned a century ago; the names of every worker who has ever died in the valley, logged with date and cause and location, a roll of the dead that fills seventeen leather-bound volumes and continues to grow.

A Ledger-Keeper's apprenticeship lasts fifteen years. They begin by copying, hand transcribing documents too fragile to survive handling, learning the scripts that have evolved through Verdemoor's history. Then they progress to indexing, building the mental maps that allow them to locate any piece of information in the archive within minutes. Finally—if they have the aptitude—they begin to *interpret*: drawing connections between disparate records, identifying patterns that span decades, advising the Engineers' Council on decisions that require historical context.

The current Chief Ledger-Keeper is a woman named Olle Manifest, and she has not left the archive chambers in eleven years. The damp makes her cough. The fluctuating pressure gives her headaches. Her world has narrowed to the dimensions of the climate-controlled rooms, and she does not mind. Everything important is here. The entire history of Verdemoor lives in these shelves, and she is its custodian.

She knows, from the death-rolls, that the average Verdemoor lifespan is fifty-three years. She knows the leading causes: lung calcium at the top, followed by falls, followed by infection from wounds that won't heal in the constant wet. She knows that the population has remained stable at approximately four thousand for the last century, births and deaths in careful balance, immigration and emigration roughly equal. She knows that eleven people have left Verdemoor and returned to die here, and that the interval between leaving and returning averages seven years.

She does not know why they come back. That is not the kind of thing the records capture.

---

**The children** grow up climbing. There is no other way to be a child in Verdemoor: the structure is vertical, the spaces between platforms are invitations, the adults are too busy to supervise every moment. So the children learn the handholds, the safe routes, the places where a fall will kill you and the places where it will only break bones. They form gangs organized by district—Drum-brats, Green-spawn, Spore-kids from the fungal caverns—and they wage elaborate games of territory and theft across the structure, racing through the ironwork, swinging from chains, navigating by the blue bioluminescent markers that they learn to read before they learn letters.

Some of them fall. The death-rolls record this in the same neutral script as everything else: *Venn Tumble, aged nine, fell from Chain-way 7, impact, immediate.* The other children attend the body's consignment to the mulch-pits. They go back to climbing the next day. There is no other way to be a child in Verdemoor.

By twelve, they're apprenticed. The games stop. The gangs dissolve, their members scattered to different trades, different districts. But something remains—a kinship that crosses occupational lines, a recognition when two middle-aged workers meet and realize they were Drum-brats together thirty years ago. They don't speak of it directly. But they nod. They touch hands. They remember the child they once were, fearless in the ironwork, certain the structure would hold them.

---

**The Engineers** are the closest thing Verdemoor has to priests.

They do not govern—governance is handled by the District Councils, by the endless negotiations between trades, by the accumulation of custom and precedent. But when the structure itself is at stake, when the pipes need rerouting or the steam pressures need balancing or something somewhere in the depths is making a noise that shouldn't exist, the Engineers are called.

There are never more than twenty of them. The training is brutal: mathematics, metallurgy, fluid dynamics, the entire accumulated technical knowledge of three centuries compressed into a decade of study. Most who begin do not finish. Those who do emerge with a different relationship to the valley. They see it as *system*, as interconnected flows of water and heat and pressure, as a single machine with four thousand human components.

This makes them strange. Necessary, but strange.

The Chief Engineer for the past eighteen years has been a man named Colm Root, though he was born Colm Thread and only took the occupational name when he completed his training. He walks the structure constantly, a slow circuit that takes him three days to complete, and he listens. The sounds of Verdemoor speak to him: a wrong harmonic in a pipe tells him where the mineral buildup is worst, a change in the rhythm of the breath tells him the eastern vents need adjustment. He carries a leather notebook and a graphite stick, and he draws as he walks—not plans, but impressions, the curves of stress and flow as he perceives them.

When he sleeps, he dreams of the valley as it will be after he dies. The structure persisting. The breath continuing. The crops growing in their endless rotations. He finds this comforting. He did not build Verdemoor, but he has maintained it, has kept it alive through two pressure crises and one partial collapse of the southern wall, and when he is mulched his contribution will persist in the ongoing function of the whole.

He does not think of this as immortality. He thinks of it as *being used up properly*.

---

**The Traders** are the interface between Verdemoor and the world outside, and they are regarded with a mixture of necessity and faint suspicion.

They ride the cable-cars up the northern ridge, they negotiate with merchants from the lowlands, they bring back the things Verdemoor cannot produce itself: metals, textiles, medicines, news. They speak languages other than the valley's dialect. They understand money, which most Verdemoor residents encounter only as an abstraction—within the structure, exchange is handled by a complex system of labor-credits and material-allocations that has evolved over centuries and which the Ledger-Keepers maintain in excruciating detail.

The Traders live in the upper districts, near the cable-car terminus, and their quarters are the least damp in the valley. Some of them have been outside so often that they no longer flinch at the sun. Some of them have married outsiders, brought them back to Verdemoor, watched them struggle to adapt to the wet and the dark and the constant gentle motion of everything.

Most of those marriages fail. The outsiders leave. But occasionally one stays, and then something interesting happens: a new perspective enters the valley, a mind shaped by elsewhere, seeing what the native-born can no longer see because it has always been there. The innovations of the last century—the automated pollinators, the improved drainage systems, the bioluminescent markers—have come disproportionately from these absorbed outsiders, people who looked at Verdemoor and said *why do you do it this way* and refused to accept *because we always have* as an answer.

The valley needs them. The valley doesn't entirely trust them. This tension never resolves.

---

At night—or what passes for night, the shift-change, the dimming of the artificial lights—Verdemoor quiets.

Not silences. The breath continues, the drums rotate, the pumps pump. But the human noise diminishes, the work-crews retreating to their quarters, the children finally sleeping, the Traders done with their negotiations. And in that relative stillness, other sounds emerge.

The structure creaks. Three hundred years of iron and wood and brick, suspended over nothing, bearing loads it was never quite designed for—it speaks, in the night, a language of stress and settling. The Engineers know this language, but ordinary workers hear it too, lying in their damp beds, listening to the valley talk to itself.

Some say they hear voices. Not ghosts—Verdemoor is too practical for ghosts—but *echoes*, sounds that have somehow lodged in the structure and replay under the right conditions. A child's laugh from a century ago, caught in a resonant chamber. A work-song that no one has sung in living memory, vibrating faintly in a pipe that happens to have the right acoustic properties. The Ledger-Keepers record these reports but do not know what to make of them.

And sometimes, on the quietest nights, when the breath of the valley falls into a particular rhythm and the mist rises to its highest point, something else happens. The plants glow. Not the bioluminescent markers, not the fungi—the *plants*, the ordinary green things in their greenhouses and drums and espaliered frames. A faint luminescence, barely visible, gone if you look at it directly. As if the chlorophyll itself has learned something from the algae on the Tenders' skin. As if Verdemoor is slowly teaching everything within it to make its own light.

The Graft-Mothers see it most often. They say the trees are dreaming. They say the whole valley is one organism now, roots and pipes and human hands all part of the same system, and on those nights the organism is thinking, processing, integrating. They say this without mysticism, in the same tone they use to discuss grafting techniques. It is simply what happens here. It is simply what Verdemoor has become.

---

When a worker dies, the body goes to the mulch-pits. This is not callousness; this is economy, this is completion. Everything in Verdemoor cycles. Water condenses, falls, is collected, is used, evaporates, condenses again. Nutrients move from plant to processor to person to waste to compost to plant again. A human body is sixty percent water and a significant percentage of useful minerals, and to remove it from the valley would be to diminish the system.

The families gather at the pit's edge. The Ledger-Keepers record the name and date. The body, wrapped in undyed cotton that will also decompose, is lowered into the steaming mulch. And then—this is the part that outsiders find strangest—the family plants something. A seedling, usually. Something the dead person tended in life, or something they loved to eat, or simply the next thing in the rotation. They push it into the mulch near where the body lies, and they say the words that have been said for three hundred years:

*From the valley, to the valley. From the green, to the green. What was borrowed is returned.*

It is not religion. It is not sentiment. It is a statement of fact. The seedling will grow. The body will feed it. The fruit of that plant will feed the workers who remain. The dead become the living, over and over, and the valley breathes.

---

They are not happy, exactly, the people of Verdemoor. Happiness implies a comparison to some alternative state, and for most of them, there is no alternative. This is simply life: the damp, the dark, the work, the green. The Drum-Walkers walk their drums. The Graft-Mothers tend their trees. The children climb until they're old enough to stop climbing. The Ledger-Keepers keep their ledgers. The Engineers listen to the structure speak.

And sometimes, standing on a platform in the thick mist, watching the faint glow of the greenhouses below and the pale steam rising from the vents above, a worker will feel something that might be called contentment. Not because anything is perfect—the cough that won't clear, the joint that aches, the friend who fell last month—but because they are *part of it*. The system includes them. Their labor matters. When they die, they will matter still, their bodies feeding the crops that feed the next generation.

The valley breathes. They breathe with it. For forty seconds, the rhythm of their lungs matches the rhythm of the vents and the condensers and the pressure cycles. They are Verdemoor. Verdemoor is them.

Then the breath shifts, and they go back to work.
