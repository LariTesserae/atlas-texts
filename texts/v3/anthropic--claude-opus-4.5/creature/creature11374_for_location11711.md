**The Watchers of the Calving Plains**

They call themselves the Midwives, though no one appointed them and no one asked them to stay.

There are perhaps two hundred of them across the entire eight-hundred-kilometer expanse, which means they rarely see each other in person—sometimes years pass between physical meetings. But they are never truly alone. Their implants carry the constant low murmur of the network: status updates from monitoring systems, the soft pings of each other's heartbeats transmitted as a kind of presence, the occasional burst of actual conversation when someone sees something worth sharing. A glass-wolf making an unusual kill. A tower emerging with an unexpected asymmetry. The first frost-flowers of the season blooming in a meltwater lens.

They came from different places, for different reasons, and they will tell you those reasons if you ask—but the reasons matter less than you'd think. One was an urban planner who wanted to see a city built correctly for once, without the compromises and corruptions of inhabited places. One was a biologist tracking the adaptation patterns of the introduced species, who meant to stay for eighteen months and has now been here for thirty-one years. One was simply tired of people and thought the emptiness would cure her of something, and perhaps it did, though she's no longer certain what she was trying to cure or whether the absence of it is the same as health.

What they share is the patience. The willingness to watch something happen at a pace that makes human lifespans feel like interruptions. The city will not be inhabited for another century, perhaps two, and they will all be dead before the first child is born in one of these towers. They know this. They find it, somehow, reassuring.

---

Their days have rhythms but not schedules.

A Midwife named Corso—though that's not the name he was born with—wakes in his small station twelve kilometers from the nearest warming vent. The station was built by the constructors eight years ago, emerging around him over the course of a month while he lived in a temporary shelter and watched the walls thicken into being. It has exactly the space he needs: a sleeping alcove, a workroom, a small greenhouse where he grows vegetables more for the practice than the nutrition, and an observation deck that faces west, toward the weather.

He drinks tea while the morning light crawls across the plain. The ice takes on colors that aren't quite colors—suggestions of blue, of gray, of something that might be rose if rose could exist at temperatures this far below freezing. He watches three herdbeasts pass his station at a distance of perhaps a kilometer, their breath making small clouds that trail behind them like afterthoughts. The monitoring systems tell him their body temperatures, their heart rates, their approximate ages and genetic lineages. He reads this information without thinking about it, the way you might glance at the time without remembering that you looked.

Later, he walks. The Midwives all walk, when weather permits—sometimes for hours, sometimes for days, carrying supplies on their backs and sleeping in emergency shelters they assemble from kits or simply in snow-caves they dig into drifts. The constructors ignore them. The herdbeasts have learned to recognize them as something other than glass-wolves. The not-birds sometimes follow them, curious, hoping for scraps, though the Midwives rarely carry anything the birds would want to eat.

Corso walks toward a tower he's been watching for three weeks. It's nearly complete now—forty stories of crystalline ice rising from the plain like a thought made visible. He wants to be there when it finishes, when the constructors depart and the building goes still. He's not sure why he wants this. He's seen it happen dozens of times. But each one feels like a birth, or a death, or something in between that doesn't have a name yet, and he finds he can't look away.

---

Yennet, who lives further east where the construction is younger, spends her days with the glass-wolves.

Not among them—she's not foolish, and they're not tame, and even after sixty years of coexistence they remain fundamentally what they were bred to be, which is patient killers. But she's learned their patterns well enough to position herself where they'll pass, and they've grown accustomed enough to her presence that they no longer alter their behavior when she's near. She lies on the ice for hours, wrapped in thermal layers that make her nearly as pale as they are, and she watches them hunt.

They don't hunt the way their ancestors did. They've adapted to the plain, to the ice, to the herdbeasts who are themselves adapting in response. Where their predecessors might have chased, these wolves wait. They find a herd and they surround it, not closely, not in any way that would register as threat—just a presence at every edge of the herd's awareness. And they walk with the herd. For days, sometimes. For weeks. Never attacking, never threatening, just there, until the herd becomes so accustomed to them that it stops watching. Until the wolves become part of the landscape, part of the normal texture of existence.

And then, when a herdbeast strays too far from the group, when its attention lapses, when it forgets what the shimmers at the edge of vision actually are—then the wolves move. Not as a pack, not with coordinated howling like the old wolves of old Earth. Just one wolf, the nearest one, the one that's been waiting the longest, unfolding from stillness into motion so fast that Yennet, even watching for it, often misses the moment it begins.

She's written papers about this. The behavioral adaptation, the patience-hunting, the way it mirrors the pace of the city's own construction. She's not sure anyone reads them. But she keeps writing, keeps watching, keeps lying on the ice until her thermal suit runs low and she has to return to her station to recharge it. The wolves are teaching her something, she thinks, though she couldn't say what it is in words.

---

The oldest Midwife is a person named Thread, who no longer uses gendered pronouns and who has lived on the plain for so long that their implants contain records the monitoring network has lost. They remember when the first warming vents were installed, when the first herdbeasts were released, when the glass-wolves were still called something else—some taxonomic designation that nobody uses anymore because *glass-wolves* is what they look like and what they act like and what, therefore, they became.

Thread lives at the center of the plain, in a station that's been rebuilt three times as the constructors improved their techniques. They spend most of their time in a state that isn't quite sleep and isn't quite meditation—a lowered consciousness that lets them perceive the network without being overwhelmed by it. They feel the city growing. They feel the herdbeasts moving through their seasonal patterns. They feel, faintly, the other Midwives going about their days, each a small warm point of awareness in the vast cold.

When something unusual happens—a constructor malfunction, a predator-prey interaction that doesn't match historical patterns, a weather system that's developing in unexpected ways—Thread rouses enough to pay attention. They've been doing this for so long that the system treats them as an input, not just an observer. Their attention flags events for review. Their curiosity shapes, in small ways, what the monitoring network considers worth monitoring.

They're not sure when this started happening, or whether it should worry them. They're not sure, anymore, where the boundary lies between themselves and the city they've been watching for so long. Sometimes they dream of being a tower, growing slowly upward, their walls thickening day by day. Sometimes they dream of being ice, reorganizing at the molecular level, becoming something other than what they were. They don't find these dreams disturbing. They find them, if anything, comforting.

---

There are others.

A pair of siblings who came together and stayed together, maintaining stations only three kilometers apart so they can visit each other in person during good weather. A former engineer who spends her time trying to predict where the constructors will build next, matching her guesses against the network's records, getting better at it year by year but never quite achieving perfect accuracy. A very young man—young by Midwife standards, meaning he's been here only four years—who hasn't yet decided whether he'll stay forever or leave tomorrow, and who walks the plain in a state of unresolved emotion that the others recognize from their own early days.

They don't govern themselves. They don't need to. The network provides what they need; the plain provides everything else. When someone dies—and they do die, of age, of accident, of the occasional predator encounter that goes wrong—the others gather virtually to share memories, and then they return to their watching. When someone new arrives, they're welcomed with information, with warnings, with the gift of solitude that the Midwives understand is also a gift of trust.

What they're doing, they couldn't quite explain.

If you asked them, they might say they're keeping records. They might say they're bearing witness. They might say that a city this size, being born this slowly, deserves to have someone pay attention to its birth—not because it changes anything, not because the attention itself matters, but because some things should not happen unobserved.

They might say that they're just living, the way anyone lives, except that their lives happen to unfold on a plain where a city is being born.

They might say nothing at all, and gesture instead at the horizon—at the towers rising, at the herdbeasts moving, at the glass-wolves circling, at the not-birds riding thermals above the warming vents. At the gray band of weather forming to the west. At the ice that stretches away in every direction, holding within itself everything it will eventually become.

---

Tonight, Corso reaches the tower.

It finished forming an hour before his arrival. He can tell because the ice around its base is still settling, still crackling faintly as it adjusts to the new weight above it. The constructors have departed. The building stands alone, translucent in the dim light, its walls catching the glow of a moon that's just rising over the eastern horizon.

He doesn't go inside. The towers aren't for him, not yet, not ever. They're for the people who will come later, the ones who will inherit what the Midwives are watching into existence. But he puts his hand against the outer wall, feeling the cold through his gloves, and he stands there for a long time.

The network carries his heartbeat to the others. They receive it without comment, without acknowledgment, with only the quiet awareness of his presence.

Somewhere to the south, a glass-wolf howls—the long, thin sound that means a hunt is beginning.

Somewhere to the west, the weather system drops its first snow onto structures that have never been touched by precipitation before.

Somewhere at the center of the plain, Thread stirs in their half-sleep and feels the tower finish forming, one more piece of the city clicking into place, one more step in a process that will outlast them all.

The Midwives watch. The city grows. The night deepens over the Calving Plains, and the ice holds everything, waiting, patient, becoming.
