They move through the thickened air in clusters of four to seven, rarely more, rarely fewer.

You could mistake them for another succulent growth at first—their bodies are that still when resting, their coloration that continuous with the blue-green and burgundy palette of the city's vegetation. They anchor themselves to vertical surfaces: the curved walls of fruiting bodies, the interlocked stems of climbing vines, the rare exposed faces of basalt where the mycelium hasn't yet reached. When still, they flatten. Their limbs—five, radially arranged, though one is always slightly longer than the others—press against whatever surface holds them, and their central mass spreads like a pad of lichen, and their skin shifts to match. Not camouflage exactly. More like courtesy. They become part of what they're resting on.

But they're not plants. They're not fungal. They're something else—something that moves with intention through the silent streets, something that watches.

---

The eyes are distributed. Twelve to sixteen of them, clustered in pairs along the upper edges of each limb, each eye a dark wet bead that catches the butter-yellow light and holds it. When a cluster rests on a wall, their eyes face outward in all directions, a ring of quiet attention. When they move, the eyes on the leading limb become primary, the others dimming to peripheral awareness, and you can see them *tracking*. Following the passage of the six-legged grazers. Noting the position of the silent scaled birds. Watching you, if you're there to be watched, with a patience that doesn't feel predatory but doesn't feel passive either.

They don't blink. They don't need to. Whatever fluid fills those eyes is self-maintaining, recycled internally, part of the closed-loop biology that lets them live without water. You learn to recognize when they're focused on you: the slight orientation of the limb-tips, the way the distributed gaze somehow *coheres* into singular attention. It isn't uncomfortable. It's like being seen by the city itself.

---

Movement, when it comes, is slow and continuous.

The longest limb reaches first, extending outward in a motion that looks almost liquid, the muscular tissue inside rippling in sequence from base to tip. It finds purchase—on a window-gill, a vine-stem, a ridge of hardened mycelium—and adheres. Then the body follows, the central mass shifting its weight, the other four limbs releasing in sequence and reaching and re-adhering in a flowing cycle that carries them across vertical surfaces, across ceilings, across the springy horizontal streets, all without apparent effort. They could move faster. Sometimes they do, when startled—a sudden blur of limbs that carries them ten meters in seconds, finding shadow, finding stillness, flattening back into the city's texture. But mostly they move at the pace of the fungal towers growing around them. Mostly they match the metabolism of their world.

When they travel in their clusters—and they almost always travel in clusters—they move in loose coordination. Not synchronized. Not following a leader. More like a flock of birds if birds made decisions by some process of distributed consensus, each individual responding to the others' movements, the whole group flowing around obstacles and through passages as a single soft-edged entity. The clusters have no fixed membership. Individuals join, leave, join different clusters, rest alone for days before finding others. But the cluster-form is their default state. Something about their cognition seems to require it.

---

They're tool-users, after a fashion.

Not tools they make—nothing manufactured, nothing shaped by intention. But they select. They carry. In the cavity where the five limbs meet, in the soft hollow of the central mass, there's a space that can hold small objects, and they use it. Spore-casings, mostly: the hard shells that certain fungal strains produce, hollow and light and strong. The shells have uses. Wedged into the cracks where wall meets ground, they create small sheltered spaces. Stacked in careful arrangements, they form platforms. Filled with the crusite mineral powder that collects in certain basalt depressions, they become... something. You see them carrying the powder-filled shells with obvious care, distributing them to specific locations throughout their range, and you never quite understand why. A territorial marker, perhaps. A nutrient cache. A form of communication with clusters who will pass through later.

They also carry seeds. The succulent plants produce them—hard-coated, dormant, waiting for conditions that may never come. The creatures collect them, sort them by some invisible criterion, deposit them in locations that seem random until you notice the pattern: always at the edges of new mycelial growth, always in the strip of raw possibility where fungal city meets volcanic stone. They're gardening. They're gardening the frontier, seeding the expansion zone with the plants that will follow the architecture that will shelter the animals that will feed the ecosystem that feeds them.

They've been doing this for longer than the city has existed in its current form. The relationship is old. The relationship may be the reason the city exists at all.

---

Communication happens in chemicals and touch.

The skin secretes. Not constantly—not a slime trail, nothing so crude—but in response to contact, in response to proximity, in response to states of attention and intention that translate into molecular signals. When two individuals meet, they press their central masses together for three seconds, four, five, and in that time they're exchanging information through a language of compounds: here is where I've been, here is what I've eaten, here is my hormonal state, here is my cluster-affiliation, here is something that might be mood or might be memory or might be something you have no word for. When a cluster rests together on a shaded wall, their pressed-together edges form a continuous surface of slow chemical conversation.

They also touch more deliberately. The limb-tips are sensitive—dense with receptors for pressure, texture, temperature, the faint electrical fields that mycelial networks generate. When investigating something new, they touch it extensively, methodically, building a tactile map that seems to matter more to them than vision. When two clusters meet in a plaza, there's a long period of mutual touching: limb-tips brushing across central masses, tracing the patterns of eyes, checking the chemical secretions at specific body-points. It looks like greeting. It looks like grooming. It looks like reading.

What it sounds like is nothing. They make no sound. Not ever. In a city that absorbs all vibration, they've never evolved to use it.

---

They have predators.

Something larger moves through the outer districts—the thing that leaves trails of disturbed vegetation, that no one sees directly. It hunts the clusters. You find evidence: a central mass half-consumed, the limbs scattered, the eyes still wet and watching nothing. The attacks happen at the frontier, where the growth is newest and thinnest, where there are fewer hiding places, fewer surfaces for flattening against. Clusters avoid the frontier at night. Clusters avoid it entirely during certain seasons, when some signal in the spore-count or the thermal currents tells them the predator is active.

But they return. They always return, carrying their powder-filled shells and their sorted seeds, gardening the edge of the city, feeding the expansion that feeds everything. The losses are part of the calculation. The clusters absorb them, reconfigure, continue.

Sometimes a cluster-loss changes behavior for generations. Sometimes they simply replace the missing members and move on, the chemical memory of death fading into the background noise of continuous existence.

---

You could watch them for years.

You could live in this city—if you could live in this city, if its warmth and its chemistry were compatible with yours—and you could spend those years learning to read the clusters. Learning which chemical traces mean anxiety, which mean satiation, which mean the particular anticipation that precedes a cluster-merge or a cluster-split. Learning to predict their movements through the shifting maze, to understand why they choose this district over that one, why they rest on this wall for three days and then suddenly relocate to the far side of a plaza. Learning the rhythms of their frontier-gardening, the logic of their seed-selection, the meaning of the crusite powder they distribute so carefully.

You would never finish learning.

They're not complex in the way you are—no language, no abstraction, no planning beyond the immediate and the instinctual. But they're complex in the way the city is complex: emergent, interlocking, shaped by and shaping the systems around them. They're the part of the city that watches. The part that carries. The part that decides, in their slow distributed way, where the gardens will grow.

Stand in a plaza at evening. Feel the warmth. Watch a cluster flow across the far wall, their eyes catching the yellowing light, their bodies barely distinguishable from the burgundy mycelium behind them. They're watching the grazers move toward the night-district. They're watching the spore-fog begin to form in a chamber three streets away. They're watching you, if you're there.

They'll still be watching when the stone finishes settling. They'll still be watching when the city reaches whatever form it's reaching toward. They're patient. They have time.

The city grows, and they grow with it.
