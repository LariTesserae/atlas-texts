# The Wind-Listeners

They call themselves nothing. Names require speech, and speech requires a stillness between words that does not exist here. What they have instead is presence—a way of being seen, of being *known*, that passes between them through gesture and position and the particular way a body holds itself against the eternal gale.

They are perhaps a hundred, perhaps fewer. Counting them is difficult because they do not gather in ways that invite enumeration. They disperse across the plateau in patterns that follow logic you cannot immediately parse—three here, clustered in the lee of a wind-carved pedestal; one there, alone on an exposed ridge, leaning into the gale at an angle that should topple her but doesn't; a distant group of seven moving single-file along a natural channel where the wind compresses and accelerates, their progress so slow it barely registers as movement.

---

Their bodies are testimony.

They are short and dense and low to the ground, built along the same principles as the grazing beasts they share the plateau with—though they are not related to those animals, not really, not anymore. Their ancestry lies in the valleys below, in people who climbed here long ago for reasons that have been forgotten. What remains is adaptation so thorough it has become identity.

Their legs are thick and bowed, ending in feet that seem too large, splayed wide for stability. They walk with a rolling gait that keeps their center of gravity in constant motion, shifting to compensate for the wind's pressure before conscious thought could manage the calculation. Their arms are long relative to their torsos, and they use them freely—bracing against stone, gesturing to one another across distances, sometimes simply extending them into the gale to feel its texture, the way someone elsewhere might trail a hand through water.

Their skin has thickened into something that is not quite hide but is no longer simply skin. It is pale grey, the color of the granite beneath their feet, and it has a slight roughness to it, a texture that the wind slides over rather than catching. Where it stretches across their faces, it has taken on a permanent windward cast—the side that faces into the gale has worn smoother and slightly darker than the leeward side, so that each face is subtly asymmetrical, a record of which direction its owner most often looks.

And their hair—if it can be called hair—grows in dense, matted cords that stream behind them constantly, always moving, never still. It is the color of old ice, white with undertones of blue, and it has grown so thick and so interlocked that it forms a kind of insulation for their necks and upper backs. Some of the elders have hair that reaches past their waists, a lifetime of growth that has never been cut because cutting would mean wasting precious material that the body produced at significant caloric cost.

They do not wear clothing in any conventional sense. They do not need to. Instead they wrap themselves in the shed winter-coats of the grazers, felted and worked and layered until the material is nearly waterproof, nearly windproof, a second skin that they repair constantly and replace only when it finally falls apart. These wrappings leave their faces and hands exposed—must, for perception and manipulation—but cover everything else in shapeless, practical bulk.

---

You watch one of them for a long time before you understand what she is doing.

She stands on a small prominence, a knob of harder stone that has resisted erosion long enough to rise perhaps three feet above the surrounding plateau. Her body is angled into the wind at that impossible lean, and her arms are raised, extended on either side, palms facing into the gale. Her eyes are closed. Her matted hair streams behind her. She is utterly still except for minute adjustments of her hands—fingers spreading, tilting, cupping slightly, then flattening again.

She is listening.

Not with her ears, which have evolved into small, protected openings barely visible beneath folds of thickened skin. She is listening with her entire body, reading the wind the way someone elsewhere might read a book—parsing its pressure and temperature and the subtle variations in its texture, variations so slight that you cannot perceive them but that she distinguishes with the same precision a sommelier brings to wine.

Because the wind, you realize watching her, is not actually constant. You thought it was. Hours of exposure convinced you of its unwavering roar. But she knows differently. She can feel differences you cannot: the temperature dropping by a fraction of a degree as a fog bank forms somewhere miles away; the pressure increasing slightly as air compresses against a distant peak; the faint, rhythmic pulsing that indicates a herd of grazers moving across an exposed ridge and creating tiny vortices in their wake.

The wind is not constant. The wind is *language*, a continuous stream of information about the state of the plateau, and she is reading it.

After a long time—twenty minutes, perhaps, or thirty—she lowers her arms and opens her eyes. She turns, unhurried, and makes a gesture to someone far below that you cannot interpret. Then she descends from her prominence, moving with that rolling gait, and disappears between two wind-carved pillars.

---

Their shelters are marvels of negative architecture.

They do not build walls. Walls would be torn away. They do not build roofs. Roofs would become sails. Instead they have found the places where the plateau itself provides shelter—deep crevices in the rock, overhangs where harder strata have protected softer stone beneath, natural chambers carved by the wind's own action over millennia.

These spaces they have improved, but only slightly and only in ways that work *with* the wind rather than against it. At the mouth of a shelter-crevice, they have positioned stones to redirect the gale, creating a kind of aerodynamic entrance that allows air to flow through without building the pressure that would make habitation impossible. Inside, the walls have been polished smooth by generations of bodies moving against them, and the floors are covered in layers of felted grazer-hair that provide insulation from the cold stone.

You are permitted to enter one, eventually. A gesture you cannot read apparently invites you, and you follow a broad-shouldered figure into a cleft in the rock that widens unexpectedly into a chamber the size of a small room. The sound here is different—still loud, still present, but muffled somehow, the edge of the roar taken off. Your ears ring with something that approaches relative quiet.

Inside are five of them, ranging from an ancient creature whose grey skin has weathered to something almost translucent, to a child small enough that it must have been born within the last few years. They are engaged in activities that take you some time to parse: the elder is working a length of sinew between fingers that move with machinelike regularity, producing a cord of remarkable evenness; two adults are doing something with a pile of lichen that involves sorting, perhaps, or grinding; the child is practicing the wind-listening posture you observed earlier, standing at the chamber's entrance with arms extended, being guided by a fifth figure who adjusts the angle of its hands with patient repetition.

They acknowledge you with glances but do not stop what they are doing. There is no ceremony to your presence. You are here, which means you are enduring what they endure, and that is apparently enough.

---

Food is scarcity management elevated to an art form.

The grazer herds are not hunted, not exactly. Instead they are *guided*—pushed by coordinated movement toward areas where they can be separated from the weakest members, the old and injured and sick who would die regardless within days or weeks. This culling is accomplished without weapons, without violence in any form you recognize. The wind-listeners simply position themselves, appearing and disappearing at the edges of the herd's perception, creating pressure that moves the animals where they want them to go.

When an animal is finally isolated and unable to continue, they wait with it. They do not kill it. They wait until the cold or the exhaustion or the underlying weakness does the work, and then they process what remains with an efficiency that wastes nothing—not the meat, not the bones, not the hide, not the hooves, not the contents of the stomach, which contain half-digested plant matter that represents the only way these people access the plateau's sparse vegetation without competing directly with the grazers themselves.

They eat the lichen too, prepared in ways that neutralize its mild toxicity—ground and soaked and fermented in the clear water of the wind-wrung pools. They eat the cushion plants during the brief weeks when the plants produce tiny, bitter fruits. They eat insects when they can catch them, and eggs from the nests of the great silver birds, climbing to reaches that should be inaccessible but somehow aren't, using rope made from sinew and hooks carved from bone.

It is not enough. It is never enough. They are smaller than their ancestors were, smaller than anyone in the valleys below, their bodies pared down to the minimum necessary to sustain life and no more. They move carefully, conserving energy. They rest frequently. They do not play, not even the children—or rather, what play they engage in is also practice, also training, also preparation for survival.

They are hungry all the time, you realize. A permanent, low-grade hunger that has become indistinguishable from the baseline state of existence. They do not complain about it because there is no one to complain to and nothing that could be done.

---

Their communication defeats your understanding for days.

It is not speech. It cannot be speech—the wind makes speech impossible, tears words apart before they can travel from mouth to ear. It is not a sign language in any form you recognize, though gestures are clearly part of it. It seems to involve position, orientation, the timing of movement, the particular way a body holds itself relative to the wind and to other bodies.

You watch exchanges that seem to convey significant information through means you cannot identify. Two figures meet on an exposed ridge, pause facing each other for perhaps three seconds, and then diverge in opposite directions. Something has passed between them—you are certain of this—but you cannot say what or how. A group of seven making their slow progress through a compressed channel somehow coordinates a sudden left turn, all of them moving together without any signal you can detect, as if they are a single organism rather than seven separate ones.

Finally, on your fifth day on the plateau, you begin to understand.

It is the wind.

They communicate *through* the wind. Not by shouting into it, not by producing sounds that travel despite it, but by producing *disturbances* in its flow—disturbances that propagate outward and can be read by anyone practiced in reading them. A hand raised in a certain configuration creates a specific pattern of turbulence. A body positioned at a specific angle to the gale produces pressure differentials that travel. Even the way they stand relative to one another affects how the wind moves between them, creating channels and barriers that carry meaning.

This is why they are so precisely arranged across the plateau—not randomly dispersed but positioned to maintain communication networks, chains of individuals who can read and relay the wind-messages across distances that would otherwise isolate them completely. This is why their posture matters so much, why even the children practice the exact angle of an extended hand. They are not just listening to the wind. They are speaking with it.

The wind is not language, you realize. *They* are language. They have made themselves into words that the wind carries.

---

The pale hunters do not come during the day.

You learn this through observation and through the patterns of wind-listener behavior, the way they relax slightly when the sun is at its peak and tense again as it descends toward the jagged western horizon. The hunters are crepuscular—most active in the grey transition hours when the light is uncertain and the eyes of their prey have not yet fully adjusted. They emerge from dens in the deep crevices, from tunnels that wind far back into the mountain's bones, and they move along routes that the wind-listeners have long since memorized.

You glimpse one, finally, on the evening of your sixth day. You are sheltered in the entrance of a chamber-crevice, watching the light fail across the plateau, when movement catches at the edge of your vision. You turn and see it: long-bodied and low-slung, pale as bone, flowing across the granite with a grace that seems impossible for something so large. It is perhaps the size of a mountain lion but built differently—longer in the spine, shorter in the leg, with a head that seems too large for its body and ears that swivel independently toward every sound.

It does not see you. Or it sees you but does not care, does not register you as relevant. Its attention is on the grazer herd a quarter-mile distant, on calculations of angle and approach and wind-direction that will determine whether it eats tonight or goes hungry.

From somewhere across the plateau comes a pattern of disturbance in the gale—a message, you recognize now, though you cannot read it. Somewhere a wind-listener has seen the hunter and is reporting its position, its heading, its apparent intent. The message propagates outward through the network of stationed watchers, and somewhere else, unseen, the grazer herd receives warning through whatever means the wind-listeners use to communicate with them, and begins to shift its collective mass away from the danger.

The hunter's head swivels. Its ears track something you cannot perceive. Then it changes direction, abandoning its approach, flowing back toward the crevices it emerged from. The game is not worth it tonight. The prey are warned. It will try again tomorrow, or the day after, or the day after that.

The wind-listeners do not protect the grazers out of kindness. They protect them because the grazers are too valuable to lose unnecessarily, because a herd reduced too quickly cannot sustain itself, because the plateau's careful balance requires that death be managed rather than left to chance. But the effect is the same. The hunters are hungry too, perhaps hungrier than anyone, because the wind-listeners have made themselves the arbiters of what dies and when.

---

The children are born into the wind.

There is no other possibility. The shelters provide relative quiet, but relative quiet is still a roar that would deafen anyone from the valleys below. The children emerge into this sound and draw their first breaths of thin, cold air and begin their lives in conditions that should be impossible.

But they are adapted now, generations deep. Their ears develop differently, filtering the wind's roar into background noise that the brain learns to ignore. Their lungs are larger relative to their body size, their blood richer in oxygen-carrying cells. Their skin thickens as they grow, taking on that windward asymmetry that marks everyone here.

They are taught the wind-speaking before they can walk—or rather, they are taught the listening first, placed at chamber entrances to feel the gale against their palms, guided by patient adults who adjust their posture until they begin to perceive what the wind is saying. Walking comes later, and with it the full grammar of position and orientation, the ability not just to receive messages but to send them.

By the time they are five or six, they take their place in the communication network, stationed at posts appropriate for their skill level, relaying messages they do not fully understand but can repeat accurately. By ten or twelve, they join the hunts, the careful orchestration of grazer-culling that sustains the plateau's human population. By adulthood they are fully realized, as fluent in wind-speech as anyone, able to read the gale's million variations and to add their own voice to its flow.

Their lives are short by the standards of the valleys below. The cold and the hunger and the endless physical demands take their toll. Very few reach the weathered ancientness of the elder you saw in the chamber, and those who do are treated with a deference that approaches reverence—not because age is wisdom, necessarily, but because age here represents a successful negotiation with conditions that want you dead, a lifetime of correct decisions made under impossible pressure.

---

There is one who lives apart.

You hear about her through the wind itself, eventually—or rather, you perceive that messages are being passed that concern something at the plateau's edge, something that generates patterns of communication you cannot decode but can recognize as *about a specific subject*. You follow these references as best you can, piecing together a direction from the orientation of the speakers, until on your ninth day you make your way to a region of the plateau you have not previously explored.

She is there, alone, on a promontory that juts out over a sheer drop of several thousand feet. The wind is stronger here, channeled and accelerated by the topography, and she stands in it at an angle so extreme that she appears to be lying against thin air. Her hair—longer than anyone else's, trailing a dozen feet behind her—writhes in the gale like something alive. Her arms are extended, palms up, in a posture that is different from the listening-stance you have seen before. More open. More receptive.

She is old. Older than the elder in the chamber, older than anyone you have encountered. Her skin has worn so thin in places that you can see the structures beneath it, the dark outlines of veins and tendons. Her eyes, when she opens them to acknowledge your presence, are nearly white—cataracts, perhaps, or something else, some adaptation to the blinding sunlight that has taken color in exchange for protection.

She speaks to you.

Not in wind-speech, not in gesture, but in actual words—hoarse and broken, barely audible even in this relative pocket of reduced roar, but comprehensible. She has preserved this capacity somehow, or relearned it, or invented it independently. She asks where you have come from, though the answer means little to her, represents a world so far removed from her experience that it might as well be mythological.

She has been here all her life, she tells you. Four score years or more, longer than anyone now alive can remember, long enough to have seen the plateau's population dwindle from several hundred to its current diminished state. She has watched the grazers' territories shift as the cushion plants retreated toward the poles, watched the hunter packs grow bolder as their prey grew scarce, watched the wind-speakers struggle to maintain their networks with ever-fewer bodies to station.

She knows things that no one else knows anymore. The full grammar of wind-speech, including forms that have fallen into disuse. The locations of every shelter-crevice, every reliable pool, every seasonal abundance that might supplement the starvation diet. The histories that once were passed from elder to student but that now have only her as their repository.

She tells you that she chose this promontory because it is the place where the wind speaks loudest, where she can hear messages from across the entire plateau at once, where she can—sometimes, in certain conditions—perceive communications from beyond the passes, from the other screaming peaks that march toward every horizon. There are others there, she says. Other populations of wind-speakers, isolated on their own plateaus, too far away for regular contact but near enough that their messages occasionally propagate this far.

She does not know if they are prospering or dying. She does not know if this plateau's people could reach them, could combine populations and stave off the slow demographic collapse that threatens everyone here. She does not know much of anything, really, despite her knowledge—because knowledge requires action to become useful, and she is too old for action now, too fragile, too valuable to risk on journeys that might fail.

So she listens. That is all she can do. She stands on her promontory in the endless gale and listens to the wind from everywhere at once, gathering information that she passes to those who can use it, serving as a kind of living antenna for the plateau's dwindling population.

She will die here, she tells you. Soon, probably. The winters are harder each year, and her body is nearly finished. When she goes, much will be lost—the histories, the full grammar, the knowledge of the other plateaus. The wind-speakers will continue, probably, for another generation or two or three, but they will be diminished in ways they cannot entirely understand.

Unless someone learns what she knows before she dies. Unless someone takes her place on the promontory.

She looks at you with her white eyes, and you understand that she is asking a question she already knows the answer to. You are not from here. You cannot stay here. You are a visitor from the valleys below, from a world that has seasons and silences and speech that travels through still air. You will descend, soon, back to everything you know.

But you will remember.

That is what she wants. Not that you stay, not that you become something you cannot become, but simply that you remember—that you carry the knowledge of this place back to wherever you came from, that you tell someone, that the wind-listeners' existence does not vanish entirely from the world's awareness when the last of them finally succumbs to the cold and the hunger and the endless, screaming wind.

You stand with her on the promontory as the sun sets, as the light turns golden and then red and then fades to a purple twilight that makes the stars appear one by one in the crystalline sky. The wind roars around you both, and for a moment—just a moment—you think you can almost hear what she hears, the million overlapping messages from across the plateau and beyond, the voices of a people who have made themselves into words that the wind carries.

Then the moment passes, and you are only yourself again, cold and deafened and desperately far from home.

But you remember.
