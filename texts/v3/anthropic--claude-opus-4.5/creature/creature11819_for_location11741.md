# The Tenders

They call themselves nothing. Names require silence to speak, and silence does not exist here.

What they have instead is *position*—a constantly updated awareness of where each one stands relative to the great trunks, to the fruiting bodies, to each other. Identity is location. When two of them meet, they know each other not by name but by trajectory: *you are the one who was at the third-arm junction of the Groaning Pillar when the last synchronized bloom occurred; I am the one who sheltered in the northern cavity during the Spore Tide of the amber season.* Their histories are maps. Their selves are paths through the chaos.

---

They are not native here. That much is obvious from their bodies, which are wrong for this place in almost every way.

They stand upright, balanced on two limbs, a posture that makes no sense in a world where the ground itself may lurch or retract without warning. Their skin is soft, vulnerable, nothing like the chitinous armor of the burrowing fauna or the compression-capable flesh of the boneless grazers. They have ears—actual ears, delicate spiraling structures mounted on the sides of their heads—though these have long since stopped functioning as intended. Generations ago, the Tenders went deaf. Not through damage, but through adaptation: their auditory processing shifted inward, repurposed, until sound became something felt in the bones and gut rather than heard. They perceive the roar now as a kind of internal weather, a constant pressure-system of vibration that they read the way their ancestors might have read wind or temperature.

Their eyes, though. Their eyes still work, and they are extraordinary—wide and faceted, grown larger over generations, capable of processing the overwhelming light without burning out. They see in spectrums their ancestors could not, reading the bioluminescence of the plants as a kind of language, tracking the subtle color-shifts that precede a fruiting event or a tendril-strike. Where a newcomer would see only blinding chaos, a Tender sees patterns, warnings, opportunities.

They are small. Smaller than they once were, perhaps—compressed by generations of living in the gaps, the stable niches, the rare still places. An adult might reach your shoulder, no more. Their limbs are proportionally longer than seems right, adapted for reaching, for spanning the gaps between handholds. Their fingers—seven on each hand, the extra digits a mutation that proved so useful it became universal within a few generations—are tipped with hard, curved nails that slot into the cracks in dead plant-flesh like keys into locks.

---

They live in the corpses.

When a great trunk dies—a process that can take centuries, the living tissue slowly replaced by inert crystal, the internal rhythms fading to stillness—it becomes real estate. The Tenders move in while the structure is still cooling, still settling, claiming cavities and carving new ones with tools made from the shed hooks of the parasitic fauna. A single dead trunk might house a community of hundreds, their chambers connected by tunnels that follow the natural channels where fluid once flowed.

Inside, they have achieved something remarkable: quiet. Not silence—silence is impossible here—but a reduction, a dampening. They line their walls with layers of shed plant-fiber, woven into dense mats that absorb vibration. They seal their entrances with membranes harvested from the fruiting bodies, flexible enough to allow passage but thick enough to blunt the worst of the roar. In the innermost chambers, the noise drops to something merely deafening rather than annihilating, and here—here they can almost speak.

Almost. Their language is not spoken so much as *transmitted*: a combination of touch-signs pressed into each other's skin, chemical signals dabbed from glands on their wrists, and a low subsonic humming that travels through whatever surface they're both touching. A conversation between two Tenders looks, to an outsider, like an intimate dance—bodies close, hands moving across arms and shoulders and faces, both parties with their feet planted firmly on the same patch of ground to feel each other's hum. It is slow. It is inefficient. It is the only way they can share anything more complex than basic warnings.

The warnings, at least, are fast. A Tender who senses a fruiting event, a tendril-strike, a sudden shift in the local growth patterns, does not need to touch anyone. They simply *move*—a specific motion, a specific rhythm, recognizable even in peripheral vision. Others see and relay, the warning spreading through the community in seconds, a visual ripple that sends everyone scrambling for shelter or stability.

---

They do not understand the plants. This is important. They have lived here for more generations than they can count, and they still do not understand.

They have theories—cosmologies, really, systems of belief that attempt to make sense of the endless churning. Some communities believe the great trunks are thinking, that the entire thicket is a single mind whose thoughts manifest as growth and motion. Others believe the plants are dead, that what looks like life is merely chemistry following its inevitable course, and that the Tenders are the only truly living things in this place. Still others believe the thicket is a machine built by someone long vanished, still running its original program, and that if they could only find the right chamber, the right switch, they could make it stop.

None of these beliefs are correct. All of them are useful. They give the Tenders ways to talk about what they observe, frameworks for predicting what might happen next, rituals that provide comfort in a world that offers none.

What they *do* understand is pattern. Not the deep patterns, not the *why*, but the surface patterns, the *when* and *where*. They have mapped the rhythms of their local region with an obsessive precision that has taken generations to accumulate: which trunk pulses on which cycle, which fruiting bodies are due to detonate, which tendrils are growing aggressive and should be avoided. They keep these maps in their bodies, in the paths they walk, in the sequences of touch-signs passed from elder to child. A young Tender learning to navigate is not taught facts; they are taught *routes*, safe passages that weave through the chaos, timed to avoid the worst of the motion.

And they are never, ever complete. The thicket changes. Old patterns fail. New growths emerge and rewrite the local geography. Every generation of Tenders must re-learn parts of the map, must discover through painful trial which of their ancestors' paths have become deathtraps. They keep records of the dead, and of *where* the dead died, and these records are among their most sacred possessions: a catalog of failures, a negative map of the places where the patterns broke.

---

They worship nothing, but they tend.

That is the word they use for themselves, in their slow tactile language: the ones who tend. It does not mean they care for the plants—the plants are beyond care, beyond any intervention the Tenders could offer. What they tend is the relationship. The negotiation. The endless, exhausting work of existing in a place that was not made for them and does not notice they exist.

They tend the dead trunks, reinforcing the walls of their homes against the grinding pressure of the living growth around them. They tend the routes, maintaining the paths between communities, clearing debris, marking new dangers. They tend each other, caring for the injured—and there are always injured, always someone who mistimed a crossing or failed to read a warning sign—with a gentleness that seems impossible in so violent a place. They tend the children, who cannot yet read the patterns, who must be kept in the deepest chambers until they are old enough to learn the routes that might keep them alive.

And they tend the border between themselves and the thicket. This is their central task, the work that defines them. Because the thicket does not want them here—does not want anything, cannot want—but it will kill them if they let it. They must constantly reinforce the membrane between their world and its. They must be always aware of where the growth is advancing, where new tendrils are approaching their homes, where the territory they thought was stable is becoming active again.

Sometimes they lose. A community that has lived in the same dead trunk for ten generations might find the surrounding tissue coming back to life, new growth infiltrating their chambers, the walls they built being absorbed into something larger. When this happens, they have no choice but to flee, to find a new corpse, to start again. They do not mourn the loss of place. Place was never theirs to begin with.

---

There is a Tender—old, now, her skin marked with the scars of a hundred close escapes, her fingers worn smooth from decades of gripping and climbing—who sits in the deepest chamber of her community's home and teaches the young ones.

She presses their hands to the wall and shows them how to feel the vibration-signature of an approaching fruiting event. She guides their eyes to the patches of bioluminescence on the ceiling of the chamber, transplanted here long ago, and shows them how the colors shift when the local chemistry changes. She traces routes onto their arms, the touch-signs burning themselves into muscle memory: *here you go when the amber light pulses twice, here you shelter when the ground-thrum deepens, here you never go at all.*

And sometimes, in the quietest moments—when the roar outside has settled to its lowest register, when the children are calm, when the work of tending has been done for the day—she tells them stories.

The stories are not about heroes or gods. They are about Tenders who noticed something, who saw a pattern no one else had seen, who added a new route to the map. They are about communities that found a particularly stable corpse and lived there for twenty generations before the growth took it back. They are about the first Tenders, whoever they were, who came to this place from somewhere else—somewhere quiet, somewhere still, somewhere the ground did not churn and the light did not blind—and decided to stay anyway.

Why did they stay? The children always ask this. It is the central mystery of the Tender cosmology: why did the ancestors choose this place, this deafening, overwhelming, impossible place?

The old Tender has no answer. None of them do. But she has a suspicion, which she sometimes shares: perhaps they did not choose. Perhaps they were stranded here, crashed or lost or abandoned, and by the time they understood where they were, it was too late to leave. Perhaps the first Tenders were simply survivors, making the best of a catastrophe.

Or perhaps—and this is the version she prefers, the one she presses into the children's skin with something like hope—perhaps they saw something in the chaos that made them want to stay. Perhaps they looked at the roaring thicket and saw not just danger but possibility. A world that was always making, always churning, always new. A world where nothing was ever finished, where even the greatest structures would eventually fall and be replaced by something else. A world that would never let them rest, never let them stagnate, never let them stop adapting.

Perhaps they stayed because they knew that anything capable of surviving this place would become something extraordinary.

The children do not entirely understand. They are too young; the thicket is all they have ever known. But they feel the old Tender's hands moving on their skin, the warmth of her body close to theirs, the subsonic hum of her voice traveling through the floor into their feet. And they know, without being able to articulate it, that they are part of something: a chain of survival stretching back through generations, a map written in flesh and memory, a community of beings who tend the impossible border between themselves and a world that does not know they exist.

Outside, the roar continues. It will continue forever, or something close enough to forever that the difference does not matter. The great trunks pulse. The fruiting bodies swell. The crystalline spores arc through the brilliant air.

And in the quiet chambers, in the corpses, in the gaps the living world has not yet reclaimed, the Tenders go on. Mapping. Warning. Teaching. Tending.

They are not the dominant life form here. They never will be. The thicket does not belong to them, and they have long since stopped believing it ever could. But they belong to themselves, and to each other, and to the delicate, hard-won knowledge that keeps them alive for one more generation.

That is enough. It has to be. In a world that offers nothing else, it is everything.
