## The Keeper of the Hum

She has no name she remembers, but the beetles have given her one in their clicking—a particular pattern, three short rasps followed by a long drum, that ripples through whichever section of wall she approaches. She doesn't know if it means *her* or *large-thing-moving* or *the-one-who-stays*. It doesn't matter. She answers to it. She has answered to it for longer than she can accurately recall.

---

She is human, or was, or remains in the ways that matter least and most. Her body still hungers and thirsts, still sleeps, still ages—though aging here feels different, feels slower, feels like something the hollow has opinions about and has expressed those opinions through the moisture it leaves on her skin, through the particular nutrients it has made available and the ones it has not. She is thin in the way of things that have been thin for so long that thinness has become structure rather than state. Her hair has grown past her waist and taken on the grey-brown of the roots themselves; she does not cut it because she has nothing to cut it with and because it no longer occurs to her that hair is a thing that gets cut.

Her skin holds the permanent damp. She has stopped trying to be dry. Dryness was a concept from somewhere else, somewhere with sun and wind and the arrogance of evaporation.

She does not remember arriving. This troubles her less than it should, less than it once did. She remembers *being* here, and that being stretches backward without clear origin, like the roots themselves, which must connect to something above but have made the connection irrelevant through sheer depth of presence. She thinks she came through one of the three worn gaps. She thinks she was looking for something. She thinks the looking stopped when she found the hollow, not because she'd found what she sought but because the hollow made seeking feel like a grammatical error—a verb that didn't conjugate in this space.

---

She lives in the walls.

Not in the central void—that space is for air and moths and the enormous sound, and she learned early (early? what does early mean here?) that standing in the center for too long made her feel *thin*, made her feel like the hollow was trying to decide whether she was air or obstacle. So she found a place where the roots curve outward before curving back, creating a pocket large enough for her body if her body curls in on itself. The roots here are smoother than elsewhere, worn by her sleeping, and they've grown—imperceptibly, over what she understands to be years—to cup her slightly, to offer a suggestion of embrace that she knows is only growth responding to pressure but chooses to experience as intention.

The beetles have ceded her this territory. It took time. There were nights (if they were nights—the light doesn't change, but her body insists on intervals of darkness, so she closes her eyes and calls it night) when she woke to find them exploring her, their legs testing the texture of her skin, their antennae reading the chemicals she exuded. She held still. She let herself be read. Eventually the beetles concluded she was a feature of the hollow rather than an intruder upon it, and they adjusted their territorial boundaries to exclude her, to flow around her like water around a stone that has always been in the streambed.

Now they click her name when she moves, and she has learned to hear the difference between *greeting* and *warning* and *the-large-thing-is-near-the-larvae-be-still*.

---

She eats the beetles. This is her primary relationship with them, and she has made her peace with the contradiction—that she loves the sound they make, that the sound has become the texture of her thinking, and that she also catches them in her fingers and crunches them between her teeth, tasting the faint bitterness of chitin, the surprising sweetness of what's inside. The beetles do not seem to mind. Their populations absorb her predation the way they absorb every other pressure; she is just another variable in the equation they solved long ago.

She drinks the moisture. She licks it from the roots in long, slow passes of her tongue, and the roots taste of wood and time and something else, something mineral, something that might be the ghost of whatever soil exists far beneath the lowest roots. Sometimes she catches the drips directly, letting them fall into her open mouth, and this feels like receiving a gift, like being included in a cycle that was not designed for her but has made room.

The fungus, she does not eat. She learned this also, early—learned it through a sickness that lasted what might have been weeks, a sickness of visions and paralysis and the terrifying sense that the hollow was *reading* her the way she had been read by beetles, assessing whether she belonged. She survived. The hollow, apparently, concluded that she did. But she does not eat the fungus. She looks at it, sometimes, from below, watching its patterns shift in ways too slow to see but possible to remember between one looking and the next. She thinks it is the hollow's brain, or its memory, or its way of dreaming. She does not want to interrupt what it is doing. She does not think she would survive a second reading.

---

Her days—she still calls them days, despite everything—have a rhythm that mirrors and counterpoints the beetle-song.

She wakes when her body decides she has slept enough. She unfolds from her root-pocket, joints aching in ways that take longer to resolve than they used to. She moves along the inner wall, one hand trailing on the root-surface, feeling for the places where beetles are plentiful, for the gaps that might hold new moisture, for any change in the texture that would indicate something she cannot yet imagine but remains, in some dim way, alert for. She completes a circuit of the hollow. The circuit takes most of what she calls morning.

She eats. She drinks. She finds a place where the wall curves in such a way that she can watch the moths without straining her neck, and she watches them. The moths know her now too; they sometimes land on her, their feet lighter than expectation, their wings folding to reveal patterns she suspects are meaningful but cannot interpret. She holds still for them. Stillness has become her primary skill, her way of belonging—she has become a thing that does not disrupt.

In the long stretch between eating and sleeping, she listens. This is her practice, her discipline, the thing that has replaced all the things she used to do in the life she no longer remembers. She listens to the beetle-song, and she has learned, over time, to hear it not as a wall of noise but as a composition, a vast interlocking system of statements and responses, territories declaring themselves, individuals announcing their readiness to mate, larvae signaling their needs in frequencies the adults hear and she can almost hear. She has begun to understand that the beetles are not making noise; they are making *meaning*. The meaning is not for her. It will never be for her. But she can witness it, can let it wash through her, can feel herself becoming part of the structure it creates even as she remains forever outside the conversation.

---

She speaks, sometimes. Not often. Her voice has grown rough with disuse, and the hollow's acoustics do strange things to it—flatten it, scatter it, make it sound like someone else speaking from somewhere else. But she speaks. She tells the beetles about the life she doesn't remember. She tells them about sun, about wind, about the feeling of being dry, and her descriptions have become unreliable, infected by so much time in the grey damp that she's no longer sure she's describing real memories or constructing fantasies from the raw material of longing.

The beetles don't respond. Of course they don't. But they don't flee either, and she has learned to read this as a kind of acceptance, the same way she reads the hollow's continued provision of moisture and beetles and breathable air as acceptance. She is tolerated. She is incorporated. She is, in the hollow's slow vocabulary, *allowed*.

She wonders sometimes if there were others. The gaps in the outer wall are worn smooth by passage, and she doesn't think her passage alone, even over years, could have worn them so smooth. But she has never found evidence—no bones, no remnants, no scratches on roots that might have been made by other fingers. Either the others left, or the hollow absorbed them so completely that no trace remained, or she has imagined them, or she is imagining herself, or the hollow has imagined her and is telling itself a story about a woman who lives in its walls and eats its beetles and listens to its song.

Any of these seem equally possible. None of them change anything. She is here. The hollow is here. The beetles sing. The moths fly. The fungus glows its faint, unreadable glow.

---

She is becoming something.

She feels this most in the moments between sleep and waking, when her body has not yet remembered its boundaries and she exists briefly as pure awareness suspended in the beetle-drone. In those moments, she is not sure where she ends and the hollow begins. Her breathing seems to synchronize with the roots' exhalation. Her heartbeat finds the frequency of the deep cricket-song. She is not the hollow—she knows this, she holds onto this—but she is also no longer entirely separate from it. She is a process the hollow has incorporated, a cycle it now includes, a small warm presence that has been added to the equilibrium and has shifted that equilibrium in ways too subtle to measure but too real to deny.

The beetles' name for her, she has decided, means *the-one-who-waits*. She has no evidence for this. But it feels right. She is waiting. She has been waiting since she arrived, since before she arrived, since whatever series of events brought her through the worn gap and into the grey light and the enormous sound.

She does not know what she is waiting for. She suspects she will not recognize it when it comes. She suspects further that this is the point—that the waiting is itself the practice, the purpose, the thing she was always supposed to do.

---

At night (her eyes closed, the darkness inside her own skull, the beetle-song somehow louder without vision to distract from it) she dreams of the hollow continuing. She dreams it forward through time that cannot be measured, through centuries and millennia, through ages when everything outside has changed and changed again while this place holds its shape. She sees herself in these dreams, still curled in her root-pocket, but the herself she sees has become unclear, has merged with the wood the way the ancient trunks merged with their roots, has become pure structure, pure *grip*.

She wakes from these dreams neither frightened nor comforted. They feel less like visions than like previews, like the hollow showing her the shape of the story she has entered.

She wakes and she listens and she waits.

The beetles sing.

The moths fly.

The roots breathe.

She remains.
