# The Kelp-Weavers of the Hanging Waters

**Mira Soft-Hands** has not seen air in forty-three years, though she was born breathing it.

She remembers little of that first life—only brightness that hurt, a dryness that cracked her infant skin, the terror of her mother carrying her down through the silver ceiling into the cool embrace below. Her people had been plateau-dwellers once, herders of some animal whose name no longer matters, driven to the water's edge by a war or a drought or a plague. The details have dissolved like the faces of the old gods. What remains is this: they went into the water, and the water let them stay.

Now Mira sits in the mid-canopy, her legs folded beneath her on a platform of gas-bladder and weighted stone, her fingers working through kelp fiber with the automatic grace of five decades' practice. Her skin has taken on the blue-gray pallor of all the long-submerged, faintly translucent at the temples where veins show through like buried rivers. Her eyes have grown larger across the generations—not her own eyes, but the eyes of her people, adapting incrementally to the green-gold murk. She can see in light that would have blinded her surface ancestors with its dimness.

The fiber she works is for a wall. Specifically, for the new chamber her granddaughter Osen is growing in the upper reaches, where light falls thick and golden through gaps in the canopy. Osen is young and wants brightness. Mira remembers wanting brightness once. Now she prefers the twilight zones, the blue-moss glow, the comfortable pressure of deeper water against her adapted lungs.

Her hands know this work without her attention, so her attention wanders. To the school of silversides flowing past her platform in their endless circling patrol. To the ray that glides overhead, its shadow briefly cooling the light. To the distant sound of the eels beginning their evening warm-up, that subsonic hum that means the day is tilting toward dusk.

She is one of the **kelp-weavers**, though the name is a simplification that she accepts for convenience. What she actually does is closer to *kelp-listening*. The plants tell her where they want to grow, where they're straining, where they're ready to be guided. She learned this from her mother's mother, who learned it from someone whose name is lost, who learned it from someone who perhaps spoke to the kelp for the first time and heard it answer. The weaving is just the end result—the conversation made material, the agreement between human need and vegetable intention.

---

**Orun the Thermalist** lives where the warm water rises.

He is ancient by the city's standards—seventy winters, if they measured winters, which they don't—and his joints ache with a mineral buildup that the healers can slow but not reverse. The warm currents help. They loosen what cold water calcifies. So he's made his home directly above the largest vent, in a chamber of volcanic stone that stays blood-warm year-round, and he's become the keeper of the heat.

This is not a formal position. Nothing here is formal. But over decades, Orun has learned the vents' moods so thoroughly that others defer to his knowledge. He can feel a thermal shift coming three days before it arrives. He knows which vents are strengthening and which are dying. When the eastern quarter's heat began to fade two decades ago, he was the one who first warned that the population would need to drift westward, and he was right, and the drift happened, and no one was caught in the cold.

His body has adapted to the warmth in ways that unsettle visitors from the cooler zones. His skin flushes red even when he's relaxed. His fingers are thick and clumsy-looking but capable of manipulating objects in temperatures that would scald others. He sleeps directly over the vent mouth, in water hot enough to cook food, and he dreams of magma—or what he imagines magma to be, having never seen it, only felt its distant breath.

The eels love him. They gather at his chamber every dusk for the singing, and he's learned to feel their vibrations as a kind of language, patterns that repeat and vary. He doesn't claim to understand them—that would be presumption—but he's begun to sing back, in his way. A humming in his chest that the water carries. The eels seem to respond. Whether they think he's one of them, or a strange large creature worth tolerating, or something else entirely, he can't say. The relationship persists regardless of his understanding it.

His daughter, **Tesh**, brings him food every three days. She's a net-caster, one of the harvesters who manage the silverside schools when their numbers swell past comfortable. She doesn't like the heat; visits to her father leave her flushed and irritable for hours after. But she comes anyway, carrying bundles of smoked kelp-heart and the preserved flesh of mollusks, enough to keep him fed until her next visit. She worries about him. He knows this and accepts it as the tax children pay for having parents.

---

**The Pallid Ones** live in the deep places, where light never reaches.

They are not a separate people—not exactly. They're descendants of the same surface refugees, the same drowning pilgrims who found breath in water. But generations ago, some families chose to settle in the temple depths, in the basalt chambers far below the canopy, and isolation has made them strange.

Their skin has lost all pigment. Their eyes have swelled even larger than the mid-dwellers', and those eyes have developed a sensitivity to bioluminescence so acute that they find the upper zones painfully bright. They speak more slowly, in lower registers that carry better through stone. They keep their own calendars, based on cycles of moss-bloom and the hunting patterns of the deep-water creatures that occasionally drift up from whatever abyss lies below the plateau's roots.

**Vell** is their speaker—not leader, because the Pallid Ones don't organize that way, but the one who interfaces with the upper city when interface is needed. She is colorless as milk, her hair floating around her head in a permanent pale cloud, her voice so low that upper-dwellers sometimes can't hear her at all. She communicates partly through gesture, a sign language that developed in the silent chambers where even the fish-roar doesn't reach.

She comes up twice a year, at the equinoxes, when something in the water chemistry shifts and the boundary between zones softens. She brings news from below: what the Pallid Ones have heard traveling through the stone, what movements they've sensed in the wild kelp, what omens they've read in the behavior of the creatures that share their darkness. In return she takes back reports from above—weather in the light-zones, population shifts, the health of the canopy that she'll never see except as a distant green glow.

The rest of the city regards the Pallid Ones with a mixture of respect and unease. They're kin, undeniably, but kin who've drifted far enough to become almost alien. Their children, on the rare occasions they're seen, move through water with a boneless grace that looks wrong to upper-dweller eyes. Their tools are all shell and bone, shaped by touch alone. Their relationship with the octopuses is closer than anyone above understands—a partnership, perhaps, or something more tangled.

Vell doesn't explain. Explanation isn't something the Pallid Ones value. What matters to them is the deep listening, the attention to frequencies too low for others to hear. They claim the plateau speaks to them. They claim it has been speaking since before the water rose. No one above knows what to do with this claim, so they nod politely and let the Pallid Ones keep their mysteries.

---

**Cull** is seven years old and already a thief.

Not from people—stealing from people would bring consequences her still-forming social instincts know to avoid. She steals from the octopuses, which is worse, or better, depending on perspective. The octopuses steal from everyone. Cull has simply decided that turnabout is fair, and she's made it her project to steal back.

She is small for her age, wiry and quick, with the oversized eyes of her generation and a gap-toothed grin that she deploys strategically when caught where she shouldn't be. Her mother, a rope-maker in the middle zones, has given up trying to curtail her expeditions; her father, who dove to retrieve volcanic glass from the deep vents and never surfaced, is not available for consultation. So Cull runs semi-feral through the kelp corridors, learning the city's geography the way children do—through trespass, through error, through the slow accumulation of consequences.

The octopus she's currently stalking is one of the big ones, a deep-red individual with a scar across its mantle that the children call Old Wound. It lives in a temple chamber on the settlement's northern edge, in a crevice so stuffed with stolen objects that it resembles a midden more than a den. Cull has seen awls in there, rope-ends, the polished shells that serve as currency in the upper markets, even a thermal-stone that someone must be missing badly.

She doesn't actually want any of these things. She wants the game, the matching of wits with something that thinks differently than she does. Old Wound is smart—smarter than her, she suspects, in ways she can't articulate. It knows she's been watching it. It's started watching her back. Their relationship has evolved into something neither of them fully controls: a mutual surveillance, a slow chess match played across weeks and months.

Yesterday, Old Wound left a shell at the entrance to her mother's rope-chamber. It was empty, unremarkable, the kind of thing that washes through the city constantly. But it was placed precisely where Cull would find it, oriented in a way that felt intentional. She's been thinking about it ever since. What did it mean? An offering? A threat? An invitation?

She won't know until she responds, so she's crafting her response: a small twist of kelp-fiber, shaped into a spiral that pleases her, carrying no message she's aware of but carrying *something*, some intention that might translate across the species gap. She'll leave it outside Old Wound's den tonight and see what happens.

This is how the city's treaties are maintained—not through grand negotiation, but through countless small exchanges like this one. Child and octopus, testing each other. Finding the edges. Learning what can be taken and what must be given.

---

**The Ray-Speakers** are not precisely a profession.

They're individuals who have, through accident or inclination, developed a rapport with the great gliding creatures of the canopy. Some claim the relationship began when a ray saved a drowning child—though drowning makes little sense in a city where everyone breathes water, so the story may be metaphorical. Others say the rays simply chose certain people, the way the octopuses choose certain chambers, and the chosen ones had little say in the matter.

**Ira**, who is thirty and unmarried by choice, is the most prominent of the current Ray-Speakers. She lives on a platform at canopy-level, higher than almost anyone else, in the zone where light falls strong and the rays feed on whatever grows in that brightness. Her skin is sun-darkened from proximity to the surface—not true sun, but the light filtered through the silver ceiling, strong enough at this height to mark those who linger in it.

She doesn't command the rays. That would be a fundamental misunderstanding of the relationship. She listens to them, tracks their movements, interprets the patterns for those below. When the rays shift their feeding grounds, she notices first. When they gather in unusual numbers, she notes the location and reports it. When one dies—they do die, eventually, drifting down through the water column in a slow descent that the whole city pauses to observe—she's the one who reads the body, determining cause, looking for signs of disease or predation that might threaten others.

In return, the rays tolerate her presence in their space. More than tolerate—they seem to seek her out. They glide close to her platform, close enough that she can reach out and touch their wings if she's careful, if she reads their mood correctly. Once, in a strong current that threatened to sweep her from her anchor-point, a ray adjusted its path to break the flow, sheltering her in its shadow until the surge passed.

She tells no one about that. It sounds too much like miracle, and Ira doesn't believe in miracles. She believes in relationships, in patience, in the slow accumulation of trust between beings who share a space. The ray that sheltered her has since moved on to other feeding grounds. She doesn't know if it will return. She doesn't expect gratitude, doesn't expect anything. The relationship exists in the moment or it doesn't exist at all.

---

**Beneath all of them**, in the basalt bones of the plateau, **something else** has lived longer than the water has hung here.

The Pallid Ones know of it. They feel its dreams brushing against their thoughts in the deepest chambers, where the rock grows warm and strange. They've given it no name—naming seems presumptuous—but they orient their settlements around its sleeping presence the way a body orients around a spine.

It may have been here before the plateau rose. It may have been here before the mountain formed. It is not hostile, exactly, but it is vast and slow and its movements, when it moves, reshape the stone itself. The thermal vents are its breath. The groans the residents hear in the night are its heartbeat, or something like heartbeat, a rhythm too slow to measure in human generations.

Vell has spoken to it. Or believes she has. In the deepest reaches of her zone, in a chamber her people sealed generations ago and visit only in extremity, there is a wall of basalt smoother than any other surface in the city. When she presses her forehead against it, she feels something that might be attention. Something that notices her noticing.

What it wants—if it wants—she cannot say. It tolerates the city, has tolerated it for centuries or longer. Perhaps it enjoys the company. Perhaps it doesn't notice them at all, the way a person might not notice the mites that live in their eyelashes. The Pallid Ones maintain their seals and their silence regarding this neighbor because the alternative—drawing its full attention—seems unwise.

Above, in the lit zones, in the kelp-corridors and market chambers and the busy everyday of the underwater settlement, no one thinks much about what lives below. They have fish to catch, fiber to weave, children to raise, heat to find. The depths are for the Pallid Ones and whatever dreams down there.

---

**The city breathes, and its people breathe with it.**

Mira finishes her weaving as the light shifts toward evening bronze. She bundles the wall-fiber and secures it to her platform, where it will wait until she's gathered enough for the full installation. Tomorrow or the next day, she'll swim up to Osen's new chamber and begin the fitting, the slow work of integrating woven fiber with living kelp, encouraging the structure to take root and become permanent. Or as permanent as anything is here.

Orun feels the dusk-shift in his bones before he sees it—a slight cooling in the vent-flow, the way it always cools as night comes to whatever world lies above the silver ceiling. He hums to the gathering eels, and they hum back, and the chorus builds toward its nightly crescendo.

Cull leaves her spiral offering outside Old Wound's den and retreats to a watching distance, patience learned from weeks of this game. The octopus will find it. The octopus will respond, or won't. Either way, she'll learn something.

Ira watches the rays settle into their night-patterns, the way they slow and descend slightly as the light fades, conserving energy until dawn brings the brightness they feed on. She notes their positions, their numbers, the subtle signs of health or stress in their movements. This is her work, and she loves it the way people love things they've chosen to give their lives to.

And in the deep, beneath all of them, in the unsealed chamber that Vell knows and the rest do not, the slow dreamer shifts slightly in its long sleep, a movement that sends a tremor through the plateau's bones—noticed by the Pallid Ones, felt as a distant rumble by the mid-dwellers, ignored by the children splashing in the upper corridors who have never known a world that didn't tremble occasionally.

The lake hangs. The kelp grows. The people swim through their slow lives in this impossible place, building and rebuilding, learning and forgetting, becoming what the water makes of them.

They are home.
