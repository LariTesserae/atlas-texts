They call themselves *the Tenders*, though that's a translation of a word that means something closer to "those who are tended while tending"—the language makes no distinction between the caretaker and the cared-for.

---

**The grandmother they call Vessel-of-Vessels** sits at the cistern's lowest inhabited level, where the warmth rising from below meets the moisture falling from above. She is perhaps seventy years old, perhaps older—age is counted here not in years but in *layerings*, the number of times you've helped add a new stratum to the walls. She has forty-three layerings to her name. Her skin has taken on the mineral quality of the clay she's worked all her life, and her eyes have gone the pale amber of the lamp-fat, and she no longer climbs to the upper reaches because her knees have calcified into something that bends only reluctantly. But her hands still shape the water-channels, still repair the drip-pipes, still feel for the hairline cracks that would let moisture go where moisture should not go.

She remembers things the others have forgotten. Not history—there is no history here, no sense of *before*, only the continuous *now* of maintenance and growth. But she remembers the *feeling* of the cistern from when she was small, the particular pitch of the water-percussion before the eastern catch-basins were rebuilt, the taste of the air when the compost-alcoves were positioned differently. These memories are useless except when they're not. Twice in her life, she has said *this is wrong* about some new construction, and twice she has been unable to explain why, and twice she has been proven right when the new work cracked or sagged or leaked. So they listen to her. They bring her fresh clay to hold, let her feel its texture, watch her face for the small frown that means *not this batch, something is off.*

She speaks rarely and only in the lowest register, a voice like water moving over stone. The children gather near her because she is warm—her body has absorbed so much heat from below that she radiates it gently, a comfort in the close air. They fall asleep against her while she works, and she does not mind. She was once one of them.

---

**The twins who are not twins** share a sleeping-alcove on the eastern curve, where the wall-gardens grow thickest. They were born three years apart to different mothers, but they move identically, finish each other's sentences in the same breath, and have never spent a full day-cycle out of each other's sight. One is called *Clicking* and the other is called *Whirring*—named for the beetles, as many here are named. They're the beetle-keepers now, having inherited the role from an uncle who fell into the deep pools and did not resurface.

Clicking tends to the ceramic houses, ensuring they're positioned where the humidity is right, that the feeding-troughs stay full, that the population doesn't exceed what the cistern can support. Whirring collects the shells of the dead—beetles here live short, frantic lives, breeding constantly in the warm dark—and sorts them by size and iridescence, preparing them for whatever purpose shells serve. Trade? Ritual? Decoration? The distinction is not meaningful here.

They're young, perhaps seventeen and twenty, and they carry with them the particular intensity of people who've found their purpose early. They talk to the beetles in a register the others can't quite hear, a high clicking sound they make with their tongues against the backs of their teeth. The beetles respond. Or seem to. They cluster when the twins approach, follow them in lazy spirals, land on their shoulders and arms as though trained.

Whether this is genuine communication or simply patterned behavior, no one asks. The question would not make sense here. Everything in the cistern communicates. The water speaks through its dripping. The walls speak through their cracks. The beetles speak through their clustering. The twins speak through the beetles. It's all one conversation, continuous and overlapping.

---

**The child they have not yet named** is four years old and will not receive a name until six. This is the way: you must survive the first illnesses, must prove you can climb without falling, must learn to pitch your voice correctly, before you earn a word for yourself. Until then, you are simply *small one*, *growing one*, *not-yet*.

This particular not-yet has made the salamanders her obsession. She knows where each of them lives on the eastern wall, has given them secret names she whispers only to herself, brings them the pale insects she catches in the compost-alcoves. The salamanders tolerate her. One of them—larger than the others, its translucent skin showing a heart that beats visibly—allows her to hold it for minutes at a time while she examines the shadows of its organs with fascinated horror.

Her mother watches from a nearby platform, not interfering. This is how you learn the cistern: by choosing something to love and following it. The child will learn the moisture-patterns of the wall by tracking salamanders. She will learn the temperature gradients by noting where they cluster. She will learn the danger of the deep pools by watching them avoid the water's edge. Every lesson here is taught by indirection, by observation, by patient accumulation.

The child has not yet seen the surface. She may never see it. Some here have climbed the smooth shaft, have touched the cracked foundation stones of the forgotten city above, have even emerged into what they call *the dry bright*, but they always return. The surface is not forbidden—nothing here is forbidden—but it is understood to be *less*. Less warm. Less alive. Less *tended*.

---

**The ones who climb** are a class apart, though they would not describe themselves that way. They're simply the ones whose bodies are suited for it: long-limbed, lightweight, with fingers that grip the fire-hardened wood and gut-cord with an assurance the others lack. There are seven of them currently, ranging from a boy of twelve who is still learning the upper routes to a woman of fifty who has made the climb to the surface more times than she can count.

They harvest the birds. They maintain the drip-pipes where they enter the cistern. They watch for cracks in the ceiling, for shifts in the smoke-patterns that might indicate blocked ventilation. And they bring back *things*—not often, not much, but occasionally they return from the surface with a fragment of metal, a handful of seeds, a piece of glass worn smooth by weather. These objects enter the cistern's economy gradually, are worked and reworked until they're no longer *from outside* but simply *from here*.

The woman who leads them—**She-Who-Ascends**, named for her first successful surface-climb at age nine—has developed a condition the others don't quite understand. Her eyes have begun to fail in the lamplight, have grown *too* adapted to darkness, so that even the low green flames cause her pain. She wears a strip of leather across her face now, sees through it only dimly, navigates the climbs increasingly by touch and memory. The others watch her and calculate: how many more seasons before she can't climb at all? Who will lead them then?

She doesn't speak of succession. She speaks of the upper reaches, of the way the smoke behaves near the ventilation cracks, of the particular consistency of the drip-pipe clay that tells her whether the rains above have been heavy or light. She speaks of the surface, too, though only rarely, only when the children gather close and ask. She describes the dry bright in terms they can barely comprehend: a light that has no source, an air that does not press close, a space that extends beyond seeing. The children shiver with delighted horror. They do not want to go there. They want only to hear about it, to feel the thrill of the impossible.

---

**The man who tends the deep pools** has no name anyone uses. He is simply *the poolkeeper*, and he has been the poolkeeper since he was twenty-two, when the previous poolkeeper died and someone had to take over the work. He is sixty now. His skin has the blue-white pallor of something that lives underwater, and his hands are never fully dry, and he speaks so rarely that some of the children believe he cannot.

He knows what lives in the pools. He will not say.

He feeds it—whatever it is—with portions of the pale root-vegetables, with the bodies of beetles that died in the night, with the occasional salamander that grew too old or too sick to cling to the walls. He lowers these offerings on a cord and feels the tug when they're taken. He monitors the water level, the color, the smell. He alone enters the pools, wading in up to his chest to clear blockages or retrieve objects that have fallen from above.

The others watch him with something between respect and unease. The pools are necessary—they moderate the temperature, they provide a reserve of water in dry seasons, they're *part* of the cistern in a way that cannot be undone. But they're also *other*. They don't obey the rules that govern the rest of the space. Things go into them and do not come out. The poolkeeper stares into their darkness for hours at a time, his face empty, and no one asks what he sees.

He was married once. His wife climbed to the surface twenty years ago and did not return. He does not speak of her. The offerings he lowers into the pools are sometimes accompanied by small carvings—figures that might be gods or ancestors or simply toys—and these, unlike the food, are not taken. They sink. They accumulate on the bottom, a layer of prayer that grows thicker each year.

---

**The structure of days here is not measured in light.**

There is no sun. There is no moon. There are only the rhythms of the body, the rhythms of the work, and the rhythms of the water. The drip-pipes slow when the surface is dry; they quicken when the rains come. The beetles breed in cycles tied to the compost-alcoves' heat. The birds nest, lay eggs, raise young, die, in patterns the climbers have learned to read.

And the people have adapted. They sleep in shifts, so that some are always awake to tend the lamps, to watch the water-channels, to listen for the particular sounds that mean *something is wrong*. A family might have one member working, one sleeping, one eating, one teaching a child, all at different stations throughout the cistern. They pass each other on the rope bridges and exchange words in the singing language, short phrases that carry information: *the eastern channel is slowing*, *the third beetle-house needs repair*, *Vessel-of-Vessels felt a crack in the upper tier*.

There are no leaders. There are no followers. There are only the ones who know things and the ones who need to learn them. Expertise flows through the community like water through the channels—always moving, always finding its level, never pooling in one place for long. The grandmother's knowledge of moisture is not more important than the twins' knowledge of beetles, is not more important than the child's growing knowledge of salamanders. Everything tends everything else. This is the only law.

---

**And then there is the one who is leaving.**

She is nineteen. Her name is **Carries-Far**, given because when she was small, she would transport things—clay jars, beetle-shells, harvested roots—from one end of the cistern to the other with inexhaustible patience. She was supposed to become a climber; her body was suited for it, and She-Who-Ascends had already begun training her. But something happened.

She went to the surface. She saw the dry bright. And she did not shiver with delighted horror.

She is standing now at the base of the smooth shaft, a pack on her back containing everything she might need for a life above: copper tools, fire-hardened wood, a ceramic lamp and enough rendered fat to burn for a month, seeds from the wall-gardens, dried meat from the birds. She has been standing here for three hours. The others pass her on the walkways, exchange words with her, do not try to stop her.

Leaving is not forbidden. Nothing here is forbidden.

But it is understood that leaving is a kind of death. The surface is *less*. The surface is *dry*. The surface does not tend you while you tend it. Carries-Far knows this. She has been taught it all her life. And yet she climbs the first few handholds, pauses, looks back at the only world she has ever known—the lamplight, the dripping, the beetle-click, the murmur of voice over voice—and then she continues climbing.

The grandmother watches her go. The twins pause in their work. The unnamed child does not understand what is happening, only knows that someone is ascending who will not return.

The cistern continues. The water drips. The beetles click. The thing in the deep pools takes its offerings and gives nothing back. And in the space Carries-Far leaves behind, someone else will grow to fill it, as someone has always grown to fill it, as someone always will.

The shell builds itself around them, and they build themselves into the shell, and the distinction between builder and built dissolves into the warm, close, ever-tended dark.
