## The Inhabitants of Mehel-Kuun

They are called the **Kuunim**, though they do not call themselves anything much at all. Names are for things that need distinguishing, and the Kuunim have lived here so long that the distinctions between them have softened like the edges of the stone, worn smooth by the patient accumulation of generations until it is difficult to say where one family ends and another begins. They share features not because they are all related—though they are all related, eventually, if you trace the lines back far enough through the genealogical records kept in the deep chambers—but because the city has shaped them the way it shapes everything. The moisture has worked on them. The minerals have accumulated in them. The slow patience of Mehel-Kuun has seeped into their bones.

Their skin has a particular quality that is difficult to describe to those who have not seen it. It is not pallor—they are not cave-dwellers, not people who have forgotten the sun. They live in light, diffuse as it is. But their skin has taken on something of the stone's character, a faint luminous sheen in certain angles, as if the mineral deposits that bloom on every surface have found their way into human tissue. The oldest among them—and the Kuunim live long, in the unhurried way of everything here—develop pale traceries along their forearms and temples, patterns that might be veins made visible or might be something else entirely, channels through which their bodies process what the water has given them. They do not consider this strange. They do not consider it at all.

---

**The Reckoners** are the closest thing to a governing class, though governance suggests an urgency that would be foreign to Mehel-Kuun. They are the keepers of the long calculations, the ones who track the mineral flows and predict—decades in advance, sometimes centuries—where the seepage will open new paths and where it will seal old ones shut. They live in the chambers carved deepest into the cliff-faces, surrounded by instruments that are half-scientific and half-devotional: great wheels of inscribed bronze that turn on hidden mechanisms, bowls that collect water and hold it until the dissolved minerals precipitate into patterns the Reckoners study like sacred texts. Their work appears to be mathematics, but it is mathematics conducted at the pace of geology, equations that take lifetimes to balance, proofs that their grandchildren's grandchildren will finally complete.

A Reckoner named **Osal Tev** has held her position for ninety-three years. She is not the eldest of them—that distinction belongs to someone who no longer leaves his chamber, who has become so integrated with his instruments that it is unclear where his calculations end and his breathing begins—but she is the one who still walks the terraces, still speaks to those outside the order, still maintains the connection between the deep work and the daily life of the city. Her eyes have the quality of the pools in the mineral gutters: clear and depthless and somehow reflective of more than what stands before them. When she speaks, it is with long pauses that are not hesitation but consideration, each word arriving when it is ready and not before. She is currently engaged in a calculation that began with her grandmother's grandmother, concerning the optimal placement of a drainage channel on the ninth terrace. The channel will not be dug for another two hundred years. The calculation must be perfect.

---

**The Condensers** work the machinery on the rooftops, the skeletal frames and dripping collectors that harvest moisture from the haze. They are younger than the Reckoners, though "younger" in Mehel-Kuun means they have only been at their work for two or three generations. Their knowledge is practical rather than theoretical: they understand the behavior of water in air, the temperatures at which the haze yields its burden, the subtle differences between moisture collected at dawn and moisture collected at dusk. They taste the water as it gathers. They can tell you, from a single drop on the tongue, which terrace it fell on and how long it took to accumulate.

Among them moves a figure called **Meht**, though whether this is a name or a title or simply a sound the others make when they need his attention is unclear. Meht is responsible for the collectors on the fifth terrace, a sprawling array of copper frames gone entirely green with verdigris, beautiful and functional and so old that the original design has been lost beneath centuries of modification and mineral growth. He climbs among them with the ease of someone who has made this landscape an extension of his body, fingers finding holds that should not exist, weight shifting in ways that suggest he has come to an understanding with gravity that the rest of us have not. His skin, where it is visible beneath the work-stained clothing, has taken on the same green tint as the copper. His sweat, they say, tastes of metal. He does not mind. He has been becoming part of the machinery for forty years now, and the machinery has been becoming part of him, and this seems to both of them—to Meht and to the collectors—like a reasonable arrangement.

---

The **Deep-Workers** are not spoken of directly, but their presence is felt in the pulse of the pumps, in the subsonic moan that makes your sinuses ache, in the occasional rumble that passes through the stone and is neither earthquake nor machinery but something in between. They tend the works below the lowest terrace, the flooded chambers and abandoned excavations that have been re-purposed for processes no one on the surface fully understands. They do not often emerge. When they do, they emerge at night, in the hours when the haze thickens and the terraces become difficult to navigate, and they move between buildings without speaking, collecting supplies, delivering containers filled with liquids that glow faintly and must not be spilled. Their eyes have adapted to the darkness below. They do not adjust well to the diffuse light of the surface. They wear wrappings, some of them, or hoods that shadow their faces, not out of secrecy but out of simple comfort.

One of them, a woman who has been down longer than most, has become a subject of quiet observation among those who notice such things. She comes up once a year, always on the same day—a day that corresponds to no calendar the surface-dwellers keep—and she walks the full length of the seventh terrace from one end to the other, touching nothing, speaking to no one, simply *walking*, as if she is measuring something, or remembering something, or performing some ritual whose purpose has been forgotten by everyone but her. Her name, if she still has one, is not known. The mineral traceries on her skin have grown more elaborate than any the surface-dwellers have seen. They form patterns that suggest the branching diagrams in the old texts, the forbidden geometries that appear in the mineral formations. She does not seem to notice them. She does not seem to notice anything except the path she walks, the stones beneath her feet, the way the haze moves around her as if uncertain whether she is an obstacle or a part of itself.

---

There are **children** in Mehel-Kuun, though fewer than you might expect, and they are not like children elsewhere. They play, in their way—games that involve patience, that reward stillness, that teach them to attend to slow changes. A common game involves placing a drop of mineral water on a flat stone and watching it evaporate, predicting where the residue will form, how the tiny crystal will grow, what shape it will take. The child who predicts most accurately wins, but winning is not celebrated and losing is not mourned. The point is the watching. The point is the learning to see.

A girl named **Suol** has become locally notable for her predictions, which are uncanny in their accuracy, which seem to draw on knowledge she should not have. She is eleven years old—young, by the standards of Mehel-Kuun—and she has already been noticed by the Reckoners, who watch her the way they watch their instruments, with patient attention and careful notation. She does not yet know what this attention means. She does not yet know that her grandmother's grandmother once displayed the same uncanny accuracy, that her line has been tracked in the genealogical records for six hundred years, that she is part of a calculation that began before her great-great-great-grandparents were born. She only knows that she likes the way the crystals grow, that she can almost *feel* where they will go, that the patterns they form are beautiful in a way she cannot explain to others her age.

She has begun to dream of the deep chambers. She has begun to wake with the taste of metal in her mouth.

---

The **Outsiders** who come to Mehel-Kuun are rare but not unknown. Traders, occasionally, bringing goods from the lowlands that the city cannot produce itself—certain fabrics, certain spices, news of the world beyond the haze. Scholars, sometimes, drawn by the old texts that mention the city, seeking to understand the processes that have been proceeding here since before their nations existed. Pilgrims, rarely, following faiths that have incorporated Mehel-Kuun into their cosmologies without ever quite understanding what it is.

The Kuunim receive them with neither welcome nor hostility. They provide lodging in the buildings near the terrace edges, in rooms that face outward rather than inward, that have not been carved into the cliff-face, that remain merely *constructed* rather than *grown*. They answer questions, after a fashion, in their slow and considered way, though their answers often raise more questions than they resolve. They do not prevent the outsiders from exploring, but neither do they guide them. They simply wait, with the patience of stone, for the outsiders to discover what they will discover.

Most outsiders leave after a few weeks, unsettled by something they cannot name. Some stay longer, drawn into the rhythm of the place, learning to move slowly, to think in longer timescales, to drink the water that tastes of nothing and affects them in ways they do not immediately notice. A few—a very few—do not leave at all. They are absorbed into the city the way the mineral deposits are absorbed into the stone, gradually, over years, becoming Kuunim in all the ways that matter even if they were not born to it.

One such man arrived thirty years ago, a scholar from the lowlands seeking to document the old machinery. He documented for a year, then two, then five. His notes became less frequent. His letters home stopped entirely. Now he works among the Condensers, having forgotten—or perhaps *released*—whatever urgencies drove him here. His skin has begun to develop the faint luminous sheen. He drinks the water without thinking. He dreams, sometimes, of the equations that the Reckoners balance, and in his dreams the equations make perfect sense, and he wakes with a feeling of peace that he cannot explain.

His name, he thinks, was Toben. He is not certain anymore. Names are for things that need distinguishing.

---

And beneath them all, below the terraces and the chambers and the deep works, in places where the water has carved spaces too vast to map and too flooded to explore, something else lives. Or does not live, exactly. Or lives in ways that the word "living" was not designed to describe.

The Kuunim do not speak of it. They do not name it. But they orient their buildings around it, the way flowers orient around a sun. They conduct their calculations in reference to it, the way sailors navigate by stars. They drink the water that has passed through it, that has dissolved something of it, that carries it up through the terraces in concentrations too small to measure but too significant to ignore.

It is patient. It is older than the city. It is perhaps older than the mountain.

It does not think, in any way that thinking can be understood. But it *processes*. It *works*. It conducts some slow chemistry that has been proceeding since the stone was young, and the Kuunim have built their lives around the rhythms of its processing, have become—in some way they do not articulate and perhaps do not understand—participants in its work.

The Deep-Workers tend it, in their way. The Reckoners calculate its needs. The Condensers feed it moisture. The children grow into its service without ever knowing they are serving.

And the city continues. And the water seeps. And the mineral deposits grow in patterns that are almost meaningful, almost language, almost a message from something that has been trying to speak for longer than human ears have existed to hear it.

The Kuunim listen. They have been listening for six hundred years.

They are learning, slowly, to understand.
