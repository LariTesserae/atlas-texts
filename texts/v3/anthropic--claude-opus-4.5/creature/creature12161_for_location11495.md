## The Sessile Ones

They were not always attached.

The elders remember swimming—or rather, the elders carry within their tissues the chemical memory of swimming, the compressed genetic record of a time when their ancestors moved freely through the street-canyons, hunting the ribbon-fish, competing for territory in the upper floors. That was seventeen generations ago, perhaps eighteen. The Sessile Ones do not count time the way mobile creatures do. They count it in layers, in the thickness of the calcium they have laid down around themselves, in the depth of the sediment that has accumulated at the bases of their stalks.

They grow from the walls of the buildings, clustered most densely on the southern faces where the thermal current runs strongest. Each one begins as a drifting larva no larger than a grain of sand, carried on the current until some chemical signal—the taste of the right mineral, the vibration frequency of a suitable surface—triggers settlement. The larva attaches, and that attachment is permanent. It will never move again. Instead, it will grow.

An adult Sessile One resembles nothing so much as a flower made of flesh andite, a meter-wide bloom of feeding tentacles surrounding a central disc that pulses with slow bioluminescence. The tentacles are feathered, fractal, each branch subdividing into smaller branches until the finest filaments are too thin to see, capable of filtering particles from the water that other creatures cannot even detect. They feed continuously, the tentacles creating their own micro-current, drawing water across sensory surfaces that taste everything the current carries.

But the visible portion is only a small part of what they are.

Beneath the feeding bloom, anchored into the wall, the body extends downward in a tapering stalk that penetrates the building's structure, threading through cracks in the coral, following the paths of ancient mortar, spreading root-like extensions through the interior spaces. A single large adult might have infiltrated three floors of a building, its tissue running through walls and ceilings like pale veins through stone. They do not consume the building—they incorporate it, using its structure as scaffolding for their own growth, reinforcing it even as they hollow it out.

The stalks of neighboring individuals meet and fuse. What appears to be a colony of separate creatures is often a single organism, a network of connected tissue sharing nutrients, sharing chemical signals, sharing what might be called thought if thought is understood as the slow propagation of molecular changes through living matter. A wall covered in feeding blooms might be one being. A whole building might be one being. There are those who believe that the Sessile Ones of the entire southern district have achieved connection, that they constitute a single distributed mind spread across six city blocks.

If so, it is a mind that thinks in seasons rather than seconds.

The Sessile Ones communicate through chemistry, releasing complex molecules into the current that carry information about food availability, threat presence, reproductive readiness. A warning signal released by one individual propagates through the network of fused tissue, then disperses into the water to reach the unfused colonies on neighboring buildings. Within an hour, every Sessile One in a three-block radius knows that the whale-thing in the plaza has begun to stir. They cannot flee—they cannot do anything—but they know.

There is a question, debated among those who study them, about whether this constitutes suffering: to be aware of danger, to have no means of escape, to simply wait and know. The Sessile Ones themselves, if they could be asked, might not understand the question. They experience the approach of predation the way they experience everything else: as a change in chemistry, a shift in probability, a factor to be incorporated into the slow calculation of resource allocation. The tentacles that might be eaten are already preparing to be sacrificed, diverting nutrients away from themselves and toward the protected core. If the attack comes, the bloom will be lost and the stalk will survive, regenerating over months what was destroyed in seconds.

They reproduce twice per year, when the thermal currents shift and some quality of the water—temperature, mineral content, the accumulated pheromones of the entire population—reaches a threshold. The spawning is synchronized across the district, a slow-motion explosion of gametes that clouds the water for days. The larvae drift upward, caught in currents that carry them away from their parents, away from the southern walls, out into the broader city where most of them will die and a few will find purchase on some distant building and begin the cycle again.

The Sessile Ones have been here for as long as anyone can remember, which in the Drowned Warrens means nothing. They were here before the whale-thing claimed the plaza. They were here before the eels colonized the interior spaces. Their calcium deposits are part of the reef now, indistinguishable from coral, and when they die—when a whole colony dies, which happens rarely, catastrophically, when disease moves through the fused network—the shapes they leave behind become habitat for other creatures, hollows and channels and sheltered spaces that the mobile fauna treat as natural features of the cityscape.

They are aware of you as you pass. The feeding tentacles orient slightly toward the disturbance, tasting the water you have displaced, analyzing the chemicals your body sheds. They note your size, your probable metabolism, your apparent direction of travel. This information propagates through the network, becomes part of the colony's model of its environment, and is retained for a span of time that might be hours or might be decades—the Sessile Ones do not distinguish between memory and identity in the way that creatures with brains do.

You are now part of them, in a sense. A pattern in their chemistry. A factor in their calculation. You will be remembered as long as the memory remains useful, and then you will be forgotten, which is not death but simply the reallocation of resources toward more pressing concerns.

They do not wish you well. They do not wish you ill. They simply note your passage and continue feeding, their feathered tentacles combing the darkness for the invisible richness it contains.

## The Processionals

Once every few months, though never on a predictable schedule, they come through.

You hear them before anything else—a rhythmic disturbance in the water, a synchronized pulse that resonates through the buildings and sets the tube worms retracting along the canyon floors. The Sessile Ones taste the chemical bow-wave and release their warning signals, and for an hour before the Processionals arrive, the entire district enters a state of tense stillness, everything that can hide hiding, everything that cannot hide preparing to endure.

They emerge from the deeper darkness beyond the thermal district, swimming in formation. There are between thirty and sixty of them in a typical group—the counts vary, and it's unclear whether this reflects actual population changes or simply the difficulty of counting creatures that move in such perfect coordination that they blur together into a single flowing shape.

Each individual is perhaps two meters long, torpedo-shaped, with four lateral fins that beat in unison with its neighbors. They are not fish, not mammals, not anything with a clear analog in the sunlit world. Their bodies are segmented, armored in overlapping plates of chitin or something like chitin, and their heads—if they can be called heads—are dominated by a circular mouth lined with rasping teeth that rotate slowly, continuously, even when the creature is not feeding. The mouth is surrounded by a ring of sensory tendrils, each one tipped with a structure that glows faint blue: bioluminescent lures, perhaps, or simply eyes adapted to wavelengths nothing else here can see.

They swim through the street-canyons in a single file that undulates like a serpent, each individual maintaining exactly the same distance from the one ahead, their fin-beats synchronized so precisely that they move as one organism. The formation never breaks. When they turn a corner, the turn propagates down the line in a wave, each creature following the exact path of its predecessor. When they encounter an obstacle—a collapsed building, the bulk of the whale-thing—they flow around it without pause, the line splitting and rejoining with mechanical precision.

The purpose of their procession is unclear. They do not appear to be hunting—the rasping mouths remain idle, the creatures they pass are ignored. They do not appear to be migrating—they circle through the same districts repeatedly, following routes that seem to have no destination. They do not appear to be spawning, displaying, or engaging in any behavior that suggests social purpose.

They simply process. They have always processed. The oldest Sessile Ones carry chemical memories of Processionals passing when their grandparent-colonies were still larvae.

Some who have observed them believe they are feeding in a way too subtle to detect—filtering microorganisms from the water, perhaps, or absorbing dissolved nutrients through their skin. Others believe they are mapping, that the synchronized movement is a form of distributed sensing, the formation functioning as a single vast organ of perception that builds a model of the city's changing topology. A few believe they are sleeping, that the procession is a form of rest for creatures that cannot afford to stop moving, the synchronization allowing each individual to enter a reduced state while the group-mind maintains course and cohesion.

The Processionals themselves offer no answers. They do not communicate, or if they do, they do so in frequencies or media that nothing else in the Warrens can detect. They do not respond to other creatures except to avoid collision. They do not vary their behavior, do not show curiosity, do not deviate from their endless circling for any reason that observation has revealed.

What is known: they are ancient. The wear patterns on their armor plates suggest individual lifespans measured in centuries. They do not appear to reproduce—no juvenile Processionals have ever been observed, no mating behavior, no eggs or larvae. The population remains stable because nothing kills them. Their armor is too thick, their awareness too acute, their synchronized movement too unpredictable for any predator to successfully attack. The whale-thing in the plaza, the largest predator in this district, does not even try. It simply waits until they pass, its bulk pressed against the plaza floor, its breathing stilled.

When the Processionals move through a district, other behaviors pause. The eels withdraw into their window-holes. The ribbon-fish descend to the silt-buried streets and go still. The filter-feeders retract. Even the bacterial mats seem to dim, their glow dampened, as if the entire ecosystem is holding its breath until the disturbance passes.

And then they are gone, the line of blue-lit mouths disappearing around a corner, the rhythmic pulse of their passage fading into the thermal current, and slowly, cautiously, life resumes. The tube worms extend their crowns. The Sessile Ones resume their feeding. The whale-thing shifts in its den, and the neighborhood reorganizes itself around the movement, and everything continues as if the Processionals had never come.

Except that they will come again. They always come again. And in the meantime, they are somewhere else in the city, following their incomprehensible routes through canyons and corridors, past buildings that were old before they were born, if they were ever born at all.

## The Congregation of the Plaza

The whale-thing has a name for itself, a sound it makes in the deepest register of its voice, below the threshold of what most creatures can hear. The sound propagates through the stone of the plaza, through the foundations of the surrounding buildings, through the compressed sediment of the streets, and other whale-things—if there are other whale-things, somewhere in the distant reaches of the city—might hear it and know: this territory is claimed.

But the whale-thing is not alone in the plaza. Nothing in the Drowned Warrens is ever truly alone.

The whale-thing's body has become an ecosystem. Its skin—thick, gray, furrowed with age and scarring—hosts a community as complex as any wall of the city. Barnacle-analogues cluster in the folds of its flesh, their feeding appendages extended into the current the whale-thing's breathing creates. Small crustaceans graze on the film of bacteria and dead cells that accumulates between the barnacles. Larger crustaceans prey on the smaller ones. Something like a sea spider, all legs and proboscis, picks its way across the whale-thing's flank, piercing the skin to drink the blood beneath—a parasite the whale-thing tolerates because removing it would require movement, and movement is expensive.

The whale-thing moves perhaps once per day, shifting position in its den to optimize its contact with the thermal seep that warms the plaza's eastern corner. Each movement is an event. The creatures that live on its skin brace themselves or flee. The creatures that live near its skin—the fish that hover in the current of its exhalations, the scavengers that wait for scraps from its infrequent meals—scatter and regroup. The Sessile Ones on the surrounding walls taste the chemical surge and prepare for the turbulence. The silt on the plaza floor billows up in clouds that take hours to settle.

And then the whale-thing is still again, and the community reconstitutes itself, and everything continues.

The whale-thing feeds rarely. When it does feed, the entire district knows. The chemical signal of its hunger—a complex molecule that triggers flight responses in everything small enough to flee—spreads through the water like ink. The whale-thing rises from its den, its bulk displacing water in waves that slap against the surrounding buildings, and it swims. Not far. Not fast. But with a terrible inevitability, its mouth opening to reveal a cavern lined with baleen-analogues, structures that filter enormous volumes of water and trap everything above a certain size.

It takes what the current provides. It does not chase. It does not select. It simply opens itself to the water and accepts what enters, and what enters is everything that failed to flee in time: fish, crustaceans, larvae, the occasional eel that ventured too far from shelter, the accumulated debris of the water column's endless rain. The whale-thing feeds for perhaps an hour, and then it returns to the plaza, and then it is still again, digesting, and the community reconstitutes itself around it once more.

The creatures that share the plaza have adapted to this rhythm. The small fish that hover near the whale-thing's mouth know to scatter when the hunger-signal comes. The scavengers that follow the whale-thing have learned to read the subtle shifts in its breathing that precede movement. The parasites that live on its skin have evolved to grip tight when the body beneath them begins to stir.

And there are others. Creatures that exist nowhere else in the Warrens, that have evolved specifically to exploit the unique conditions the whale-thing creates.

There are the cleaners: small, quick, fearless creatures that dart into the whale-thing's mouth between feedings to pick scraps from between the baleen-analogues. The whale-thing tolerates them, perhaps benefits from them—the scraps would otherwise rot, would foul the filtering structures, would reduce feeding efficiency. The cleaners are bright orange, the only splash of warm color in the plaza's gray-blue gloom, and they move in coordinated groups that suggest communication, cooperation, a social structure built around the exploitation of a single resource.

There are the followers: ribbon-fish that have given up independent hunting to trail in the whale-thing's wake, feeding on the organisms stirred up by its movement. They are smaller than their free-swimming relatives, paler, their sensory organs attuned to the whale-thing's vibrations rather than the vibrations of prey. When the whale-thing is still, they are still. When the whale-thing moves, they move. They have outsourced their hunting to a creature that does not know they exist.

There is the other thing.

No one is certain what the other thing is. It lives in the whale-thing's shadow, literally—in the lightless space beneath the whale-thing's belly where the thermal seep cannot reach and the bioluminescence cannot penetrate. It is large, perhaps half the whale-thing's size, and it is patient, and it waits.

The whale-thing knows it is there. The whale-thing has always known it is there. The chemical signature of the other thing is part of the plaza's baseline, as familiar as the taste of the thermal seep or the vibration of the Processionals passing in the distance. The whale-thing does not try to drive it away. The other thing does not try to attack.

They have an arrangement. The nature of the arrangement is unclear. Perhaps the other thing feeds on the whale-thing's waste, processing what the larger creature's digestion cannot extract. Perhaps the other thing provides some service—protection from parasites, perhaps, or warning of dangers the whale-thing's senses cannot detect. Perhaps there is no arrangement at all, just two large creatures occupying the same space because neither has the energy to contest the other's presence.

The other thing does not rise when the whale-thing feeds. It does not follow when the whale-thing moves. It simply waits in the darkness beneath, and whatever it is waiting for has not yet arrived.

The plaza is a kingdom, and the whale-thing is its king, and the king does not rule so much as exist, and the kingdom organizes itself around the fact of that existence, and the arrangement continues because it has always continued, because nothing has yet arrived to disrupt it, because the Drowned Warrens reward patience above all else, and the whale-thing is very, very patient.

## The Hermit Engineers

They are small. That is the first thing to understand about them—small enough that you might not notice one unless it moved, small enough to fit in the hollow of your palm, small enough that the predators of the Warrens largely ignore them as not worth the energy of capture.

They are crustaceans, or something like crustaceans: segmented bodies, multiple limbs, eyes on stalks, antennae that taste the water with every flicker. They are pale, almost translucent, their internal organs visible as shadows through their carapace. They are unremarkable in every way except one.

They build.

The Hermit Engineers do not grow their own shells. Instead, they construct them from salvage: fragments of coral, chips of stone, pieces of ancient material that might have been ceramic or glass or something else entirely, scavenged from the city's endless supply of debris. Each engineer carries its construction on its back, a mobile shelter that it adds to continuously, shaping and refining, sealing gaps with secreted adhesive, reinforcing weak points with carefully selected fragments.

No two shelters are alike. Some are rough aggregations, purely functional, assembled from whatever was nearest at hand. Others are elaborate structures, architectural in their complexity, with multiple chambers and reinforced walls and apertures positioned to direct water flow across the engineer's gills. The variation suggests individual preference, aesthetic sensibility, or perhaps just the accumulated legacy of different construction histories—each shelter growing from the one before it, shaped by the circumstances of the engineer's life.

They live in the walls. The Sessile Ones tolerate them, or perhaps do not notice them—the engineers are too small to register as threats, too mobile to serve as food, too quick to be caught by the slow-pulsing feeding tentacles. They travel through the gaps in the coral, the spaces between encrusting organisms, the channels that were once mortar joints and are now highways for creatures small enough to use them. They are everywhere in the southern district, hundreds of them, thousands of them, each one carrying its shelter, each one building.

But they do not only build for themselves.

The engineers maintain the city. Not intentionally, not with purpose or plan, but as a byproduct of their endless search for construction material. They clear debris from channels that would otherwise clog. They fill cracks that would otherwise widen. They shore up structures that would otherwise collapse. They do this because the debris is useful, because the cracks are opportunities, because the collapsing structures provide the raw material for shelters—but the effect is the same. The city persists because the engineers are always working.

They communicate through vibration: tapping their limbs against their shelters in patterns that carry meaning. The patterns are complex, layered, capable of encoding information about food sources, threats, productive salvage sites. An engineer that finds a rich deposit of building material will signal, and other engineers will converge, and for a few hours the site will swarm with activity as each one takes what it needs. Then they disperse, returning to their solitary wandering, their endless construction, their patient maintenance of a city that does not know it is being maintained.

The engineers are not intelligent, not in any way that implies planning or foresight. They do not know that their activities preserve the structures around them. They do not understand that the city would decay without their intervention, that the coral would eventually overwhelm the architecture, that the streets would fill with sediment, that the buildings would crumble and become unrecognizable. They simply do what they have always done, because doing it has always worked, because the engineers that built survived and the engineers that did not build did not.

But the effect is there. The city endures. The streets remain streets, the buildings remain buildings, the structure that makes the Warrens what they are persists across centuries because something small and patient is always working to preserve it.

There is a theory—unprovable, perhaps even unthinkable—that the engineers are not native to the Warrens. That they were brought here, long ago, by whatever intelligence built the city in the first place. That they are tools, living tools, designed to maintain what their creators could not maintain themselves. That the city endures not by accident but by design, a design so old and so patient that it has become indistinguishable from ecology.

If true, the engineers do not know it. They build because building is what they do. They maintain because maintenance is a side effect of building. They carry their shelters on their backs and tap their signals through the water and live their small lives in the gaps of a city that was ancient before their species existed.

And the city, in return, provides them with endless material, endless opportunity, endless scope for the only activity that gives their lives meaning. It is an arrangement. It is perhaps the only arrangement that has ever truly worked.

## The Memory Keepers

In the deepest part of the thermal district, where the seep runs hottest and the water shimmers with dissolved minerals, there is a building that the other creatures avoid.

It is not obviously different from its neighbors. The same coral encrusts its walls, the same tube worms cluster at its base, the same Sessile Ones spread their feeding blooms across its southern face. The Hermit Engineers travel through its structure, salvaging and building as they do everywhere else. The Processionals include it in their incomprehensible routes. Nothing about the building announces that it is special.

But the creatures that live in the Warrens have learned to avoid it. The ribbon-fish do not hunt in its shadow. The eels do not den in its window-holes. Even the whale-thing, when it rises to feed, curves its path away from the building's entrance, as if the displacement of water near that dark doorway carries some warning it prefers not to test.

The Memory Keepers live inside.

They are cephalopods—or something that functions like cephalopods, soft-bodied creatures with multiple limbs and large eyes and the ability to change their skin's color and texture with a thought. They are larger than the engineers, smaller than the ribbon-fish, and they move with a fluid grace that makes them seem almost boneless, their limbs trailing behind them like ribbons as they flow through the building's interior spaces.

The building was a library, once. Or an archive. Or something that served a similar function—a place where information was stored, where records were kept, where the city's memory was preserved against the erosion of time. The Memory Keepers found it long ago, when they were still a young species, and they claimed it, and they have held it ever since.

The original records are gone. Whatever medium they were stored on has long since dissolved, degraded, been consumed by bacteria or incorporated into coral. But the Memory Keepers remember.

They remember through their skin.

The patterns they display—the shifting colors, the pulsing textures, the complex geometries that flow across their bodies—are not random. They are information, encoded in a form that cephalopod eyes can read and cephalopod brains can interpret. The Memory Keepers have developed a language of display, a visual vocabulary capable of encoding and transmitting knowledge across generations.

An adult Memory Keeper's skin is a library. The patterns it carries include the accumulated knowledge of its ancestors: the locations of thermal seeps throughout the city, the territories of predators, the timing of the Processionals' routes, the chemical signatures of toxic organisms, the history of the whale-thing's movements over the past century. When Memory Keepers meet, they display to each other, sharing information, updating their records, adding new observations to the collective store.

When a Memory Keeper dies, its patterns die with it—unless it has had time to transmit them. Young Memory Keepers spend years in the building, learning to read the displays of their elders, copying the patterns onto their own skin, becoming living repositories of everything their species has learned. The process is slow, imperfect, subject to copying errors and individual interpretation. But it works. The Memory Keepers know more about the Warrens than any other species, remember things that happened before any living creature was born.

They know that the Processionals have been following the same routes for at least three hundred years, and that the routes have changed twice in that time, both changes corresponding to major architectural collapses.

They know that the whale-thing in the plaza is not the first whale-thing, that there have been at least four, and that the transitions between them were violent and brief.

They know that the thermal seeps are slowly shifting, that the hot water that sustains the southern district will eventually migrate elsewhere, that in a century or two this neighborhood will cool and the ecosystem will collapse and something new will emerge in a different part of the city.

They know these things because they remember them. Not because they were there, but because their ancestors were there, and their ancestors transmitted the patterns, and the patterns have been copied and copied and copied until they reached the present day.

The other creatures avoid the Memory Keepers' building because the Memory Keepers are territorial, aggressive, willing to defend their archive with the sharp beaks hidden at the center of their limbs. But they also avoid it because of something harder to name—a sense, perhaps, that the building contains something dangerous. Not physically dangerous. Informationally dangerous. The Memory Keepers know things, and knowledge in the Warrens is not neutral. Knowledge is power. Knowledge is survival. And knowledge hoarded is knowledge that could be used against you.

The Memory Keepers do not share freely. They trade. They negotiate. They exact payment in the form of new observations, new data points to add to their archive. A creature that wants to know where the thermal seeps will shift must offer something in return: information about a part of the city the Memory Keepers cannot reach, or a service performed, or simply time spent displaying while the Memory Keepers copy what they see.

The archive grows. The patterns multiply. And the Memory Keepers continue their work, recording and transmitting, preserving the knowledge of a city that has long since forgotten what it was, ensuring that at least something will be remembered when everything else has been lost.

## The Substrate

Beneath everything, beneath the streets and the foundations and the geological layers of compressed sediment, there is something else.

It is not alive in any conventional sense. It does not move, does not feed, does not reproduce. It has no organs, no metabolism, no behavior that observation can detect. It is simply there, a presence in the stone, a pattern in the rock that underlies the city.

The Hermit Engineers know about it. They do not understand it, but they know it. When they tunnel deep enough, when their salvage operations carry them below the level of the foundations, they encounter a boundary—a place where the stone changes, where the texture becomes wrong, where their instincts scream at them to turn back. Most of them do. The ones that don't are never seen again.

The Memory Keepers have records of the substrate. Ancient patterns, copied from generation to generation, warn that the city's true foundation is not stone but something stranger, something that was here before the buildings, before the water, before anything that swims or grows or feeds. The patterns do not explain what it is. They simply record its presence and counsel avoidance.

The Sessile Ones, whose roots penetrate deeper than any other organism's, have grown around it. Their tissue approaches the boundary but does not cross it. The chemical signals that guide their growth turn them away, redirecting their expansion laterally, keeping them in the stone that is merely stone. The oldest Sessile colonies carry traces of what their deepest roots have tasted: something that is not mineral, not organic, not anything their chemistry can categorize.

The whale-thing in the plaza sleeps above the substrate's thickest concentration. Perhaps this is coincidence. Perhaps the thermal seep that warms the plaza originates in the substrate, in whatever process sustains it. Perhaps the whale-thing knows something that no surface-dwelling creature can know, and chooses its den accordingly. The other thing that waits in the whale-thing's shadow seems to press itself downward, as if it could push through the sediment and reach what lies beneath. It has been waiting for a very long time.

The Processionals' routes, when mapped over sufficient time, trace a pattern. The Memory Keepers noticed this long ago and have been refining their understanding ever since. The routes do not follow the streets, not exactly. They follow something else, something beneath the streets, some topology that is not visible from above but that the Processionals can sense and navigate by. The pattern looks almost deliberate, almost meaningful, as if the Processionals are tracing a design that was laid down before the city was built.

There are those who believe the substrate is a machine. A vast and ancient mechanism, buried so deep that the city is just its uppermost layer, running so slowly that its operation appears static, serving a purpose that nothing living can comprehend. The evidence for this is thin: the patterns the Processionals trace, the boundary the engineers encounter, the chemical signatures the Sessile Ones taste. But the alternative—that the substrate is simply strange geology, unusual but ultimately explicable—does not satisfy. The Warrens are too organized. The ecology is too stable. Something is maintaining the system, and the substrate is the only candidate that makes sense.

If the substrate is a machine, it has no interface. No way to communicate, no way to receive instructions, no way to be modified or controlled. It simply does what it does, has always done what it does, will continue doing what it does until something changes that is beyond the power of any creature in the Warrens to change.

The creatures of the Warrens live above the substrate the way insects live above the earth that contains their burrows: aware of its presence, dependent on its stability, utterly incapable of understanding what it truly is. They build their lives in the space the substrate provides, organize their ecologies around the conditions it creates, and never ask what purpose their existence serves from the substrate's perspective, if indeed it has a perspective at all.

Perhaps it is dreaming. Perhaps the Warrens are its dream—a slow, mineral dream of life and complexity and endless variation, played out in the medium of water and flesh over timescales too vast for any organic mind to grasp. Perhaps when the dream ends, the Warrens will end with it, and something else will take their place, some new dream emerging from the ancient substrate to fill the space the old dream occupied.

But for now, the dream continues. The creatures live and feed and reproduce and die. The city persists, maintained by engineers who do not know they are maintaining it, recorded by Memory Keepers who do not know what their records mean. The whale-thing breathes in its plaza. The Sessile Ones feed on the current. The Processionals trace their incomprehensible routes.

And beneath them all, patient and silent and utterly unknown, the substrate endures.
