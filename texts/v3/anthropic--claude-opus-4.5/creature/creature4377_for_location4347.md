## The Congregation of the Cistern

---

**Mother Filament** came here eleven years ago, following a fever-dream and a map she'd drawn in her sleep. She was a telegraph operator then, her fingertips still callused from the key, her ears still tuned to the rhythms of dots and dashes. Now she lives in the alcove opposite the Alderman's, in a nest she's woven from pipe-ivy stems and salvaged wool from the mills above. The ivy doesn't eat her shelter the way it eats metal—she's learned which stems are dormant, which can be cut without waking the whole network. Her hair has grown colorless down here, not white but *absent*, and her eyes have developed a second set of lids, thin and nictitating, that she doesn't remember growing.

She tends the eels. Not controls them—no one controls them—but tends. She's learned that if she hums at a particular frequency, just below the lowest note the tramway sings, the schools will part around her. She's learned that they respond to illness, clustering around infection, their light shifting toward the purple-bruise end when they taste sickness in the water. Twice she's used them to diagnose her own fevers before she felt them. Once she watched them save a man's life—a surface-dweller who'd fallen through a collapsed grate, his leg broken and going septic. The eels swarmed the wound for three days, their light so bright it hurt, and when they dispersed the flesh had sealed itself wrong but clean. He went back up. He never returned. She didn't expect him to.

She believes the eels are a language being spoken by something that has no mouth. She's filled nineteen notebooks with transcriptions of their schooling patterns, converting their movements into symbols she invented herself. The notebooks are wrapped in oilcloth and stored in a crevice where the water never reaches. She doesn't know what the language says. She suspects it isn't speaking *to* her. But she transcribes it anyway, the way a monk might copy scriptures in a tongue they'll never understand, trusting that the act of preservation is itself a form of prayer.

---

**The Cartographer** has no other name anyone remembers, possibly including himself.

He arrived four years ago with surveying equipment that shouldn't have fit through the drainage grate—yet here it is, set up on a platform of salvaged bricks he's built in the northwest corner, just high enough to stay dry during the highest floods. He measures things. The fissure's width, yes, but also the exact distance between columns (it changes; he's documented 347 variations), the temperature differential between the spring-water and the standing water (erratic, following a pattern that almost matches lunar cycles but doesn't), the precise frequency of the tramway's singing (drifting downward by .003 hertz per year, as if the rails are slowly relaxing into a lower key).

He speaks to no one except Mother Filament, and then only to share data. His theory—which he's never stated outright but which fills the margins of his survey-sheets—is that the cistern is a mechanism. Not metaphorically. Literally. The columns are pistons. The water is hydraulic fluid. The fissure is an intake valve. The entire chamber is *doing something*, performing some function that the city above requires without knowing it requires it. He believes if he can complete his measurements, he'll understand what.

He eats the pale crabs raw, cracking their too-many-jointed legs with his teeth. He drinks the spring-water directly, dipping his cupped hands into the fissure despite the way it makes the eels flicker and the listeners pulse faster. He says it tastes like pennies and absolution. He's developing a cough that won't clear, wet and deep, and the eels have started to orbit him at night, their light shifting toward purple.

---

**The sisters are not sisters.**

They call themselves that because they arrived on the same night, through the same grate, drawn by the same sound that neither of them can describe except as "the opposite of an echo." There are three of them. They live in the water itself, spending more time submerged than anyone else who dwells here, their bodies adapted in ways that suggest the adaptation was always latent, waiting for the right conditions.

The first sister has gills now—or something like gills—fine slits along her ribs that flutter when she surfaces. She was a midwife before. She says the water here reminds her of amniotic fluid, not in composition but in *intention*: water that is waiting for something to be born into it.

The second sister has lost the ability to thermoregulate; her body runs at whatever temperature surrounds her. In the cold spring-water, she slows to something like hibernation, her heartbeat dropping to six beats per minute, her thoughts stretching into long ribbons that take hours to complete. She says she's learning to think the way the chamber thinks: in geological time, in the patient tempo of rust and growth.

The third sister doesn't speak anymore. She communicates in a system of gestures she's developed with the listeners, her hands moving in patterns that make their throat-pulsing synchronize with her movements. She believes the listeners are recording *her* now, that she's become part of whatever archive they're building. She seems happy about this. Her eyes have gone the same translucent shade as theirs.

They share a sleeping space in a section of pipe large enough to curl inside, waterlogged but somehow warm. They care for each other with an intimacy that suggests years of practice, though none of them knew the others before they came here. Mother Filament has noted that the eels behave differently around them—not avoiding, not swarming, but *escorting*, as if the sisters are dignitaries from a nation the eels recognize.

---

**Then there is the child.**

No one knows when she arrived. No one saw her come. She was simply *present* one day, sitting on the edge of the fissure with her feet dangling into the cold upwelling, humming along with the spring's pre-linguistic murmur as if she'd been born knowing the tune.

She appears to be seven or eight. She does not age. The Cartographer has been measuring her height for three years and it has not changed by a millimeter. Her skin has the same luminescent quality as the eels, a faint amber glow visible only in total darkness, which comes rarely but comes. She calls herself Silta, a word that also means "bridge" in a language no one in the cistern speaks, though the second sister recognized it from a dream.

Silta moves through the space as if she owns it—which perhaps she does. The pipe-ivy curls away from her footsteps. The Alderman, who watches everything with unblinking suspicion, *closes its eyes* when she passes, the only time it has ever been seen to do so. The listeners turn their bodies to follow her but do not record her; their throats go still in her presence, as if her sounds are not meant to be archived.

She talks to the spring. Hours at a time, lying on her stomach with her face inches from the fissure, whispering into the rush and murmur, pausing as if listening to responses. She has never told anyone what she discusses. When Mother Filament asked, Silta looked at her with an expression that was not unkind but was very, very old, and said: "It's not ready to meet you yet. It's still deciding what shape to wear."

---

**The visitors come irregularly, unpredictably, from above.**

Some fall through collapsed grates and are rescued by the sisters, given water and warmth and guidance back to the surface. Most of these never return. A few do—drawn back by something they can't name, a sense of incompleteness, a feeling that they left something vital in the wet dark below the city. These are the ones who might, eventually, stay.

Others come deliberately, having heard rumors. A university researcher who wanted to study the eels; she lasted two weeks before the sound of the spring began appearing in her dreams and she fled, leaving behind a notebook full of observations that Mother Filament added to her archive. A city engineer investigating reports of foundation instability; he measured, mapped, filed a report recommending the entire chamber be sealed with concrete, and was found three days later sitting in the shallow water, weeping, unable to explain why he'd returned. He stayed for six months before the cough took him. The Cartographer buried him in a dry crevice near the southern wall. The pipe-ivy has almost reached the grave.

And once—only once—a woman came who was not a woman.

She descended the seventeen rungs and stood at the ladder's base for a long moment, her body flickering in the eel-light like a projection on an unstable surface. She walked to the fissure, knelt, and placed her palm flat against the upwelling water. The spring *stopped*. For thirty-seven seconds—the Cartographer counted—the murmur ceased, the cold current stilled, and the entire cistern held its breath.

Then she stood, nodded once, and climbed back up without speaking to anyone.

The spring resumed. The eels resumed. But something had changed. Mother Filament says the transcription patterns shifted that night, a new symbol appearing in the schooling configurations that she hadn't seen before. The Cartographer says the fissure began widening faster after that visit, its growth rate doubling. Silta says nothing about it at all, but she's started spending more time at the spring's edge, her whispers more urgent, her pauses longer, as if the conversation has entered a new phase.

---

**They live together without rules, without hierarchy, without anything resembling a formal arrangement.**

Mother Filament tends and transcribes. The Cartographer measures and coughs. The sisters swim and slow and gesture. Silta whispers to something that is not ready to be met. The pale crabs scuttle. The moths drink from the pipe-ivy. The Alderman watches.

And the eels—always the eels—paint their incomprehensible grammar on the walls, lighting the chamber enough to see by, narrating in frequencies just below understanding the story of a place the city forgot to finish building, where a spring that would not be governed has gathered around itself a congregation of those who needed, for various reasons, to be somewhere the surface world could not follow.

They are waiting for something. They might not know what. The fissure widens. The pipe-ivy grows. The listeners record. Above, the tramway sings its slowly descending note, and the mills process wool into cloth, and the city goes about its business, unaware that seventeen rungs down, in a wet and breathing darkness, its forgotten children are keeping vigil over a birth that has been coming for a very long time.

---

**If you stay—and you might stay—they will not welcome you or reject you.**

They will simply make room. Mother Filament will offer you a sip of spring-water, tasting of pennies and absolution, and watch your face to see how you receive it. The sisters will brush past you in the shallows, their adapted bodies cool and strange, their attention already elsewhere. The Cartographer will measure you once—height, weight, the frequency of your breathing—and add you to his records without comment. Silta will look at you with ancient eyes and say nothing at all, or everything.

And in time, you will begin to change.

Not in ways you'll notice at first. A sensitivity to the eels' light. A rhythm in your breathing that matches the spring's murmur. A loosening of your need for the world above, gradual as rust, certain as the pipe-ivy's slow digestion of iron into tendril, of architecture into growth.

The cistern is patient. It has been becoming for a long time. It can wait for you to become with it.
