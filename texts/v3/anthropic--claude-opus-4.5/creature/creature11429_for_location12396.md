# The Menders

They do not remember what they were building.

This is the central fact of their existence, the truth that shapes everything else: once, there was a plan. Once, the configuration *meant* something. The oldest among them—and there are old ones, individuals who have lived through thousands of cycles of construction and deconstruction, their carapace plates worn thin as paper, their joints calcified into particular angles—the oldest among them carry fragments of that meaning in their bodies. A specific sequence of movements when approaching a cornerstone. A particular pause before placing a capstone. Rituals that once corresponded to blueprints, now performed for their own sake, the blueprint lost.

They are called, in the vibration-language that hums through the stone beneath their feet, something that translates roughly as *those-who-mend*. Not builders. Menders. The distinction matters to them in ways they cannot articulate but feel with absolute conviction. They are not creating. They are *repairing*—correcting an error, fixing what has come undone. The fact that the repair never holds, that the error keeps recurring, that the undoing undoes itself in endless permutation, does not diminish their certainty. Something is wrong. They are fixing it.

---

Watch a single individual for a day.

She emerges at what serves for morning—a slight cooling of the air as moisture cycles through the dome's convection patterns, a dimming of the stone's glow so subtle only creatures born to it would notice. Her carapace is the blue-white of deep ice, shot through with veins of brighter luminescence where her shell has cracked and healed and cracked again over centuries. She has fourteen legs, though three are vestigial, carried folded against her thorax—ancestral limbs from a time when her kind moved differently, perhaps climbed, perhaps swam. Her eyes, if they are eyes, are distributed across her forward plates in a constellation of photosensitive patches that perceive not images but *intensities*, gradients of the omnipresent light.

She does not eat. Not exactly. Twice during the day she will press her ventral surface against the luminous stone and remain motionless for an hour, absorbing something—energy, minerals, information encoded in the glow itself. The stone feeds her. The stone feeds all of them. They are, in a sense, organs of the basin, extensions of its slow mineral metabolism.

Her first task is inspection. She moves along a newly completed wall, her forward limbs brushing each seam, her body vibrating at frequencies that make the stone *answer*. She is listening for wrongness. For misalignment. For the particular signature of error that justifies dismantling. She finds it—or believes she finds it—in the seventeenth block from the left, a stone that sits three degrees off true by some internal standard no external observer could verify. She marks it with a secretion from her mouthparts, a faintly darker smear that other workers will read as *here, begin here*.

By evening, the wall will be gone. By the following morning, it will exist again, differently. She will inspect it again. She will find new errors. The cycle will continue.

She has been doing this for four hundred years. She will do it for four hundred more, if her carapace holds, if the stone keeps feeding her, if the plan—the lost plan, the forgotten configuration—does not suddenly reveal itself and bring the mending to its end.

She does not hope for that revelation. She does not dread it. She simply works.

---

But there are others, and they are not all the same.

In the deepest part of the basin, where the pools gather and the light glows brightest, a cluster of structures has stood unchanged for eleven years. This is unprecedented. This is, by the standards of the Menders, either miraculous or catastrophic, and they have not yet determined which.

The buildings there are different. Not in their construction—they are made of the same blocks, fitted with the same precision, rising to the same modest heights. But they are *occupied*. The Menders who built them have not left. They live inside, emerging only to drink from the pools, spending their days in the dim interiors, their bodies pressed together in configurations that serve no architectural purpose.

They are thinking. Or they are refusing to think. Or they are thinking *differently*, in ways that the constant motion of building and unbuilding never permitted.

One of them—larger than most, her carapace marked with the scars of a long-ago ceiling collapse—has begun making marks on the interior walls. Not construction. Not repair. *Representation*. Crude shapes scratched into the luminous stone with a harder tool, shapes that might be other Menders, might be the basin itself seen from above, might be something else entirely. She works on them for hours, then stops, then works again. She has never tried to build anything outside her dwelling. She has never tried to unbuild what she has marked.

The other Menders avoid this cluster. They flow around it like water around a stone, their paths diverging instinctively, their inspections never quite reaching its walls. It is not forbidden—they have no mechanism for forbidding. But it is *wrong*, somehow, in the same way a misaligned block is wrong. It is an error that no one has yet been able to articulate clearly enough to mark for correction.

The eleven years stretch toward twelve. The scratched shapes multiply. The occupied buildings stand.

---

And then there are the *deep workers*.

Below the basin floor—below the luminous stone, below the pools, below the foundations that are not foundations but merely the current lowest level of construction—tunnels descend into darkness. The light does not reach there. The stone is different:ite,ite,ite, cold, non-luminous, the bedrock beneath the bedrock, the world that existed before the basin was carved.

Menders go down into those tunnels and do not return for years at a time. They go alone, always alone, carrying nothing, communicating with no one. They are not mending. They are not building. What they do in the darkness is unknown to those who remain in the light.

When they return—*if* they return—they are changed. Their carapaces have grown thick and rough, crusted with minerals that exist nowhere in the basin above. Their photosensitive patches have atrophied, leaving them nearly blind in the omnipresent glow. They move differently, more slowly, with a deliberation that suggests they are listening to something others cannot hear.

They do not speak of what they found. They do not resume the work of mending. They linger at the basin's edges, at the highest terraces, pressed against the curved walls where the dome meets the floor. They wait for something. The other Menders avoid them even more carefully than they avoid the occupied buildings.

One of the deep workers, a male whose carapace has turned almost black with accumulated minerals, has been waiting at the dome's edge for thirty-seven years. He has not moved. He has not fed. His legs have fused to the stone beneath him through some process of slow mineral exchange, so that it is no longer clear where his body ends and the basin begins. He is either dying or transforming. The Menders who pass near him do not inspect him. He is not a block. He is not an error. He is something else, something outside the plan, something they have no category for.

Sometimes, in the silence between the dripping of condensation, you can hear him vibrating. A low, subsonic hum that travels through the stone, that makes the luminescence flicker almost imperceptibly, that carries information no one can decode.

---

The young are born in the pools.

Not hatched—*assembled*. The pale filaments in the water are not separate organisms but components, building blocks of flesh, mineral-organic precursors that drift and combine according to patterns encoded in the water itself. A new Mender takes shape over months, growing from a seed-crystal of genetic material shed by adults into a framework of accumulated matter. By the time she emerges from the pool, she is already the size of a small dog, already armored, already possessed of the urge to mend.

She has no parents. She has no teachers. She does not learn the work; she *knows* it, the knowledge written into the mineral structure of her carapace, the patterns of construction and deconstruction hardcoded into her nervous system. She emerges from the pool and immediately begins inspecting the nearest wall.

But the knowledge is imperfect. Degraded. Each generation carries the patterns less precisely than the last, the plan becoming hazier with each iteration, the sense of what they are mending—of what the *correct* configuration would look like—fading like a memory of a memory. The oldest Menders remember more. The youngest remember almost nothing, only the urge to work, only the conviction that something is wrong and must be fixed.

In another ten thousand years, perhaps, the knowledge will be gone entirely. The Menders will still build and unbuild, still inspect and mark and carry blocks across the glowing stone, but they will have no sense of purpose at all, not even the faded ghost of purpose that drives them now. They will be pure motion, pure change, a living system that perpetuates itself without reference to meaning.

Or perhaps the eleven-year cluster will have grown. Perhaps the deep workers will have transformed into something new. Perhaps the scratched marks on the interior walls will have become a language, and the language will encode a different plan, a new configuration, a purpose invented rather than remembered.

---

They do not know you are watching.

If they knew—if they could perceive you as a thinking entity rather than an obstacle to flow around—what would they do? Inspect you, perhaps. Search for the misalignment in your structure, the error that makes you different from them, the wrongness that might justify dismantling you into components and rebuilding you correctly. They would want to mend you. They would want to incorporate you into the plan, the lost plan, the configuration that once meant something and will again, if only they can find the right arrangement of blocks, the correct sequence of walls and doorways, the precise architecture of a city that has never been finished because it was finished long ago and they are merely maintaining it, correcting its endless drift toward entropy, keeping the shape of meaning alive even as the meaning itself fades.

The light glows cold and steady from every surface. The dripping continues. Somewhere in the basin, a wall goes up. Somewhere else, a wall comes down. The Menders carry blocks across the luminous ground, and the ground carries them, and the dome curves overhead like a skull, like a ceiling, like the inside of an egg that will never hatch because it hatched already, long ago, and this is what emerged: not a creature but a *process*, not a city but a *becoming*, not a plan but the memory of a plan, fading, fading, held together by motion alone.

They are still working.

They will not stop.
