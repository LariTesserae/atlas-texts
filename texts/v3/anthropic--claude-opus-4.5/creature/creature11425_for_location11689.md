## The Keth

They call themselves *keth*, a word that means something like "those who hold on." They have been here for eleven generations—long enough that their origin stories have worn smooth, like the stones.

The keth are small. Not dwarfed, but compact—dense with muscle, low centers of gravity, bodies that learned long ago that height is a liability when the wind wants to take you. Their skin runs to grays and blue-grays, colors that disappear against wet stone, and their hands are extraordinary: wide-palmed, strong-fingered, with a grip strength that exceeds what their size would suggest by a factor that surprises anyone who has ever been pulled from the water by one of them.

They live in the ruins, mostly. Not out of sentiment but because the old structures, for all their decay, were built to anchor. The keth have carved into the concrete and rusted steel, expanding the industrial bones into warrens that stay dry even when the spray coats everything for days. In the old processing facility, seventeen family groups occupy what were once holding tanks, the curved walls now covered with carvings that record current-patterns, fish migrations, the names of those taken by the water. The lighthouse base hosts the closest thing they have to leadership—a rotating council of elders who are elected not for wisdom but for demonstrated survival. Anyone who has lived past sixty in the Drowning Peaks has something to teach.

---

Their bodies bear the evidence of adaptation. The children are born with ear canals that seal reflexively in water, and most keth can hold their breath for four, five, six minutes without training. Their eyes adjust quickly between the brightness above the spray and the green murk below. Many have a faint webbing between their fingers—not enough to mark them as something other than human, but enough to matter when swimming through current.

The current-swimmers are different. They are chosen young, trained younger, and by adulthood they have become something the rest of the keth both admire and fear. Their lungs have expanded; their blood carries oxygen differently; they can dive to depths that would rupture an untrained body and stay there long enough to harvest, to hunt, to map the drowned valleys that most keth know only as stories. The current-swimmers grease their skin with kelp-oil before every dive, and the oil leaves a residue that never quite washes off, so that the oldest swimmers are slick to the touch, their skin carrying a permanent sheen that smells of salt and iodine and the deep.

When a current-swimmer dies—and they die often, taken by pressure or predator or the simple mathematics of too far, too long, too cold—their body is rarely recovered. The keth believe the drowned peaks keep what they are given. They do not mourn with burial. They mourn with carving: a new line on the tank walls, a name that will outlast memory.

---

They eat from both worlds. Above the water: the mushrooms, which they cultivate in the dampest parts of the ruins, feeding them on decay, harvesting them before the spores release. The wave-bird eggs, pried from their cement anchors during the brief windows when the adult birds are feeding at sea. A bitter moss that grows on the northern faces of exposed rock, which tastes like grief but contains something their bodies need during the long storms. Insects, roasted, their armor crunching between teeth. The occasional canopy mammal, though these are difficult to catch and not worth the effort unless the fishing has failed.

Below the water: everything. The current-swimmers harvest the kelp holdfasts for the crustaceans that colonize them, bringing up baskets of creatures that fight and snap even as they're hauled to surface. They hunt the muscular fish with spears tipped in sharpened bone—metal rusts too quickly, and the old steel left in the ruins is too precious to waste on weapons that will be lost. The seagrass meadows yield tubers if you know where to dig and when, and the current-swimmers know. The deep valleys offer stranger fare: pale flesh that glows faintly when cut, organs that can be dried and traded for their medicinal properties, bones that hold an edge better than any stone.

The keth do not farm the sea—the currents would tear any fixed cultivation apart. They follow and adapt and take what offers itself, reading the water the way a mainland farmer might read soil.

---

Trade exists, barely.

Once or twice a year, when the current-weather permits, boats arrive from elsewhere. Where, exactly, the keth are not certain. The traders speak a language that shares roots with theirs but has drifted, and they bring things the keth cannot make: metal tools, ceramic vessels, woven cloth that does not rot in the damp, and occasionally medicine or pigments or small luxuries that pass through the community for a season before being lost or worn out. In exchange, the keth offer what they have in surplus: the deep-harvested organs, kelp-fiber rope that holds knots in wet conditions better than any mainland material, carvings made from the bones of valley creatures.

The traders do not stay long. They anchor in the lee of the largest island, conduct their business in a day or two, and leave before the current shifts. They seem afraid of the water here, and the keth do not blame them. The water here is afraid of nothing.

Some keth leave on the trading boats. Young ones, usually, who have not yet learned that the world they know is the world they are made for. A few return years later, quieter, unwilling to say what they found. Most do not return. The keth do not speculate about what happens to them. The elsewhere is as unknowable as the deep valleys once were, before the current-swimmers learned to map them.

---

They worship the water, in their way.

Not as a god—the keth are too pragmatic for that, too aware that the water takes without preference and gives without intention. But as a force that deserves acknowledgment. Before every dive, the current-swimmers speak a phrase that translates roughly as "I enter you, I will leave you, I do not assume I will leave you." Before every meal, the keth pause for a moment of silence that might be gratitude or might be simple awareness: *this came from there, and I am here, and the distance between is not as far as it seems.*

The dead are given to the sea. All of them, not just the drowned. When a keth dies on land—of age, of illness, of the thousand small accidents that claim bodies in a place this unforgiving—the body is carried to the water's edge during a transition, when the currents fight, and released into the chaos. The keth believe the transition will take the body down, will deliver it to the drowned valleys, will let it become part of the slow life that has been exactly what it is since before the mountains became islands.

There is a word for people who die in their sleep, peacefully, without violence or drowning: *keth-absent*, which might translate as "those who did not hold on long enough." It is not an insult, exactly. But it is not a compliment either.

---

The children learn the currents before they learn to count. They are taken to the water's edge as infants and dipped into the surge, so that their bodies know the cold and the pull before their minds know the words for either. By the time they can walk, they can read the surface for what moves beneath. By the time they can run, they are swimming—first in the calm pockets that exist in the lee of certain rocks during certain current-states, then farther, longer, testing themselves against the water that tests everything.

The old tell stories during the long storms, when going outside means risking the wind, when the warrens fill with the sound of rain and wave and bodies breathing in the dark. They tell stories about the time before the water, when the mountains were mountains and people walked the valleys that are now drowned. They tell stories about the first keth, who chose to stay when the water rose, who watched the elsewhere people flee to their boats and decided that leaving was another kind of drowning. They tell stories about the processing facility when it still processed, about the machines that hummed and clanked and pulled something from the water—something the keth no longer know how to name, something that might have been the reason anyone was here at all.

These stories are worn smooth, like the stones. The keth tell them anyway.

---

On the eastern edge of the tallest island, there is a place where the rock forms a natural amphitheater, open to the sky and the spray and the endless sound. This is where the keth gather when gathering is called for: to elect new council members, to witness the return of current-swimmers from the deep, to mark the transitions between seasons (which here are measured not in temperature but in current-pattern, in fish-movement, in the color of the water at dawn).

When they gather, they stand close. Not for warmth—though the warmth helps—but because standing close is what the keth do. Their community is not large: perhaps four hundred people across all the inhabited islands, small enough that everyone knows everyone, that a death is felt by all, that a birth is celebrated with a relief that borders on desperation. They cannot afford to lose each other. The water takes enough.

At the center of the amphitheater, there is a stone. It is not natural—it was placed there, dragged up from somewhere below the waterline, by keth who are now only names on the tank walls. The stone is carved with a single image: a hand, gripping. The keth do not know who carved it or when. They know what it means.

*Hold on.*

---

The current-swimmers say the deep valleys are changing.

They have been saying this for two generations now, and the elders listen with the careful patience of people who have heard warnings before. But the swimmers are specific: the pale creatures are moving higher. The seagrass meadows are migrating, slowly, up the slopes. The kelp holdfasts that were once found only below forty meters are now establishing at thirty, at twenty-five.

The water is rising. Still. Not fast—not fast enough to see in a single lifetime—but the swimmers can read the valleys, and the valleys are shrinking. The peaks are losing their battle with the sea. Another few centuries, perhaps, and the tallest island will be waist-deep at high current. Another millennium, and there will be nothing but water where the keth now stand.

The keth do not know what to do with this information. They discuss it, sometimes, during the long storms. Some say the elsewhere boats will have to become more than trade—will have to become escape. Some say the keth can learn to live on the water itself, to build floating homes, to become a people of the surface rather than the edge. Some say that when the mountains finish drowning, the keth will simply hold on to the last rock until there is no rock left, and then they will not be keth anymore, and that will be a kind of ending that does not need to be feared.

The council has made no decision. The council may never make a decision. The water will decide for them, eventually. It always does.

---

There is a woman—old, older than anyone on the council, her skin mapped with scars from a youth spent swimming the deep—who sits at the edge of the lighthouse base on days when the current permits. She does not gather with the others. She does not tell stories. She watches the water, and sometimes she speaks to it, words that no one else can hear over the roar of the surf.

Her name is Sethka. She was the greatest current-swimmer of her generation, and she has outlived everyone she trained with, everyone she loved, everyone who ever pulled her from the water when she went too far. She is *keth-present*, the opposite of those who die in their sleep: one who held on past all reasonable holding.

When the children ask her what the deep valleys are like, she tells them the truth: they are dark, and cold, and quiet in a way that makes the surface noise unbearable by comparison. They are full of things that have never seen light. They are beautiful in the way that drowning is beautiful, if you do not fight it—a kind of peace that asks only that you give up everything else.

The children listen. They do not understand yet. But they will, if they survive long enough to become swimmers themselves. They will understand that the keth are not native to this place. They are survivors of it. They have made their home in the slow disaster of the drowning peaks because someone, eleven generations ago, decided that holding on was preferable to letting go.

Sethka watches the water. The water watches nothing. This is the arrangement they have come to, and it will hold until it doesn't.

---

In the processing facility, in the deepest tank, where the walls are so thick with carving that no bare stone remains, there is a space that the keth do not enter.

It is not forbidden. There are no guards, no locked doors. But the keth do not enter. They have not entered for generations. The elders say there is nothing there—just old machines, rusted past recognition, and the bones of something that was dead before the keth arrived. The current-swimmers, who fear very little, admit that the space makes them uneasy. It is dry when it should not be. It is quiet when nothing here is ever quiet.

Once, a child wandered in. She was young, too young to understand the unspoken rules, and she was gone for perhaps an hour before her family found her. She was sitting in the center of the space, surrounded by machines that looked almost intact, staring at something on the wall.

When they asked her what she saw, she said: *It was drawing. Before I came in, it was drawing. It stopped when I looked.*

She could not describe what "it" was. She could not describe what the drawing showed. Her family brought her out, and she grew up to be a current-swimmer of moderate skill, and she never spoke of it again.

The keth do not enter the deep tank. The deep tank does not bother them in return. This is another arrangement, older than the first, and if it ever breaks, the keth do not know what will happen.

They hold on. They do not assume they will keep holding on. The water rises, the machines rust, the mountains continue their slow drowning, and somewhere in the space between one wave and the next, the keth endure—not because they are strong, but because they have forgotten how to do anything else.
