They call themselves the Tenders, though this is a translation of a concept that does not quite mean *tending* as you would understand it. It is closer to *being-present-for* or *accompanying-through-change*. They have no word that separates the worker from the work, the maintainer from the maintained.

You will not see them. Not because they hide—they have no concept of hiding—but because they move in rhythms your attention cannot track. A Tender might spend eleven days crossing a single terrace, pausing with each step to feel the water's depth through the soles of their feet, to listen for the frequencies of flow that indicate a channel beginning to narrow with deposits. When the fog thickens, they become still, and their stillness is so complete that your eye slides over them like water over stone. They are there, on the platforms. They have always been there. You looked at one for six full breaths and saw only wet stone and waiting tools.

Their bodies are suited to this work in ways that suggest the work has shaped them as much as they have shaped the terraces. Their skin holds a calcium pallor, not white but a living gray-cream that seems to drink the sourceless light rather than reflect it. It is not smooth—it cannot be smooth, in this place where everything accrues—but textured with fine mineral deposits that they do not scrape away, that form patterns over decades like the growth rings of trees. The eldest among them are half-ite themselves, calcium whorls rising along their shoulders and skulls, their movements slower but their patience deeper, their understanding of the water's needs refined to something approaching prophecy.

They have no eyes that you would recognize. The brightness that overwhelms you is their native medium; they navigate it through organs that sense the polarization of reflected light, the subtle differences in luminosity that tell them where water is thin, where stone is wearing, where the mineral content has shifted. To them, the terraces are not a uniform glow but a symphony of variation, each pool distinct, each channel singing its own quiet frequency.

---

The Tenders do not live in the terraces. They live *as* the terraces—or rather, the distinction does not exist for them. When one sleeps, they lower themselves into a pool and let the mineral water coat them, breathing through spiracles that filter the calcium-rich moisture, dreaming in the language of slow accretion. When they wake—after days, sometimes, or weeks—they rise with a new layer of pale deposit on their skin and an understanding of what that pool needs, what the water told them while they lay submerged.

They eat nothing you would call food. The supersaturated water provides. They take it in through their skin, through their breathing, extracting the minerals and the microscopic organisms that you cannot see because you do not know to look—the things that live in that sterile water, that are not life as you recognize it but that pulse and divide and carry information in chemical gradients too subtle for your instruments. The Tenders do not think of these as separate creatures. The water is alive, to them. The stone is alive. They are alive. It is all one slow metabolism.

There are young ones, though *young* here means something measured in centuries. They move faster—you might actually perceive one, if you knew where to look, as a blur of motion that resolves into stillness between one breath and the next. They are learning the terraces, building their internal maps, developing the patience that will eventually slow them to imperceptibility. The eldest watch them with something that might be fondness, or might be the same attention they give to a channel that flows a little too quickly: *this will settle, in time*.

---

They communicate, but not in ways you could intercept. When two Tenders meet—which happens rarely, since they spread themselves across the terraces like a distribution of care, each one knowing which sections fall under their attention—they press their palms together and share the mineral traces accumulated on their skin. It is a language of residue, of history written in calcium and silica and the faint organics that the water carries down from the fog. One Tender can read another's entire season in that touch: where they have worked, what they have repaired, what they have dreamed in the pools.

They do not speak of the builders because they do not understand the question. *They* built the terraces. Their ancestors, who were also them, since the Tenders do not mark generational boundaries the way you would—the calcium in their bones is the same calcium that lines the pools, cycled through water and flesh and stone in loops that close over millennia. The tools on the platforms are old, yes. But *old* means only that they have been used many times, that they have become good at what they do. A scraper worn to the perfect curve by ten thousand hands is more valuable than a new one. Nothing is discarded. Nothing is replaced until it has given everything it can.

---

The formation in the basin—the white frozen fountain, the candle-wax city—is sacred to them, but *sacred* is another word that does not translate. It is the place where the water completes its work. It is where the Tenders go when their mineral accumulation becomes too great to move, when their patience has deepened beyond motion entirely. They descend to the basin over years, decades, making their slow way down the terraces one last time, and they settle into the growing formation and they let the water build over them. They do not die. They become the stone. Their consciousness—if it can be called that—disperses into the calcium lattice, becomes part of the basin's dreaming, joins the others who have made the same descent over centuries.

The formation thinks. Slowly. In mineral time. It considers the health of the terraces, the patterns of the fog, the needs of the water. When a Tender sleeps in the pools, they receive its guidance as images that take months to fully resolve: *here, a channel is narrowing; there, a lip has begun to crack; above, the fog has shifted and a catchment needs extending*. The formation is the memory of the terraces, the accumulated wisdom of every Tender who has joined it, and it speaks through the water itself.

---

You could stay for a year and never be certain you had seen one. You could stay for ten years and begin to glimpse them—a figure on a distant platform that was not there a moment ago, a hand pressed to wet stone in a gesture of listening, a face turned toward you with organs that see light you cannot perceive. They would not mind your presence. You are fast and loud and blind to everything that matters, but you are also temporary. The water will outlast you. The terraces will outlast you. They have time.

If you left a gift—a stone from somewhere far away, a tool made of unfamiliar metal, even just your footprints preserved in mineral crust before the next maintenance cycle—they would find it, eventually. They would touch it and read it and add its story to their own. They would not understand where you came from or what you wanted, but they would understand that something passed through, something that moved in the wrong rhythms, something that did not know how to wait. They would feel something like pity, or something like wonder.

Then they would return to their work, and the fog would thicken, and you would be alone again in the sourceless light, in the valley of held water, where the only sound is the almost-nothing of ten thousand droplets falling too softly to hear.
