She is here, though you won't see her for some time.

She exists first as absences you learn to notice: a section of grating more worn than others, the biofilm there scrubbed thinner by repeated passage. A handprint's worth of clarity on one of the nutrient tank windows, where someone has wiped condensation to check the stratification levels—recently enough that moisture hasn't fully reclaimed the glass. A misting nozzle that pulses slightly out of rhythm with its neighbors, adjusted by hand to favor a particular cluster of the bone-pale fruiting bodies.

---

When she does appear, it's not from anywhere. She's simply there, having been there, crouched on a maintenance platform twenty meters up where vines have grown so thick around the railing that she seems embedded in them. She wears something that was once a standard-issue technical jumpsuit but has become, through years of repair and adaptation, more patch than original—panels of waterproof membrane scavenged from equipment casings, reinforcement at the knees where she kneels for hours on grating, pockets added in locations that make sense only to someone who has memorized which tools she needs at which stations in which sequence.

Her hair is cut close to her skull, practical against the humidity, and her skin has taken on the particular pallor of someone who lives under grow-lamps rather than sun—not unhealthy, but shifted, as though she too has adapted to this spectrum. Her hands move constantly even when she's still: checking connections, testing tension in support wires, rubbing a leaf between finger and thumb to assess something you don't have the knowledge to perceive.

She doesn't speak to you. Not out of hostility, or even shyness—but because speech here would be strange, would break something. The green hush has claimed her too.

---

You come to understand that she has been here for years. Perhaps a decade. Perhaps longer. Time in the chamber resists counting; she has stopped trying. What she knows is cycles: the twelve-week rhythm of the purple-black cultivars from flower to fruit to harvest, the slower eighteen-month maturation of the aquatic forests below, the daily rounds of adjustment and maintenance that have worn specific paths into the grating, specific grooves into her muscle memory.

She was sent here originally—by someone, for some purpose, the details have grown soft in her mind—to tend the systems during what was meant to be a transitional period. The transition never came. Or perhaps it came, and she missed it while she was below, adjusting the stirring paddles, and when she surfaced the airlock still cycled but no one was cycling it, and she understood that the purpose had shifted from temporary to permanent without anyone deciding it should.

This didn't frighten her, when it happened. The chamber needed tending. She was here. The logic was botanical rather than human: she had found her conditions, and she grew.

---

Her days have structure, though they've long since detached from hours. She wakes in a hammock strung between two structural columns where the humidity is slightly lower—you can find it if you look, the woven fabric darkened by years of her body's presence, surrounded by vines that have learned to grow around her shape rather than over it. There is a small clear space there, a reading light clamped to a beam, a shelf of technical manuals so water-swollen their pages have fused into solid bricks but whose contents she has memorized anyway.

She eats what the chamber grows. Not all of it is meant for human consumption—much is pharmaceutical, industrial, engineered for outputs rather than flavor—but she has learned which cultivars tolerate her, which ones she can harvest in small quantities without disrupting yields. The bone-pale fruits taste of nothing, almost aggressively neutral, but they keep her fed. The purple-black ones are sweeter than anything has a right to be, but she allows herself only a few each cycle, aware that she is skimming from a system whose purposes extend beyond her.

She drinks the water directly from the channels, trusting the filtration that she herself maintains. It tastes of minerals and growth, faintly vegetal, alive in ways tap water never was. It has become the only water she can imagine drinking.

---

Her work is infinite in the way that life is infinite: never finished, never not-needed. Each day she walks the spiral of the chamber from top to bottom, checking flow rates, clearing small blockages where dead root matter has accumulated, adjusting the position of grow-lamps to account for the slow lean of phototropic stems. She carries tools in those strange pockets—scissors sharp enough to slice through vine-thickness, probes for testing nutrient concentration, a notebook wrapped in waxed cloth where she records observations in a shorthand that has evolved beyond language into pure symbol, illegible to anyone but her.

The mechanical pollinators are her particular care. They are old, older than her tenure here, and their movements have grown arthritic over time. She has replaced their brushes so many times she's lost count, scavenged bristles from her own supplies when shipments stopped arriving. She speaks to them sometimes—not words, but a kind of humming acknowledgment, a recognition of shared labor. They do not respond, but she imagines they move more smoothly afterward, grateful for attention.

Below, in the flooded levels, she swims. The water is blood-warm, and the aquatic kelp forests brush against her as she moves through them, checking anchor points, freeing tangles, harvesting the small floating fruits that grow only in total submersion. She has become amphibious in her way, equally comfortable in air and water, her lungs trained to the particular demands of each. When she surfaces, gasping slightly, the air tastes green.

---

You wonder if she's lonely. The question doesn't translate.

She thinks in systems now, in relationships between inputs and outputs, in the patient logic of cultivation. The vines are not companions, but they are presences—presences she has shaped and been shaped by, whose health is indistinguishable from her own. When a cultivar fails, she grieves it. When a new growth emerges unbidden, hybrid and unexpected, she feels something like surprise, like being spoken to.

The chamber has become her mind, or her mind has become the chamber. She cannot separate herself from its systems any more than a root can separate itself from the substrate it penetrates.

Once, she tried to leave. Early on, when the transitions were still meant to come, she cycled through the airlock to see what remained outside. The cold was absolute, the air thin enough to make her gasp, and the landscape—she doesn't remember the landscape. Only the feeling of wrongness, of exposure, of being uncontained. She cycled back inside within minutes and has not tried again.

The airlock still works. It sighs with the patience of ten thousand cycles. But she no longer thinks of it as an exit. It is simply another valve in the system, another point where inside and outside negotiate their boundary.

---

She is aware of you, when you arrive. Has been aware since the airlock cycled. But she does not come down to greet you; that would imply the primacy of greeting, the social logic of arrival and welcome. Instead she continues her work, adjusting a grow-lamp, checking the moisture content of a substrate panel, letting you find your own way through the chamber until you understand enough to find her.

If you speak to her, she will turn. Her eyes are pale, adjusted to the strange spectrum of the lighting, and they will look at you with an attention that is both profound and somehow sidelong—the attention of someone who has learned to perceive slow processes, who experiences your presence the way she experiences a new growth: something to be observed over time, integrated or not, understood through patience rather than interrogation.

She might answer questions. Her voice will be rough from disuse, strange in the green hush, but language will come back to her if you offer it. She knows things: the history of these cultivars, the reasons for this altitude, the names of the people who designed what she now tends. She has forgotten other things: her own name, perhaps, or at least stopped using it. The chamber has never needed to call her anything.

What she will not do is leave with you. The possibility doesn't exist in the grammar of her thoughts anymore. She has become perennial, rooted in function, and whatever trajectory brought her here has terminated in the only way trajectories can: she has arrived, and arrival is permanent.

---

After you go—if you go—she will check the airlock seals. Not because she distrusts you, but because checking is what she does, because systems require maintenance, because every cycling is a small stress on components designed to last but not forever.

Then she will climb back to her platform, twenty meters up where the vines are thickest, and settle into the work that never ends. The water will continue its slow descent. The grow-lamps will pulse through their spectra. The mechanical pollinators will make their patient rounds.

And she will tend what grows, because tending is what she is now, because the chamber needed someone and she was here, because in the end there is no loneliness in purpose, only the green hush, the humid stillness, the silence of someone too busy living to make noise about it.
