**The Minders**

They call themselves nothing. Names require someone to speak them to, and the Minders have not spoken to outsiders in three generations—not since the last inspector climbed up from the lowlands, checked his gauges, made his notes, and never returned to check again. They watched him go. They watch everything go.

There are perhaps forty of them now, though counting is difficult when some spend months in the deep cisterns and others range across seven peaks without returning to any fixed shelter. They are small people, not from malnutrition but from selection—the condensery passages are narrow, the crevice-shelters tight, and for a century the peaks have favored those who fit. Their skin has taken on the gray-brown of basalt, not through any strangeness but through the simple arithmetic of sun and stone-dust worked into every pore. Their eyes are pale, almost colorless, with pupils that expand enormously in the dark of the cistern-caves and contract to pinpricks in the bright heat of midday.

They tend the machines. This is not sentiment or duty but something closer to symbiosis—the condenseries give them water, and they give the condenseries their hands. A Minder child learns to read the twitch of a pressure gauge before she learns to speak, knows the meaning of each particular creak in the expanding pipes, can taste the difference between good condensation and water that has picked up too much mineral from a corroding joint. But they do not think of themselves as caretakers. The machines are simply part of the landscape, no different from the stone or the fog or the corvids. A Minder will spend a week repairing a conduit with the same unselfconscious attention she gives to finding water-fat beetles or avoiding the thing that hunts at night. It all belongs to the same world. It all requires the same patient attention.

**Daily rhythm:**

They wake before dawn, when the brief cool allows movement without the stupor of heat. This is when they travel—picking their way along the ridge-paths worn into the stone by their ancestors, checking collection points, clearing debris from channels. They move in silence, communicating through a language of hand-signs and touched shoulders that has grown so refined it can convey not just information but mood, intention, the particular quality of a morning's fog. Sound carries strangely here, swallowed by stone, and they learned long ago that the peaks reward quiet.

By midday they are still. They shelter in the crevices, in the abandoned control rooms, in hollows they have carved into the shaded sides of cisterns. They sleep, or they work at small tasks that require no movement—weaving mesh, rendering fat, teaching children the catechism of gauge-readings. The heat presses down. The stone breathes warmth. They wait.

Dusk brings the second activity. This is when they harvest—collecting the day's condensation from surfaces the machines don't reach, trapping moths in fine nets for their fat and protein, checking snares set for the rock lizards. They eat twice: once at dawn, once at dusk. The food is monotonous but sufficient. Lizard meat dried on hot stone. Beetle paste. The inner pith of succulents, which tastes of almost nothing but fills the stomach. Occasionally corvid eggs, if a nest can be raided without inviting the birds' long memory for faces.

Night they spend in groups, never alone. Because of the hunter.

**The Pad-Walker**

They do not know what it is. They have never seen it clearly, though several have seen it *unclearly*—a shape moving between peaks in the darkness, larger than a person, lower to the ground, with a gait that seems wrong for any animal they know. The tracks tell them it has four feet and significant weight. The kills tell them it is efficient and methodical. The fact that it has never taken a Minder tells them it is either uninterested in their meat or smart enough to know the difference between easy prey and prey that watches.

They have named it without naming it. In their hand-sign language, it is represented by a gesture: four fingers drawn across the palm, the motion of something heavy passing. When a child asks what the gesture means, she is told *the thing that walks where we do not.* This is considered sufficient explanation.

Some of the older Minders believe the Pad-Walker is a descendant of something the original builders brought—a guard animal gone feral, generations removed from whatever domestication it once knew. Others believe it came up from below, following the water-pipes in reverse, drawn by the concentration of prey. A few, the ones who spend the most time in the deep cisterns, believe it has always been here, that it is as much a part of the peaks as the stone faces that watch from certain angles.

The Minders do not hunt it. They do not worship it. They simply account for it, the way they account for the heat and the moisture and the slow decay of metal. It is a pressure in the system. They adjust.

**The Deep Cistern Keepers**

Not all Minders live on the surface. Perhaps a third of them, at any given time, dwell in the cavern-cisterns where the collected water pools before continuing its journey downward. These spaces are lightless, warm, and wet—the stone sweating, the water still and deep, the darkness so complete that the Keepers have developed a way of clicking with their tongues to map the space around them. They are not blind; their eyes work when they return to the surface. But they have learned not to need them.

The Keepers maintain the interior works: the grates that filter debris, the overflow channels that prevent flooding, the sluice gates that control the water's release. They know the cisterns as bodies, can feel when a cavity is silting up or when water is pooling wrong or when a pipe below has partially blocked. They swim in the dark, treading the body-warm water, running their hands along surfaces to check for cracks.

They also guard something. In the deepest cistern, the one that feeds the main outflow pipe, there is a chamber that the original builders sealed. The seal has never been opened, though the Keepers check it regularly, running their fingers over the rivets, listening at the metal for any sound from within. They do not know what is inside. The knowledge, if it ever existed, was lost with the builders. But the oldest Keeper, a woman whose name-sign means *water-that-listens*, believes the seal is meant to stay closed. Not because of what might escape, but because of what might be disturbed.

She has felt, she says, a pressure on the other side. Not water. Not air. Something that pushes back when she presses her ear to the metal. Something that is waiting with the same patience the mountains have always rewarded.

She does not think it is dangerous. She thinks it is *like them*—adapted, enduring, part of the system. But she also thinks some valves are closed for reasons, and the reasons do not need to be understood to be respected.

**The Children**

There are always children, though never many. The peaks do not support excess. A Minder woman bears perhaps two children in her lifetime, and one of those may not survive the first summer's heat or the first winter's lean months. The children who do survive are cherished without sentiment—taught early, expected to contribute, given the same patient attention that everything here receives.

They play in the abandoned control rooms, climbing the dead consoles, pretending to read gauges that have meanings they will later learn are real. They catch beetles in jars made from salvaged glass, study the iridescence, release them again because nothing is wasted and beetles have their role. They learn the hand-signs before they learn to walk steadily, their small fingers forming *water* and *heat* and *careful* and *the thing that walks where we do not.*

By the age of seven or eight, they are working. By twelve, they have chosen or been chosen for their role—surface-tender or cistern-keeper, mesh-weaver or hunter of small game. By the time they are old enough to bear or sire children of their own, they have become so thoroughly part of the peaks that the distinction between person and place has begun to blur at the edges.

This is not metaphor. The oldest Minders, the ones who have lived sixty or seventy years in this landscape, begin to *feel* like the mountains in ways that are difficult to describe but impossible to ignore. They know when a storm is coming not from the gauges but from a sensation in their joints that mirrors the pressure in the pipes. They sense the Pad-Walker's movements without seeing tracks, as though they share some proprioception with the stone itself. They sit for hours in the presence of the watching faces, and something—perhaps something—passes between them.

They do not call this communion. They do not call it anything. It simply is what happens when you belong to a place completely and the place, perhaps, begins to belong to you.

**What They Believe**

The Minders have no religion, exactly, but they have a cosmology. It goes like this:

The mountains were here first, and will be here last. The water is the mountains' blood, cycling through stone and air and stone again. The machines are the mountains' new organs, grafted on by clever animals who understood something true about how the water wanted to move. The creatures—lizards, beetles, moths, corvids, the Pad-Walker, the Minders themselves—are all part of the same system, each playing a role that keeps the whole in motion.

Nothing is wasted. Nothing is permanent. Everything transforms.

They do not believe in an afterlife, but they believe in *continuation*. They trust, without quite thinking of it as trust, that it all connects.

**At Night**

When the darkness is full and the Minders have gathered in their crevices and chambers, when the moths are rising and the Pad-Walker is walking and the cistern-keepers are swimming in the warm black water, something happens that none of them could describe to an outsider even if outsiders still came.

The mountains settle. The heat that has built all day releases in a slow exhale, and the stone makes sounds—not creaks, not cracks, but something lower, something felt more than heard. The pipes conduct these sounds downward. The cisterns resonate with them. The Minders, pressed against the warm rock in their sleeping places, feel the vibration in their bones.

It is not language. It is not communication. But it is *something*—a confirmation, maybe, that the system is still running, that the water is still flowing, that the balance holds for another day.

The children sleep through it. The adults have learned to find it soothing. The oldest Minders, the ones who have begun to blur into the landscape, sometimes stay awake to listen, their pale eyes open in the darkness, their hands pressed flat against the stone.

They are waiting for nothing. They are expecting nothing. They are simply *with* the mountains, in the way that the machines are with the mountains, in the way that the water and the beetles and the watching stone faces are all with the mountains.

In the morning, they will wake and begin again. Check the gauges. Clear the channels. Avoid the heat. Watch for the Pad-Walker. Teach the children. Tend the machines.

The water will collect. The water will flow. The water will go where it has always gone.

And the Minders will continue, because continuing is what they do, is what the peaks have made them for, is the only purpose that has ever made sense in a landscape that rewards nothing so much as patience, persistence, and the willingness to become part of something larger than yourself.
