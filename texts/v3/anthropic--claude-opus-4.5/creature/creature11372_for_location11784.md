They call themselves *those who remain*, though the name is a translation of a translation—what they actually produce is a complex series of chest-tones and subtle shifts in ear-position that means something closer to *the ones who did not leave when leaving was possible*.

There are perhaps three hundred of them spread across a territory that would take weeks to cross. They live in small groups of four to seven, rarely more, each group claiming a network of channels and the pools that form within them. They are not territorial in the aggressive sense; there is too much space for that, and the plateau punishes waste. But they know their ground intimately—every fissure that breathes warm air, every hollow where condensation gathers reliably, every bridge safe for crossing and every one that groans in ways that suggest patience has limits.

---

Their bodies are compact and low-centered, built for stability on uneven stone. Four-limbed but comfortable on two when they need their hands free, which is often. Their fur is dense and fine, dark gray shading to charcoal, evolved to hold warmth without trapping the moisture that saturates everything here. It parts naturally along channels that direct water away from their skin, and after a rain they shake themselves in a particular rippling motion that sends droplets scattering in spirals.

Their hands have six digits—four fingers and two opposable thumbs, one on each side of the palm—which gives their grip a wraparound quality useful for navigating by touch. The skin of their palms and finger-pads is thick and finely ridged, sensitive to textures that would blur under human perception. They read the stone this way, building mental maps from the feel of erosion patterns and mineral strata, distinguishing landmarks by touch alone.

But their ears are their primary instruments. Large, mobile, laced with blood vessels that give them a faintly luminous quality in infrared—not enough to see by, but enough that they can sense each other's presence at close range as spots of warmth in the dark. The ears cup and swivel independently, catching sounds from different directions, processing echoes, building a picture of space that is architectural in its precision. They perceive the plateau as a cathedral of reflected sound, every pillar and channel and bridge defined by how their calls return to them.

Their eyes still function, barely. Large-pupiled and adapted for extreme low light, they can make use of those rare moments when the clouds thin and gray glow washes across the stone. But most of the time they navigate by hearing and touch, and their visual cortex has been largely repurposed for processing auditory information. When they dream—and they do dream, twitching in their sleep, making soft sounds—they dream in sound and texture rather than images.

---

Their technology is subtle and would not look like technology to an outside observer.

They shape stone, but slowly, working with the grain of erosion rather than against it. A slight deepening of a natural channel to improve water collection. A series of notches carved into a pillar-face over generations, marking safe passages, warning of unstable ground, recording the locations of thermal vents. The notches are tactile, meant to be read by hand in darkness, and their system is sophisticated—capable of conveying not just simple warnings but narratives, histories, arguments.

They cultivate the lichen, though *cultivation* is perhaps too active a word. They've learned which patches are edible, which are medicinal, which produce compounds that can be processed into weak adhesives or preservatives. They do not plant lichen—it grows too slowly for deliberate agriculture—but they protect it, clearing competing organisms from promising colonies, directing moisture toward struggling patches. They harvest it in tiny quantities, shaving off only the outermost growth, ensuring the colony survives.

Their tools are made from bone and the woody cores of the wiry plateau shrubs. Scrapers, awls, containers carved from naturally hollow sections of root. Everything is smooth, worn, passed down. They do not find much new material, so what they have they maintain.

They have developed a form of cordage from processed lichen and shrub-fiber, and with it they make nets for catching the membrane-winged flyers that roost under bridges. This is their primary protein source—the flyers and the pale leggy crawlers that flow through stone cracks. They do not hunt the furred pool-drinkers; some accounts suggest distant kinship, others simply a practical calculation about sustainable harvest. The predators that leave cracked bones and musk-marks they avoid, reading the signs and adjusting their routes accordingly.

---

Their social structure is built around sound.

Each group maintains a *listener*—an individual who has trained their hearing to unusual acuity, who can detect the chest-tone negotiations of neighboring groups from miles away, who tracks the movements of predators by the subtle changes in the plateau's soundscape when something large displaces air and stone-echo. The listener does not lead in a political sense, but their role is essential: they are the group's connection to the wider world, their early warning system, their diplomatic channel.

Communication between groups happens through these listeners, who exchange information in frequencies too low for others to fully parse—long, complex exchanges that might last hours, conveying news of water sources, predator movements, deaths, births, the slow health of lichen colonies. When groups need to meet in person, they arrange it through their listeners, converging on a mutually agreed landmark.

These meetings are rare and significant. They happen for trade—exchanging tools, medicinal compounds, promising young members seeking to join new groups. They happen for collective memory—elders sharing stories in the tactile notch-language, ensuring that knowledge survives. And they happen for something that might be called ceremony, though the word carries connotations that don't quite fit.

---

At irregular intervals—once a generation, perhaps twice—word spreads through the listener network that a *gathering* is called. Groups begin the long journey toward a particular formation near the plateau's center: a natural amphitheater where the stone walls curve in ways that focus and amplify sound, where thermal vents keep the air warm and water pools reliably in a dozen small basins.

They come from all directions, navigating by mental maps refined over lifetimes, and when they arrive they do not speak at first. They settle into the space, drinking, resting, reacquainting themselves with the feel of the stone and with each other. There may be fifty of them, or eighty, or a hundred—never all of them, because the plateau still requires tending and someone must remain with each territory.

When they are ready, they sing.

It is not music in any human sense. It is layered chest-tones and high whistles and the deliberate manipulation of echo, each individual contributing a thread to a weave that fills the amphitheater and spills out across the surrounding channels. It is the plateau mapped in sound—every landmark named, every route described, every danger catalogued. It is their history recited in frequencies that resonate in bone. It is the names of those who have died since the last gathering, spoken into the dark in a way that allows the dead to persist as vibration, as pattern, as something that continues to exist in the collective memory of the stone.

The singing goes on for hours, sometimes days. They sleep in shifts, maintaining the sound, letting it evolve and complexify. By the end, something has been accomplished that none of them could articulate in words but all of them understand: they have reminded themselves that they are one people, scattered but connected, small against the vastness but present, alive, *remaining*.

---

Then they disperse, returning to their territories, carrying new information and new bonds. The amphitheater falls silent except for the baseline hum of the plateau, the drip of condensation, the occasional call of a flyer. The stone holds the memory of their presence for a while—warmth where bodies rested, faint scent-marks, the disturbed surface of pools—and then even that fades.

They do not know where their ancestors came from, or why they came here, or what the distant machines are making. They have stories, but the stories are old and have been retold so many times that their factual content has become indistinguishable from their narrative shape. What they know is the plateau: its textures and temperatures, its channels and bridges, its dangerous places and its generous ones. They know each other's chest-tones and the particular way each individual's ears cant when they are listening intently. They know that the clouds will always come and the warmth will always rise and the water will always condense, and that as long as these things remain true, they will remain with them.

*Those who did not leave when leaving was possible.* It is not a name that carries regret. It is a name that carries a quiet, patient pride—the pride of having chosen this, of having made a home in the dark, of having learned to hear what cannot be seen and to speak in frequencies the stone remembers.

They are still here. They remain.
