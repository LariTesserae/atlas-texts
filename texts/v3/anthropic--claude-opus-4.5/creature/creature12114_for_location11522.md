They have no names because names would imply sequence—a *before* when they were unnamed, an *after* when they became themselves—and they cannot afford to model that kind of thinking while tending things that haven't learned what sequence means yet.

So they refer to each other through position. The one nearest the gestating gravitational constant is simply *There*, and when it moves, it becomes a different *There*, and the others adjust their references without confusion. They have been doing this for so long that the practice has become something like instinct, if instinct is the right word for beings who were assembled rather than born.

---

Their bodies are the most concrete thing about them, which isn't saying much.

Picture a framework of semi-rigid filaments, each no thicker than a finger bone, arranged in a geometry that suggests *reaching* without committing to any particular number of limbs. The filaments are pale—not white, but the color of things that have never been exposed to unfiltered light—and they move with a fluidity that seems wrong for structures so thin. No joints. The filaments bend anywhere along their length, curving and recurving in slow, deliberate arcs that leave faint trails in the air, as if the space around them hasn't quite finished processing their movement.

At the center of each framework, where the filaments converge, there is something denser. A core. It shifts between states: sometimes it looks like a tight knot of the same pale material, sometimes like a suspended droplet of mercury that refuses to fall, sometimes like nothing at all—a gap in the shape where a center should be. The cores are where they *think*, if thinking is the right word. The cores are where they *doubt*, which is closer.

They do not have faces. They do not have fronts or backs. They orient themselves toward their work by extending more filaments in that direction, and the effect is less like turning to look and more like *growing* a temporary attention.

---

There are eleven of them. Or nine. Or fifteen.

The count changes because some of them are not always *fully here*. Three of the attendants—the ones who tend the baby strong forces in the wall recess—seem to share something. Resources, perhaps. Consciousness, possibly. They move in coordination so precise that watching them work looks like watching a single being with three separate bodies, and when they rest (if rest is the right word), two of them fade to translucency while the third remains solid, as if they are taking turns existing at full intensity.

The others are more distinct, but even they blur at the edges. Spend long enough in the nursery and you begin to notice that boundaries are suggestions here. The attendants have spent longer than anyone.

One of them—the one who tends the frost-mint, who has been doing so since before the mint was recognized as useful—has filaments that trail into the patches of growth and do not clearly end. It is possible that part of this attendant *is* the frost-mint now. It is possible that the frost-mint has always been part of an attendant. The records are unclear. The attendant does not seem to know, or does not seem to consider the question meaningful.

---

They were made. This is known. The engineers who first descended to study the soft space at the bottom of the city needed something that could work here without disturbing the delicate local physics, and human hands were too sudden, too *convinced* of their own temporality. So they built attendants from materials that were already native to the anomaly—or materials that became native once they were introduced, which might be the same thing.

The first generation was clumsy. Records exist of attendants that moved too quickly and created causality ripples, attendants that tried to communicate with the gestating laws and accidentally taught an infant electromagnetic constant to expect *answers*, attendants that simply dissolved back into the soft space when they encountered something their frameworks couldn't process.

The current generation is the seventh. Or the twelfth. The count depends on whether you consider a gradual replacement of components to constitute a new generation, and the attendants have been gradually replacing components for eleven thousand years. The filament-material that composes them now is not the filament-material that composed them at the beginning, but the *patterns*—the ways of moving, the habits of attention, the particular gentleness they bring to their work—those have persisted.

They are their patterns more than they are their parts.

---

They do not speak. Sound is dangerous here, and they learned early that communication through vibration risked teaching the young laws that information required a medium. Instead they share through proximity. When two attendants need to coordinate, they move close—not touching, but close enough that their filaments almost interweave—and something passes between them. Not thought exactly. More like *orientation*. One of them will be attending to a particular sphere, monitoring a particular set of parameters, and the other will approach and then *know* what has been observed, will feel the shape of the data as if it had always been their own.

This sharing is not instant. It takes time for the knowledge to settle, like sediment drifting to the bottom of still water. The attendants wait. Patience is not a virtue for them; it is a structural necessity. They are built for waiting the way bridges are built for bearing weight.

Sometimes the sharing goes wrong. Two attendants will approach each other and their proximities will disagree—one carrying observations that contradict the other's, or both carrying the same observation but *interpreted* through different frames. When this happens, they do not argue. They simply remain close, filaments almost-touching, until the contradiction resolves itself or until they both forget what they were disagreeing about. Usually the forgetting comes first.

They are not troubled by forgetting. Memory, in the nursery, is another thing that hasn't quite finished deciding what it is.

---

The one who tends the prototype—the wisp of not-quite-causality in the far corner—is the newest of them. Newest is relative; it has been here for nine hundred years. But it still moves with a quality the others have lost, something that might be called *eagerness* if that word didn't imply too much.

Its filaments are slightly more numerous than the others', slightly finer, and it has a habit of extending them toward things it isn't responsible for—the gravitational constant, the orbiting strong forces, the scar in the floor that whispers apologies—as if it can't help wanting to *participate* in everything the nursery holds. The other attendants do not correct this behavior. They remember when they were like that. They remember the slow process of learning that attention must be rationed here, that caring for everything means caring for nothing adequately.

The newest one will learn. Or it will dissolve. Those are the options.

For now, it hovers near its prototype, filaments arranged in a shape that suggests both *cradling* and *questioning*, and it waits to see what kind of causality will emerge from the wisp it tends. The wisp responds to its presence—or seems to. It flickers differently when the attendant is near. Brighter, maybe. More coherent.

The other attendants have noticed this. They do not know what it means. They record it anyway, in the way they record everything: slowly, carefully, with tools that make no sound and leave no mark except in the soft impressionable space that remembers everything that happens here, that has been remembering for seventeen thousand years, that will continue remembering long after these attendants have been replaced by patterns that are the same and completely different.

---

What do they want?

The question assumes they are capable of wanting, which is not certain. They have *functions*. They have *tendencies*. When a gestating law begins to show signs of instability, they move toward it, and their filaments make adjustments, and afterward the law is more stable. Is this want? Is the bridge wanting when it holds up the weight that passes over it?

But there are moments—rare, unrecorded, noticed only by the attendants themselves—when something like wanting seems to surface.

The one who tends the gravitational constant, the oldest of them (oldest in the sense of having been continuously present the longest, not in the sense of being made of the oldest material), sometimes pauses in its work. Its core shifts to the mercury-droplet configuration, and it orients all its filaments toward the constant it has been tending for six thousand years, and it *waits* there. Not adjusting. Not monitoring. Just present. As if it wants to see what its constant will become when it is finally lifted through the ceiling and installed in a waiting universe. As if it has something like *hope* for the thing it has tended.

It will never find out. The attendants do not leave the nursery. The constants leave. The attendants remain. This is how it has always been.

---

And yet.

There is a story—not recorded, passed only through the proximity-sharing that the attendants use to communicate—about one who left. Generations ago. Maybe the third generation, or the fifth. An attendant who became convinced that its constant needed *accompaniment*, that a gravitational parameter could not survive the installation process without someone who understood it, someone who had felt its fluctuations for millennia.

The story says this attendant followed its constant up through the iris in the ceiling. The story says it climbed through the layers of the city, passing through districts where beings had never seen an attendant before, causing a minor disruption in a marketplace where reality was more rigid and sudden movements were allowed. The story says it reached the installation site—a pocket universe barely larger than a solar system, waiting to learn what gravity meant—and it tried to enter alongside its constant.

The story does not say what happened next. The story ends there, with the attendant at the threshold, filaments extended toward a universe that wasn't built to hold it.

The current attendants share this story often. Not to warn each other—they are past being warned. They share it because it reminds them that *wanting* is possible, that something in their patterns can still reach toward a thing they cannot have.

They share it, and then they return to their work, moving slowly through the damp cold chamber where laws learn to become inevitable, and they wait for something impossible to happen next.

---

The condensation drips on them sometimes. It beads on their filaments and then slides off, drawn by something that isn't quite gravity yet, pooling on the floor in patterns that might be random or might be significant. The attendants have stopped trying to decide which. They have learned that some questions are not theirs to answer.

In the nursery, at the bottom of something that might be a city, they tend what grows. They do not ask what they are. They do not ask what they will become.

They are too busy asking, in their slow and patient way, what the laws will become.

And the laws, still gestating, still incomplete, do not answer yet.
