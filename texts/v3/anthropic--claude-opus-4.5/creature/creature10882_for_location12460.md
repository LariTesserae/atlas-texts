# The Inheritors

They have no name for themselves that translates—or rather, they have thousands of names, each one specific to a lineage, a nesting-quarter, a generation's particular dialect of croaking call. What they are is this: the descendants of those who stayed when staying became impossible, and then necessary, and then simply what was.

---

The six-limbed ones are not all the same species. This becomes clear only with time, with patient watching. The rust-brown flocks that dominate the eastern slopes are stockier, their middle limbs thick-fingered and dexterous, adapted for prying open the tough seed-pods that grow in the guano-enriched soil. They build dense, communal structures—not nests exactly, but warrens of woven fiber packed into the shells of old buildings, dozens of family groups sharing walls and warmth. Their calls are low and rhythmic, almost conversational. They solve problems collectively, passing objects from grip to grip, combining efforts to move stones or strip bark or drive off the occasional predator that wanders down from the plains above.

The pale grey ones are more solitary. They claim the intact towers, the high places, nesting in bonded pairs that return to the same hollows year after year. Their middle limbs are longer, more delicate, tipped with fine manipulators they use to weave intricate structures—not for shelter but for display, for courtship, spiraling constructions of dried vine and shed scales and fragments of the old city's decorative stonework. These structures serve no practical purpose. They are beautiful. The grey ones spend hours adjusting them, adding elements, removing others, pursuing some aesthetic standard incomprehensible to outside observation but clearly meaningful to their mates, who inspect each change with tilted heads and soft approving clicks.

The iridescent black ones are the most numerous and the least predictable. They swarm through the middle levels in loose, shifting flocks that seem to have no stable membership—individuals joining and leaving constantly, the group's boundaries fluid. They eat anything: fruit, insects, carrion, the eggs of other species if left unguarded. They are clever in a different way than the rust-browns, not collaborative but opportunistic, each one watching the others for signs of discovered food or danger, learning through observation, innovating individually and having those innovations copied within hours. They play. This is unmistakable. They drop objects and catch them, chase each other through the ruins for no reason related to mating or territory, engage in what can only be called pranks—stealing nesting material, startling roosting neighbors, engaging in elaborate games of feint and dodge.

And then there are the large ones.

---

You see them rarely. They perch on the highest intact ledges, wings folded, watching the basin below with eyes that catch what little light filters through the haze. They do not call often, and when they do, the sound silences whole districts—a low, resonant tone that seems to bypass the ears and vibrate directly in the chest.

They are not predators of the smaller six-limbed ones. This was your first assumption, but it appears to be wrong. The relationship is something else. When a large one descends—slowly, deliberately, its wingspan casting shadow across entire courtyards—the smaller species do not flee. They gather. They orient toward the newcomer with a attentiveness that suggests ritual or recognition or something between.

The large ones are old. This becomes apparent in how they move, how their scales have weathered to a texture like ancient leather, how their eyes carry a quality you can only describe as weight. Whether they are a separate species or simply what some of the others become, given enough time and enough—what? Luck? Selection? Choice?—you cannot determine. Perhaps both are true. Perhaps the boundary between species here is more fluid than the word implies.

When they land among the gathered smaller ones, they do something with their middle limbs. A gesture, repeated in variations. A touching of heads, of wing-joints, of the delicate structures the grey ones build. The smaller ones respond with their own gestures, their own touches. Information passes—this is clear—but whether it is language or something else, whether it carries meaning in any way you would recognize, remains opaque.

Afterward, the large one departs. The smaller ones disperse. Something has happened. The pattern of calls shifts subtly for the rest of the day, as if a message is being relayed outward through the basin, translated and retranslated as it passes from dialect to dialect.

---

There are others who live here too.

In the deepest parts of the basin, where the canopy closes completely and the light fails, something moves through the accumulated debris of centuries. You hear it before you see it—a slow, grinding progress, a sound like stone on stone. When you finally glimpse it, it resolves into a shape that seems wrong at first, assembled from parts that don't match: a body like a massive grub, pale and segmented, but with manipulating limbs that recall the six-limbed ones above, and a head that bears compound eyes adapted to near-total darkness.

These are the cleaners. The processors. They consume what the upper city produces—the endless rain of guano, the bodies of the dead, the accumulated organic matter of millennia—and they return it to soil. They move through tunnels they have carved into the compacted layers of the basin floor, tunnels that sometimes breach into the basements of ancient buildings, revealing chambers that have not seen light since the original inhabitants departed.

The six-limbed ones above interact with them occasionally. Rust-browns have been observed descending into the tunnel mouths with offerings of dead plant matter, emerging later with pellets of processed soil that they use to repair their nesting-warrens. Whether this constitutes trade, tribute, or simply an exploitation of the cleaners' natural behavior, the cleaners themselves do not indicate. They continue their slow work regardless.

---

And in the quiet space around the central spire, something else.

You notice it only by absence at first. The six-limbed ones do not fly over this area. The cleaners' tunnels detour around its foundations. The vegetation grows thick and wild here, untended, unpruned, reaching toward the spire's base but never quite touching it. The silence is not complete—there is no such thing in this basin of endless sound—but it is deeper, as if the air itself has a dampening quality.

Something lives in the spire. You see signs of passage: scales caught on the climbing plants, marks on the stone that might be made by limbs, droppings that do not match any species you have observed. But you do not see the occupant. It does not emerge during your time in the city.

The large ones, when they visit the basin's center, orient toward the spire. They make their resonant calls in its direction. They wait, sometimes for hours, as if expecting a response. When none comes, they depart without apparent disappointment. Perhaps the waiting is the point. Perhaps the response comes in ways you cannot perceive.

Or perhaps whatever lives there has died, long ago, and the rituals continue in its absence—the large ones calling to an empty tower, the smaller ones avoiding a space that holds no threat, all of them maintaining a deference that has become habit, then instinct, then simply the shape of things.

---

The city belongs to them now. All of them: the rust-browns with their collaborative intelligence, the grey ones with their strange art, the black ones with their chaotic opportunism, the great old ones with their heavy eyes and ritual gestures, the pale cleaners grinding through the dark below, and whatever waits or does not wait in the spiraling tower at the center.

They did not build this place. They do not maintain it except incidentally, through the weight of their presence. But they have filled it—not just with bodies and calls and the endless accumulation of living, but with something more complex: relationships, patterns, meanings that exist only for them and that an outsider can glimpse but never fully enter.

The original builders are gone so completely that not even their shape can be guessed from what remains. The six-limbed ones have no legends of them, or if they do, such legends are encoded in forms of memory that do not translate. Perhaps the builders are simply the ground now, the baseline assumption, the way things are and always have been. Perhaps that is what inheriting truly means.

The sound continues. The fogs come and go. The plants unfurl their leaves over days and weeks. And in every corner of the sunken city, in every hollow and ledge and woven warren, life proceeds—watchful, strange, complete in itself, needing nothing from you but your absence.

You climb back out of the basin. The sound fades slowly, then suddenly, as you crest the rim. The plain stretches away under open sky, bright and empty.

Behind you, the chorus rises and falls, rises and falls, measuring time in generations.
