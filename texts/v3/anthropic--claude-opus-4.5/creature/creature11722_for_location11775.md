# The Vault-Runners

They call themselves nothing. Names are for people who need to be found.

---

She emerges from a crevice you hadn't registered as passable—a diagonal slip in the basalt that she navigates sideways, her body knowing exactly which angle admits passage, which protrusion requires the shoulder to drop, the hip to cant. She is small and dense with muscle, her skin carrying the pallor of someone who has never seen direct sun, though not the sickly translucence of the deeper fauna. There's pigment in her still, a warmth beneath the pale, as though her ancestors remembered light and passed that memory down through generations of darkness.

Her eyes are wrong for the surface. Not blind—far from blind—but adapted. The pupils are vast, drinking what little light exists, and they don't contract the way yours do. She's squinting against your presence, against whatever faint luminescence you've brought with you, and you realize that to her, you are probably haloed, difficult to look at directly.

She carries tools the way you carry bones inside your body: invisibly, essentially, in places that seem impossible until you see them deployed. The obsidian blade comes from somewhere at her hip. The bone hook was tucked against her forearm. The net—folded so small you wouldn't have believed it could expand—rides against the small of her back, held by a strap of that strange woven material.

She watches you for a long moment, her head tilted at an angle that suggests she's listening as much as looking. Reading the way your weight settles. Tracking something in your breathing, maybe, or your heartbeat, or the particular frequency of your fear.

Then she clicks her tongue against her teeth—three short sounds, one long—and the shadows behind her produce two more.

---

The Vault-Runners move in family groups, though "family" means something different down here than it does above.

There's the woman who emerged first—she's the scout, the point-runner, the one who goes ahead and determines whether a passage is safe, whether a hunting ground is occupied, whether a stranger is a threat. She is perhaps thirty, though age is difficult to gauge in people who have never weathered under sun. Her hands are a map of small scars, obsidian-cuts healed pale against her paleness, and her feet are bare and calloused to something approaching leather.

Behind her comes an older man, his hair gone white in patches, his movements slower but more deliberate. He carries the weight of the group's supplies—dried meat in pouches that hang from a harness across his chest, water in bladders made from something's stomach, spare blades and cordage and the small precious things that cannot be manufactured here. He is the keeper of the cache knowledge, the one who remembers which shelter holds which tools, who maintains the network of resources spread across the vault's impossible expanse.

The third is young, perhaps sixteen, and carries nothing but a long pole tipped with a barbed bone point. They're thin in a way that speaks of recent growth, their body still learning its new proportions, and they watch you with a curiosity that the older two don't share. They haven't learned yet that strangers are more likely to bring danger than wonder.

There's a fourth presence you feel more than see—someone in the passage behind them, hanging back, ready to run or fight or disappear. The escape route, kept open. The contingency.

This is how they survive: distributed, prepared, always with somewhere to retreat to. The vault has taught them that nothing is permanent, and so they have learned not to need permanence.

---

They don't speak to you, not at first.

Instead, the scout begins a slow circuit around your position, staying at the edge of what she probably calculates as your striking range. She's not afraid—you can see that in the looseness of her shoulders, the way her blade hand stays relaxed. She's assessing. Determining what you are, what you might want, what resources you might represent or threaten.

The older man settles into a crouch, his weight balanced, and begins to unpack something from his harness. Food, you realize—a strip of dried meat that he tears with his teeth and chews slowly, never taking his eyes off you. It's a demonstration: *I am comfortable enough to eat. You do not panic me. But I am watching.*

The young one edges closer, and the scout clicks at them sharply—that same three-short-one-long pattern—and they freeze, then retreat a step. Discipline, maintained through sound. You wonder how many of their communications happen in that register, in clicks and tongue-sounds that ride beneath the constant roar of the colonies, too subtle for outsiders to parse.

Finally, the scout completes her circuit. She stops directly in front of you, closer than you expected, close enough that you can see the fine down of hair on her forearms, the particular pattern of scarring on her hands, the slow steady movement of her breath.

"Surface," she says. Her voice is rough from disuse, the word scraped out like something excavated. "Why?"

---

They don't have names, but they have roles, and the roles shift.

The scout was not always the scout. Before her growth finished, before her reflexes settled into their current razor-state, she was a watcher—stationed at the colony-edges, tracking the patterns of the ceiling-creatures, learning to read the chaos for signs of imminent eruption. Before that, she was a gatherer, one of the children sent to harvest bacterial mat from the safer pools, her small fingers quick and precise.

The old man was a hunter once, back when his joints didn't ache in the humid air, back when he could sprint through a boulder field without calculating the cost. Now he carries and remembers and teaches, his value shifted from action to knowledge. He can tell you which passage floods when the thermal activity increases, which shelter has a fault line running beneath it, which trap-sites produce reliably and which have been abandoned by the prey.

The young one is learning everything at once, as all the young ones do. They hunt and gather and watch and scout, rotating through duties, building the comprehensive knowledge that survival here requires. In a few years, if they live, they'll specialize—their body will make the choice for them, developing the particular strengths that suit one role over others. Until then, they're a generalist, which means they're also the most expendable.

The fourth one, still hidden in the passage, is pregnant. Seven months along, maybe eight. She stays back because the group cannot afford to lose her—not her specifically, but what she carries. Children are rare. The vault takes many of them before they can take the vault in return.

---

Here is what they know that you do not:

They know that the colony-creatures sleep in shifts, a rolling wave of dormancy that circles the ceiling in a pattern tied to thermal fluctuations far below. If you time it right, you can harvest sleeping individuals from the lower-hanging clusters—protein and leather and small bones useful for hooks and needles. If you time it wrong, you trigger a panic-loss that can knock you from your climbing perch and break you on the stones two hundred meters below.

They know that the serpentine hunters are more dangerous in the cooler periods, when they seek the warmth of living bodies, when they're drawn to you across distances that seem impossible. They know that the mineral-stranded webs of the spider-analogues can be harvested carefully, dissolved in the acidic pools near the southern vents, reconstituted into a thread stronger than anything that exists in the world above. This is what their nets are woven from. This is what holds their shelters together.

They know the rhythm of the vault's geology—which areas are actively forming and must be avoided, which collapses are imminent, which new passages have opened and what they might connect to. They read the stone the way you read weather: an intuitive parsing of signs that has become so instinctive they couldn't explain it if they tried.

They know where the good water collects, filtered through limestone, cool and clean. They know which bacterial mats are edible in desperation, which mineral deposits can be ground into pigments for the maps they scratch into stone, which bones burn longest and hottest.

They know that the vault is growing. Year by year, generation by generation, the thermal activity is increasing, the passages are extending, the habitable range is expanding in directions their grandparents never explored. They are not a remnant population clinging to a shrinking territory. They are pioneers, edging into newly-formed wilderness, claiming spaces that have existed for less time than they have.

They know that they are not alone.

---

Other groups move through the vault system. Some are related to them by blood or barter or old alliance; these are greeted with the three-short-one-long click, with the lowering of weapons, with an exchange of news and resources before each group continues on its circuit. Some are competitors, desperate or territorial, and these are avoided where possible, fought where necessary. The scars on the scout's hands are not all from obsidian.

There are places in the vault where the groups meet by agreement, neutral grounds where violence is prohibited by a consensus no one remembers codifying. Here, they trade: dried meat for obsidian blanks, mineral-thread for cached water, information for information. Here, the young ones eye each other across the steam-fog, recognizing the possibility of partners who won't share their bloodline.

And here, occasionally, they speak of the deeper places.

---

The scout doesn't tell you this directly. She asks her questions—where you came from, how you found the fissures, what you want—and she weighs your answers against something you cannot see. Eventually, she makes a decision. She flicks her blade back into its hidden sheath. She clicks twice, a different pattern, and the others relax in ways you barely perceive.

She's not welcoming you. She's permitting you. There's a difference, and the difference matters.

"Follow," she says. "Stay behind the carrier. Don't touch the pools. Don't startle the young one."

The young one bristles at this, and the scout ignores them with the ease of long practice. She turns and begins to walk, and the old man falls in behind her, and you fall in behind him, and the young one and the pregnant woman—you catch a glimpse of her now, finally, her belly round beneath a wrap of woven mineral-thread—bring up the rear.

They're taking you somewhere. You don't know where. You don't know why. You only know that you've been measured against criteria you can't identify, and you've been found acceptable for something, and the something is about to become apparent.

The vault continues around you: the endless sound, the endless motion, the endless birth and consumption and rebirth. A salamander-thing skitters across your path and the old man's hand moves with unconscious speed, snatching it, killing it with a quick twist, stowing it in one of his pouches. Provisions. Nothing wasted.

Ahead, the scout navigates passages that look identical to you, turning with confidence, never hesitating. Her bare feet know this stone. Her body knows these walls. She was born down here, in one of those shelters carved from the living rock, and she will die down here, and her bones will be scattered by the scavengers, and the beetles will consume them for calcium, and the vault will reclaim her the way it reclaims everything.

She doesn't mind this. It doesn't occur to her to mind it. This is not a tragedy; it's an ecology. She is part of something vast and hungry and generative, and her part is to move through it for a time, harvesting what she can, contributing what she must, until her time runs out.

You follow her deeper into the darkness, into the warmth, into the endless process of becoming.

Somewhere ahead, something is waiting to be shown to you. Something the Vault-Runners have found. Something they don't understand.

That's why they permitted you. You come from the surface. You might know what it means.

The scout glances back at you once, her vast pupils catching some faint gleam, her expression unreadable.

"Far now," she says. "Very far. You keep up, or we leave you."

She doesn't wait for your answer. She already knows you'll follow.

In the vault, everyone follows eventually. The only question is whether you follow the living or join the dead.

She clicks once—a single sharp sound—and the group picks up its pace, and the darkness closes around you like a throat, and the world continues being born.
