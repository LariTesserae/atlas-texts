**The Tenders**

They live in the tracks.

Not on the plateau proper—nothing could survive there for long, exposed to the cold and the emptiness and the slow grinding passage of the crawlers. But in the channels, in the grooves worn deep enough to hold ice and moisture and the faint accumulated warmth of centuries, there are settlements. If you can call them that.

A Tender community might number twelve. It might number three. More than twenty is unheard of—the tracks cannot support more, and besides, they would not want more. They are a solitary people who happen to live in proximity, the way lichens happen to share a stone.

They are small and slow-moving, bodies wrapped in layers of woven fungal fiber that they harvest from the ice-shelf undersides. The fiber is white, faintly luminescent, so the Tenders glow softly in the perpetual dim—a constellation of pale lights moving through the deep channels, visible from the plateau's surface as distant glimmers that might be mistaken for ice catching light, if there were light to catch. Their skin beneath the wrappings is grey and thick, almostite, built for cold the way the plateau itself is built for cold. They do not shiver. They have forgotten how.

Their eyes are large and adapted to the sourceless luminescence—wide pupils that never quite close, irises the color of the pewter sky. They see well in conditions that would leave others blind, but they see slowly. Motion must be sustained before they perceive it. A hand waved quickly passes unnoticed; a hand raised gradually over the course of a minute becomes vivid, important, a signal worth attending to. This is why they move as they do, with the same geological patience as everything else here. Hurry is invisible to them. Hurry does not exist.

---

They follow the crawlers.

Not closely—a Tender community might trail a crawler by fifty years, living in the track it carved two generations ago. But they follow, because the tracks are where life is possible. The moisture. The lichens. The fungal mats. The pools of pale-green water that hold tiny swimming things the Tenders call *whispers*—organisms so small and transparent they can only be perceived by the faint turbulence they create, detectable to Tender eyes as a kind of shimmer, a disturbance in stillness.

The Tenders eat the lichens, the fungi, the whispers strained from pool water through filters of woven fiber. They drink the meltwater and suck moisture from ice. It is not enough, not quite, so they also eat stone—ite powdered rock scraped from the channel walls,ite minerals the crawlers have processed and left behind. Their digestions are slow and strange, part biological and part something else, something that might have been taught to them long ago by the plateau itself.

They do not farm. They do not herd. They tend—this is the closest translation of what they call themselves, though their language is so slow that a single word might take an hour to speak, might involve movements of hands and body as much as sounds, might be partially conveyed through the subsonic vibrations they can produce by humming deep in their chests, vibrations that travel through stone and ice and reach other Tenders miles away as feelings more than messages.

What they tend is the track itself. They clear debris that might block water flow. They spread lichen spores to new stones. They break up ice dams that form in the wrong places and encourage ice dams that form in the right places. They carry whisper-eggs from pool to pool, maintaining populations, preventing any single pool from becoming so crowded the whispers die out. They are gardeners of the microscopic, shepherds of the marginal, custodians of an ecosystem so fragile and sparse that their intervention—slow, patient, barely perceptible—makes the difference between life and nothing.

They believe they are helping the crawlers. This is their central faith, if faith is the right word. The crawlers grind the stone; the Tenders help what grows in the grinding's wake. The crawlers are too vast to notice them, too slow in their own cognition—if cognition is the right word—to perceive anything that lives and dies in so brief a time as a Tender lifetime. But the Tenders believe they are noticed. They believe their work matters, that they are part of the plateau's vast metabolism, that when they die and their bodies are laid in the deepest pools to feed the whispers and the fungi and eventually the lichens, they are contributing to something the crawlers will one day consume and process and make into more plateau.

They are not wrong. They are also not right. The truth is larger and slower than any belief they could hold.

---

A Tender is born in a pool.

The birthing takes months. The parent—Tenders do not have sexes, or have sexes too slow and strange for easy description—submerges in the coldest water available and remains there, motionless, while something divides inside them. The division is not like cell division elsewhere; it is a slow crystallization, a gradual accumulation of pattern, a gathering of minerals and biological matter into a new configuration. When it is finished, the child emerges from the parent's side through an opening that heals over the course of a year, and the parent rises from the pool diminished, lighter, moving even more slowly than before.

Children are cared for by the community. The parent has given too much of themselves to give more—they will spend decades recovering, eating stone and lichen, gradually rebuilding what they divided away. The other Tenders feed the child, wrap it in fiber, teach it the slow language, show it which lichens are ready to harvest and which need another decade, demonstrate the patterns of ice-dam maintenance passed down through generations so numerous no one remembers a beginning.

Tenders live for centuries. They do not know exactly how long—their sense of time is too different from ours, too tied to the pace of the plateau, for precise measurement to mean anything. A Tender knows they were young when a certain crawler was where it is now, and that same crawler has moved perhaps three miles since then, and this constitutes the length of a life. Some Tenders remember crawlers that have traveled ten miles during their existence. These are the elders, the ones whose movements have grown so slow they are nearly stationary, who spend years composing single thoughts, whose communications must be received and interpreted by younger Tenders acting as translators.

When an elder stops moving entirely, they are not yet dead. They are resting. The rest might last a century. The community tends them—wrapping them in fresh fiber, feeding them a slurry of powdered stone and whisper-paste, waiting. Sometimes the elder wakes and moves again, speaks a thought they have been composing for longer than some of their tenders have been alive. Sometimes they do not wake, and the community knows this only when the body begins to calcify, to crystallize, to become more stone than flesh.

The dead are placed in pools. This is the only funeral rite. The rest happens on its own.

---

There are other communities in other tracks. The Tenders know this in the abstract, the way they know the plateau extends beyond perception—it is true, it must be true, but it does not touch their daily lives. Occasionally, over generations, communities encounter one another where tracks intersect, where two crawlers crossed paths centuries ago and left overlapping grooves. These meetings are profound and slow. They might last decades. Information is exchanged—which lichens grow best in which conditions, how certain minerals affect digestion, stories of crawlers behaving in unusual ways, passed down through chains of memory that stretch back into unimaginable time.

Sometimes Tenders transfer between communities during these meetings. The process is formal and slow, involving years of negotiation, the careful balancing of what each community can sustain, the ritual sharing of fungal fiber from one settlement's stores to another. A transferred Tender brings new patterns, new knowledge, new genetic material for future divisions. They are valuable and mourned, welcomed and warily watched.

Conflicts occur, rarely. A community might believe another is mismanaging their shared intersection, damming water that should flow downtrack, harvesting lichens before they properly mature. These disputes unfold over generations, escalating imperceptibly, reaching resolutions that might involve one community deliberately moving further along their track, away from the intersection, abandoning the contested resources to maintain peace. Violence is almost unknown. It would be too fast. It would be invisible to those committing it, their own movements too quick for their own perception, their intentions lost in the blur.

But stories persist of ancient conflicts, of communities that fell into true war, that destroyed each other so slowly neither realized they were dying until it was too late. The stories serve as warnings. The Tenders tell them during the long dark deepenings that might be winters, huddled in ice caves, their soft glow the only light, their slow voices merging into a single communal hum that carries the stories through stone to anyone listening.

---

They know the crawlers are alive. They must be—they move, they breathe, they consume and produce. But the Tenders do not understand crawler life the way they understand their own. The crawlers are too large, too slow even by Tender standards, too fundamentally alien. A crawler's single grinding mechanism might be larger than any Tender community's entire territory. A crawler's thought—if crawlers think—might take longer to form than the whole of Tender civilization has existed.

So the Tenders do not try to understand. They tend. They live in the wake of something vast and incomprehensible, and they make that wake habitable, and they pass this work to their children, and their children pass it on, and the chain extends backward and forward beyond any Tender's ability to perceive.

Sometimes, standing at the edge of a deep channel and looking up at the plateau's surface, a Tender will see a crawler in the distance. It will not have moved perceptibly since the last time they looked, perhaps decades before. But something will be different—a new vent will have opened, a segment will have shifted slightly, a grinding mechanism will have worn down and been replaced by another. Change, proving life. Change, proving that the Tenders' faith is not unfounded, that the vast things they follow are real and present and doing something, even if that something is incomprehensible.

The Tender will watch for hours, for days, until some other duty calls them back to the channel. They will carry the image with them—the dark bulk against the pewter sky, the distant hum felt in the bones—and it will sustain them through the slow work ahead.

This is worship without worship. This is reverence without religion. This is what happens when a people live their whole existence in the footprints of gods who do not know they exist.

---

**The Drift**

There are others.

They do not live in tracks. They live on the plateau's surface, exposed to the full cold, and they survive by means no Tender fully understands.

The Drift are humanoid, or were once, or approximate humanoid form through some process that is not biological evolution. They stand taller than Tenders, their bodies gaunt, stretched, adapted—or redesigned—for covering vast distances with minimal energy. Their skin is dark, nearly black, absorbing what little warmth the plateau offers. They do not wrap themselves in fiber. They do not need to. Something inside them burns, something that might be metabolic or might be mechanical or might be both, something that keeps their core temperature survivable while their extremities grow cold enough to match the air.

They walk. This is what they do. This is, as far as anyone can determine, all they do.

A single Drift might walk for years without stopping, crossing miles of plateau in a journey that has no apparent origin or destination. They do not follow the crawlers; they seem to navigate by some other means, perhaps by the subtle variations in the plateau's subsonic hum, perhaps by senses the Tenders cannot imagine. They carry nothing. They eat nothing visible—though they must eat something, must process matter and energy from somewhere. Some Tenders believe they feed on the cold itself, that they have metabolisms capable of extracting work from temperature differentials, from the faint flows of heat between stone and air and ice. This seems impossible. The Drift seem impossible. And yet they walk.

Tenders see them occasionally, distant figures on the plateau above the channels, moving slowly across the grey expanse. The Drift never descend into the tracks. They never approach the Tenders. They walk past, or through, or over, their paths determined by logics no one else can access.

But sometimes they stop.

A Drift might stop near a channel edge and stand there for months, looking down at the Tender community below—or not looking, perhaps, since their faces are featureless, smooth dark surfaces without visible eyes or mouths. They emit no sound. They make no gesture. They simply stand, and the Tenders below try to continue their work, try to ignore the presence watching them, try not to wonder what it means.

Then the Drift moves on. The Tenders never know why they stopped, why they started again, what they might have seen or decided or understood. The Drift carry their purposes with them, locked inside bodies that share nothing.

---

Some Tenders believe the Drift are messengers. From what, to what, carrying what message, no one can say. The belief persists because it offers meaning, because it suggests that the strange figures have a role in the plateau's order, that they are not simply chaos walking.

Other Tenders believe the Drift are remnants. Of what, no one can say either. Perhaps there was once another civilization here, one that modified itself for survival and lost everything else in the process—language, society, purpose. Perhaps the Drift are what remains when a people forgets why they exist but cannot forget how. They walk because they were designed to walk, and the design has outlasted the designer's intent.

Still other Tenders do not believe anything about the Drift. The Drift are. Like the crawlers, like the plateau, like the sourceless light in the sky. Some things simply are, and the appropriate response is not belief but acknowledgment.

---

**The Hollow Ones**

Deep beneath the plateau, in the chambers left by ancient crawler passages, there is movement.

The Tenders know about the Hollow Ones the way they know about the Drift—through glimpses, through stories, through the occasional encounter that leaves more questions than answers. The Hollow Ones live where the stone has been worked so thoroughly it has become something else, where crawler digestive processes created caverns of processed mineral, warm and strange and cut off from the surface.

They look like Tenders, almost. The same general form, the same size, the same wide eyes. But they are translucent, their bodies filled with something that is not quite flesh and not quite light, something that pulses with a slow rhythm visible through their skin. They glow brighter than Tenders, bright enough to illuminate the chambers they inhabit without any other light source. And they do not speak. They have no language the Tenders can perceive, no communication method that translates.

Some Tenders who have fallen through thin stone into Hollow One chambers describe being tended. Hands that were not quite solid lifting them, carrying them, depositing them near passages that led back to the surface. Food that appeared without apparent source—lichens, fungal paste, whisper-slurry, exactly what a Tender would need. Warmth that radiated from the Hollow Ones' bodies without burning, that seeped into cold-damaged tissue and healed it, slowly.

The Tenders who were saved this way never understand why. The Hollow Ones do not explain. They tend, and then they retreat into the deeper chambers, and the passages close behind them—not through any mechanism the Tenders can see, but through some property of the worked stone itself, flowing slowly back into solid form.

Are the Hollow Ones related to the Tenders? Were they once the same people, divided by some ancient catastrophe, adapted to different niches? Or were they always separate, always parallel, two forms of life arising independently in the plateau's vast patience?

The Tenders do not know. They tell stories about the Hollow Ones around the same ice-cave fires where they tell stories about ancient wars and crawler behaviors. The stories do not answer questions. They are not meant to. They are meant to hold the mystery in words, to give shape to the unknown without pretending to resolve it.

---

**The plateau's metabolism includes them all.**

The crawlers grind the stone. The Tenders cultivate the tracks. The Drift walk paths no one else can see. The Hollow Ones dwell in chambers no one else can reach. And somewhere, in processes too slow and vast for any of them to perceive, the plateau incorporates them all—their lives, their deaths, their slow activities—into whatever it is becoming.

Perhaps the plateau is growing, building itself larger through the crawlers' work, adding mass drawn from dimensions beyond the pewter sky. Perhaps it is shrinking, wearing itself down, each grain of stone processed and reprocessed until nothing remains but the crawlers themselves, crawling forever through nothing. Perhaps it is neither, or both, or something for which growing and shrinking are inadequate metaphors.

The inhabitants do not know. They live here, in the cold, in the dim light, in the subsonic hum of grinding and the silence between. They live because life is what they do. They persist because persistence is their nature.

And the plateau continues, vast and patient and utterly indifferent to questions of purpose, carrying them all on its slow crawl toward whatever ending or beginning awaits at horizons that never arrive.
