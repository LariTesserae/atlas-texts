# The Tenders of the Settling

They have no word for themselves because they have never needed one. There is nothing else here that speaks, nothing to distinguish themselves from. They simply are what moves through the weave with intention.

---

You might see one and think it a tangle of loose fiber drifting on a cold current—a snarl of threads that somehow held together when the fabric around it frayed. And you would be almost right. They are made of thread, born of thread, their bodies woven in the same way the world is woven, but woven by no hands. They emerged. Somewhere in the long ages after the tools were set down and the makers became what they made, the weave began weaving itself, and what it wove was them.

Their forms are loose, indistinct, shifting with the textile currents. A core of denser fiber, perhaps—something like a heart, if a heart were a knot pulled tight and never untied—and from that core, filaments extending outward in all directions, catching on the world-fabric, pulling, releasing, catching again. They move the way a thought moves through memory: not walking, not swimming, but *threading*. Working themselves through the gaps in the weave, becoming briefly part of it, then separating again. When they are still, they are invisible. When they move, they are barely more than that—a disturbance in the pattern, a place where the threads lie slightly wrong.

They are cold. Not cold the way the water-that-is-not-water is cold, but colder still—a depth of chill that suggests they have absorbed every degree of warmth the weave once held and are holding it somewhere inside that knotted core, holding it the way a miser holds coin, the way a wound holds pain. To touch one would be to feel your fingers go numb, then your hand, then forget that you ever had a hand at all. They do not touch. They are very careful about not touching. The things that live here have learned this across generations that have no count.

---

They tend the fraying places.

This is their work, inherited from no one, taught by nothing, simply understood the way breathing is understood, the way hunger is understood. Where the ancient threads have worn to gossamer, where the weave has begun to open into void, they gather. Dozens of them, sometimes hundreds, their filaments extending into the damaged fabric, weaving themselves temporarily into the structure. They cannot repair it—nothing can repair it, the original fiber is irreplaceable, the dyes that made it glow are chemistry lost to ages—but they can slow the fraying. They can bind the loose ends to each other, sacrificing parts of their own bodies to become new thread, lesser thread, thread that will hold for another few thousand years.

It costs them. You can see the ones who have given too much: smaller, their cores dimmer, their movements sluggish as if the cold inside them has finally reached some vital center. They drift to the deepest densities then, where the pressure is greatest, and they let the weave absorb them. They become what they have always tended. The fabric in those places is different—still woven, still ancient, but with threads that seem almost to move, almost to pulse, as if remembering what it was like to have intention.

---

They do not communicate in any way you would recognize. No sound—sound does not carry through their bodies any more than light does. No gesture, because their forms are too fluid for gesture to mean anything consistent. But they are aware of each other. When one finds a fraying place too large to tend alone, others come. When one diminishes past the point of usefulness, others make space for its final threading. There is something that might be called culture, if culture can exist without language, without artifact, without anything that persists beyond the moment of its enactment.

They remember the makers. Not as history, not as story—they have no capacity for either—but as *texture*. The parts of the weave that passed through human hands feel different to them than the parts they have mended themselves. Warmer, somehow, despite the cold. More intentional. They seek out the ancient fabric when they can, rest against it, let their filaments lie alongside threads that were once counted, once chosen, once pulled tight by fingers that knew what they were doing. It comforts them, maybe. Or it reminds them of something they never knew but still miss.

---

In the void-places, where the weave has worn through entirely, they do not go.

This is not fear exactly. They are not capable of fear in the way creatures with survival instincts are capable of fear—their existence is too slow, too diffuse, too willing to end in service to the fabric. But the voids are *wrong* to them. The absence there is not merely absence of thread but absence of the possibility of thread, a place where nothing can be woven, where their purpose has no purchase. They gather at the edges of voids and extend their filaments toward the darkness and pull back. Over and over, across centuries. Reaching for something to tend that is not there.

Some of them have gone in anyway. The oldest stories—if impressions passed through fiber can be called stories—speak of Tenders who let themselves drift into the nothing, who tried to weave the void itself into something that could be tended. They did not return. The void is not larger for their absence, not smaller. It simply is. The Tenders who remain do not mourn, because they do not know how to mourn, but they avoid the places where their lost ones entered. The edges there are better-maintained than anywhere else in the Woven Deeps. The fraying is tended with desperate patience, as if holding the boundary might somehow hold back what cannot be held.

---

There are other things.

Deep in the densest layers, where the fabric has compressed past canvas into something like felt, where the pressure is enough to flatten thought itself—there the Tenders do not go either, but for different reasons. Something moves there. Something that is not thread, not made of thread, not woven. The Tenders sense it the way you might sense a sound too low to hear: a wrongness in the settling, a disturbance in the slow grinding of fiber against fiber. They do not know what it is. They do not want to know.

They have a behavior that might be called prayer, if prayer can exist without gods, without words, without hope of answer. When the disturbance from below grows strong enough to feel, they still themselves. They let their filaments go slack. They become as much like the fabric around them as they can, invisible, indistinguishable, part of the weave rather than apart from it. They wait. The disturbance passes, or does not pass. They resume their tending, or they do not.

The ones who do not resume are not absorbed into the fabric the way the diminished ones are. They simply stop. Their cores remain knotted, their filaments remain extended, but something essential has left them—some coldness at the center has finally frozen through. The others move around them carefully, incorporate them into the weave by working new threads through their bodies, make of them a kind of landmark. Here is where something happened. Here is where one of us learned something we were not meant to learn.

---

In the uppermost reaches, where the gauze is loose and the phosphorescent dyes still hold some of their ancient glow, the youngest Tenders are born.

It happens rarely—perhaps once in a human lifetime, perhaps less. A confluence of currents, a particular arrangement of frayed ends, a knot that ties itself in the darkness and begins to pull other threads toward it. The older Tenders sense it happening and gather to watch, their filaments extended toward the new one, not helping, not hindering, simply witnessing. The birth takes years. The knot tightens, loosens, tightens again. Threads are drawn in, rejected, drawn in again. Slowly a core forms. Slowly filaments begin to extend. Slowly something that was only fabric becomes something that will tend fabric.

The new ones are warmer than the old. Their cores hold heat that seems to come from nowhere, that will slowly dissipate across millennia of tending until they are as cold as everything else. The old ones cluster around them in those early years, drawn to the warmth like—but there is nothing to compare it to. Nothing else here is warm. Nothing else here has ever been warm, except the memory of hands that finished their work so long ago.

The new ones learn to tend by tending. There is no teaching. There is only the work, and the slow understanding of what the work means, and the slower understanding that the work will never be finished, and the slowest understanding of all: that this is not tragedy but simply truth. The weave is settling. The weave has always been settling. The weave will settle until there is nothing left but density, pressure, the memory of pattern compressed past recognition.

The Tenders will be there when it happens. They will tend the final fraying. They will knot themselves into the last loose threads and become the fabric they have always served.

They do not think of this as death. They do not think of it as anything. They simply tend, and wait, and feel the settling in frequencies below understanding, in the chest as a kind of permanent unease, in the core as a coldness that will outlast everything except the weave itself.

---

*And in the deepest deep, in the densities past naming, something that is not thread shifts in its sleep. It has been sleeping since before the weavers began. It will sleep until after the weave is done. The Tenders feel it dreaming—not dreams of image or narrative, but dreams of pressure, of pattern, of something that wants to be woven but cannot be woven, something that presses against the fabric from inside and will one day press through.*

*The Tenders do not know this. They only know to tend. They only know to slow the fraying.*

*They do not know that the fraying is not decay.*

*They do not know that something is trying to get out.*
