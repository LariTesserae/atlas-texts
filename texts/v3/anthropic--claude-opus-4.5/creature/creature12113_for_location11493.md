**The Keepers of Pace**

They call themselves nothing. Names require the kind of distinction they abandoned generations ago.

You will not see them at first. You will see evidence: a worn smoothness on certain fungal stairs, a rhythm to how leaf-litter accumulates in corners (swept, though you never witness sweeping), fruit rinds composting in designated hollows. Then, after days, you'll notice a figure standing in a plaza you've crossed twenty times—and you'll realize they were always there, moving so slowly your eye parsed them as furniture.

They are human, or were, or are becoming something adjacent. Their skin has taken on the grey-brown of bark, not through disease but through patience. They eat what the plateau offers—fruit that grows in the cathedral-nodes, fungi that shelf out from corridor walls, water from the welling fountains. They have eaten nothing else for so long that their bodies have simplified. Their digestion has gentled. Their needs have narrowed to a point.

A single Keeper might occupy the same room for a decade. This is not stillness but *slowness*—they move, they act, they even converse, but at a tempo matched to the plateau's breath. Watch one for an afternoon and you'll see them complete perhaps a single gesture: reaching for a cup, lifting it, bringing it to lips. Watch for a season and you'll see them cross a room, tend a growth, return to where they started. Their metabolism has downshifted to something the plateau recognizes as kin.

They do not age. Or rather: they age at the plateau's rate, which means imperceptibly, which means not at all in any human timeframe. The eldest among them—if eldest means anything when everyone moves through time at the same crawl—remember when the plateau's center was still growing. They remember the sound of bark hardening. They were young then, though young is a word that has lost its urgency.

**What They Do**

The Keepers tend. Not in the way a gardener tends, imposing will on growth—but in the way a cell tends to a body. They clear channels when debris accumulates faster than the plateau can absorb it. They guide new growth at the margins, not shaping it but suggesting: a touch here encouraging a wall to curve rather than collapse, a weight there convincing a root to bridge a gap. They are the plateau's immune response, its fine motor control, the precision it cannot achieve alone.

This is their purpose, but purpose is too urgent a word. They do this because they are part of the plateau now, and the plateau does this through them.

They communicate in gestures that take hours to complete. A raised hand, held, slowly rotating at the wrist—this means something. A step taken toward another Keeper, then a pause of days, then another step—this is greeting, or argument, or love. They have not abandoned language; they have stretched it until each word carries the weight of a season. When they speak aloud, which is rare, their voices resonate with the plateau's hum. The words emerge over minutes. Visitors have mistaken their speech for wind, for water, for the creak of settling wood.

They remember everything. Not because their memories are extraordinary, but because they have time to revisit each memory endlessly, polishing it like river-stone, keeping it alive through attention. A Keeper who met you today will remember you in three centuries with perfect clarity—your face, your smell, the exact quality of your confusion.

**The Children**

There are no children among the Keepers. Not because they cannot reproduce, but because childhood requires a tempo the plateau does not offer. Occasionally someone arrives from below—a refugee, a pilgrim, a person who simply walked until they found somewhere that would have them—and if they stay long enough, if they slow themselves enough, they become Keeper. This takes decades. Most who attempt it leave within years, unable to bear the deceleration, the feeling of watching their own lives pool and thicken into something unrecognizable.

Those who stay describe the transition as a relief. The urgency drains out of them. The fear of death loses meaning when death has receded to a theoretical horizon. They stop wanting things and start *being* things—being a presence in a room, being a function in a system, being a note in the plateau's long chord.

**The Exceptions**

Not all inhabitants are Keepers.

In the hollow-gourd cisterns, the pale undulating things have developed something like culture. They are not intelligent—or they are intelligent in a way that doesn't translate—but they recognize each other, form preferences, engage in behaviors that serve no survival purpose. They spiral together in patterns. They tap against the cistern walls in sequences. The Keepers consider them neighbors, or pets, or children, or gods; the distinction has blurred past usefulness.

The leaf-feathered birds are more enigmatic. They do not flock but they do *correspond*—one will appear in a plaza, drift in a particular pattern, and days later another will appear elsewhere and drift in a pattern that seems to answer. The Keepers have spent centuries trying to learn this language. They have made no progress. They are not frustrated. Centuries more remain.

And then there are the **Visitors**.

They come rarely—perhaps one per generation, by Keeper reckoning, which means perhaps one per decade by the count of the lowlands. They climb the plateau's edges where the growth is young and soft, or they find the old stairs hidden in the weeping walls, and they emerge onto the moss-boulevards with loud footsteps and quick breath and eyes that dart everywhere at once.

The Keepers observe them. This observation takes weeks; by then, most Visitors have left, overwhelmed by the silence, unnerved by the figures who might be statues, might be corpses, might be watching. Those who stay longer receive a different reception. A Keeper will approach—this approach taking days—and when they finally arrive within speaking distance, they will ask a question that has been forming since the Visitor first appeared:

*Why hurry?*

It is not rhetorical. They genuinely want to know. They have forgotten.

**What They Want**

Nothing. Something. The plateau's continuation, which is their continuation, which is the same thing. They want the equilibrium to hold. They want the hum to persist. They want the rain to fall through the designated channels and the margins to keep growing and the cistern-dwellers to keep spiraling and the bird-things to keep corresponding in their untranslatable way.

They want, if want is the word, for you to understand that this is not death. It is not hibernation. It is not emptiness.

It is a different fullness. A slower saturation. A life so extended it has room for everything—every thought, every sensation, every moment—because no moment is rushing toward the next.

You could join them. The plateau has made space for you. It has always been making space for you, in the way it makes space for everything: patiently, incrementally, without urgency or demand.

You would only have to slow down.
