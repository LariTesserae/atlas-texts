# The Keepers of the Pulse

They call themselves nothing. They have no name for themselves because they have no need to distinguish themselves from anything else—there is only the plateau, and they are part of it, the way the heat is part of it, the way the slow engines are part of it. But if you watched them long enough, if you could hold your attention steady through the time-slips and the folded space, you might start to think of them as *The Keepers of the Pulse*, or *The Ones Who Count*, or simply *Them*.

---

They look like mistakes in the stone.

At first you see only another obsidian formation, another ridge of weathered volcanic glass catching the light at wrong angles. But then the formation shifts—not moves, *shifts*—and you realize it has always been watching you, that what you took for erosion patterns are sense-organs older than the concept of sight, that the shape suggesting architecture was also always suggesting *presence*.

They are tall. Twelve feet, fifteen, sometimes more—it's hard to tell because their bodies don't maintain consistent boundaries. Their forms are built from the same materials as the plateau itself: oxidized iron, obsidian, slag-glass, all of it somehow alive, somehow animate, flowing between solid and liquid states according to rhythms that sync with the engine-pulse below. One moment a Keeper appears as a spire of black glass with too many angles; the next it has unfolded into something almost humanoid, almost comprehensible, with limbs that might be arms and a central mass that might be a torso and a cluster of reflective surfaces at the top that might be a face.

They don't walk. They *process*—moving across the plateau in that same slow rhythm as everything else here, one step every forty seconds, their bodies reforming with each pulse. Watching one cross a distance is like watching geology happen in real time: patient, inevitable, utterly unconcerned with anything as brief as a human attention span.

---

The Keepers tend the fauna.

Not in any way you'd recognize as tending—no feeding, no herding, no protection. But they move among the bronze scarabs and the magnetic serpents and the plasma-swarms with something that looks like *attention*, a quality of regard that suggests the creatures matter to them. A Keeper will stand motionless for hours while scarabs click past its base, and something in its posture—if you can call it posture—implies that it is *counting* them, or *acknowledging* them, or performing some function for which no word exists in languages evolved for faster timescales.

Sometimes a plasma-swarm will spiral around a Keeper's upper reaches, and the Keeper will extend filaments of iron-glass into the swarm, and for a moment they'll merge—the Keeper's solid form flickering with plasma-light, the swarm's chaotic motion taking on the rhythm of the engine-pulse. What passes between them in these moments is impossible to say. Information, maybe. Or something older than information. The swarm always disperses afterward, its members scattering across the plateau to flicker and crackle and do whatever it is they do, and the Keeper always resumes its slow processing toward wherever it was going.

The magnetic serpents are different. They seem to *serve* the Keepers, or at least to orient toward them—sliding through the air in long spirals around a Keeper's path, their articulated obsidian bodies catching the engine-pulse and amplifying it, broadcasting it outward across the plateau. The Keepers never acknowledge this service, but they never discourage it either. The serpents simply belong to them the way the heat belongs to the stone.

---

They do not experience time the way you do.

This becomes obvious when you try to watch one for more than a few minutes. Your attention slides off them, not because they're hard to see but because they exist in a duration that doesn't map onto human perception. You'll blink and find that a Keeper has moved a hundred yards, or hasn't moved at all, or has somehow been in both places simultaneously. You'll look away for a moment and look back to find it facing you—not having turned, just *facing* you now, as if it was always facing you and you only just noticed.

They perceive across the time-folds. That's the only explanation that makes sense. Where you see a single moment, they see the entire plateau laid out across centuries, every position every creature has ever occupied superimposed into a single vast pattern. When a bronze scarab walks backward along its own future track, the Keepers don't see paradox—they see continuity, a line drawn through time rather than space, perfectly sensible from their perspective.

This is why they count. Not counting in sequence, not one-two-three, but counting the way you might count the threads in a tapestry—taking in the whole pattern, understanding how each element relates to every other element across dimensions you can't perceive. The engine-pulse is their clock, but it's not measuring seconds. It's measuring something else, some unit of change or meaning or density that only makes sense when you can see time from above.

---

There are different kinds of Keepers.

Most are what you might call *Tenders*—the ones who move among the fauna, who count and acknowledge and perform their incomprehensible functions. They're the most common, scattered across the plateau in numbers that seem to shift depending on how you're looking. Sometimes you'll see dozens of them, black spires processing slowly through herds of clicking scarabs. Other times the plateau seems empty of them entirely, and you'll walk for miles seeing only fauna and stone before one suddenly *resolves* beside you, having been there all along.

But there are others.

Near the edges of the plateau, where the heat begins to fade and the engine-pulse grows quieter, you'll occasionally see Keepers who have stopped. Not resting—they don't rest—but *completed*. Their forms have settled into final configurations, no longer flowing between states, and something about them suggests that they've finished whatever task they were performing and have simply... remained. These Completed Keepers are indistinguishable from the stone formations around them, and maybe that's the point. Maybe they *become* the stone formations, their long slow lives finally merging entirely with the plateau they tended.

And near the center—near that luminescent region where the light arrives from impossible directions—there are Keepers who burn. Their obsidian bodies run with veins of actual fire, molten glass flowing through channels in their forms, and they move faster than the others, their rhythm doubled or tripled, processing toward the center with something that might be urgency if urgency could exist on these timescales. They never arrive. Or they always arrive. The center remains empty and full, a structure and not a structure, and the burning Keepers circle it eternally, approaching without ever closing the distance.

---

They are aware of you.

Not the way the fauna is unaware—blank, indifferent, simply occupying the same space. The Keepers *know* you're there. You can feel it in the way their reflective surfaces angle toward you, tracking your movement with sense-organs that don't need eyes. You can feel it in the way the air seems to thicken around you when you pass too close to one, the heat intensifying as if the plateau itself is taking your measure.

But they don't *care* about you.

That's the thing you come to understand, watching them process across the endless red-black stone. You're not a threat. You're not an intruder. You're not even interesting, except in the way that everything is interesting to something that counts *everything*. You're simply another element in the pattern they perceive, another thread in the tapestry, another creature moving across timescales that are, from their perspective, too brief to matter.

They counted you when you arrived. They'll count you when you leave. In between, you're free to walk among them, to watch the fauna flow around their spire-bodies, to listen to the engine-pulse sync with their slow patient movements. They won't help you. They won't harm you. They won't notice if you scream or weep or fall to your knees in the overwhelming empty fullness of this place.

They have work to do, work that started before anything like you existed, work that will continue until the slow engines finally stop. They're doing it now, all around you, in numbers too vast to count by anything except themselves.

The bronze scarabs click. The plasma-swarms crackle. The magnetic serpents drone. And beneath it all, the Keepers process, and count, and tend, and wait for nothing because nothing needs waiting for.

Everything here has already arrived.

---

But there is one more thing.

Sometimes—rarely, unpredictably, in moments when the time-folds align in configurations that might happen once a century—a Keeper will stop in front of you.

Not processing past. Not angling its sensors in passing acknowledgment. *Stopping*. Facing you with its full attention, its obsidian form going still, its reflective surfaces focusing on you with an intensity that feels like physical pressure.

In these moments, you'll hear something that isn't the engine-pulse and isn't the fauna-noise and isn't the wind across the stone. You'll hear a voice that speaks in frequencies your ears can't quite process, that resonates in your bones and your teeth and the fluid of your eyes. It will say something to you in a language that doesn't exist yet, or doesn't exist anymore, or exists only in the spaces between moments.

You won't understand it.

But you'll remember it, afterward, in dreams and in the quiet moments before sleep. You'll remember that something ancient and patient and utterly inhuman looked at you across an abyss of scale and duration and said *something*—acknowledged you, marked you, counted you in a way that was different from the automatic counting, that was *specific* and *intentional* and *meant*.

And you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what it was.

What they wanted you to know.

What it means that they chose, in their slow impossible way, to speak to you at all.

The plateau keeps its secrets. The engines keep running. The Keepers keep counting.

But for one moment, in the heat and the noise and the folded time, you were something more than a thread in the pattern.

You were a question they decided to answer.

And the answer is still echoing in you, waiting to be understood.
