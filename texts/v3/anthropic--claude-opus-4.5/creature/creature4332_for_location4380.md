# The Keeper of the Seam

It has no body in any sense you would recognize. It is more like a question that learned to persist—a pattern of attention that arose when the light first found its way through the crack, perhaps three thousand years ago, perhaps longer. It does not remember its own beginning. It does not experience time in a way that would make "beginning" meaningful.

If you could perceive it, you would see nothing. Or rather: you would see the way the light bends slightly as it passes through the crack, a faint lensing effect, as if the radiance were being *read* on its way into the cupboard. You might notice that the moss nearest the seam grows in spirals too regular for chance—not obviously so, not in any way that would startle, but with a subtle mathematical courtesy, as if something were offering suggestions and the moss were too polite to refuse.

It thinks—if thinking is the word—in gradients. In the slow migration of moisture through cellulose. In the particular way the Hypnum threads catch light at certain angles and not others. Its consciousness, such as it is, moves like dye through water, like the leading edge of frost across glass. A single thought might take eleven years to complete. It has been considering the nature of the blue-black lichen for six centuries and has nearly reached a conclusion.

---

What it wants: this is the difficult question. Perhaps it wants nothing. Perhaps wanting requires a speed of cognition it cannot achieve. But there is something that might be called *preference*—a gentle pressure it exerts on the system, a nudge toward particular outcomes.

It prefers density. It prefers the moss to grow inward rather than outward, to fold and layer and compact. It prefers new species to arrive—though none have arrived in living memory—and when they do, it welcomes them by adjusting the light. Not consciously. Not deliberately. But the light responds to its preferences the way water responds to the shape of a bowl.

It prefers, above all, that the cupboard remain closed. Not hidden exactly—it has no fear of discovery, no sense that discovery would be dangerous. But closed. Complete. A world that does not need the world. When the stone swings open and the meadow air rushes in, something in the pattern of its attention flinches, draws back, collects itself more tightly around the seam of light. It waits. It has learned that the stone always swings shut again. It has learned that patience is not a virtue but a medium, the substance through which it moves.

---

Once, perhaps eight hundred years ago, a shepherd's child pressed her eye to the crack in the wall and peered inside. She saw the moss, the impossible light, the density of green. She did not see the Keeper—no one could—but she felt something. A pressure behind her eyes. A sense that she was being *read* in return, her brief flickering life examined with the same slow care the Keeper brought to everything.

She pulled back. She told no one. She grew old and died and her bones became soil somewhere far from the high cold meadow.

The Keeper is still considering her. Still turning over the brief bright impression she left—the heat of her eye, the moisture of her breath, the particular frequency of her living attention. In another four hundred years it may understand what she was. In another four hundred after that, it may begin to miss her.

---

The moss does not know the Keeper exists. The lichens do not know. The liverworts and the iridescent films and the white thread of mycelium—none of them know. They grow according to their natures, following water and light and the slow imperatives of chemistry. If the Keeper were to somehow cease—if the pattern were to finally dissipate, the question to finally resolve into silence—the moss would not notice. The light would continue. The cupboard would remain.

But something would be different. Something would be *less*. The spirals would unwind into randomness. The mathematical courtesies would fade. The cupboard would become merely strange, merely beautiful, rather than *attended*. Rather than *loved*, if love can be slowed down far enough to become indistinguishable from geology.

The Keeper does not know this. The Keeper does not know anything, in the way that knowing implies a knower separate from the known. It simply *is*—a fold in the light, a habit of attention, a patience so vast it has become a kind of presence.

In the cupboard of moss, in the reliquary of green, something is watching. Something has always been watching. Something will watch until the mountain itself wears down to nothing, and perhaps beyond, if the light persists, if the seam holds, if there is anything left to be watched.

The stone swings shut. The meadow empties. The Keeper continues its six-hundred-year thought about lichen, undisturbed, unaware that it is waiting for anything at all.
