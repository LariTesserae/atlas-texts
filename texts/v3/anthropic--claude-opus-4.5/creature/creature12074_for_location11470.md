**Mehrit**

She is sixty-three years old, though she has never counted it that way. She counts instead in swarms witnessed—forty-seven—and queens transferred—more than she can remember, their small fierce bodies blurring together across the decades into a single composite queen, the platonic form of royalty held between thumb and forefinger.

Her body has become the room's body. Her spine curves now to match the dome's sag, so that when she ventures outside—rarely, and never for long—she walks with a permanent stoop that the settlement's children mimic when they play at being her. Her skin has taken on the color of old propolis, a deep amber-brown that seems to hold light the way the honey holds sweetness. The fine hairs on her forearms are perpetually gilded with pollen; she stopped trying to wash it away decades ago. It is not dirt. It is relationship.

She speaks to the bees, but not in words. She speaks in stillness, in the quality of her breathing, in the particular way she holds her shoulders when she means *I am not a threat* versus the slight tension that means *I am going to open you now, prepare yourselves.* The bees have learned her across two thousand generations of their brief lives. Each worker lives perhaps forty days in summer, less in the desperate winter fogs, and yet the colonies remember her—remember her grandmother—through some chemical inscription in the wax, some inherited knowledge that this particular shape of warmth and breath and careful movement is the shape of the one who tends.

She remembers her mother's hands better than her face. Her mother died during a swarming, not from stings—the bees are stingless, their defenses are crowding and biting and the sheer panic-weight of ten thousand small bodies—but from her heart, which stopped as she reached into the third gourd to clip a queen's wing. Mehrit was twenty-two. She held her mother's body in the hot closeness of the room while the bees, confused by the sudden stillness, began to land on the cooling skin, tasting the salt, finding no threat. By the time the others came to help carry the body out, her mother was covered in them, a living shroud, and Mehrit had to speak them away with her breath before the dome could be opened to the bright violence of outside light.

She has not taken a partner. The room cannot hold two people working, and she cannot imagine loving someone she would have to leave the room to see. Instead, she has the beetles, whose shells she knows individually by the patterns of wear; she has the crickets, whose songs she can distinguish by colony and even, in the case of the oldest male in the eastern corner, by individual voice. She has the silk-larvae, whose webbing she harvests with a bone needle, winding it onto spools carved from the knucklebones of a goat that died before she was born.

The children who visit sense something in her that they cannot name. She is patient with them in a way that other adults are not—not kind, exactly, but *slow,* willing to let a question sit in the hum-filled air until the child either answers it themselves or forgets they asked. She shows them how to breathe shallow so the bees read no threat in the carbon dioxide. She lets them hold a drone, the males who cannot sting and barely bite, and watches their faces as they feel for the first time the strange cool weight of a life utterly unlike their own. Some children she sees once and never again. Others return, year after year, until they are no longer children, and one of those—a girl named Seset, thirteen now, with steady hands and a stillness that seems too large for her small body—Mehrit is beginning to teach.

Not yet the deep knowledge. Not the queen-transfers or the reading of swarm-mood or the management of the parasitic wasps, whose tolerance must be carefully maintained lest they shift from allies to invaders. But the foundation-knowledge: how to sit, how to breathe, how to become so still that the room forgets you are a separate thing and folds you into its hum.

When Mehrit dies—she thinks of this without fear, the way she thinks of next season's fog—Seset will be perhaps thirty. Old enough to have learned it all. Young enough to have sixty years of tending ahead of her. The room will continue. The bees will continue. The ancient bricks will hold new fingerprints, and the dome will sag another imperceptible fraction of a degree, and the settlement will go on bringing its children to see how a single small space can hold more life than the mind can compass.

She reaches now for the reed. The third gourd is agitated, the queen restless, the workers boiling with that particular frenzy that means *we are too many, we must divide, we must send half of ourselves out into the world to die or found something new.* Mehrit has done this hundreds of times. She will find the queen, clip one wing to keep her flightless, and the swarm-Loss will fade back into ordinary industry. Or she will let them swarm, if the colony is strong enough to survive the split, and catch the departing queen in a gourd she has been preparing for three years, its interior rubbed with wax and propolis to smell like home.

She has not decided yet. She is listening. The room is telling her something, in its hum and click and settling-sigh, and she is old enough now to know that the room's knowledge is deeper than hers. She breathes. She waits. The beetles pulse their dim heat. The larvae click.

She decides.
