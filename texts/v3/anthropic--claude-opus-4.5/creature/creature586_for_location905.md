## The People of the Plateau

They are born patient. This is what they say of themselves, and watching them move through their days, you would believe it. The people of Three Springs have a quality of unhurriedness that goes deeper than temperament—it lives in their bones, in the particular way they lower themselves to sit, in how they hold a conversation across the span of an afternoon without either party feeling the passage of time as loss.

They are not a tall people, nor a short one. They are moderate in most visible ways: brown-skinned from ancestry rather than sun, since the diffused light neither burns nor bronzes; dark-haired, though the hair tends toward a softness that holds the moisture in the air, curling slightly at the temples and nape. Their eyes are the color of the water in the basins—brown deepening toward black, tea-stained, reflective. When they look at you, they look *at* you, with a steadiness that can feel uncomfortable to outsiders. They are not searching for anything in your face. They are simply present to it.

---

**The grandmother Sedna** has lived in the same house for sixty-three years, since she was given it by her own grandmother upon reaching womanhood. The house is three rooms: one for sleeping, one for working, one for receiving those who come. The receiving room looks out over the largest of the three springs, and Sedna spends most of her waking hours there, weaving baskets so tightly they could hold secrets.

She speaks rarely, but when she does, people remember the words. Not because she says wise things—she has no interest in wisdom as a performance—but because her voice has a particular quality, low and unhurried, that seems to come from the same place as the springs themselves. Underground. Patient. Inevitable.

Sedna knows the water. This is her function in Three Springs, though no one appointed her to it and she holds no title. She knows which basin will overflow first after a heavy mist, which channel has begun to silt in and needs clearing, where the tea-dark color runs deeper than usual and what that might mean. People come to her with water questions, and she answers them, or sometimes she simply looks at them until they realize they already know the answer and were only seeking permission to trust themselves.

She had a husband once, and children, and the children had children. Most of them are still here, scattered through the settlement in their own houses. She does not visit them often. They come to her, following the same pattern the water follows: when there is overflow, it finds its way downhill. She is the lowest point in her family's topology, and what gathers around her gathers naturally, without effort or demand.

---

**The boy called Kettle**—a nickname earned in early childhood for reasons no one quite remembers, though theories persist—spends his days at the edges of the marsh-islands, studying the flickering things in the water.

He is perhaps eleven, perhaps twelve. The people of the plateau do not count years with precision; they mark time by the replacing of reed-roofs, by the wearing-down of stone steps, by the slow shift of channels across the rock. Kettle is old enough to be trusted alone, young enough to have time that belongs to no one but himself.

He lies on his stomach on the wet basalt, chin propped on his hands, watching. The water is shallow here, no deeper than the length of his forearm, and if he stays still long enough—truly still, not the restless stillness of someone waiting but the genuine stillness of someone who has forgotten he is waiting—the flickering things begin to move as though he is not there.

They are silver. They are quick. They are not quite fish, though they have the shape of fish, the movement of fish. But fish have eyes you can meet, and these creatures have something else: a smoothness where eyes should be, a surface that reflects Kettle's own face back at him when he leans close enough. He has never caught one. He has never tried. The trying would end the watching, and the watching is what he wants.

His mother worries about him, in her mild way. She mentions it sometimes to her sister, how Kettle seems to live half in the water, how he comes home with his clothes perpetually damp and his silence perpetually deepened. But she does not stop him. No one on the plateau stops anyone from finding their equilibrium.

---

**Morrow and Lessen** are twins, though they look nothing alike. Morrow is tall and angular, with hands that seem too large for her wrists; Lessen is compact, rounded, quick in her movements where her sister is deliberate. They are the plateau's primary stone-workers, a function they inherited from their father and his mother before him, and they spend their days at the quarry-face on the settlement's northern edge, reading the basalt for its natural joints and coaxing blocks free without forcing.

They work in tandem without speaking. Morrow studies the rock, traces its stress-lines with her long fingers, marks the points where a chisel might find purchase. Lessen does the striking—sharp, precise taps that seem too gentle to accomplish anything until suddenly a block separates from the face with a sound like a sigh, as though the rock is relieved to become something other than what it was.

The twins share a house but not a life. Morrow keeps to herself, takes her meals alone, sleeps early and wakes with the first lightening of the sky. Lessen is the one who attends the infrequent gatherings, who knows everyone's name and the names of their children, who laughs at things that are not quite funny because she understands that laughter is sometimes simply a way of being present with others. They love each other in the way of people who have never had to choose to—absolutely, without examination, without the need for expression.

When they finish a block, they mark it with a small spiral, the same pattern carved on the standing stones at the springs. They do not know why. Their father did it, and his mother before him. The spiral means something, or it means nothing, or it means only that some things persist because no one has yet found a reason to stop them.

---

**There is a man who lives alone** at the plateau's eastern edge, close enough to the cliffs that on quiet mornings he can hear the forest below him breathing. His name is his own; he does not offer it, and the people of Three Springs do not ask. They call him the Edge-keeper, which is not a title but a description, and they bring him things sometimes—food, reed-rope, the occasional copper implement when one can be spared—leaving them at the boundary marker he has placed a hundred yards from his small stone house.

He came from somewhere else. This is obvious in the way he holds himself, a certain tension in the shoulders that the plateau has not yet unwound, a tendency to look toward the cliffs as though expecting something to rise from the forest below. He has been here for nine years or eleven years or some number of reed-roof replacements, and in that time he has learned the plateau's rhythms without quite surrendering to them. He still moves with urgency sometimes. He still speaks too quickly when he speaks at all.

What he does at the edge, no one knows precisely. He watches. He maintains the cairns that mark the cliff-line, replacing stones that tumble over, adding new ones where the rock has shifted. He has worn a path along the precipice, a track of slightly smoother basalt that traces the plateau's boundary like a scar.

Once, a child from Three Springs asked him what he was watching for. He considered the question for a long time—long enough that the child grew uncomfortable, shifted her weight, began to formulate an apology for asking.

"The coming back," he said finally. "Or the not coming back. I haven't decided which I'm hoping for."

The child did not understand, and he did not explain further. She returned to the settlement and reported his words to her parents, who nodded as though they made perfect sense. Perhaps they did, to people who have learned that some questions are not meant to be answered but only to be carried.

---

**The keeper of names** is a woman of middle years named Threshold, which is not the name she was born with but the name she took when she assumed her function. Before her there was another keeper, and before that another, stretching back to a time before Three Springs had its current name, when the settlement was smaller and the stone-houses fewer and the plateau itself was younger by whatever measure young rock uses to count its age.

Threshold lives in a house built directly over one of the smaller overflow channels, so that water runs constantly beneath her floor. The sound of it—too quiet to be called a sound, more a vibration, a presence—never stops. She says it helps her remember.

She knows every name that has been given in Three Springs since she became keeper, and every name the previous keeper taught her, and every name that keeper learned from *her* predecessor. She holds them in her memory without writing, without records. The people of the plateau do not write. They carry what matters in their minds, and when they die, they pass it to someone who will carry it after them.

When a child is born, Threshold comes to see it. She looks at the child for a long time, holding it if the parents allow, studying its face with the same attention Kettle gives to the flickering things in the water. Then she offers three names. The parents choose one, or none, or ask her to look again. The choosing can take days. There is no hurry.

The names she offers are not new. They are names that have been used before, names that belonged to people now gone, names that carry histories the child will grow into whether or not anyone tells them the stories. When you are named for someone, Threshold says, you are not replacing them. You are continuing them. You are proof that some things do not end.

She has never married, never had children of her own. When people ask her about this—rarely, because the people of the plateau do not pry—she says only that she has enough names to look after. This is not sadness. This is not sacrifice. This is simply the shape her life has taken, the channel her water has carved.

---

**In the evenings**, when the light dims through its long gradual fade from pearl to silver to the darkness that is never quite complete, the people of Three Springs gather in small groups. Not every evening. Not on any schedule. But often enough that the gathering-places are worn smooth: a particular causeway-junction near the largest spring, a flat-topped boulder in the middle marsh-island, the steps leading down into the basin east of the twins' quarry-face.

They do not gather to accomplish anything. They gather to be together in the silence, to share the space without filling it. Sometimes someone speaks, tells a story or describes a dream or mentions something they noticed that day—a new overflow channel beginning to form, a clutch of reed-flowers blooming earlier than expected, the particular quality of light on a particular morning. Sometimes no one speaks at all.

The children fall asleep against their parents' legs. The elders sit with their eyes half-closed, neither awake nor asleep but somewhere in between, present to the gathering without needing to participate in it. The young adults—those who have recently taken up their functions, who are still learning the shape of their adult lives—sometimes fidget, not yet fully at home in the plateau's patience. They will learn. Everyone learns, given enough time. And time, here, is the one thing no one lacks.

---

**The water** does not care about them. This is important to understand. The springs do not flow because the people need them; the basins do not fill to provide for anyone. The plateau existed before Three Springs, will exist after Three Springs, is utterly indifferent to the settlement spreading across its surface like lichen on a stone.

And yet.

The people have shaped themselves to the water's patterns so completely that the distinction between caring and not caring has become meaningless. They do not ask the springs to provide; they simply accept what is provided. They do not worship the water; they simply live within it, as the flickering silver things live within it, as the reeds live within it, as the basalt itself lives within the slow saturated becoming of the plateau.

When someone dies, they are returned to the water. The body is wrapped in woven reed, weighted with stones, and lowered into one of the three springs at its deepest point. The springs have no bottom that anyone has found. The bodies sink and sink and do not return. What happens to them below is not something the people of Three Springs speculate about. They have given back what was given. The water will do with it what water does.

---

There is a word in the plateau's language—spoken, unwritten, passed from mouth to ear across generations—that has no equivalent in the tongues of the lowland forests. The word is something like *stillness* and something like *fullness* and something like *the moment before overflow when the basin is exactly as full as it can be without spilling*. It is the word for the state the plateau exists in: not empty, not excessive, but precisely sufficient. Enough.

The people of Three Springs use this word often. They use it to describe a good meal, a well-made basket, a conversation that has reached its natural end. They use it to describe each other, when they are at their best: *She is [that word]*, they say, meaning she has found her level, her equilibrium, the channel her water was always meant to carve.

It is what they aspire to, all of them, in their patient unhurried way. Not happiness—happiness is a peak, and peaks require valleys. Not peace—peace implies the absence of conflict, and they have never had enough conflict for its absence to register as meaningful. Simply this: the state of being exactly what they are, where they are, without strain or lack.

The plateau offers them nothing but the opportunity to become this. They have taken the offer. They are becoming it still, each of them, generation after generation, the water flowing and the stone slowly softening and the people of Three Springs carrying their names and their silences and their patient watching into a future that looks, from here, exactly like the present, and the present exactly like the past, and all of it saturate, sufficient, still.
