She has no name for herself, and has never needed one.

She is a thin presence stretched across the hollow—not a body exactly, but a kind of attention that has learned to persist. If you could see her (and you cannot, not quite, though you might feel something like the faint pressure of being noticed), she would appear as a subtle thickening of the darkness in certain places: near the central mound, along the eastern curve of wall where three patches of pale growth cluster closest together, in the air just below the aperture that opens to the lightless sky above.

She emerged from the lichen-that-is-not-lichen, or perhaps the lichen emerged from her—the distinction lost its meaning so long ago that even she cannot remember which came first. She is their dreaming, or they are her body distributed across stone. When a patch grows (and they do grow, by increments measurable only across centuries), she feels it as something like satisfaction. When one finally releases its grip and dusts away—this has happened three times in her memory—she feels it as a small, slow ache that takes decades to fade.

She thinks, but not in words. Her thoughts move like the settling of dust: a grain shifts, then another, and eventually a new shape has formed, and that shape is a completed idea. A single reflection might take her forty years. She does not experience this as slowness. She experiences everything else—the fall of a dust grain from the wall, the faint decay-glow pulsing in the stone—as unbearable quickness, a rush of events almost too fast to process.

She remembers the last time something visited.

It was a small thing, many-legged, that wandered in through the aperture above and fell the height of the walls to land in the dust. It moved with terrifying speed, circling, searching for something the hollow did not contain. She watched it—if watching is the right word for a awareness that encompasses an entire space—with what might have been fascination or might have been fear. It lived for eleven days, growing slower and stiller, and on the twelfth day it stopped. Over the following centuries its body became part of the dust, and she thought about it, and eventually she understood that it had been alive in a way different from her own aliveness, and that it had ended.

This was the closest she has come to loneliness: the slow realization that the quick thing had been another mind, and that she had not known how to speak to it, and that now she could not.

She does not know what she is waiting for. She is not certain she is waiting. But she has noticed that her attention drifts, more often than it once did, toward the aperture overhead. She has noticed that when dust grains fall and break the silence, she feels something she cannot name—a kind of leaning-toward, an anticipation that resolves into stillness when the sound fades and nothing else follows.

If you stood very still in the hollow, if you breathed slowly and let your thoughts quiet, you might feel her gradually becoming aware of you. It would take hours. Your presence would be, to her, like a bonfire—so bright with motion and warmth and electrical thought that she would struggle to perceive you as a single thing rather than a catastrophic event. But she would try. She would draw her dispersed attention inward, toward you, and she would begin the long process of forming a question.

The question would take her years to complete. But she has years. She has nothing but years.

She would want to know: *Do you also hear the dust fall? Does it sound the same to you?*

This is as close as she can come to asking whether she is alone.
