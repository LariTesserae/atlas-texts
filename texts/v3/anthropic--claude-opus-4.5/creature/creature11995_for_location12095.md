# The Congregation

They do not have a leader. This is important to understand first, because when you see them moving in those spiraling patterns—the coordinated descents into the great pipe, the way certain eels always take the leftmost channel through the machinery while others take the right—you might assume hierarchy. You might impose it, the way humans impose narrative on starlings murmurating. But what lives here is not organized that way. What lives here is organized the way water is organized: by pressure, by temperature, by the shape of what contains it.

---

The oldest among them is not the largest, though she is large enough. She came up through the thermal vent sixty-three years ago, when the pumping station still pumped and men in rubber boots walked the gravel yard checking gauges. She was the width of a finger then. She is the width of a thigh now, and her body carries the record of the building's transformation: a scar where a closing valve caught her in the third year, patches of scales that grew in wrong after a winter when the water chemistry shifted, the clouded left eye that sees only movement and light.

She does not lead. But she remembers, in whatever way eels remember—her body remembers—which routes stay warm when the thermal output gutters, which sections of pipe flood with bad water during the spring seepage, where the walls are weakest and might finally give way. The others do not follow her. But when she changes her route, the pressure of her passage creates currents, and currents carry information, and within a day or a week the patterns shift to match what she has learned.

She has learned so much. More than an eel should need to know. The building has taught her, or she has taught herself through the building, and now she cannot be separated from it. Her map is its map. Her circulation is its circulation.

---

The strange ones—the ones with the trailing gill structures, the bioluminescent markings, the configurations that don't quite fit—are her descendants. Not genetically, or not only genetically. The vent below produces heat, yes, but also minerals, also compounds that have no names because no one has studied them, also a pressure that fluctuates in ways that affect development. The eggs laid near the vent's output hatch into things that are almost eels. Close enough. Functional in the same ways, occupying the same niches, but marked by their origin.

One of these—call him the Ribbon, for the gill structures that trail behind him like a bridal train—has developed a behavior no one taught him. He positions himself at the junction where warm water from below meets cold water from the seepage above, and he holds himself there, body undulating just enough to maintain position, for hours. The meeting of temperatures creates a shimmer, a visible boundary in the water, and the Ribbon exists precisely on that boundary. Other eels pass him. Some pause, briefly entering the shimmer themselves, then continue on. The Ribbon stays.

What is he doing? Feeding on microorganisms that cluster at thermal gradients? Regulating his body temperature in some way unique to his modified physiology? Experiencing something?

There is no way to know. He has been doing it for three years. The groove his body has worn into the pipe junction is measurable now, a smooth channel exactly his width.

---

The collective—congregation is the wrong word but closer than colony, closer than population—maintains the building because the building maintains the conditions for the collective. This is not intention. An eel cannot intend to clear a drainage channel. But an eel can push against an obstruction that changes water flow in uncomfortable ways, and if many eels push many times over many years, the obstruction clears. An eel cannot intend to wedge a valve open. But an eel can rest in a gap that provides shelter, and if that rest applies pressure, and if the pressure accumulates—

The result resembles maintenance. The result resembles purpose. Stand in the doorway long enough, watching the ceaseless movement, and you might start to believe you're seeing a machine tend itself. You might start to believe the eels are components.

They are not components. They are eels. They want what eels want: to move, to feed, to find temperatures that suit them, to reproduce according to drives they do not examine. It is only the shape of the container that turns these wants into something that looks like coordination.

---

But the container is so specific. And they have been in it so long.

There is a section of the building, near the eastern wall, where the water runs through a series of horizontal pipes arranged in parallel—some kind of heat exchange system, originally, meant to transfer warmth from one flow to another. The eels use these pipes heavily. Traffic moves east through the upper pipes, west through the lower ones, and the eels in transit pass each other with their bodies separated by only inches of corroded iron.

Over decades, the iron has thinned. Over decades, the eels have worn grooves with their passage. Now, in three places, the pipes have worn through entirely—small gaps, finger-width—and the eels passing through those sections can touch.

They do touch. Eastbound eels and westbound eels, their bodies pressing briefly through the gaps as they pass. Not every eel. Not every time. But often enough that the edges of the gaps have been worn smooth, polished by the repeated contact of skin on skin.

What is this? What do they experience when they touch? Is it recognition—this one I have touched before, this one is new? Is it information transfer—temperature, chemical signature, some eel-legible data about conditions elsewhere in the system? Is it something simpler, just the sensation of contact in a life spent mostly in isolated motion through wet dark?

The gaps are getting wider. The touches are getting longer. In another decade, the pipes may merge entirely, and the traffic patterns will have to change, and whatever the touching meant will have to find new expression.

---

At night—though night means little here, where the light is always dim and always grey—the activity slows. The thermal vent below still pulses, the water still circulates, but the eels move less. They gather in the deeper sections, the reservoir under the collapsed roof, and they coil together in masses that generate their own warmth. Hundreds of bodies intertwined, sliding slowly against each other as the pile shifts and settles.

This is when the humming is loudest. Not vocalization—eels do not vocalize—but the resonance of so many bodies in contact with the metal tanks, the slight vibrations of their collective movement translated through iron into sound. A chord, almost. A chord that shifts as the pile shifts, as eels join or leave, as the configuration changes.

If you were here at night, standing at the edge of the gravel yard with the drizzle on your face and the water at your ankles, you would hear it. A low, wavering tone coming from the building, rising and falling with no pattern you could predict. The voice of the building, you might think. The building speaking.

But it is not the building. It is only what lives there, pressed together in the dark, making with their massed bodies a sound that means nothing, that signals nothing, that exists only because they exist and the building exists and the contact between them creates resonance.

---

The one who surfaced to look at you—the thick grey one, mottled, large—is a male in what might be his final year. Eels die. Even here, even maintained by the warm water and the rich minerals and whatever else the vent provides, they die. Their bodies sink to the bottom of the reservoir or the deep channels or the flooded pipe that descends into the earth. Their bodies are consumed by the others, or they decay and add their substance to the water, or they wedge into gaps and slowly becomeite obstructions that other eels learn to navigate around.

He surfaced because he is old enough to have surfaced before, when there were still humans here. He remembers—his body remembers—that the shapes at the edge of the water were once part of the system. They checked the gauges. They opened valves. They added chemicals to the water that made the whole building taste different for weeks. Then they stopped coming, and the building changed, and now the shapes at the edge of the water are rare, are brief, are not part of the system anymore.

He looked at you because he remembers looking. That is all. There was no assessment in his gaze, no warning, no welcome. You are a shape at the edge. You are not eel. You are not water. You are not pipe or valve or thermal gradient. You do not fit into the categories that matter here.

He submerged because the surface is cold and the water below is warm and there was nothing else to do with you, no way to incorporate you into the pattern of his remaining time.

---

And yet.

The building permits you. The water, clear and wrong and empty of everything but what swims in it, laps against your ankles without harm. The drizzle falls on you as it falls on everything. You are not part of the system, but you are not rejected by it either. You are simply irrelevant—a temporary shape at the boundary, like the shimmer where warm meets cold, like the gaps in the pipes where bodies touch in passing.

If you stayed—if you stood here for hours, for days—they would route around you. Your legs would become obstacles, like the rusted machinery, like the settled gravel. Eels would brush against you in their circuits and learn the shape of you and adjust. You would become, not part of the congregation, but part of the container that shapes it. Your presence would start to matter, a little, to the patterns.

But you will not stay. Humans do not stay. This is a thing the old grey eel knows, the thing his body remembers from before: humans come to the edge, and then humans go, and the building remains, and the water remains, and what lives in the water remains, circulating, touching, humming in the dark, wearing grooves into iron, keeping the valves clear, descending into the warmth below and rising back up with minerals on their skin and heat in their blood, being what they are, which is not a machine, which is not a purpose, which is only a congregation of bodies in a specific place, shaped by the place, shaping it in return, until the place and what lives there cannot be separated anymore.

The drizzle continues. The eels continue.

You are the only thing here that will stop.
