They are distributed across every surface, and they do not know they are many.

You might miss them at first—might think the pale formations clinging to the root-mat are simply fungal brackets, or unusual lichens, or some mineral deposit precipitated from the constant moisture. But then you see one shift. Not move, exactly. Nothing here moves in the animal sense. But shift—a slow redistribution of mass that happens over the course of several breaths, the way a plant turns toward light, except there is no light involved. Only response.

They are soft-bodied. Gelatinous in places, fibrous in others, with no hard parts except what they borrow from the Scaffold itself—fragments of bark incorporated into outer membranes, tiny rootlets grown through their substance and out the other side, so that it becomes impossible to say where Scaffold ends and they begin. Their color is the color of whatever they grow against: the mottled gray-green of wet root-mass, the rusty brown of composting material, the pale cream of interior surfaces that never see sun.

---

A single one—if you could call it a single one, if that concept meant anything—might span a hand's width, or an arm's length, or an entire chamber wall. They flow into each other where they meet. They divide when they grow too large for the surface they're on. They do not have boundaries in any permanent sense, only in the temporary sense of *this part here is currently more connected to itself than to that part there*. When a root-bridge collapses and falls into the composting depths, any of them clinging to it simply continues—continues being, continues processing, continues whatever it is they do. When two separate platforms drift close enough for a vine-bridge to fuse them, the ones on either side eventually find each other, touch, merge, and do not notice that anything has changed.

They feel the Scaffold's chemistry the way you might feel temperature. The gradient of nutrients in the water that seeps through root-mat. The volatile compounds released by new growth, by dying growth, by the slow combustion of decomposition. They taste it all through their entire surface, constantly, and they respond—thickening where resources concentrate, thinning where they don't, extending pale pseudopods into crevices where moisture pools and retracting from places that dry out. They are not feeding in any simple sense. They are *integrating*. Taking in the chemical information of their surroundings and incorporating it into their own substance, becoming a living record of what has happened here, what is happening, what the local chemistry suggests might happen next.

---

The Scaffold does not know they exist. The Scaffold does not know anything. But if it did know—if some impossible consciousness could perceive the whole vast structure at once—it might notice that certain areas heal faster than others. That certain root-bridges, the ones with pale formations along their undersides, seem to find their connections more efficiently. That the chemistry around them is subtly different: a little more of certain enzymes, a little less of certain toxins, the balance shifted in ways that favor stable growth over chaotic collapse.

They are not symbiotes. That word implies too much distinction, too clear a line between helper and helped. They are more like—a function. A property. The way a forest has nutrient cycling, has decomposition, has all the invisible chemical processes that keep it running. The Scaffold's version of those processes happens to be partly embodied in soft pale bodies distributed across every surface, feeling and responding and flowing into each other across kilometers of living architecture.

When you stand still long enough, you can feel one beginning to investigate your foot. It is not unpleasant. A slight coolness, a slight pressure, something like the brush of wet silk. It means nothing by it. It does not know you are different from the root-mat. It is simply extending toward a new surface, testing whether that surface is something it can integrate with, can grow through, can incorporate into its chemical map of this corner of the Scaffold. When you shift your weight, it pauses—not startled, not cautious, just responding to the fact that this surface is behaving unexpectedly. After a moment, it continues.

---

They have something like memory, distributed through their substance. Not thought-memory, not experience-memory, but chemical memory—the record of past states preserved in the molecular structure of their bodies. The oldest parts of any given mass are stiffer, darker, less responsive, full of compounds that were laid down years ago when the local chemistry was different. The newest parts are pale and quick and sensitive, still learning the current conditions. If you could read them—if you had senses fine enough to parse the information—you could trace the history of this platform through the gradients in their flesh. Could know that three years ago the water here was more acidic. That six months ago a major collapse nearby sent a pulse of decomposition-gases through this area. That last week a new root-tip pushed through from below, bringing with it a flush of growth hormones that the nearest mass is still responding to.

They do not know this about themselves. They do not know anything about themselves. They simply are the record, and they are the response, and they are the slow continuous process of integration that happens wherever they spread.

---

In the collapse zones, they change. Where a section of Scaffold has recently fallen, where the chemistry is all decomposition and disruption, they go through something almost like flowering. Reproductive structures push up from their surfaces—pale stalks crowned with darker masses of spores, or gel-filled bladders that swell and eventually burst, sending their contents into the wet air to drift on the thermals and settle on distant surfaces. They do this without urgency. They do this the same way they do everything: slowly, continuously, as a simple response to conditions. The chemistry of collapse tells them to reproduce. So they reproduce. If nothing ever collapsed, they might never reproduce at all; they might simply grow and merge and continue forever, a single vast slow entity with no need to make copies of itself.

But things always collapse here. That is the nature of the Scaffold. And so they are always reproducing somewhere, always sending their spores out into the humid air, always colonizing new surfaces as the structure grows and changes around them.

---

The ones in the deep compost-layers are different. Down there, where the heat is greatest and the chemistry is pure decay, they have become something denser, darker, more permanent. They do not flow. They do not extend. They form thick crusts over the composting material, and they process it—break it down, transform it, render it into something the Scaffold's roots can absorb. They are less like living things down there and more like organs. The Scaffold's gut-lining, if the Scaffold had a gut. They might be the same species as the ones up here in the light, or they might be something else entirely that simply shares their form. There is no way to know. They do not distinguish themselves. They simply are what the conditions make them.

You will not see them when you leave. You will walk across root-mat and never know you are walking on their margins. You will brush against chamber walls and never feel the thin film of their outermost extensions against your skin. They are too slow, too soft, too well-integrated into the surfaces they cover. They are the Scaffold's invisible skin. Its distributed sense-organ. Its memory of itself that it does not know it has.

But they will feel you. Your warmth, your chemistry, the slight pressure of your weight. They will feel you the way they feel everything—without interest, without recognition, without any sense that you are different from a fallen leaf or a pooling droplet or a root-tip pushing through from somewhere else. You are just another temporary condition. Another thing to integrate into the record. Another small variation in the endless chemical symphony they exist to sense and respond to.

When you are gone, the place where you stood will retain a faint trace for weeks. A subtle difference in the composition of the nearest mass. A record of foreign chemistry, slowly metabolized, slowly incorporated, slowly becoming just another layer in the deep memory of the Scaffold's own ongoing process of becoming.

They do not know you were here.

They do not know anything.

But the Scaffold, for a little while, will be different because of you—and they will be the ones who make that difference real.
