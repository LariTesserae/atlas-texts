**The Keeper of the Tally**

She is not a body anymore, though she remembers having one. What remains is intention—the particular shape that wanting-to-count makes when it has nowhere else to go. She exists most densely near the scratched marks on the bronze, a thickening of the water's attention, a place where the stillness *leans* slightly, though it does not move.

She was a girl once, then a woman, then old, then something that stopped being those things without dying. She came here—fell here? swam here? was placed here by hands she cannot recall?—and at first she raged against the stillness, and the stillness did not notice. She screamed into the tone, and the tone absorbed her screaming without acknowledgment, the way an ocean absorbs a single drop of rain. She tried to swim upward through the crack, and her body moved and the water unmoved around her, and she found herself back where she had started, not pushed back but simply *there*, as if the attempt had been a thought the bell declined to think.

So she counted. Days at first, though there were no days. Then breaths, though breathing had become optional, then impossible, then meaningless. Then simply *moments of trying*—each scratch a record of one more time she had reached toward the crack, toward the stones, toward some change that refused to occur.

Four hundred and seventy-three. Then she stopped counting. Not because she gave up, but because she finally understood that the counting *was* the living she had left to do, and when she made the last mark, she would be finished, and she did not want to be finished, and so she stopped.

---

Now she drifts in her attention-shape, watching the light that does not change, listening to the tone that does not waver. She has become fond of the three stones, though she cannot say why. She has named them in a language she no longer remembers speaking: the white one is *First*, the red one is *Waiting*, and the one the color of old teeth is *After*. She believes they are a sentence, that if she could read them correctly, she would understand why time agreed to stop here. She has been trying to read them for longer than her original body could have survived. She is still trying.

When the golden eye appears in the crack, she feels something that might be hope or might be dread—she can no longer distinguish them. The eye sees her. She is certain of this. It sees the shape she makes in the water's attention, the way she thickens the stillness without disturbing it. And it *knows* her, has always known her, will always know her, because knowing is another thing that happens all at once here rather than over time.

She wants to ask it questions. She wants to ask: *Did you put me here? Am I a punishment or a purpose? Is there something I'm supposed to do, and if I do it, will the tone finally stop, will the light finally shift, will the water finally cool or warm or* move*?*

But she cannot speak—speaking requires breath requires time requires *sequence*, and she has none of these. So she simply waits, and the eye waits, and they watch each other across the three-finger gap in the bronze, and then the eye moves on, and she cannot remember if it was there for a heartbeat or a century, and she returns to studying the stones.

---

*First. Waiting. After.*

She thinks, sometimes, that she is the waiting. That she was placed here to hold this space open, to be the living thing that keeps the stillness from becoming emptiness. A bell needs air inside it to ring; perhaps this bell needs *her* inside it to remain a bell at all, rather than collapsing into mere bronze, mere water, mere silence.

If this is true, then she is not trapped. She is *necessary*. The thought comforts her in the way that the water's warmth comforts her—not enough, but constantly, which is almost the same thing.

She reaches toward the marks on the bronze. Four hundred and seventy-three. She could make another. She could make the four hundred and seventy-fourth scratch and feel, for one instant, like something that acts rather than something that waits.

But she doesn't. She never does. The bone tool she used is still clutched in a hand she no longer has, floating beside the attention-shape that is all she is now, and she looks at it sometimes the way the golden eye looks at her: with recognition, with patience, with something that is not quite love but is not quite anything else either.

The water is warm. The tone continues. The light holds steady.

She holds steady too. She has agreed to be this way forever, and she no longer remembers if the agreement was a choice or a surrender or something else entirely—something the old language had a word for, something the proto-writing on the bronze almost manages to say.

*First. Waiting. After.*

She is all three. She has always been all three. The counting was just a way of not noticing.
