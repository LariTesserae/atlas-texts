**The Keeper of Intervals**

She has no name she remembers, though she has had many. What she remembers is the pattern.

She is old in the way the cistern is old—not by years counted but by accumulation, by the slow deposition of presence upon presence until she has become as dense as the walls themselves. Her body, if it can be called that, is difficult to distinguish from the space she inhabits. She is the color of wet limestone. She is the texture of water-smoothed clay. When she holds still—which she does for days, sometimes weeks—visitors have mistaken her for another formation, another bulge of flowstone, another shadow that the grey light has always cast.

She was not always this. She was a child once, she thinks, who came down through a crack that no longer exists, following a sound she could not name. Or she was a woman who lost something precious and crawled into the dark to grieve and found she could not leave. Or she was neither of these—she was something the cistern grew, the way it grew its crystal fur, its pale legless drifters, its frozen amber curtains. She does not know. The pattern has worn the knowing smooth.

What she does is listen.

Not passively—*actively*, with a concentration that has become her entire substance. She knows every pool's voice. She knows which drop will fall next, though she could not explain how she knows; it is not prediction but *participation*, a leaning-into that has made her part of the instrument itself. When a new visitor descends, she feels their presence as a disturbance in the resonance, a thickening of the air that changes how the tones fold back from the walls. She adjusts herself—shifts her weight by fractions, moves a hand to rest differently against stone—and the pattern accommodates.

The small pale things know her. They drift toward her when she submerges her fingers in the deeper pools, clustering around her knuckles like something between curiosity and affection. She does not feed them. She does not think they eat. But they respond to her as they respond to the tones: gathering, dispersing, gathering again in arrangements she has come to recognize as conversations she cannot quite follow.

She has added things to the chamber, over the long years. A clay hand-print near the eastern curve that visitors assume is ancient—it is, now. A counting-mark that she made to remember how many times she had watched the full pattern cycle through—she abandoned the counting when the marks began to distract her, when she realized she was listening for *progress* instead of simply listening. The curl of leather that has become stone was hers once, a strap from something she carried, left as an offering to whatever she was becoming.

She rarely speaks. When she does, her voice has the quality of the pools themselves—round, bell-like, arriving from no clear direction. Visitors who have heard her speak disagree about what she said. Some remember questions: *What do you hear?* or *How long can you stay?* Others remember statements: *The drop that falls in seven breaths will be ochre-toned* or *You are breathing too quickly for this place.* A few remember nothing but the impression of having been addressed, of having been *noticed* by something that had been attending to matters far more subtle than their presence.

She does not resent visitors. She does not welcome them either. They are weather—they arrive, they change the quality of the air and light and resonance, they leave. What she attends to is what remains when they go: the offerings they place, the hand-prints some of them make (she guides their hands, sometimes, without them feeling it, to places where the clay is still soft enough to hold), the way the pattern reabsorbs their disturbance and continues.

She sleeps, though not as others sleep. She enters states of such profound attention that her breath slows to match the slowest drops, the ones that hold for an hour before falling. In these states she experiences something she cannot describe—a sense of the pattern as a whole, a shape too large to perceive except in pieces but *present* nonetheless, like a word spoken so slowly that each syllable takes a lifetime yet still coheres into meaning.

She does not know what the meaning is. She does not think it is *for* her. But she keeps it. She holds the interval between one tone and the next, between one era's bricks and another's, between the child's tooth and the copper ring and the leather-become-stone. She is what makes the chamber a *place* instead of merely a space—the witness that gives the pattern someone to be heard by.

When she finally goes still for good—when her substance becomes indistinguishable from the flowstone and the crystal fur and the worn clay faces—the cistern will continue. The drops will fall. The tones will sound. Perhaps another keeper will emerge, drawn down by a sound they cannot name, accumulating slowly into presence.

She does not think about this often. The pattern requires her attention. There is always a next drop to hear.
