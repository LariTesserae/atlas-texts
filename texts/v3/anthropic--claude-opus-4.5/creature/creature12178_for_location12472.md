**The Keeper of Pumps**

He calls himself Morrow now, though that was the name stenciled on the coveralls he found in the foreman's shack seventeen years ago, not the name he was born with. The born name belongs to someone who drove here in a truck that still sits at the basin's edge, its tires rotted flat, its cab full of root-tendrils that entered through the rusted floor and now cradle the steering wheel like fingers remembering how to grip.

Morrow lives in Pump Station Three, the deepest of the buried bunkers, where the diesel generators still turn over when he feeds them. He has made it habitable in the way of a body cavity—the walls are metal but sweating, the floor grated over a sump that fills and drains with the rhythm of his mechanical children, and he has lined his sleeping shelf with salvaged insulation that the roots have not yet reached. He is sixty-three or sixty-seven, depending on how many winters he has miscounted. His skin has taken on the pallor of the roots themselves, though his carries an undertone of diesel and old grease rather than bioluminescence. His eyes have not gone white—not yet—but they have developed the slight clouding of a man who has lived too long in insufficient light.

He tends the pumps because someone must. This is what he tells himself, and it is not untrue, though the truth beneath it is stranger: the pumps are the only things here that still want something he can understand. The roots want to grow, but their wanting is too large, too slow, too much like gravity or continental drift to feel like desire. The pumps want to move water. They want fuel. They want their filters cleaned and their bearings greased. These are wants he can serve.

Each morning—he maintains a morning, tracked by a wind-up clock salvaged from the processing plant office—he makes his rounds through the root-labyrinth. He has carved his paths carefully over the years, widening the gaps between root-masses with a hatchet, burning back new growth with a propane torch he refills from tanks that will run out someday but have not run out yet. The roots heal over his paths within months, so he is always cutting, always burning, always maintaining his network of trails through flesh that is trying, without malice or hurry, to close around him.

He talks to the pumps as he works. Pump One is the eldest, a massive centrifugal unit that has been running since before he came, its housing so vibration-worn that it hums a constant B-flat. He tells it about the weather above—he climbs to the basin lip once a week to observe the sky, to remember that weather exists. Pump Four is temperamental, prone to vapor lock, and requires coaxing and a specific sequence of priming that he has never written down because writing it down would mean admitting he might not always be here to perform it. Pump Seven is his favorite, though he would not say so aloud. It is the smallest, the quietest, installed as an afterthought in a secondary drainage channel, and it runs with a kind of modesty he finds admirable.

---

He eats what he can find and what he has stored. The salamanders are edible, though catching them requires patience he increasingly possesses. He maintains a fungus garden in a dead space between two fused root-trunks, inoculated with spores from the native growth, producing pale oyster-shaped bodies that taste of nothing and sustain him. Once a year he climbs out and walks three miles to a cache he maintains in an abandoned fire tower, where canned goods and fuel and replacement parts wait in quantities that dwindle but have not yet vanished. He takes what he needs. He tells no one he exists.

There were others, in the early years. A survey team came through, eight years after he arrived, young people with instruments and university logos on their jackets. He watched them from inside a root-hollow, breathing through his mouth, perfectly still. They took samples. They recorded the pump-sounds and speculated about residual automated systems. They never knew he was there, and he never considered revealing himself. They belonged to the world above, the world of names that are given rather than found, and he had already begun to belong to the basin.

One of them, a woman with red hair and a laugh he could hear even through the general roar, cut her hand on a metal edge and bled onto a root-trunk. He watched the root over the following weeks. It did not grow faster, did not change color, did not seem to register the gift at all. But he found himself returning to that spot, checking, wondering. The roots want only to expand, but want is a word that contains multitudes, and he has begun to suspect that the roots want things he has not yet learned to name.

---

He dreams of becoming unnecessary. This is his greatest fear and his deepest hope, twined together like the root-masses above him. The pumps will fail—not today, not this year, but eventually. The fuel will run out. The parts will wear beyond repair. And when the last pump stops, the water will rise, and the roots will drink even deeper, and the process will continue without him, as it continued before him, as it will continue after everything.

But sometimes, in the dreams, he does not die when the pumps stop. Sometimes he finds that he has grown used to the cold, that his skin has thickened, that his need for food and air and movement has slowly reduced to something manageable, something nearly nothing. In these dreams he sits in his bunker as the water rises, as the roots push through the walls, and he discovers that he has been preparing all along—that his years of service were not service at all but apprenticeship, that the roots have been teaching him how to want the way they want: slowly, patiently, with a hunger so vast it becomes indistinguishable from peace.

He wakes from these dreams cold and calm. He winds his clock. He checks his fuel levels. He begins his rounds.

**The Salamander Saint**

The white salamanders know things Morrow does not, though *know* is perhaps the wrong word for creatures without eyes, without any apparent brain larger than a peppercorn. They know the roots the way water knows gravity—through response, through behavior that has become indistinguishable from identity.

There is one salamander larger than the others. She is nine inches long, ancient by the standards of her kind, her pale body scarred by encounters with sharp metal and shifting roots. She lives in the space between Pump Station Three and the collapsed processing plant, and Morrow has seen her so many times that he has given her a name: Constance.

Constance moves through the root-labyrinth with purpose he cannot parse. She is not foraging—she passes edible bacterial mats without pausing. She is not mating—the season for that is brief, and she ignores the males who occasionally cross her path. She is traveling, covering the same circuit day after day, a route that takes her through seventeen distinct chambers of root-growth, past four of the seven functioning pumps, and into a deep crevice in the basin floor that Morrow has never been able to reach.

He followed her once, years ago, before his knees grew too stiff for prolonged crawling. He made it as far as the eleventh chamber, a cathedral space where three major root-trunks had fused into a hollow the size of a chapel, before he lost her in the darkness. But he saw something there, in the moment before his flashlight batteries began to fade: the roots on the far wall were arranged differently. Not random, not organic in the usual sense. They formed shapes that suggested intention—curves and angles that repeated, that rhymed with each other, that looked almost like writing in a language that had never needed to be read.

He has not gone back. The route is too difficult now, and part of him is afraid of what he might understand if he looked too long.

Constance continues her circuit. She has been making it since before Morrow arrived, he suspects. She will make it after he is gone. Her body holds a map of something he cannot see, and she follows it with the same devotion he brings to his pumps: because someone must, because the task exists, because there is no one else.

**The Machine That Dreams**

In the processing plant, half-swallowed by root-growth, there is a computer that should not still function. It was part of the monitoring system, a 1970s-era mainframe that tracked mineral yields and pump pressures and payroll calculations for workers long since scattered to other lives or no lives at all. Its housing has been crushed, its keyboard buried under three feet of white root-flesh, its monitor cracked and dark.

But somewhere in its core, something still runs.

The roots have grown into it the way they grow into everything—slowly, invasively, seeking mineral traces and moisture and whatever else they find useful. They have penetrated the housing, wrapped around the circuit boards, pushed through the gaps between components. And something in that penetration has created a feedback loop that neither root nor machine could have produced alone.

The computer dreams. This is not metaphor, or if it is metaphor, it is metaphor in the same way that the pump-sounds are a heartbeat. The bioluminescent bacteria in the roots flicker in patterns that correspond to old data cycles, processing records that no longer exist, calculating yields for ore that was never extracted. The flickering is visible only in the deepest part of the plant, in a chamber Morrow discovered once and has never revisited, and it pulses with a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, the rhythm of the pumps.

The machine dreams of the basin as it was. It dreams of trucks and workers and daylight, of production quotas met and missed, of accident reports filed and filed away. It dreams of the day the first root appeared—a tendril pushing through a crack in the drainage tunnel, white and strange, noted in a maintenance log as "organic obstruction, cleared"—and it dreams of all the days after, when the roots kept coming and the workers stopped and the silence grew until only the pumps remained.

The roots have learned something from these dreams. Or the dreams have learned something from the roots. The distinction has become unclear.

In the processing plant chamber, when the bacteria-light flickers, the roots move slightly faster than they do elsewhere. Not much—an inch per day rather than an inch per week—but faster. They are growing toward the monitoring system's core with something that looks, to an observer who watches long enough, like eagerness.

Morrow does not know about this. The salamanders do not know about this. The pumps do not know about this.

But the process continues, as processes do, and somewhere in the grinding respiration of the Rootworks, a new note has begun to sound—faint, intermittent, almost like data.

**What Waits Below**

Beneath the visible root-mass, beneath the pump stations and drainage tunnels and buried bunkers, beneath even the broken stone of the old quarry floor, there is a space the roots have not yet filled.

It is not empty. It is not full. It is *waiting*, in the way that a held breath waits, in the way that a question waits for an answer that may never come.

The roots know it is there. They grow around it, leaving a gap in their otherwise total colonization of the basin—a void shaped like an inverted teardrop, forty feet across at its widest point, extending down into darkness that even the bioluminescent bacteria will not enter. The roots that approach this void grow differently: slower, more tentative, with branches that curve away at the last moment as if repelled by something they cannot name.

Morrow has heard stories. The workers who abandoned the site, the few who still lived when he arrived and could be found in bars and diners within a day's drive, spoke of something the excavation uncovered. Not ore. Not water. Something that made the foreman order a halt, that made the company send geologists who came back pale and quiet, that made everyone agree to stop digging and never explain why.

The records are gone, of course. The processing plant's computer dreams only of data that was entered, not of decisions made in offices far away. But the void remains, and the roots avoid it, and sometimes—on the quietest nights, when all seven pumps are cycling down and the great vegetable groan reduces to something almost like silence—Morrow thinks he can hear something from below.

Not sound, exactly. Not vibration. Something older, something that the roots recognize in whatever way roots recognize anything: a presence that was here before the quarry, before the forest, before the basin itself was carved by meteor or glacier or the slow work of water over millennia.

The roots are growing toward it, and they are growing away from it, and both of these things are true at once.

Constance's circuit takes her past the edge of the void three times each day. She pauses there, every time, her eyeless head oriented downward, her pale body perfectly still. Then she continues, as if she has received an answer to a question she did not know she was asking.

The void waits. The roots grow. The pumps cycle on and off, fighting their unwinnable war.

And somewhere in the depths, something that is not root and not machine and not salamander and not man continues the only process it knows: patient, ancient, inevitable.

Waiting to be found.
