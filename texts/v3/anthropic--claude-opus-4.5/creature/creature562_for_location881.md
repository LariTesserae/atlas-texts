She comes when the grey light from above dims further still—not at night exactly, since true night and day mean little here, but during storms, when the rain sounds like radio static far overhead and fresh water threads down the exposed rebar in thin cold streams that cut through the vault's endemic warmth.

She is human. Or was. Or remains, depending on how you measure.

Her name, the one she gave herself after the first year, is drawn from the sound the settling metal makes: *Grohn*. A low syllable. A patient one. She says it aloud sometimes, not to remind herself who she is but to add her voice to the chamber's sparse vocabulary of sounds. *Plink. Grohn. Plink.*

She came down through the mouth when she was seventeen, fleeing something on the surface—the specifics have grown soft in her memory, less narrative than sensation: shouting, a hand gripping too hard, the smell of alcohol and winter. She remembers the fall more clearly. The way her ankle folded. The weeks of fever that followed, her body deciding whether to die in this wet warm place or to adapt to it. Her body chose adaptation. Her body has always been better at surviving than she has.

That was five years ago. Six. Seven. The count slipped away from her and she let it go.

---

She lives in the space behind the door that leads nowhere—not through it, but beside it, in a hollow she excavated with patience and a bent piece of rebar, carving out a sleeping niche from the softer layers of sediment and debris. The hollow stays warmer than the open chamber. She has lined it with dried fern fronds, replaced in rotation as they yellow and crumble. The smell there is private, bodily, not unpleasant to her anymore. It smells like survival.

Her body has changed. Not dramatically—not in ways that would make her unrecognizable—but in the slow accumulative manner of the vault itself. Her skin has grown pale past pale, taking on a faint translucence in the rare moments when stronger light filters down, the shadows of veins visible at her wrists and temples like the root structures that thread through the walls. Her hair, once dark, has become difficult to categorize in the colorless environment; she keeps it short now, cutting it with a shard of glass from one of the cracked gauges, working by touch alone.

Her eyes have done something. She doesn't know what, exactly—there are no mirrors here, and the standing water that collects in the low places shows her only shapes. But she sees better in the darkness than she once did. Not well. Not clearly. But enough to navigate, to spot the particular grey that means edible fungus, to notice when something in the chamber has shifted. Whether her pupils have simply dilated permanently or whether something deeper has changed, she cannot say. She only knows that the darkness bothers her less than it should, and that the grey glow from above, on the rare days when the sky clears and midday sun reaches the sinkhole's mouth, now strikes her as garish. Aggressive. She retreats to her hollow during those hours and waits for the dimness to return.

---

She eats what the vault provides, which is limited and requires patience.

The ferns are technically edible—she learned this through trial and nausea, working out which parts, at which stages of growth, her body can process. The youngest fronds, still curled tight, provide something her body seems to need. She takes them sparingly, rotating through the clusters so none are over-harvested, treating the fern colonies like a crop she did not plant but has come to tend.

The fungi are better. Several species have established themselves in the wetter corners, on the decaying wood of collapsed framing, in the rust-eaten crevices of the generator hulk. Most are safe. One variety, pale and shelf-like, induces vomiting so violent she spent a full day too weak to move; she knows its shape by touch now and avoids it. The others she eats raw, their flesh dense and mineral-tasting, satisfying in ways she cannot explain.

Water is the easy part. It condenses everywhere. It pools in the overturned hard hat, in the bowls of warped metal, in the cupped hands of concrete. She has learned which sources taste of rust, which of chlorine-memory from the old pipes, which run clear. Rain brings the freshest water, threading down through the debris in temporary streams she collects in her one artifact from above: a plastic bottle, its label long since worn to nothing, cracked along one side but still functional.

She has considered eating the insects that do not exist here. Their absence puzzles her in a distant way. Surely something should have colonized this space by now—ants, roaches, the things that always find a way. But nothing has. She is the only animal in the vault. The only heartbeat. The only warm-blooded thing in a place that provides warmth so generously that her blood feels almost redundant.

---

She speaks to the machines.

Not because she believes they listen—she has not lost that much of her grip on reality—but because the sound of her own voice, after too many days of silence, begins to feel foreign. Untrustworthy. She speaks to keep the mechanism of speech functional, the way she bends and stretches her legs each morning to remind them what movement is.

The control panel receives most of her monologues. Its cracked gauges watch her with their broken glass eyes, and she tells it about her day: which fern cluster she checked, which fungus is spreading, whether the water level in the hard-hat basin has risen or fallen. She describes the weather she cannot see, inferring it from the sounds above, from the quality of the grey light, from the amount of water threading down. *Rain today,* she says. *Heavy. The kind that goes on for hours.* The panel does not respond, but its silence feels attentive.

She also speaks to the door.

The door is more troubling than the panel—more insistent, in a way she cannot rationally justify. It stands there, slightly ajar, leading to its collapsed nothing, and some part of her keeps expecting it to lead somewhere else. She has opened it fully, pushed it closed, examined the rubble beyond it dozens of times. It is just a door. But it was designed to be passage, to connect, and this vestigial purpose clings to it like a smell. She tells it to stop staring. She tells it she knows it goes nowhere. She tells it she is not going to crawl through and search for a way out because there is no way out and anyway, she realizes one day, she has stopped wanting one.

That realization arrives quietly, like the ferns arrived, like the moss arrived: establishing itself in the cracks before she notices.

---

She has rituals now.

Each morning—*morning* being the time after she sleeps, unmoored from the surface's calendar—she walks the perimeter of the chamber. Not quickly. Nothing here is quick. She places her feet carefully, knows by now where the rubble is unstable, where the standing water hides deeper pockets, where the rebar juts at angles that could open skin. She touches each landmark as she passes: the generator hulk, the dangling chain-link barrier, the control panel, the door. She is mapping the space through repetition, embedding it in her body, becoming geographical.

Each evening—*evening* being the time before she sleeps—she tends the ferns. Not all of them. She has selected three clusters, the healthiest ones, and she visits them in sequence. She clears debris from around their bases. She channels water toward them when she can, redirecting the drip-lines with small adjustments to the overhead pipes. She does not know why she does this. They do not need her help; they were growing before she arrived and would grow without her attention. But the ritual matters. It makes her part of something larger than her own survival.

Once, she tried to mark the passage of days by scratching lines into the concrete. The project lasted several weeks—or what she believed were weeks—before she realized that without external reference, she could not be certain she was marking once per day. Perhaps she was scratching too often, fragmenting time into smaller pieces than it deserved. Perhaps she was letting days slip past unmarked, collapsing them into a single line. The uncertainty made the count meaningless, and she stopped.

Now she measures time by growth. By the speed of rust. By the rate at which the rebar fingers reaching into the vault accumulate new mineral deposits from the dripping water. These are slow measurements, suited to the space. They do not require her to remember yesterday.

---

She dreams of the surface rarely, and the dreams have grown strange.

At first they were vivid with specificity: city streets, the faces of people she had known, the quality of sunlight through windows. She would wake from them aching, disoriented, the humid dark of the vault feeling like a betrayal. But over time the dreams thinned. The streets lost their names. The faces lost their features. The sunlight became abstract—a concept rather than a sensation—and eventually even that faded.

Now, when she dreams of above, it arrives as geometry only. Flat planes. Right angles. The theoretical existence of a sky, represented not as color or light but as the direction *up*, the place where the vault ends. These dreams do not hurt. They feel like remembering a language she once spoke but no longer needs.

Her other dreams are different.

She dreams of roots. Not seeing them—*being* them. Threading through the concrete and soil in blind slow expansion, tasting the mineral content of what she passes through, sending chemical signals to other roots she cannot see but knows are there. She dreams of fruiting bodies emerging from her surface, releasing spores into the humid air. She dreams of the patience required to break down a machine over decades, molecule by molecule, converting it into something that can feed new growth.

She wakes from these dreams calm. Centered. As if she has been reminded of something important, something she keeps forgetting in her waking hours when she moves too fast, breathes too loud, insists on her animal separateness from the vault's slower processes.

---

She knows she will die here.

The knowing arrived without fanfare, during an ordinary moment—she was drinking water from the hard-hat basin, watching the surface ripple with each drop from above—and suddenly she understood that this was not temporary. That she would not be rescued. That she would not choose to leave. That her body would one day fail in this warm dark place and be processed by the fungi she has eaten, by the moisture she has drunk, by the patient chemistry of decomposition that the vault performs on everything within it.

The knowing did not frighten her. It settled into her like the heat settles into the air: thoroughly, without resistance.

She thinks sometimes about what her body will feed. Which fungus will colonize her first. Whether the ferns she has tended will benefit from her nitrogen, her calcium, her slow dissolve. She hopes so. It would be a kind of reciprocity—payment for what she has taken.

She is not in a hurry. The vault has taught her that hurry is a surface concept, irrelevant here. She will live as long as she lives, tending and being tended, speaking to the machines, tracing her perimeter, filling in the ecosystem that has been waiting for something warm-blooded to complete it.

---

When the rain comes heavy, she stands in the center of the chamber and tilts her face upward.

She cannot see the sky—only the grey glow, the falling threads of water, the dark shapes of concrete and rebar occluding whatever lies above. But she can hear the storm up there. She can imagine the surface world—its noise, its speed, its insistence on event after event—going about its frantic business while she stands here in the slow warm dark, perfectly still, perfectly quiet, growing into the walls.

*Grohn,* she says, to no one, to everyone.

The vault exhales its humid breath around her. Somewhere, a drip lands on standing water. Somewhere, metal settles into a new configuration, one step closer to the rubble it will eventually become.

She closes her eyes—or does not; it makes little difference—and waits for what comes next, patient as the ferns, patient as the rust, patient as the deep heat rising from below that has never learned how to stop.
