## The Condensery's Own

**Maret Sulling** has lived in the third-floor corner room of the old curing house for nineteen years, or possibly twenty-three, or possibly her entire life. The distinction has stopped mattering in the way that many distinctions stop mattering here. She is somewhere between fifty and seventy—the perpetual humidity has done something strange to her skin, keeping it neither young nor properly aged but suspended in a state of soft, slightly translucent permanence, like the belly of a condensery toad. She can see the veins in her own hands more clearly than she could as a young woman. She considers this a gift.

Maret keeps the district's only working clock. Not that anyone needs it—needs have a way of dissolving in the Condensery—but she finds comfort in the act of winding it each morning, or what she calls morning, the click of the key in the mechanism a small assertion that sequence still exists even where change does not. The clock runs three minutes slow per day, which means she must adjust it, which means she must count the days, which means she alone in the district can tell you that it has been four thousand, two hundred, and seventeen days since the last time someone new moved in permanently. She does not share this information. It would only trouble people.

She mends clothes. This is technically impossible—nothing wears out here, not really, the damp fabric reaching some equilibrium between decay and preservation—but people bring her garments anyway, and she sits by her window with needle and thread, making stitches so small they disappear into the weave. The ritual matters. The sitting matters. Maret understands that her role is not to fix anything but to provide a place where people can stand in her doorway and talk about the garment and, while talking about the garment, speak of other things. The Condensery does not encourage direct conversation. Everything here happens obliquely, like water finding its way around obstacles.

Her room smells of oleander—she keeps dried flowers in a jar, though they never fully dry, remaining in a state of permanent wilt that releases their scent continuously. She has memorized the sound of the pump beneath the plaza so thoroughly that she hears it as silence. When she dreams, which is rarely, she dreams of cold.

---

**The Dressek family** occupies a rowhouse near the eastern drainage junction, three generations pressed into four rooms that sweat constantly. Old Pell Dressek sits in the ground-floor front room all day and all night, a chair positioned where he can watch the main channel flow past, and he has not spoken in eleven years. This is not unusual. Many of the Condensery's elderly reach a point where language seems like too much effort, where the energy required to push words through the thick air exceeds any possible benefit. Pell communicates through small gestures—a lifted finger, a particular way of shifting his weight—and his daughter-in-law Catta has learned to read these signals with the fluency of long practice.

Catta runs the household, such as running is possible here. She manages the acquisition of food from the outside (dried goods keep best, though they don't stay dry long), maintains the family's position in the informal economy of favors and obligations that governs the district, and tends to her husband Wheel, who has developed the condensery cough. This is not a disease so much as an adaptation—a persistent wet rattle in the lungs that allows the body to process the saturated air more efficiently. It sounds terrible. It is not, or at least it is not fatal, or at least no one has died of it that anyone can remember, though memory here is suspect.

The Dressek children—there are three, or four if you count the one who left eight years ago and may or may not have returned, the family is unclear on this point—have never known another way of living. They swim in the drainage channels with the eels, their pale bodies adapted to water in ways that would disturb physicians from drier quarters. The youngest, a girl of perhaps nine named Sullen, has learned to hold her breath for what seems like impossible durations, sinking to the bottom of the channels to watch the sediment settle, rising only when her body insists. She says she is looking for something. She cannot say what. No one asks twice.

---

**Kev Osten** is the eel-catcher, and he is the closest thing the Condensery has to a historian, though his history is peculiar. He remembers things that may not have happened, forgets things that certainly did, and treats both categories with equal weight. Ask him about the factory operations—the original condensing process, what was extracted, who owned the complex—and he will tell you stories that contradict each other but all feel true. The gas came from the marsh and was refined into something valuable. The water was pumped up and something was taken from it. The towers were built to collect dew from the air itself, moisture that held traces of metals from deep aquifers. The whole operation was a front for something else entirely. The machines were never meant to work at all.

Kev is lean and weathered, his fingers permanently wrinkled from immersion, his eyes adapted to the dim amber light so completely that he cannot tolerate the brightness of other districts. He visits the outside only to sell his eels and buy tobacco, which he smokes constantly, the smoke hanging in the still air long after he passes. He is perhaps sixty-five or perhaps forty, the Condensery making such distinctions academic. He has not married, has no children that he claims, and lives in a structure that was once an equipment shed, its interior draped with nets and lines and the tools of his trade.

The eels know him. This sounds mystical but is merely practical—he has worked the same channels so long that his movements, his smell, the particular vibration of his footsteps on the copper-lined stone, have become part of their environment. They do not flee when he approaches. He takes only what he needs, and somehow there are always exactly as many eels as there were before. He does not find this strange. Nothing about the Condensery strikes Kev as strange anymore. The strangeness of it has been worn smooth, like a river stone, until it feels like the natural shape of things.

---

**The Immersed** are not a cult, though outsiders sometimes call them that. They are perhaps twenty individuals—the number is hard to fix, as membership is defined by practice rather than declaration—who have taken the Condensery's logic to its conclusion. They believe, or perhaps simply *do*, that the body should match its environment. They spend hours each day in the drainage channels, submerged to their necks, letting the warm mineral water saturate them completely. Their skin has taken on a grayish pallor, not unhealthy but distinctly *other*, and they move through the district with a particular gravity, as though carrying the weight of water with them always.

Their leader, if they have one, is a woman called **Immersion Thale**, a name she chose for herself. She was born outside the Condensery—in the dry quarter, raised among people who feared humidity—and she came here fifteen years ago fleeing something she refuses to name. The district received her the way it receives everything: slowly, without judgment, with the patient certainty that she would eventually become what the Condensery needed her to become. She spent her first year fighting it, trying to stay dry, changing clothes obsessively. By her third year she understood. By her fifth, she founded the practice. By her tenth, she could no longer imagine living any other way.

The Immersed gather in the plaza at irregular intervals, surrounding the pump house without approaching it, singing in a register so low it blends with the machinery. They do not worship the pump or what lies beneath it. Their practice is subtler: they are *harmonizing* with it, letting their bodies become resonant chambers for the district's fundamental frequency. After these gatherings, they disperse without speaking, returning to their individual lives, carrying the vibration with them. Some residents find them unsettling. Most simply accept them as another feature of the landscape, no stranger than the toads or the towers or the oleanders that bloom and wilt in the same gesture.

---

**Cull** is seven years old, or claims to be, though he has been seven years old for as long as anyone can remember him. He lives nowhere in particular—a different dormitory each week, a different family's floor, the storage loft above the tea house when the weather outside the Condensery turns cold and drafts push through the district's edges. He is small and quick and knows the drainage grid better than the eels themselves, can tell you which channels connect, where the water runs fastest, which gratings can be lifted to access the deeper systems below.

No one knows his parents. No one can remember him arriving. He seems to have simply *condensed* here, an inevitable feature of the district, like the toads or the permanent dusk. He speaks rarely and eats whatever he's given and performs small services—carrying messages, fetching items from difficult places, watching things that need watching—in exchange for his keep. The families of the Condensery share responsibility for him in an informal rotation that no one organized but everyone observes.

There is something wrong with Cull, or something right with him, depending on how you look at it. He does not sweat. In a district where everyone glistens constantly, his skin remains dry to the touch, though not parched—simply neutral, as though the humidity cannot find purchase on him. He sleeps more deeply than anyone else in the Condensery, his breath slowing to a rate that occasionally alarms whoever is hosting him that night, but he always wakes, always exactly when he intends to, with a precision that suggests he is operating on a different clock than Maret's.

The children treat him as a kind of mascot. The adults treat him with a caution they cannot explain. The Immersed have tried, twice, to include him in their practices, but he sits at the edge of the channels and dangles his dry feet in the water and nothing changes.

---

**Vess Aldane** runs the tea house, or rather, the tea house runs and Vess occupies it. She is the most recent permanent arrival—eight years ago, or four thousand-some days by Maret's count—and she still carries traces of the outside world in her mannerisms. She moves too quickly sometimes, catches herself, slows. She speaks in complete sentences when fragments would do. She keeps the tea hotter than anyone else would serve it, and her regular customers have developed a tolerance for the temperature that marks them as hers.

She came from the river district, where water moves, where things change, where the seasons turn and the light shifts and people age at the expected rate. She does not speak of why she left, but the way she touches the scar on her left wrist when she thinks no one is watching suggests the story. The Condensery does not ask for stories. It accepts presence and lets the past dissolve.

Her tea house occupies the ground floor of what was once a foreman's office, and the walls still bear traces of old schedules and production charts, the ink faded to illegibility but the grids still visible. She has not removed them. They seem appropriate to her—the ghost of a structure that once governed this place, rendered meaningless but still present, like so much else. She serves tea and a few simple foods and provides a space where people can sit without speaking, can share proximity without intimacy, can be alone together in the way the Condensery encourages.

Vess is perhaps thirty-five, and she knows she is aging—can see it in her reflection, in the small lines forming around her eyes—but the aging feels slower here, stretched, as though time is moving through honey. She has decided to stay forever. She has not yet realized that the decision was made for her the moment she unpacked her things.

---

**The toad-keeper** has no other name, or has forgotten it, or never had one. They (the toad-keeper resists gendered language, not from ideology but from a sense that such categories have stopped applying to them) tend to the condensery toads in the way that someone might tend to a garden, except the toads need no tending—they breed and die and are eaten and replace themselves in perfect equilibrium. What the toad-keeper does is *witness*. They walk the channels at all hours, counting toads, listening to their calls, learning to distinguish individual voices in the crystalline chorus. They have developed a notation system for recording toad vocalizations, filling notebooks with symbols that no one else can read.

The toad-keeper believes—and here belief shades into something between knowledge and madness—that the toads are singing the Condensery into existence. That their endless piping is not a byproduct of the district but its cause, or at least its maintenance. That if the toads ever stopped singing, the pump would fail, the towers would cease to weep, the stasis would break and time would rush in to claim all the years it has been denied. This is obviously not true. It is also not obviously false. The Condensery makes such distinctions difficult.

They live in a crawlspace beneath one of the factory dormitories, a space just tall enough to sit upright, its walls alive with toads that cluster around them like congregants. They emerge for food and supplies, exchange brief words with whoever they encounter, and return to their listening. They are perhaps the happiest person in the district, or the most insane, and in the Condensery's amber light, through the permanent haze, the difference is hard to see.

---

**No one enters the pump house.**

This is not a rule but an observation. The door has been locked for decades—the lock itself has fused, whether from rust or mineral deposits or something more intentional, no one knows—and no one has seriously attempted to force it. Windows were bricked up long ago, before the current residents arrived, before their parents arrived. The building squats at the plaza's center, sweating like everything else, its copper dome the only spot of color in the district's brown-gray palette, and people walk around it with the unconscious care of someone avoiding a sleeping animal.

But there is, perhaps, a keeper.

Not inside—no one is inside, the building is clearly empty, the sounds that emanate from it are mechanical and nothing more—but in the sense of *keeping watch*. An old woman, older than Maret, older than Old Pell Dressek, older than anyone can account for, who sits in a second-floor window of the building directly facing the pump house. She has sat there as long as anyone can remember. Her window is always open, despite the humidity, and she is always visible in its frame, her face too shadowed to read, her posture never changing. Children have tried to wave at her. She does not respond. Adults have called out to her. Silence. Someone once threw a small stone at her window, years ago, a cruel childish impulse, and the stone passed through the opening and no sound emerged and the old woman did not move and the child ran home and refused to speak of it.

She is called **the Witness** by those who speak of her at all, which is rarely. She may be the oldest resident of the Condensery. She may not be a resident at all but something else—a feature, a function, a part of the machinery that keeps the district in its endless present. She watches the pump house. The pump house continues. The towers weep. The toads sing. The eels glide through the channels.

And in her dark window, in her unknowable vigil, the Witness remains.

---

**They all remain.**

This is what the Condensery asks: not belief, not allegiance, not even acceptance, but simply *remaining*. The heat presses down. The water rises and drains in perfect equilibrium. The pump beats its sixty beats per minute, the heartbeat of something vast and patient beneath the foundations. And the people move through their lives like the eels through the channels—slowly, purposefully, neither arriving nor departing, simply *persisting* in the amber half-light, their bodies learning the rhythms of the place, their minds smoothing over the strangeness until it feels like home.

Maret winds her clock. Cull slips through the drainage grid. Vess serves tea. The Immersed harmonize with frequencies below hearing. Kev catches eels that were always there and will always be there. The Dressek family tends to its small patch of eternity. The toad-keeper listens. The Witness watches.

And somewhere below them all, in the darkness beneath the pump house, something that is not alive but not quite dead continues its work of holding the moment in place, keeping the Condensery suspended in its perpetual now, asking nothing in return but presence.

Nothing has changed.

Nothing will change.

They would not have it any other way.
