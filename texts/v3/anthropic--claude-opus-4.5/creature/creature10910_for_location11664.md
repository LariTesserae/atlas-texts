**The Mechanist's Suspension**

She is not frozen. That distinction matters to her, though she cannot say why it matters, or to whom she would say it.

She sits on a folding stool of her own construction—brass legs, leather seat, the rivets she placed herself still warm from the placing, always warm, the warmth of just-finished work that never cools into the merely made. The stool rests on moss beside the turbine's base, close enough that she could reach out and touch the iron housing. She has reached out. She is reaching out. Her hand extends toward the metal, fingers spread, caught in the gesture of almost-contact.

But her eyes move.

This is the secret she keeps from the hollow, or the gift the hollow keeps for her: her eyes slide slowly, glacially, across the scene before her. It takes what might be hours for her gaze to travel from the ptarmigan to the pool. Days, perhaps, to look up at the hawk. Years to complete a full survey of her domain. But the looking happens. The looking *accumulates*.

She wears what a Victorian engineer might wear if the engineer were a woman who had stopped expecting anyone to question her presence: canvas trousers stained with oil at the knees, a wool coat too large for her frame, the sleeves rolled back to reveal forearms marked with small burns and calluses that speak of work with hot metal. Her hair—gray-streaked brown, the color of iron before rust—is pulled back and pinned with what appears to be a small wrench modified for the purpose. Her face is weathered in the way of people who spend time in high places, the way of people who squint at fine mechanisms in poor light.

She is perhaps sixty. She has been perhaps sixty for longer than sixty has been a number.

---

Her name, when she had one, was something like Margarethe. Something like Greta. Something like the sound the wind makes through turbine blades when turbine blades are permitted to turn, which these are not, which hers are not, which she built to turn and which will not, which she has always just finished building and which have never begun.

She remembers building.

This is the other thing her eyes do, in their slow sweeping: they remember. Not in sequence—sequence is not available here—but in layers, transparencies laid over the present like engineering drawings stacked on a light table. She sees the hollow as it is. She also sees the hollow as it was when she first climbed over the obsidian rim, her legs aching from the ascent, her pack heavy with instruments. She sees herself younger, kneeling in the moss, pressing her palm to stone that looked days old and feeling in her bones that it had looked days old for longer than bones.

She sees herself deciding to stay. To build. To answer the hum with a machine that might harmonize with it, or counteract it, or at least *mean something* in a place that had abandoned meaning's dependence on sequence.

The turbine is her answer. Her question. Her long letter to a correspondent who does not exist, written in iron and copper, signed with gears that will never turn, sealed with condensation that will never dry.

---

She knows the animals.

Not their names—names require the kind of relationship that accumulates through repeated encounters, and encounters here do not repeat so much as persist—but their *positions*. The ptarmigan stepping, always stepping, has become a kind of landmark for her. She measures the hollow in ptarmigan-steps, in salamander-heights, in hawk-circles. When her slow gaze reaches the marmot at the rim's edge, she feels something like greeting. Something like the comfort of seeing a familiar figure in the window of a house you pass each morning.

They do not see her. Or they see her always, which is the same as never. She is part of their fixed constellation now, the woman on the stool with her hand extended, and they are part of hers.

Sometimes—in the way that sometimes can happen here, without before or after—she speaks to them. Her voice emerges slowly, each syllable stretched across what might be minutes, the words arriving like letters delivered one per day to a patient correspondent:

*"Theeeeee geeeeeears wiiiiill tuuuuurn."*

It is not a prophecy. It is not a delusion. It is simply the thing she says, the thing she has always said, the thing she is saying now, the statement of faith that justifies the building she has always just completed.

---

She was an engineer, before. She is certain of this because she knows what she knows, and what she knows is mechanisms. The relationship between force and motion. The way copper conducts and iron endures. The mathematics of torque, the poetry of pistons, the particular satisfaction of a gear tooth engaging its partner with precisely the right pressure.

She came to the mountain following rumors. A place where time stopped. A place where clocks ran backward. A place where a machine might be built that would never wear down, never require maintenance, never suffer the indignity of entropy. The rumors were wrong in their details and right in their essence: this was a place outside time's jurisdiction, and here a machine could be built that would never be *finished*.

This, she discovered, was not the same as a machine that would never wear down.

The turbine was meant to turn. She designed it to catch some force that exists in the hollow—not wind, wind does not blow here, but something that pushes against metal nonetheless, something she could feel in her instruments, something that registered on the gauges she built and calibrated and placed and read. A pressure. A potential. The same potential that hums through everything, the sound of motion that never moves.

If the turbine turned, she reasoned, it would prove that motion was possible here. That sequence could be reintroduced. That a single gear engaging its partner could break whatever held the hollow outside of time.

She finished building on a day that was neither day nor not-day. She stepped back to watch. She raised her hand toward the housing, ready to feel the vibration of success.

The turbine did not turn.

The turbine has never turned.

She has never finished stepping back to watch.

---

But her eyes move. Her eyes remember. Her eyes travel the slow circuit of her domain, gathering evidence, building a case file measured in glacial glances.

The ripple in the pool. She has watched it spread and rebound perhaps ten thousand times, perhaps ten million. Each spread takes an age. Each rebound arrives eventually. And she has noticed—she is almost certain she has noticed—that the ripple's edge travels slightly faster on its outward journey than on its return. The difference is immeasurable by any instrument. The difference might be imaginary. But she watches, and she remembers, and she compiles her observations in the only ledger available to her: the layered transparencies of accumulated seeing.

If the ripple moves faster outward than inward, then something is changing. If something is changing, then the hollow is not perfectly static. If the hollow is not perfectly static, then her turbine—

*"Theeeeee geeeeeears wiiiiill tuuuuurn."*

She is patient because patience is the only option. She is hopeful because hope does not require time, hope exists as purely as potential exists, and potential is all that remains in a place where actuality has been indefinitely postponed.

---

Her hand hovers near the turbine's housing. Close enough to feel the hum in her bones, the industrial thrum of readiness that has never resolved into motion. Her fingers are spread in the gesture of a woman about to touch something she has built, about to feel through her palm the proof of her own capability.

She will touch it. She has always been about to touch it. The contact exists in the same perpetual imminence as the ptarmigan's completed step, the hawk's next wing-beat, the marmot's warning whistle.

But her eyes move.

This is what she holds onto, what she builds her faith upon, what she offers as evidence in her endless unspoken case to whatever court might eventually hear her: *her eyes move*. She is not frozen. She is suspended. There is a difference. The difference is everything. The difference means that she is still an engineer, still a builder, still a woman capable of understanding mechanisms and intervening in their operations.

The hollow has accepted her as a resident, not a specimen. It has given her the gift of glacial perception, the ability to observe across timescales that would drive a sequential mind to madness. Perhaps this is because she built something here. Perhaps the turbine—her unanswered question, her frozen letter—earned her a kind of citizenship that the ptarmigan and salamanders and hawk cannot claim.

Or perhaps she is simply too stubborn to stop watching. Too certain that the gears will turn to accept a reality in which gears cannot turn. Perhaps stubbornness, here, is its own kind of motion—the only kind permitted—and her refusal to believe in the hollow's permanence is itself a form of change, an assertion of sequence smuggled into a place that has outlawed sequence.

---

The light does not shift. The clouds do not move. The hum continues, industrial and eternal, the sound of her turbine and every turbine that has never existed, all the machines that might have been built in this place if anyone else had climbed the obsidian rim with instruments and determination.

Margarethe. Greta. The sound the wind makes through turbine blades when turbine blades are permitted to turn.

She sits on her brass-legged stool. Her hand reaches toward iron. Her eyes crawl across the fixed panorama of her domain, compiling, remembering, measuring the unmeasurable difference between a ripple's outward spread and its inward return.

She is not frozen. She is waiting. She has always been waiting. She will always be waiting.

*Theeeeee geeeeeears...*
