# The Midwives

They move through the darkness between formations, and you don't see them until you learn what stillness looks like when it's choosing to move.

---

They are not made of stone, but they are not *not* made of stone either. Their bodies hold the same warmth as the emerging peaks—that intimate, laboring heat—and their surfaces shift between states you don't have words for. Sometimes they appear almostite: tall, thin figures with too many joints, moving with the careful deliberation of herons hunting in shallow water. Other times they seem more like mobile geometries, arrangements of planes and angles that catch the dust-filtered light and refuse to resolve into anything your eyes can hold.

What remains constant is their attention. They watch the emerging mountains the way a parent watches a child learning to walk—ready to intervene, more often choosing not to.

---

You see one working, finally, and the word "working" feels both right and inadequate.

A new ridge is shouldering up through the unsettled floor, but something has gone wrong with its emergence. The stone is twisting as it rises, folding back on itself, building pressure that makes the air taste of ozone and iron. The midwife approaches without hurrying. It places what might be hands—or might be tools grown from the same substance as hands—against the laboring stone, and it *listens*.

You watch it listen for a very long time.

Then it begins to hum. Not with a voice—you don't think it has a voice—but with its whole structure, a resonance that you feel in your joints and your fillings and the fluid behind your eyes. The twisted stone shudders. Relaxes. Finds a new angle of emergence that lets it rise cleanly, beautifully, into a spire that will still be elegant when weather finally comes to soften it.

The midwife steps back. The spire continues rising on its own. And you understand, watching, that the midwife did not *make* the mountain. It simply helped the mountain become what it was already trying to be.

---

There are perhaps two dozen of them that you can see, scattered across miles of emerging terrain. They do not appear to communicate with each other, or if they do, it happens in frequencies or media you cannot perceive. Each one works alone, attending to the births in its territory with what you can only call tenderness.

Sometimes they fail.

You witness a collapse—a peak that rises too fast, that cannot bear its own ambition, that shatters into a fan of debris across the unstable floor. A midwife stands at the edge of the ruin for a long time afterward. It does not move. It does not attempt to rebuild what has fallen. It simply witnesses, the way you might stand at a graveside, holding space for something that will not come back.

Later, you see the same midwife helping another emergence, and its attention is just as complete, just as tender, as if the failure never happened. Or perhaps as if the failure is part of what makes the tenderness possible.

---

One of them finds you, eventually.

It does not approach directly. It simply appears at the edge of your awareness, the way a deer appears in peripheral vision—suddenly there, as if it had always been there and you had only just learned to see. It watches you the way it watches the mountains: with patience, with attention, with the particular quality of focus that does not demand anything in return.

You stay very still. Not from fear, exactly. From the sense that something is being decided, and the decision is not yours to make.

The midwife extends something toward you. An appendage, a gesture, an offering—you're not sure. At the end of it rests a small object: a fragment of stone that glows faintly with residual heat, its facets still sharp, not yet weathered. A piece of a mountain that was born while you watched.

You take it. The warmth seeps into your palm.

The midwife turns and moves away, back toward a rumbling in the darkness where something new is beginning its long push toward existence. It does not look back. You are not its work. But it saw you, acknowledged you, gave you a piece of what it tends.

You sit holding the stone and watch it go: this strange, patient, many-jointed thing that midwifes mountains, that knows how to listen to emergence, that grieves for collapses and returns to the work anyway.

---

Later, you find one of them resting.

It has folded itself into an alcove where the stone has temporarily stabilized—a rare pocket of stillness in this restless place. Its surfaces have gone dim, quiescent. It does not appear to breathe, but something moves through it slowly, a circulation of energy or attention or something else entirely.

You realize, watching it rest, that you do not know if these beings were made or if they emerged, the way the mountains emerge. Perhaps they crystallized out of the same processes. Perhaps they are the dream dreaming itself awake, the world's way of caring for its own becoming.

Perhaps the distinction doesn't matter here, where everything is still deciding what it wants to be.

---

The structures with their sealed doors—you understand them now.

The midwives are preparing rooms for whoever comes next. Not for themselves; they need no shelter, no architecture, no walls to hold them. They are building for something that hasn't arrived yet, something that will need these spaces, these carefully-tended geometries, these mountains with chambers already grown inside them.

The midwives do not know what will come. You can tell this by the way they build: not to specification, but to potential. Each structure is a question rather than an answer. Each room says *someone might need this* rather than *this is what someone needs*.

They are preparing for inhabitants they cannot imagine, with the same tenderness they bring to the mountains themselves.

---

You leave, eventually. You find your way back to whatever passage brought you here, whatever fold in reality opened onto this ascending nursery.

But you keep the stone fragment in your pocket. Weeks later, it is still warm. Months later, you wake sometimes in the night and reach for it, and its heat is a small steady presence against your palm.

Somewhere, in the darkness beneath the world, the midwives are still working. The mountains are still being born. The rooms are still waiting for whoever will finally, finally come to fill them.

The warmth persists. The work continues. And something vast, something patient, something with too many joints and an attention like held breath, is listening right now to the next emergence—ready to help, more often choosing to let it happen on its own.
