**The Tender**

She has been here for eleven years, or perhaps twelve. The distinction stopped mattering when the surface communications degraded to monthly bursts of static, then quarterly, then nothing at all. She knows the year only by her own body's calendar—the way her joints predict the cycling of the heating system, the silver threading through the dark rope of her hair, the reading glasses she fashioned from salvaged lenses and wire that now live permanently on her face, held there by a strap of braided kelp fiber.

Her name is Yuen. She stopped using it aloud when she realized there was no one to hear it, but she still thinks it sometimes, a small assertion: *I am Yuen. I tend the kelp.*

---

She sleeps in a maintenance alcove originally designed for equipment storage, a space barely large enough to lie flat. Her bed is a foam pad from the original crew quarters—recovered during her first year when she systematically explored every accessible area of the vault—and she has lined the walls with dried kelp blades, their oils long since evaporated but still carrying a faint mineral smell that she finds comforting. The warmth of the water seeps through the alcove's walls, and she sleeps in a perpetual state of almost-sweating, her thin thermal suit clinging to her skin.

She wakes when the heating system cycles. Not from the sound—she no longer consciously hears that—but from the subtle pressure change in the water, a shift in the way the warmth moves against her exposed hands and face. Her body has synchronized itself to the vault's rhythms so completely that she cannot remember what it felt like to wake to sunlight, to the sounds of other people, to anything external.

---

Her days follow patterns she has refined over years into something approaching ritual.

First: the inspection route. She moves through the kelp forest sideways, as the space demands, her hands trailing along the fronds with the automatic attention of someone checking fence posts. She knows every platform, every rack, every anomaly in the growth patterns. When she finds a blade with signs of disease—discoloration, unusual texture, the early stages of something bacterial—she removes it with a small knife she wears on a cord around her neck. The blade has been sharpened so many times it's half its original width.

She talks to the kelp while she works. Not words exactly—more a continuous murmur, half humming, half narration. *Yes, that one. Growing well. This one's slow. Need to check the nutrient line.* The sound of her own voice has become necessary, a way of confirming she still exists. The silence of the vault would swallow her otherwise.

Second: the equipment rounds. She knows every pipe, every valve, every flickering light panel with the intimacy of a physician who has tended the same patient for a decade. The heating system requires the most attention—she has replaced seals using materials scavenged from other vaults (the ones she can reach through the maintenance tunnels, the ones where the kelp has died or never grew, their platforms standing empty in cold water). The nutrient dispensers she has repaired so many times that most of their original components have been replaced with improvised substitutes. She is proud of this work. The machines continue because she wills them to continue.

Third: she eats. Her diet is kelp—raw, dried, fermented in stone crocks she discovered in a supply cache. The red dulse when she can reach it. Occasionally one of the transparent crustaceans, caught and eaten quickly before she can think too much about the crunch of its body between her teeth. She has grown thin over the years, her body adapting to the sparse nutrition, but she has not grown weak. The work keeps her strong.

---

She was not supposed to be alone.

The vault was designed for a crew of four, with rotating shifts to the surface station for rest and resupply. She was the most junior technician—young then, barely trained, assigned to kelp cultivation because it was considered simple work, because she had shown an aptitude for patience and solitude during her assessment. The others were supposed to teach her. They were supposed to be here.

She does not know what happened. One day the surface elevator stopped responding. Then the communications. Then the supply drops. She waited for rescue—for weeks, then months. She maintained the equipment because it was her job, and because the routine kept the panic at a distance. Eventually she understood that no one was coming, and by then she had already become what the vault needed her to be.

---

She has dreams, still. This surprises her—she would have thought they'd fade with the sameness of the days, the absence of new input. But her sleeping mind invents constantly: sunlight on water, voices she half-remembers, a childhood home that may or may not have existed as she pictures it. Sometimes she dreams of the kelp growing through her body, anchoring her to the platform, turning her into another growing thing in the dim light. She wakes from these dreams without fear. It seems like a reasonable way to end.

---

The maintenance port in the far corner troubles her.

She checked it once, in her second year—squeezed through the half-open gap into the tunnel beyond, felt the cold of the true ocean creeping in, saw things moving in the darkness that her lamp could not fully illuminate. She retreated. She has not gone back. But she watches the port sometimes from across the vault, noting the way the cold currents disturb the warm water, the way the small pale things drift in and die.

Something larger came through, once. She was sleeping when it happened—woke to find a shape tangled in the kelp near the port, thrashing weakly. By the time she reached it, the warmth had already begun to kill it. She cannot describe what it was. The body was wrong in ways that suggested deep adaptation, pressure and darkness and time. She removed it before it could foul the water, dragging it back toward the port and pushing it through into the cold tunnel beyond.

She sealed the port as far as the corroded wheel would allow—an inch further than it had been. It wasn't much. It was something.

---

She has marked the walls near her sleeping alcove with small scratches—not counting days, but noting events. A particularly good harvest. A repair she's proud of. The time she found a cache of preserved supplies in a vault three tunnels over, enough vitamin supplements to last years. The time she woke to find one of the crustaceans had crawled onto her chest and was resting there, its translucent body rising and falling with her breath, and she let it stay because she wanted to feel the weight of another living creature.

She marked the last communication from the surface, eleven or twelve years ago. The voice had been calm, official, discussing supply schedules that no longer mattered. She cannot remember what it said exactly. She remembers the sound of it—human, alive, somewhere above the weight of all that water—and she marks that day every year, or what she calculates to be a year, as a kind of anniversary.

---

When the harvesting machinery wakes—and it does, still, following some automated calendar she has never been able to fully decode—she overrides it manually. The cutting arms unfold, begin their programmed patterns, and she interrupts them with commands entered on a keypad whose labels have worn to illegibility. She knows the codes by muscle memory now. The machinery accepts her intervention and returns to sleep.

She cannot explain why she does this. The vault was built to produce kelp for some purpose, to harvest and process and send it somewhere. The harvesting is part of the design. But she has spent too many years watching the forest grow, tending it, listening to its small sounds in the silence. She cannot allow it to be cut. Not until she knows what it was for. Not until someone comes to explain.

---

She is fifty-three years old, or perhaps fifty-four. Her body has begun to betray her in small ways—the glasses, the aching joints, a persistent cough that worsens when the heating system fails and the water temperature drops. She thinks about this sometimes, in the quiet hours between inspection rounds: what will happen when she can no longer maintain the equipment. When she can no longer move through the forest sideways, checking the blades, pulling the diseased tissue.

The kelp will continue for a while. The automation will cycle on without her. But eventually something will fail that she would have fixed, and then something else, and the careful balance she has maintained will collapse into entropy. The cold will creep in through the maintenance port. The lights will fail entirely. The warm dark will become simply dark, and then cold, and the kelp will die, and the vault will become another empty space in the honeycomb beneath the seafloor.

She finds this thought less distressing than she expected. The kelp has grown here for longer than she has been alive. It will continue until it cannot. This is the nature of things.

---

For now, she is Yuen. She wakes when the heating system cycles. She moves through the forest sideways, her hands trailing along the fronds, murmuring her half-song into the silence. She repairs what breaks. She eats what grows. She sleeps in her alcove lined with dried kelp, dreaming of sunlight she can barely remember.

The vault hums its patient frequencies into the dark, and she hums with it, and together they continue because no one has told them to stop.
