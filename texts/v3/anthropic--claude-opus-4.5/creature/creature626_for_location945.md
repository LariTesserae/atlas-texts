## The Dwellers

They call themselves nothing. Names require voices raised above a murmur, and voices raised above a murmur draw attention—from the seams, from the geometry, from whatever principle governs the unfolding. In the oral histories, whispered parent to child in the thick-walled curve-and-corner rooms, they are simply *those who remained* or *those who learned* or sometimes just *us*, spoken so softly it barely separates from breath.

But they exist. They persist. Like the winterweed, they have found ways to continue.

---

The adults move through the settlements in patterns that mirror the protective architecture—never quite straight, never fully curved, their paths tracing the same interrupted geometries as the doorways and walls. Watch one cross a courtyard and you'll see her arc toward a destination, then break into a sharp angle, then curve again. It looks ritualistic. It is ritualistic. It is also, they insist, practical: the seam-logic has difficulty tracking movement that refuses to resolve into prediction. A straight line is a vector. A pure curve is a function. But a curve that keeps breaking—that's something the geometry beneath the stone can't quite hold in its awareness.

They learn this walking before they learn language. Infants are carried in slings against their parents' chests, feeling the rhythm of this stuttering movement, their small bodies absorbing the grammar of evasion. By the time they can toddle, they toddle in the patterns. By the time they can run—though they never run, running is loud, running draws attention—they have forgotten that walking could be any other way.

Their clothing is the color of the plateau: gray-brown, ash-pale, the precise shade of worked stone and worn bedrock. The fabric comes from winterweed, from the brief window when newly-exposed fronds can be beaten into fiber before they remember their brittleness. The process takes years of patient cultivation, tending a depression through its entire water-cycle for a harvest of cloth that might outfit three people. They wear these garments until the fabric begins to luminesce faintly, echoing its source material, at which point it must be burned in carefully contained fires that produce no smoke, only a smell of ozone and endings.

---

The builder-priests are not priests, exactly. The people of the plateau have no gods—divinity requires a voice loud enough to call to, and they cannot afford that volume. But the builder-priests serve a function that looks like priesthood from the outside: they are the keepers of the angles, the memorizers of the geometries that protect the settlements from seam-propagation. Each one carries in their memory a portion of the pattern-language, the accumulated observations of hundreds of generations compressed into rules for where to place a corner, how to interrupt a curve, what ratios keep the stone from remembering it wants to unfold.

They identify themselves young. A child who stares too long at the seams, who traces their shapes in the condensation on a water-collection vessel, who asks quiet questions about why the darkness doesn't behave like darkness—that child is watched. If the watching reveals aptitude, they are taken into the builder-priests' care, raised in the innermost rooms of the settlement where the angles are most precise, taught to see the way the plateau sees.

Some of them go mad. Madness here is quiet too—a slow withdrawal, a tendency to walk in straight lines, a growing ease in the presence of seams. The mad are not restrained or confined. They are simply allowed to walk, and eventually they walk to where the seam-darkness waits, and they fold themselves into it with expressions of profound relief. The builder-priests consider this an acceptable attrition rate. Understanding the geometry requires proximity to it, and proximity has costs.

Those who survive to mastery move differently than the other adults. Their evasive patterns are more complex, incorporating angles that hurt to watch, curves that seem to bend in directions the eye can't follow. They can stand at a seam's edge and *not be noticed by it*—can study the propagation patterns from inches away while the darkness slides past them like water around a stone. They claim no special power. They have simply learned to be a shape that the unfolding cannot grip.

---

Children are precious and children are common. The plateau's people reproduce steadily, methodically, with none of the passion that lowlanders associate with procreation. Passion is loud. Desire is a vector. They come together in darkness, in silence, in movements so restrained that the geometry beneath them feels nothing but the faint background hum of warm blood continuing to circulate.

The children that result are raised collectively. Not because the people have no sense of parentage—they track lineages carefully, incorporating them into the oral histories—but because children make noise, and noise must be contained, distributed, absorbed by the settlement's protective architecture rather than concentrated in any single dwelling. A child's cry is the most dangerous sound on the plateau. It is sharp, sudden, unpredictable—everything the seam-logic finds easy to track. So the children live in the central buildings, the ones with the most complex angle-work, their sounds muffled by walls that have been refined over centuries to catch and disperse exactly this frequency of distress.

They learn silence as they learn to walk: bone-deep, before conscious memory. By the time a child is old enough to string words together, they have already internalized that words are weapons to be used sparingly, that every sound is a small violence against the safety of everyone they love. They communicate in gesture, in expression, in the angle of their bodies as they move through the interrupted patterns. Full sentences are for emergencies. Names are for intimacy so profound it is nearly erotic.

The children play, though play is not what lowlanders would recognize. They build miniature architectures from winterweed-bone fragments, testing geometries, learning which shapes feel stable and which make the small hairs rise on their arms. They map imaginary seams in the courtyard dust and practice stepping around them. They compete, in their silent way, to see who can move most slowly across an open space, who can take up the least presence, who can make themselves most nearly invisible to the watching geometry. The winners are admired. The losers practice more.

---

The elders are thin to the point of translucence. A lifetime of eating sparingly—food must be carried from the settlements to the winter-weed tending sites to the water-collection points, and every gram matters—has pared them down to essentials: bone, sinew, the slow circulation of blood that has learned to run cool. Their skin has taken on the gray-brown of their clothing, of the stone, of the plateau itself. In certain lights, at certain angles, they are difficult to distinguish from the worked walls of their dwellings.

This is considered a form of mastery.

The oldest among them have begun to forget the difference between themselves and their surroundings. They sit in the curved-and-cornered rooms for days at a time, barely breathing, their eyes fixed on middle distances, their presence so diminished that the seam-logic slides past them entirely. The younger generations bring them water, tilt their heads back, let small sips slide down throats that have nearly forgotten swallowing. It is not death. It is not quite life. It is a third state, appropriate to a place where water exists as solid, liquid, and gas simultaneously.

Some of these elders have sat in the same positions for decades. The stone beneath them has worn smooth in the shape of their sitting. The angle-work of the room has been subtly adjusted, generation after generation, to incorporate them into the protective geometry. They have become architecture.

The people do not mourn them when they finally stop. How can you mourn something that has been part of the building for longer than anyone can remember? They simply adjust the angles to account for the absence, and continue.

---

The traders are another matter entirely.

Every settlement has three or four individuals who travel—who load the winterweed-bone sleds with hand-polished lenses and ceramic vessels and precious cloth, who set out across the worn expanse toward the distant smudges of other settlements, who make journeys that take weeks of walking in the interrupted patterns across stone that wants to unfold beneath their feet.

These people are different. They have to be.

A traveler on the open plateau has no protective architecture, no carefully-angled walls to confuse the seam-logic. They have only their own movement, their own silence, their own ability to take up as little presence as possible. They train for years before their first journey—running the complex patterns until their legs ache (silently, always silently), learning to sleep in positions that hold the protective angles even in unconsciousness, practicing the specific quality of attention that lets them sense a seam's edge before they step into it.

The traders walk alone or in pairs, never more. Groups draw attention; groups make patterns that the geometry can track. They follow the cairn-lines, those ancient signal-towers that mark the routes between settlements, but they never walk directly toward a cairn. They spiral in, approach at angles, arrive from directions that don't make sense. The cairn-builders designed the routes this way deliberately, hundreds of generations ago, and the traders trust the design with their lives.

They carry sounding-stones: small ceramic instruments that produce precise frequencies when struck just so. Not to make noise—never to make noise—but to *answer* noise. If a traveler hears something on the plateau, something that isn't the whisper of their own breath or the soft crunch of their careful footsteps, they strike the sounding-stone. The frequency it produces is specifically calibrated to confuse the seam-logic, to create a moment of geometric uncertainty. In that moment, the traveler moves—fast, for once, faster than they would ever move within a settlement—and gets somewhere else before the geometry resolves.

It works about nine times out of ten.

The traders who return from their journeys are celebrated in the quiet way everything is celebrated here: with small gatherings in the central rooms, shared meals of preserved winterweed-heart (the densest part of the frond, faintly nutritious, tasting of ozone and patience), and the exchange of news from distant settlements whispered so softly that listeners must lean close to hear.

The traders who do not return are remembered in the oral histories. Their routes are traced on the settlement's teaching-stones, their names spoken in the intimacy of silence. And then someone else begins training for the journey.

---

What do they believe?

The question assumes categories that don't quite apply. They do not believe in gods, but they believe in the seam-logic—not as a divine force but as a property of existence, like gravity, like the tendency of water to flow downhill. They believe that reality has a structure, that the structure is being revealed, that the revelation is neither good nor evil but simply *happening*. They believe that their role is to persist for as long as persistence is possible, to record what they observe, to pass their knowledge to their children, and eventually to fold into the geometry themselves when their time comes.

They believe in the oral histories: the long chain of whispered transmission that connects them to ancestors who climbed up from below, who watched the first seams open, who figured out the protective angles through centuries of trial and error and quiet death. They believe that this knowledge is sacred in the sense that it saves lives, profane in the sense that it is merely practical, and inevitable in the sense that it was always going to be discovered by someone.

They believe in the winterweed, though belief is perhaps too strong a word. They *trust* the winterweed—trust that it will persist, trust that it will provide fiber for clothing and bone for sleds and heart for food, trust that its geometry is related to the seam-geometry in ways that make it useful even if they don't fully understand. Some of the builder-priests think the winterweed is *of* the seams, a kind of surfacing of whatever lies beneath the unfolding stone. Others think it predates the seams entirely, that it was here when the plateau was mountains, that it will be here when the plateau is nothing but dimensionless dark. The winterweed offers no opinions.

They believe, quietly and without much discussion, that they are the only people who matter. Not in a moral sense—they hold no ill will toward the lowlanders, those distant masses who live in a world where sound is safe and walking can be straight. But the lowlanders' concerns are irrelevant here. The lowlanders' gods cannot help on a plateau that punishes prayer. The lowlanders' knowledge cannot protect against a geometry that existed before knowledge was possible. The plateau people have their own wisdom, hard-won and specific, and that wisdom is all that stands between them and the unfolding dark.

It is a lonely belief. It is also true.

---

In the deepest room of each settlement, in the space where the angles are most complex and the walls have been worked and reworked over generations, there is a keeper of the map.

The map is not drawn. It cannot be drawn. The seam-logic would track a drawing, would trace the lines, would recognize its own shape and propagate toward the recognition. Instead, the map is held in a single human mind: the locations of every known seam on the plateau, their rates of propagation, their predicted paths over but the coming generations, the dwindling spaces of whole stone where life can continue.

The keeper is chosen young—even younger than the builder-priests—and trained in techniques of memory that have been refined over millennia. They learn to partition their minds, to hold the map in a separate space from their ordinary thoughts, to access it only in the specific trance-state that the seam-logic cannot penetrate. They eat specific foods, perform specific movements, enter specific sleeps that consolidate the information in ways that ordinary memory cannot.

There is always only one keeper per settlement. The knowledge is too dangerous to distribute—if multiple people held the map, their triangulation would give the seams something to track. So the keeper lives alone in the deepest room, emerging rarely, speaking to almost no one, maintaining the map against the slow growth of the unfolding geometry.

When a keeper grows old, they begin the process of transmission. A young candidate is selected, trained for years, and then—in a ritual that takes place in absolute darkness, in absolute silence, over the course of weeks—the map is passed from one mind to another. The old keeper speaks in a voice below a whisper, a voice that is more vibration than sound, and the young keeper absorbs, cross-referencing against the observations brought by builder-priests and traders, updating, correcting, *becoming* the map.

Then the old keeper walks out into the open plateau, and the seams that have been waiting for that particular geometry find what they were looking for, and the unfolding takes them.

It is considered an honor. It is also considered a necessity. The map is too valuable to let the seams have it while it is still attached to a living mind.

---

There are perhaps fifteen thousand people on the plateau, across all thirty settlements. The oral histories suggest there were once more—fifty thousand, a hundred thousand, in the generations just after the first seams opened. But the unfolding takes its toll, and the careful birth rates barely replace the careful deaths, and every generation the whole ground shrinks a little more.

They do not despair. Despair is loud. Despair is a vector the seam-logic can follow.

Instead, they continue. They walk in their interrupted patterns, tend their winterweed gardens, train their children in silence and their builder-priests in angles and their traders in the long routes between settlements. They observe the seams with their hand-polished lenses, record their observations in the oral histories, update the maps that live in the keepers' partitioned minds. They eat sparingly, love quietly, grow thin and gray and eventually translucent.

And when the time comes—when a settlement is finally surrounded by seams that have propagated past the point of geometric defense, when the protective angles fail and the unfolding begins to reach toward the central buildings—they do what they have always done.

They walk to the next settlement, slowly, in interrupted patterns, carrying what they can carry.

They fold themselves into the waiting architecture.

And they continue, as they have always continued, on a plateau that is dying beneath them, under a sky that watches from the wrong angle, surrounded by a silence so complete that their own heartbeats sound like drums.

They continue.
