# The Dwellers of the Screaming Flats

## The Filament-Sleepers

They are not born so much as *accumulated*.

In the warmest pools, where the water reaches blood-heat and the bubbling from below creates a constant gentle roiling, matter begins to gather itself. It starts as threads—pale fibers no thicker than hair, drifting through the murk with apparent aimlessness. But over hours (or days, or weeks—time being what it is here), the threads find each other. They tangle. They mat together into clumps, then masses, then shapes that begin to suggest intention.

A Filament-Sleeper in its earliest form looks like a waterlogged bird's nest the size of a human torso, drifting just beneath the surface. But the fibers continue to organize. They weave tighter in some places, looser in others. Gaps appear that might be spiracles, might be mouths, might be neither. Denser regions develop that could be organs or simply knots in the weaving. The whole mass slowly elongates, develops appendages—not limbs exactly, but extensions, protrusions, places where the fiber-mat reaches outward as if testing the medium.

When a Filament-Sleeper is *finished*—a word that means little here, where everything is always finishing and unfinishing—it resembles nothing so much as a six-foot tangle of pale rootwork given the vaguest suggestion of bilateral symmetry. Two longer extensions serve as primary means of locomotion, dragging the body through shallows or pushing off the soft substrate. Smaller tendrils emerge seemingly at random from the central mass, some temporary, some persistent, all in constant slow motion. The interior of the weave is darker, denser, and sometimes pulses with rhythms that suggest circulation of *something*.

They do not have faces. They do not have fronts, not reliably. The direction they move becomes the front; the direction they came from becomes the back; but they reverse without turning, simply shifting which extensions lead and which trail. When still—and they are often still for very long periods—they become almost impossible to distinguish from the reed-growths or from masses of organic debris accumulated in pool-corners.

This is perhaps intentional. Or perhaps intention is not a category that applies.

## What the Filament-Sleepers Do

They listen.

In a place defined by its sound, by its endless layered cacophony of reed-song and mud-voice and water-roar, the Filament-Sleepers have become organs of attention. Their fibrous bodies are exquisitely sensitive to vibration—every strand a receptor, the whole tangled mass a single distributed ear tuned to the full spectrum of the Flats' expression. They drift through the shallows with their extensions spread wide, maximizing surface area, *drinking* the sound through every inch of their being.

When they encounter each other, they merge. Not permanently—usually—but for hours or days, two or three or seven Filament-Sleepers will mat their bodies together into a single larger tangle, combining their receptive surfaces, pooling whatever passes for their perception. These merged-states are more common in places where the sound is most complex: near dense stands of reeds, above active venting zones where the mud speaks loudest, in the vicinity of surfacing ruins whose corroded surfaces add strange harmonics to the air.

What they do with what they hear, no observer has determined. They have no apparent means of expression, no voice, no visible method of recording or transmitting. They simply *attend*. For years, perhaps for centuries—Filament-Sleepers show no signs of aging, only of occasional catastrophic dissolution when something in their fiber-weave finally fails and they come apart over the course of hours, their threads scattering into the water to perhaps begin the process again elsewhere.

Some who have studied them (from what distance study is possible here, given the Flats' resistance to measurement) believe they are the Flats' own sensorium—extensions of the landscape grown to witness itself, to close some loop of perception that the mud and water and reed cannot close alone. Others believe they are parasites, feeding on the vibration itself, existing because the sound is dense enough to sustain them the way light sustains a plant.

The Filament-Sleepers offer no clarification. They do not respond to attempts at communication. They do not avoid contact with travelers but they do not seek it either. They are not hostile because hostility would require a focus, a direction of intention, a sense of other-than-self, and it is not clear that Filament-Sleepers experience anything as other. The Flats are continuous. The sound is continuous. They are continuous with both. What would there be to oppose?

## The Ruin-Keepers

These are something else.

Where the Filament-Sleepers emerge from accumulation, the Ruin-Keepers emerge from absence. They are found—when they are found—in the vicinity of surfacing structures, those walls and foundations and standing stones that the mud periodically offers up and reclaims. They are waiting there when the ruins rise, and they remain as long as the ruins remain visible, and when the structures sink again the Ruin-Keepers sink with them.

Or perhaps they do not sink. Perhaps they simply stop being present, the way a wave stops being a wave when it reaches shore, and become present again later, elsewhere, when the conditions recur. The Flats do not distinguish clearly between these possibilities.

A Ruin-Keeper looks almost human in silhouette, which is the only way they are usually seen—as dark shapes against the amber murk, standing motionless atop a surfacing foundation or framed in a doorway whose door dissolved eons ago. They have what could be heads, what could be arms, what could be torsos tapering into what could be legs. But they do not resolve into detail as they are approached. They remain silhouettes regardless of distance, regardless of the directionless light, as if they exist in a shadow the Flats otherwise forbid.

This is because they are not entirely *here*. Or they are here in a way that does not include surface, does not include texture, does not include the specificities of presence that would allow the eye to register them as solid. They have extent but not substance. They occupy space but they do not fill it.

They do not move while observed. The stories agree on this. You can approach a Ruin-Keeper, close the distance until you stand close enough to touch—though no one has touched one; the accounts that include attempted contact do not include the aftermath, only the attempt and then a gap and then the traveler somewhere else with no memory of interval—and they will remain perfectly still, perfectly silhouette, facing always the structure they attend. But look away, look back, and they are elsewhere. Closer. Further. Turned. Or simply *more*, a second shape now visible behind the first, or a third in peripheral vision that was not there before.

They are tending the ruins. This is the only interpretation that makes their behavior coherent. When a structure surfaces, the Ruin-Keepers are present, and they remain present, and the structure remains stable for as long as they attend it. Walls that should collapse stand. Foundations that should dissolve hold. Something is being maintained, some principle of persistence being applied, and the Ruin-Keepers are the instrument of that application.

Why they do this is unclear. The ruins do not seem valuable. They do not seem to contain anything. They are not legible as records or useful as shelter. They surface, they persist briefly under the Ruin-Keepers' attention, they sink again having accomplished nothing obvious. But the Ruin-Keepers continue. They have been seen at the same ruins surfacing decades apart, waiting in identical positions, as if no time had passed—or as if they experience the submersion as traversal, the decades-underwater as no different from a walk between two points.

They do not acknowledge travelers. They do not threaten or welcome or forbid. But people who spend too long in the presence of Ruin-Keepers report a sense of *requirement*—a growing feeling that they should be doing something, participating in some maintenance the nature of which they cannot identify, contributing to a project they cannot perceive but which is clearly underway and clearly incomplete.

Most travelers leave. A few do not.

The accounts do not describe what happens to those who stay.

## The Choir-Migrant

There is one.

Whether there has always been one, or whether it is old, or young, or whether such concepts apply, the travelers' accounts do not agree. But they agree that somewhere in the Flats, moving according to its own inscrutable pattern, there is a single entity of different order than the Filament-Sleepers or the Ruin-Keepers or any of the glimpsed swimming things. It is not larger than they are—not necessarily—but it is more *present*. It takes up room in the experience of the place that nothing else quite manages.

It is called the Choir-Migrant because it carries the sound with it.

Not the ambient sound, the endless cacophony that fills every inch of the Flats—that continues regardless, irreducible, defining. But layered atop it, or woven through it, or somehow *carved out of it*, a different sound moves through the basin in the Choir-Migrant's vicinity. A shaped sound. A composed sound. Something that might be music if music could be made from the raw material of the Flats' voice—from the reed-keen and the mud-belch and the water-chuckle, reorganized, restructured, made to mean something it did not mean before.

Those who have encountered it—who have found themselves in its path as it moved through a region, or who have spent long enough in one place that its migration eventually brought it past—describe the experience as *hearing the Flats talk about themselves*. Not in words. Not in any grammar. But in themes, in motifs, in phrases of sound that recur and develop and resolve in ways the ambient chaos never does. The Choir-Migrant brings coherence to a region, briefly, and leaves incoherence in its wake, as if it is extracting something from the noise and carrying it elsewhere.

No one has clearly seen it. The accounts describe a sense of presence, of something vast and distributed and mobile, something that might be a single entity in many pieces or many entities moving in perfect coordination. Shapes in the mist. Ripples in pools with no visible source. Reeds bending toward something as it passes. But never a clear form, never a moment of recognition, never the satisfaction of *that is what it looks like*.

It may be that the Choir-Migrant does not look like anything. It may be that it exists purely as a pattern of influence, a traveling coherence that has no need for substance because it *is* the relationship between substances, the grammar by which the Flats' noise momentarily becomes speech.

It does not harm travelers. It does not communicate with them, or acknowledge them, or seem to notice them. But travelers caught in its passage sometimes emerge changed in ways they struggle to articulate. They find themselves humming phrases they cannot remember hearing. They find themselves avoiding certain tones, certain rhythms, as if these have become taboo. They find themselves listening to the noise of the world—any world, even after leaving the Flats—with a new expectation, a new sense that there is something to be heard beneath the surface, something that could become meaning if only it were properly arranged.

The Choir-Migrant has a territory that encompasses the entire Flats, but it is never in more than one region at a time. It moves slowly, continental-drift slowly, taking years to complete a circuit. Some believe it is maintaining something—keeping the Flats coherent through its endless migration, its endless composition, its endless extraction of pattern from chaos. Others believe it is *recording*—carrying the sound-history of each region to the next, creating an ongoing archive of the Flats' self-expression that exists only in its motion.

It does not seem to age. The oldest accounts describe it. The newest accounts describe it. It is as permanent as the Flats themselves, as patient, as unconcerned with time. It will still be moving through this landscape when everything that currently witnesses it has dissolved and reformed and dissolved again.

## The Transients

Not everything here is eternal.

The Flats produce temporary things—configurations of matter that persist for hours or days before rejoining the universal churn. Some of these are recognizable as failed Filament-Sleepers: tangles of fiber that almost achieved coherence but didn't, that move briefly with something like purpose before unraveling. Others are shapes in the mud itself, humanoid or animal-like or purely abstract, that rise from the substrate and walk or crawl or flow across the surface before sinking again. Still others are condensations in the mist, the same phenomenon that creates the temporary archways and columns but somehow *mobile*, drifting through the amber air as vague masses of density that suggest eyes, suggest intention, suggest awareness, before dispersing.

These transients do not communicate. They do not persist long enough to develop whatever qualities might make communication possible. But they are present, constantly, a population of mayflies flickering in and out of existence throughout the basin. They seem drawn to the more permanent dwellers—gathering around Filament-Sleepers, approaching the vicinity of Ruin-Keepers, moving in the wake of the Choir-Migrant's passage. Whether they are attracted to stability, or trying to achieve it, or simply moving according to currents that happen to coincide with the permanent dwellers' locations, no observer has determined.

Some travelers have reported the transients taking interest in *them*. Following at a distance. Mimicking postures. Forming shapes that echo the traveler's silhouette. This may be pattern-recognition errors, the mind finding meaning in random configurations. Or it may be that the transients are attempting something—trying to learn, to copy, to become. The Flats produce them in endless variety, endless profusion, and perhaps in that profusion there is something like experimentation. Perhaps the Filament-Sleepers themselves were once transients that happened to persist. Perhaps every permanent thing here began as a temporary thing that did not dissolve.

The transients do not survive removal from the Flats. The few attempts to bring one out—scooped into containers, carried in careful hands—have resulted in immediate dissolution, the substance losing whatever coherence it briefly held and becoming inert mud, inert water, inert mist. They are native to a degree that permits no separation. They are the Flats expressing themselves, and outside the Flats there is nothing for them to be.

## What Lives Here

In the end, the answer is: the Flats themselves live here, and everything else is detail.

The Filament-Sleepers are the Flats listening. The Ruin-Keepers are the Flats remembering. The Choir-Migrant is the Flats speaking. The transients are the Flats trying. None of them are separate from the place that produces them; none of them could exist elsewhere; none of them have purposes that extend beyond the Flats' own endless self-involvement.

Travelers pass through. Some of them emerge. Some of them do not, and whether they died or dissolved or simply became more transient, more fiber, more silhouette, more phrase in the migrating song, the Flats do not report. The place absorbs. The place incorporates. The place has been here longer than anything else and will be here when everything else has forgotten itself, and in the meantime it fills every inch of its basin with the noise of its own existence, the warmth of its own waters, the churn of its own mud, the presence of its own children who are not children but expressions, not inhabitants but organs, not alive but not dead either.

The Screaming Flats have no room for anything that is not already themselves.

But they have so much room for themselves that the distinction ceases to matter.
