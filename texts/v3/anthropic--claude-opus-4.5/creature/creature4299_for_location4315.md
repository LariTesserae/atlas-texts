# The Kethveli

They are smaller than you expect. Not dwarfed—proportioned as any people—but compact, as if generations of turning sideways through root-gaps and curling into nested sleeping arrangements have winnowed away whatever the warrens could not accommodate. Their shoulders are narrow and notably mobile, capable of a shrugging rotation that lets them slip through passages you would swear were impassable. Their fingers are long, the joints pronounced, and they move them constantly even in stillness—touching walls, tracing root-ridges, reading the space around them in a language of texture you cannot parse.

Their skin holds the pallor of the roots, though it is not the same pallor. The roots are white as absence; the Kethveli are white as something that was once other colors and has slowly, over many lifetimes, let those colors go. There is a bluish undertone in some, a faint grey in others, visible mostly at the wrists and temples where the skin thins. Their hair grows in fine pale strands that they wear long and unbound, letting it catch on root-surfaces as they pass, trailing behind them like the filaments of the youngest growth. Children sometimes have darker hair—brown, rust, occasionally black—but it lightens as they age, as if the warrens were slowly claiming them.

Their eyes are adapted to the root-light. Large, pale-irised, capable of dilating to an extent that would look alarming on the surface. In the brightest phase of the light-wave, their pupils contract to near-invisibility, giving their gaze a blank quality that outsiders find unsettling. In the dimming, those same eyes open into dark wells that seem to gather what little luminescence remains. They see you in conditions where you cannot see your own hands. They navigate the complete darkness of ceremony times by memory and touch, but also—they will admit if pressed—by something else, some perception they do not have words for, something to do with the way the roots reshape the air around them.

---

The first thing you notice about how they move is the stillness between movements. A Kethveli crossing a chamber does not simply walk; she pauses, shifts, pauses, proceeds, each motion discrete and considered. It looks ritualistic until you understand it as practical: in a space where the geometry changes over months, where a root might have extended into your habitual path since yesterday, you learn to check before you commit. The children move more fluidly, and the elders speak of this with something between fondness and concern, the way surface peoples might discuss a child's recklessness near water.

When they gather—for meals, for the public speech-making they conduct in the acoustic nodes, for the social grooming sessions that seem to serve the function of both gossip and record-keeping—they arrange themselves in configurations that mirror the root-structures around them. Three or four will cluster in a triangle or square, then connect to another cluster at a single point, then branch again, the whole assembly forming a living map of the organic architecture. They are not conscious of doing this. When you point it out, they look at their own arrangement with surprise, then shrug. *This is how people fit*, one of them says, and you cannot tell if she means in this space or in any space.

They touch each other constantly. A hand on a shoulder during conversation, fingers brushing a forearm in greeting, children draped across adults in casual piles. The cold necessitates some of this—body heat is a shared resource—but it goes beyond necessity into a kind of continuous confirmation of presence. In the acoustic strangeness of the warrens, where a voice might arrive from anywhere or nowhere, touch is the reliable sense. *I know you are here because I feel you here*. The Kethveli word for stranger translates roughly as *one whose skin I have not learned*, and the process of welcoming an outsider involves, eventually, an extended session of mutual touch that maps the newcomer's body into the community's knowledge.

---

Their language is built for the space. Consonants are soft, sibilants avoided, vowels drawn out in ways that interact oddly with the selective acoustics. In the nodes where sound carries far, this produces a singing quality, each phrase arriving at distant ears as a kind of tonal sequence. In the chambers that swallow speech, the same words become intimate murmurs, barely louder than breath. They have developed two registers: the carrying-voice and the close-voice, and children learn early which to use where, the way surface children learn to whisper in sacred spaces.

What they speak of most is change. Not change in the abstract—they have no philosophy of impermanence, no teachings about flux—but change in the specific. *The junction outside Merreth's chamber has thickened since last bright-cycle. The substrate near the deep-nests is softening; we should watch for emergence. Old Teth says the wind-that-is-not-wind has shifted in the memorial alcove; voices are arriving there now that used not to.* Their conversations are thick with this spatial journalism, this ongoing collaborative survey of the warrens' becoming. To participate in community life is to contribute observations; to withdraw from community is to stop noticing, stop reporting, stop feeding the collective map.

They do not write. The concept puzzles them more than metalwork does. *But the roots write*, one elder says when you try to explain, gesturing at the ridged surfaces around you. *Everything is already written. We only read.* Their history is stored in the carving-traditions, each generation adding to the patterns in styles that trained eyes can date by subtle variations in line weight and curve. Their personal records are stored in each other—the grooming sessions involve recitation, a call-and-response of memory where one person's history is rehearsed by the group until it becomes shared property. When someone dies, their memories are spoken aloud in one of the far-carrying nodes, distributed through the warrens to whoever is listening, absorbed into the collective knowledge. Nothing is lost, they say. The warrens remember.

---

The social structure follows the root-logic. There are no chiefs, no councils, no formal hierarchies—but there are densities. Certain people have maintained relationships with their root-clusters for longer, have negotiated more successfully, have developed reputations for reading the substrate's changes with accuracy. These people are consulted more often, their observations weighted more heavily, their dwelling-chambers visited more frequently. They do not command; they accrete. Influence gathers around them the way root-mass gathers around a healthy node, and it can disperse just as organically if they misjudge, if their predictions fail, if they misread the warrens' intentions.

Families are defined not by blood but by root-relationship. Those who maintain the same cluster, who have negotiated together for generations, who share the fruits of the same nodules—these are kin. Marriage, if you can call it that, involves a transfer of root-allegiance, one partner leaving their cluster to join another's, or two partners establishing a relationship with an unclaimed growth in the deeper warrens. The negotiations involved are delicate and lengthy; they involve not just the human families but the roots themselves, observed over months for signs of acceptance or resistance. A root that withdraws from a new dwelling is an ill omen. A root that fruits unexpectedly is a blessing profound enough to be spoken of for generations.

Children belong to the cluster as much as to their parents. They are raised collectively by everyone who shares that root-relationship, taught the history of their particular node, trained in its specific negotiations. The deep knowledge—how to speak to this root, what this root requires, what this root has given in the past—passes from elder to child without formal instruction, absorbed through presence and participation. By adolescence, a Kethveli knows their cluster the way you know your own body: not through study but through inhabitation.

---

Their religion, if it is religion, centers on the emergence.

In the deep warrens, where the new roots form, there is a class of people who have given their lives to witnessing. They sit for days in the cramped chambers, breathing slowly to conserve the air, watching the substrate for signs of softening. When emergence occurs, they speak to it—not prayers, not invocations, but introductions. *I am here. You are arriving. I see you.* The new root does not respond, cannot respond, has no capacity for response. But the witnesses believe—know, they would say—that the act of observation shapes the growth. A root that emerges witnessed will grow differently than one that emerges alone. Will tend toward the spaces where people live. Will become part of the negotiation rather than growing wild into the uninhabited deep.

The witnesses are not a priesthood. Anyone can take up the practice; anyone can set it down. But those who persist in it for years develop a quality the other Kethveli recognize and respect—a slowness, a patience, an ability to perceive changes that others miss. They become the most reliable readers of the warrens, the ones consulted when a root's behavior seems uncertain, when the substrate is acting strangely, when the wind-that-is-not-wind shifts in ways that worry people. They are not leaders, but they are anchors. The community orients around them the way the community orients around the oldest root-masses: not by following but by growing toward.

The ceremonies conducted in complete darkness—they will not discuss these. But you learn, over time, that they involve the Pale Walker. The Long Guest. The One Who Passes. The witnesses are central to these events, whatever they are. And afterward, for days, a hush settles over the warrens, a stillness even more profound than usual. People move more carefully. Speak more softly. Touch each other more frequently, as if confirming that everyone is still present, still accounted for, still among the living.

---

Their crafts are root-crafts. The rootite tools are shaped by grinding, by patient abrasion, by techniques that take months to produce a single implement. The fiber-weaving is similarly slow—the fibers must be harvested from roots that are shedding naturally, never cut, and the supply varies with the growth-cycles in ways that the weavers track across years. The carvings are added to in increments so small that a single carver might contribute only a hand's-length of pattern in a lifetime.

But there is one craft that moves faster, and it is the craft of food.

The root-nodules fruit unpredictably, and when they fruit, they fruit abundantly. The harvesters move through the warrens in the bright-phase, gathering the pale globes before they soften past usability, and the grinding and processing must happen quickly—within a few cycles at most. This is when the Kethveli are most energetic, most coordinated, most visibly a people rather than a collection of slow individuals in slow negotiation with their slow environment. The grinding-stones sing against each other, the starchy meal piles up in woven containers, the distribution is managed through a complex system of obligation and reciprocity that ensures every cluster receives according to its need. For a few cycles, the warrens feel almost like a surface village at harvest time: purposeful, collective, alive with shared effort.

Then the nodules are processed, and the slowness returns, and the Kethveli settle back into their patient negotiations with the roots, and you wonder if you imagined the urgency entirely.

---

They do not fear death. This is not a boast or a philosophy; it is simply a feature of their relationship with the warrens. When a Kethveli dies, the body is taken to one of the designated chambers—the same chambers where the pale segmented creatures process waste—and left there. The creatures consume it. The nutrients enter the substrate. The substrate feeds the roots. The roots may fruit. The fruit may nourish the living. The cycle is so clear, so visible, so materially evident, that death becomes simply a change in form, a transfer from one part of the system to another.

The grief is real; the loss is mourned. The speaking-ceremonies in the acoustic nodes ensure that the dead person's memories persist in the community. But the terror that surface peoples sometimes feel at death—the sense of ending, of annihilation, of departure into unknowable absence—this is not present here. *Where would they go?* an elder asks when you try to explain it. *The warrens are everything. The warrens contain everything. There is no outside to go to.*

You think about the surface, about the frost-crack, about the world above the permafrost ceiling. You do not mention it.

---

The children are the key to understanding what the Kethveli are becoming.

They are born slightly different from their parents—this is true of all peoples, but here the differences are visible, trackable, accumulating. Their fingers are longer. Their eyes are larger. Their skin is paler. Their hair grows finer. Each generation is more adapted to the warrens, less suited to any other environment. The elders remember stories of ancestors who could tolerate warmer air, who had darker coloring, who might have survived on the surface if they'd chosen to leave. The children could not survive there. The children are becoming something that can only exist here.

And they are comfortable with this. More than comfortable—they belong here in a way that even the elders do not quite manage. They slip through root-gaps without checking, trusting their bodies to fit. They speak in the carrying-voice and the close-voice with perfect instinctive calibration. They read the substrate's changes as easily as breathing. When they grow up, when they become the elders, the community will be more deeply integrated with the warrens than it has ever been. And their children will be more integrated still. And so on, generation after generation, until—

Until what?

The witnesses in the deep warrens, the ones who observe the emergence, sometimes speak of this. The roots are growing toward the people, they say. The people are growing toward the roots. The substrate is changing too, in ways that are difficult to measure but impossible to miss if you've been watching long enough. The whole system is converging on something, and no one knows what that something is, and the not-knowing is part of the faith, part of the patience, part of the reason the Kethveli continue their slow negotiations with the pale tendrils that shape their world.

*We are not finished*, a witness tells you, in the closest thing to prophecy you hear during your time in Kethvel. *The roots are not finished. The warrens are not finished. We are all still becoming. We will know what we are when we arrive.*

She turns back to the softening substrate, to the translucent patch where a new root is beginning to form, and resumes her vigil.

---

When you leave, climbing the braided ladder toward the unbearable brightness of the surface, you carry their pallor with you for days. Your eyes ache in the sunlight. Your skin feels raw in the moving air. The sounds of the upper world—wind, water, birdsong, the whole cacophony of life that doesn't negotiate—arrive as an assault, and you find yourself flinching away, longing for the layered quiet of the root-warrens.

It fades. In a week, you are readjusted. In a month, you can barely remember what the silence felt like.

But sometimes, in the stillness before sleep, you feel it: a hum beneath hearing, a sense of slow growth, a patient elaboration happening just beyond the threshold of perception. The Kethveli are still down there, still becoming, still waiting to arrive.

The roots are growing. The people are changing. The warrens are extending upward, a finger's width each century, and somewhere in the unimaginable future, they will breach the permafrost, and something will emerge—root and person and substrate and wind-that-is-not-wind, fused into a single becoming—and you will not be there to see it.

No one you know will be there to see it.

But the Kethveli will be there. Or whatever the Kethveli have become by then. They are patient enough. They are slow enough.

They have learned to grow.
