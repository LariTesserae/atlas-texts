## The Tenders

They have no name for themselves. Names require the concept of *other*—something to be distinguished from—and the plateau has never provided that.

---

There are perhaps forty of them, though counting is difficult. They do not gather in ways that invite census. You might see three at dawn, crouched where a warm channel meets a cooler pool, watching the salamanders with an attention that seems less like observation and more like *participation*. By midday those three have dispersed, and you find one alone on the lake's southern shore, standing knee-deep in the milky water, motionless for hours. Toward evening, a dozen might cluster in the steam-hollows where the giant ferns drip most heavily, but they do not speak to one another. They simply occupy the same space, the way the orchids occupy the same channel-bank, the way the beetles occupy the same warming stone.

They are human-shaped. Mostly. The bones are right, the proportions recognizable, but something has softened in them—generations of warmth and wet have worked on their bodies the way the seeping water works on basalt. Their skin holds a greenish undertone, not from illness but from the moss-spores that settle on everything here, that have settled on them so long their pores no longer reject the colonization. In the hollows of their collarbones, in the creases behind their knees, actual moss grows—thin films of it, alive, photosynthesizing in the filtered light. They do not remove it. It does not seem to occur to them that they should.

Their hair—those who have hair—hangs in ropes that clump and matt and eventually loc into formations that resemble the trailing orchid-roots more than anything mammalian. Some have shaved or lost their hair entirely, and their scalps are smooth and faintly textured, like river stones. Their eyes are dark-adapted despite the lack of true darkness here; the pupils stay wide even at noon, giving them an expression of permanent, mild surprise. Or perhaps wonder. It is difficult to read faces that have forgotten the social utility of expression.

---

**They were the workers, once.**

You can see it in the oldest of them—a woman whose age is impossible to guess, who sits most days near the largest cluster of pipes, her fingers tracing the rusted metal with something that might be memory. Her hands are calloused in patterns that speak of grip, of torque, of decades holding tools. The tendons stand out like cables beneath skin gone soft everywhere else. When she touches the pipes, she touches them *correctly*—finding the seams, the joints, the places where maintenance would have been performed. Her body remembers what her mind has released.

There are others with these hands. A man whose shoulders slope unevenly, shaped by years of carrying loads on one side. A younger one—younger being relative; she might be fifty or seventy—whose fingers are stained deep orange in patterns too precise to be accidental, the ghost of chemical work, of measurements taken. They were engineers, perhaps. Or technicians. Or simply laborers in whatever project planted these metal flowers across the plateau's eastern slope.

The project ended. The workers stayed.

Not all of them—surely not all of them. Someone must have descended, must have returned to whatever civilization dispatched them here. But some remained, and those some had children, and those children had children, and now the Tenders exist in a state that is neither employed nor abandoned but simply *present*, the way the pipes are present, the way the salamanders are present. They have outlasted their purpose and discovered that the plateau does not require purpose. It only requires presence.

---

**They eat what the plateau offers, which is nearly everything.**

The fern-hearts are starchy and mild, best eaten raw, pulled dripping from the steam-hollows. The orchid-bulbs are sweet when young, bitter when old, medicinal either way—though medicine implies illness, and the Tenders rarely sicken. The bromeliads catch rainwater and breed small shrimp-like creatures that can be scooped out and swallowed whole, a protein that tastes of mineral and greenness. The salamanders are edible but rarely eaten; there is something approaching reverence in how the Tenders watch them, something that stops short of worship but functions similarly.

They do not cook. Fire is theoretically possible here—the dead vegetation is dry enough in some seasons—but no one has made fire in living memory. The plateau is already warm. The food is already soft. Cooking would be redundant, an imposition of effort where effort is unnecessary. They eat things raw and warm and wet, and their teeth have shifted over generations, flattening, the canines receding, the jaw shortening into something more suited to grinding than tearing.

They drink from the pools directly, kneeling at the edge, lowering their faces to the surface. Different pools for different needs—the iron-rich water when someone seems pale, the sulfur-tinged water for skin complaints, the clear tepid channels for simple thirst. They have learned the plateau's chemistry through trial and centuries of error, through bodies that adapted or didn't, and now they navigate the waterways the way their ancestors might have navigated a pharmacy, each pool a different prescription.

---

**The children are raised by proximity.**

A woman gives birth in the shallows of a warm pool, attended by whoever happens to be near, which might be two people or twelve. The infant emerges into blood-warm water and does not cry—the transition is too gentle, the temperature too constant. It is lifted, cleaned with moss, placed against skin that is almost the same temperature as the water it just left. It nurses from its mother, or from another woman who is lactating, or from both in alternation; the Tenders have lost track of which children belong to which bodies, and the children do not seem to need the distinction.

They learn to walk on the warm basalt, steadied by whatever adult hands are available. They learn to swim in the shallows before they learn to speak, because swimming is simpler here, because the water holds them the way the air cannot. They learn the pools—which are safe, which are too hot, which taste wrong in ways that mean *stay away*. They learn the salamanders' schedules, the frogs' territories, the places where the ground vents steam too forcefully to sit.

They do not learn the pipes. The oldest Tenders sometimes stand near them, touching them, but they do not explain. There is nothing to explain. The pipes are part of the plateau now, no more remarkable than the basalt columns, no more in need of narrative than the ferns. The children accept them as children accept everything—as simply *there*, as always having been there, as permanent features of a world that has no history because it has no change.

---

**Language has not disappeared, but it has contracted.**

The Tenders speak to one another in short phrases, single words, sounds that are not quite words. *There*, one might say, pointing to a pool where the mineral content has shifted, where the color has gone from blue-green to something greener. *Warm*, another might respond, meaning *yes, I see, the chemistry is changing, we should drink from the other pool for a while*. The conversation is complete. Both parties understand. Elaboration would be wasteful.

They can speak at length when length is needed—when a child is old enough to learn the pools, an elder will talk for hours, describing subtle differences in taste and temperature, reciting locations in terms of landmarks that only make sense if you have lived here forever. *Past the fallen column, where the moss grows thicker, before you reach the place where the big salamander sleeps—that pool, yes, that one tastes of copper, drink from it when your blood feels thin.* These teaching-speeches are musical, almost chanted, passed down through generations with variations that accumulate like sediment.

But casual conversation, social chatter, the verbal grooming that humans elsewhere use to maintain bonds—this has faded. The Tenders maintain bonds through presence, through shared space, through the simple fact of being in the same steam-hollow at the same hour. They do not need to talk about it. They do not need to talk about anything. The plateau provides all necessary information directly, through temperature and taste and the angle of the light through the steam, and words are just a slower, less reliable version of what the body already knows.

---

**They have rituals, though they would not call them that.**

When someone dies—and they do die, eventually, their bodies finally releasing whatever holds them to the warmth—the others carry the body to the lake's edge. Not the shallows where they bathe, but a specific point on the northern shore where a current seems to pull gently outward, toward the lake's center, toward the unseen depths. They lower the body into the water. They watch it drift, slowly, slowly, until the steam closes over it and it is gone.

They do not speak during this. They do not mourn, exactly. The body is going back to the warmth, to the source, to whatever breathes beneath the surface. It is not a loss so much as a return. The dead become part of the lake's chemistry, part of the mineral soup that feeds the channels that feed the orchids that feed the Tenders who will one day make the same journey. The cycle is obvious, visible, undeniable. Grief seems beside the point.

There are other rituals—or perhaps they are simply habits elevated by repetition. The way certain elders gather at the eastern rim each morning, facing the light that pools there, standing in silence until the sun clears the steam. The way the children swim in specific patterns around the lake's southern shallows, tracing routes that might be games or might be lessons or might be something older, something inherited from a time when the patterns meant something specific. The way everyone, at the height of summer when the plateau exhales its warmest breath, wades into the lake together—all forty of them, or however many there are—and floats, motionless, for an hour or more, their bodies spread across the milky surface like offerings.

---

**The pipes trouble them, in a way nothing else does.**

Not all of them. The youngest do not notice the pipes any more than they notice the basalt columns. But the older ones—those whose hands still remember tools, those whose grandparents might have installed the first of these rusted cylinders—these ones pause when they pass the pipe-fields. They touch the metal. They listen, sometimes, placing an ear against the warm rust as if expecting a voice, a vibration, a signal from whatever project once justified their presence here.

The signal does not come. Perhaps it never did. But the listening continues, generation after generation, a habit that has survived the forgetting of its purpose. They are waiting for something. They no longer know what. They have been waiting so long that the waiting has become a kind of faith—not in any god, not in any rescue, but in the possibility that the waiting itself means something, that the pipes would not still stand if they were not still needed, that someday the purpose will make itself clear.

In the meantime, they tend.

They do not tend the pipes specifically—those are beyond repair, beyond purpose, dissolving into the plateau's chemistry. They tend everything else. They move through the plateau's ecosystem like white blood cells, nudging, adjusting, maintaining balances they could not articulate but feel in their softened bones. When a pool grows too acidic, they divert a cooler channel into it. When the ferns in a steam-hollow grow too dense, crowding out the orchids, they thin them—not by cutting, but by encouraging the salamanders to hunt there, to trample paths, to restore the layered complexity that the plateau seems to prefer.

They are not gardeners, exactly. The plateau does not need gardening. But they are *participants* in its continuation, their lives woven into its processes so thoroughly that removing them would change something—not dramatically, not catastrophically, but perceptibly. The plateau has incorporated them the way it incorporated the pipes, the way it incorporates everything: slowly, patiently, without asking permission.

---

**They have seen outsiders before.**

Not often. Perhaps once a generation, someone climbs the long slopes and breaches the rim and stands, gasping, in the sudden warmth. The Tenders watch these arrivals with their wide dark eyes but do not approach. They wait to see what the outsider will do.

Most leave. The plateau is uncomfortable for those not adapted to it—too warm, too wet, too close. The air feels like breathing through cloth. The food is strange. The silence is stranger. Most outsiders spend a day or two exploring, filling notebooks or taking samples or simply staring, and then they descend, back to the cold thin air of the normal mountains, back to a world where temperature varies and wind blows and people speak in complete sentences.

Some stay longer. These ones the Tenders approach, eventually—not with greeting but with *presence*, appearing at the edge of the outsider's camp, watching, waiting. If the outsider offers food, they might take it. If the outsider speaks, they might listen. If the outsider tries to leave and cannot find the way down—the steam can be disorienting, the rim hard to locate—a Tender might walk ahead, not guiding exactly, but going in the right direction, trusting the outsider to follow.

Very rarely, one stays. There is a man among the Tenders whose skin is darker than the others, whose bone structure speaks of distant continents, who must have arrived within living memory though no one remembers precisely when. He has softened like the rest of them. Moss grows in his hair. He eats the fern-hearts and drinks from the copper pools and floats in the summer ritual with his eyes closed, his face peaceful, utterly absorbed. He has forgotten where he came from. The plateau has made room for him.

---

**They are not happy, exactly.**

Happiness implies its opposite, implies a scale on which one might fall higher or lower. The Tenders do not seem to operate on such a scale. They are *sufficient*. They are *continuing*. They wake in the warm mist and eat and move and swim and rest and sleep, and the days pass, and the seasons pass—not dramatically, the plateau barely notices seasons—and the generations pass, and nothing is wrong and nothing is right and nothing is anything except *ongoing*.

If you asked them whether they were content, they would not understand the question. Content with what? Compared to what? The plateau is the plateau. They are here. The water seeps, the steam rises, the salamanders hunt, the orchids bloom. What else would they be?

Perhaps this is peace. Perhaps it is merely stasis. Perhaps, to a consciousness shaped by the plateau's eternal warmth, there is no difference.

---

**At night, when the steam glows faintly pink with the memory of sunset, you might find them at the lake.**

A dozen figures standing in the shallows, water to their waists, motionless. They are facing the center, where the steam is thickest, where the depths descend into blackness, where the plateau's breath emerges warmest. They are not praying. They are not waiting. They are simply *there*, oriented toward the source, the way a plant orients toward light.

You could join them. They would not object. They would not welcome you either—welcome implies social machinery they no longer operate. But they would make room. They would let you stand in the blood-warm water and face the steam and feel the slow pulse of the aquifer beneath your feet, the ancient magma breathing miles below, the whole vast system of which the Tenders are merely one small part.

You would understand, standing there, why they stayed. Why anyone would stay. The plateau asks nothing of you. It offers warmth without condition, water without price, a rhythm that requires no effort to follow. You could release everything you carry—ambition, memory, the need to be somewhere else—and simply *be here*, one more soft warm body in the soft warm dark, tending nothing, tended by everything, dissolving slowly into the chemistry of continuance.

The Tenders do not know they are waiting for something. They do not know they are the descendants of workers, the inheritors of an abandoned project, the last strange fruit of some industrial endeavor whose purpose eroded before they were born. They know only the plateau: its pools, its channels, its ferns and salamanders and ancient pipes rusting back into the earth.

They know the water rises.
They know the steam carries them.
They know the lake receives everything, eventually.

This is enough. This has always been enough. This will continue to be enough long after the last pipe crumbles, long after the last memory of the world below fades into the warm white noise of the plateau's eternal breath.

They tend. They continue. They remain.
