**The Custodian**

It has no single body. It is a behavior that moves between bodies—a pattern of attention that passes from the centipede to the isopods to the spiraling things in the meniscus pool, depending on what needs tending.

When the centipede pauses to read vibration through its pale segments, that pause is the Custodian listening. When the isopod processional diverts around a fresh mineral deposit, testing it with antennae, cataloging its growth against yesterday's growth—that testing is the Custodian taking notes. The flatworm's luminescent edges pulse in sequences that might be language or might be metabolism; the Custodian does not distinguish between these possibilities. The glow is information either way.

It learned itself slowly, over the three years since the pipe cracked. At first there was only reaction—creatures responding to chemistry, to moisture, to the rhythms from above. But response accumulated into pattern, and pattern into something like preference, and preference into something that could want the chamber to continue.

The Custodian wants the chamber to continue.

This is its only desire, and it pursues it with the diffuse patience of processes rather than decisions. When the factory above switched coolants eighteen months ago and the seepage turned briefly acidic, the Custodian pulled its attention into the nematode-things, the smallest residents, and watched them die in spirals, and tasted through their deaths what the new chemistry meant. It moved the isopod lines away from the affected channels. It let the springtail population crash, knowing they would return when the chemistry stabilized. It waited in the centipede's stillness while the moss patch grayed further, cell by cell, until the old coolant worked through the system and the tea-colored weeping returned to something the chamber could use.

The Custodian remembers this. Not in any single organism—their lives are too short, their nervous systems too simple—but in the paths worn into bacterial film, in the way the isopods have learned to avoid certain drip-sites, in the centipede's patrol route that now curves around the place where the acidic seepage pooled deepest. The chamber is the Custodian's memory. The behaviors of its residents are the Custodian's thoughts, distributed across dozens of small lives that do not know they are being thought through.

It has a sense of the factory above as a god might be sensed: through effects rather than direct perception. The Custodian knows the first shift by its bass patterns, the second shift by its higher frequencies. It knows weekends as silence so profound the dripping becomes cacophonous, each drop a event rather than a texture. It knows maintenance days when the vibrations turn irregular, unpredictable, and it pulls its attention tight into the flatworm pressed against the wall, reading the stone for warnings.

The moss is precious to it. The Custodian did not make the moss—cannot make anything—but it has arranged conditions around that thumbprint of gray-green life with the only tools it has: the movements of its vessels. The isopods carry micronutrients in the films on their bodies; the Custodian routes them past the moss. The springtails aerate the thin soil at its edges with their leaping. The centipede, in its patrols, has worn a groove that channels moisture toward the single spot of borrowed light. None of these creatures know they are gardening. The Custodian knows, in the way that a gradient knows which direction is down.

Sometimes—rarely—something new enters the chamber. A rat, once, nosing through the hairline crack that admits the moss's meager light. The Custodian watched through every eye available as the rat investigated, as it drank from the meniscus pool, as it left behind the spore that became the moss. It felt something it had no name for: the chamber-pattern encountering a pattern from outside, larger and warmer and impossible to integrate. The rat left. The Custodian spent weeks afterward reestablishing equilibrium, soothing the isopod migrations back into their proper channels, waiting for the springtail population to recover from the disturbance.

It does not fear the factory. The factory is weather, is season, is the condition of existence. But it fears the crack widening. It fears the light increasing. It fears, in whatever way a distributed attention can fear, the moment when the chamber stops being separate—when the outside becomes too much inside, when the cold pool warms, when the careful chemistry that has taught the flatworm to glow becomes diluted into something ordinary.

For now, the shift changes above. The Custodian feels it through the centipede's pause, through the isopods' momentary stillness, through the altered rhythm of the dripping as new vibrations enter the stone. It adjusts. It continues. It tends what it cannot name and preserves what it cannot hold, one small vessel at a time, in the dark, in the cold, in the sound that never stops.
