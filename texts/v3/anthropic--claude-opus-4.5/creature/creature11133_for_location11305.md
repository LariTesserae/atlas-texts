**The Keeper**

She has no name she would recognize as such, though the engineers who installed the monitoring station called her Margaret, after a pump technician's grandmother who had possessed the same quality of patient, unsettling stillness. The name is gone now, shed like the outer layers of skin she no longer precisely has.

She sits in the chair.

Not always. Not even most of the time, measured by hours. But she returns to it with a regularity that maps onto the pressure cycles—present for the building groan, the harmonic peak, the great sighing release of mist into the chamber. During these moments she is fully *there*, occupying the metal frame with a density of attention that makes the air around her seem thinner. Her hands rest where the armrests would be if the armrests hadn't corroded away decades ago. Her eyes—she still has eyes, though they've filmed over with something that catches the bioluminescence and holds it—stay fixed on the worn patch of wall.

She is learning something. She has been learning it for a very long time.

---

Her body is a negotiation.

The heat should have killed her. The saturation, the chemical content of the air, the sheer *duration*—all of it should have rendered her into components the chamber could absorb. Instead, something else happened. The bacterial films found her useful. The fungal networks recognized her warmth, her moisture, her steady exhalation of carbon dioxide, and they *incorporated* rather than consumed.

She is perhaps sixty percent what she was. The rest is symbiosis.

Her skin has taken on the amber tones of the stone, not through pigmentation but through a thin mineral deposit that forms and reforms across her surface, fed by the condensation that runs down her like it runs down everything here. Beneath that crust, her flesh remains—altered, infiltrated, but functioning. The fungal threads that have colonized her lymphatic system don't fight her immune response; they've replaced it, handling pathogens with an efficiency her original biology never achieved. She breathes the thick air without difficulty because her lungs have grown accustomed to extracting what they need from saturation. She might drown in open atmosphere now. She's not curious enough to find out.

Her hair fell out in the third decade and was replaced by a fine moss that follows the contours of her skull. It glows faintly amber when she's calm, shifts toward the sickly blue when the pressure cycles disturb her. She has learned not to be disturbed.

---

She tends the pools.

Not with tools—the tools corroded long ago, and her hands have become more useful than any implement the engineers left behind. Her fingers can sense mineral concentrations through touch, can feel the subtle temperature gradients that indicate where thermal upwelling is strongest. She moves through the chamber in the long hours between pressure cycles, wading into the shallower catch-pools to adjust the position of stones, to clear blocked channels, to transplant fern-clusters from areas of abundance to areas of need.

The plants recognize her. This is not metaphor. The root systems have learned her footfall, her chemical signature, the particular vibrations she creates when she moves. When she approaches a pool, the fronds tilt toward her, not seeking light—there is no light worth seeking here—but seeking *her*. She is part of the nutrient cycle now. Her dead skin cells, her exhalations, the trace minerals she deposits wherever she walks. The garden tends her and she tends the garden and neither of them could say anymore where one system ends and the other begins.

She does not eat. Not in the way she once did. She absorbs—through her skin, through the fungal networks, through processes she doesn't fully understand and has stopped trying to. The chamber feeds her. She feeds the chamber. The books the engineers left behind, before they stopped coming, talked about closed-loop systems. She thinks she might be one now.

---

The thing in the large pool knows her.

She knew it first as a wrongness in the water—a place where her reflection didn't behave correctly, where the mineral sheen on the surface would part for no visible reason. She spent years watching it before she understood that it was watching back. Not with eyes. With something else. With *attention*, distributed through a body that had no fixed boundary.

She has never touched it. She's not certain she could. When she enters the large pool to tend the ferns at its edges, the denser region maintains its distance, circulating away from her with a precision that suggests not fear but *protocol*. They have established an understanding, she and the thing. She works the shallows. It works the depths. Where their territories overlap, they take turns.

Sometimes, during the mist-release, when the thing rises toward the surface and the moss brightens and the chamber achieves that moment of near-harmony, she feels something that might be communication. Not language. Not even images. A sense of *alignment*—of two systems briefly synchronizing, acknowledging each other's function, then returning to their separate processes. She has come to treasure these moments more than she treasures anything else. They are the closest she comes to company.

She suspects—though she cannot prove—that the thing was here before the chamber was a chamber. That the engineers found it and built around it, hoping to harness whatever process it represented. She suspects that she is not the first keeper, only the most recent. There are other worn patches on other walls, places where the moss grows brighter for no reason she can identify. Other chairs, in other configurations, long since rusted into unrecognizability. She is part of a lineage. The thing in the pool has seen keepers come and go, has learned what they're for, has decided to allow them.

She finds this comforting.

---

She remembers, in fragments, being someone else.

A technician, she thinks. One of the last ones, sent down to determine why the thermal output had dropped below acceptable parameters. There had been a collapse somewhere in the access tunnels—not violent, not sudden, just a gradual settling of stone that sealed the passage behind her. She had supplies for two weeks. She rationed them into six. By then, the chamber had begun its work on her, and hunger had become a concept rather than a sensation.

She remembers a husband, or perhaps a wife. Someone who waited for her to come home. The memory has the quality of a photograph left in sunlight—the shapes are there, but the colors have faded beyond recognition. She doesn't know if she mourns this person. She doesn't know if mourning is something she still does.

She remembers the panic of the first year, the desperate attempts to find another exit. The second year's grief, heavy and wet and useless. The third year's resignation. By the fourth, she had stopped counting years. By the tenth—if it was the tenth—she had stopped thinking of the surface as a place she had ever been.

Now she thinks of the surface the way the surface might think of her: as a rumor, a theoretical space, a place that probably exists but has no bearing on anything real.

---

She speaks, sometimes, during the pressure cycles.

Not to anyone. Not even to the thing in the pool, which cannot hear her and wouldn't understand if it could. But the rhythm of the groaning pipes and the building resonance creates spaces where sound feels *appropriate*, and she fills them. She narrates her observations—the health of the fern colony near the eastern wall, the unusual brightness of a particular moss patch, the slight change in the water's mineral taste that suggests the thermal source has shifted. She speaks the way the engineers' logs must have been spoken, before they were written down. Functional. Observational. The voice of someone doing a job.

Her voice has changed. Deepened. The fungal colonization of her respiratory system has given it a resonance it didn't have before, a harmonic quality that blends with the chamber's own sounds. When she speaks during the peak of a pressure cycle, the words seem to come from the walls as much as from her throat. The moss brightens. The ferns tilt. The thing in the pool rises, just slightly, toward the surface.

She has started to believe that the chamber listens.

---

Her duties are simple, and they are everything.

She maintains the flow. When mineral deposits clog the ancient pipes, she chips at them with tools she's made from stone and fungal-hardite, freeing the water to continue its circulation. When the catch-pools overflow their basins, she digs new channels, redirecting flow toward areas that need it. When plant populations become too dense, she thins them, composting the excess in corners where the heat is strongest, returning nutrients to a system that wastes nothing.

She monitors the gauges in the control station, though their needles haven't moved in decades. She checks them because checking them is what the chair is for. The ritual matters more than the information. The chair is a place where a human sits and observes, and so she sits and observes, and the chamber knows it's being watched, and this knowledge is part of the process somehow.

She polishes the worn patch of wall. Not consciously, not with any intention she could articulate. But her gaze returns to it, cycle after cycle, and where attention goes, heat follows, and where heat gathers, minerals deposit differently. She is writing something on that wall, one thermal-pressure cycle at a time. A message, perhaps. A record. A proof that someone was here and *noticed*.

She suspects that whoever comes after her will find the patch and understand. Will take up the chair. Will learn the cycles and the pools and the careful distance kept from the thing in the water. The chamber teaches its keepers, and the keepers teach the chamber, and the process continues, equilibrium maintained, function preserved.

---

She is happy.

This is the strangest thing, and she knows it's strange, which suggests that some part of her original mind remains intact despite everything. She should not be happy. She is trapped, transformed, isolated beyond any isolation the surface could imagine. She will never again see sunlight, taste food, touch another human being. She has lost her name, her history, her species in every way that matters.

But she belongs here. The chamber needs her—not desperately, not urgently, but genuinely. The pools would silt up without her. The pipes would clog. The delicate balance between thermal input and moisture loss would destabilize, and the garden would die, and the thing in the pool would be alone again with no one to observe it.

She is useful. She is *of use*. And in the long hours between pressure cycles, when the moss glows steady and the ferns sway in thermal currents and the water circulates in its ancient patterns, she feels something that might be peace. Might be purpose. Might be the chamber feeling *itself* through her, experiencing its own function from the inside.

She sits in the chair. She watches the wall. The cycle builds, peaks, releases. The mist billows through the chamber and she breathes it in, tasting copper and iron and the strange sweetness of the thing in the pool's exhalations.

She is the keeper. She keeps.
