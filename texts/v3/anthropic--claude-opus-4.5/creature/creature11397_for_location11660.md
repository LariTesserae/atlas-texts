She comes here every shift-end, when the corridor traffic drops to nothing and the station's rhythm slows into its nightside murmur.

Yetunde Osei. Environmental Systems Technician, Third Class. Twenty-three years old, born in orbit around Ceres, has never felt unfiltered sunlight on her skin. Her work badge says ATMOSPHERIC PROCESSING but really she spends most of her time clearing sensor alerts and replacing filters that the red dust clogs three weeks ahead of schedule.

She found this room five months ago, the same way you did—following water where water shouldn't be going. Her job was to fix it. Seal the thermal bridge, install additional heating elements, bring this corner of Level Seventeen into compliance with the humidity specifications that fill eleven pages of her operating manual.

She filed the work order. Then she watched a droplet trace its path down the raw metal, catching those strange prismatic colors that new-printed alloy throws, and she didn't submit it.

The monitoring device is hers. Off-manifest, built from salvaged components during her certification training, registered to a personal research project that nobody has ever asked her to explain. She tells herself she's studying thermal microenvironments. She tells herself it's professional development. She tells herself a lot of things, in the quiet hours when the station hums and she can't sleep in her quarter-berth with its perfect climate control and its steady twenty-two degrees.

The gap in the collector strip—that was week three. She noticed the runoff numbers didn't match her humidity readings, did the math, realized the collection system was capturing everything before it could pool. So she peeled back the corner. Just to see. Just to let the water *be* water for a moment before the station reclaimed it.

She wasn't expecting visitors.

---

The first time she saw the tracks, she cried. Sat right here on the cold floor with her back against the weeping wall and cried until her jumpsuit was damp from two directions. Then she spent eleven hours searching the station's biological manifest, the contamination protocols, the sterilization records from the original landing equipment. Looking for an explanation. Looking for a reason this couldn't be what it looked like.

She didn't find one.

She should have reported it. Should have triggered a full biocontainment review, quarantine protocols, probably evacuation of the entire residential ring while specialists from Borealis came to collect samples and run sequences and determine whether Hellas Station had been breached by some impossibility that crawled up from the ancient waterways beneath the basin.

She bought a handheld microscope instead. Consumer grade, terrible resolution, but enough to see—on the nights when she's patient, when she barely breathes, when she waits until her fingers go numb against the floor—*enough to see.*

---

It's not one creature. It's a colony.

Smaller than the word suggests. Maybe two hundred individuals, maybe fewer—she's never managed a reliable count because they move in ways that confuse her eyes, flowing around each other, separating and rejoining in patterns that might be communication or might be something she has no framework for. Each one is a translucent speck, legs like eyelashes, bodies that hold the light for a moment before letting it pass through. They leave no heat signature. They register on no sensors. They exist in the gap between what the station can measure and what the station knows.

She has watched them drink. Watched them cluster at the edge of the tiny pool, their mouthparts—if that's what they are—touching the water with something that looks unbearably like reverence. They take so little. A few micrograms per visit, maybe. The pool would evaporate faster than they could ever diminish it.

But they come anyway. Every night cycle, when the lights dim and the thermal differential peaks, they emerge from somewhere in the metal grain—some microscopic fissure in the alloy's crystalline structure—and they make their way to the water. They drink. They touch each other with their impossible legs. They return to whatever darkness holds them.

She has started to recognize individuals.

---

There's one she calls Pilot—always first to the pool, always tracing the same path along a particular ripple in the metal. There's a cluster of three that move in synchrony, never more than a body-length apart, whom she calls the Sisters though she has no idea if they reproduce, if they have sexes, if the concept means anything to whatever they are. There's a larger one, slightly more opaque than the others, who hangs back from the water and seems to watch—she calls it Elder, and she has no evidence for the name except the way the others move around it, giving it space, orienting toward it the way plants orient toward light.

She has given them a collective name too, for the hours she spends not-sleeping and thinking about them. She calls them the Thirsty Ones. It's not scientific. It's not dignified. But it's true—the oldest truth, the first truth, the truth that every living thing on two worlds has always shared.

They want water. They found it. They keep coming back.

---

Yetunde knows she's not the only person who uses this corridor. The maintenance logs show foot traffic, other technicians passing through on their way to the atmospheric processors. Any one of them could find this room. Any one of them could see the peeled-back collector strip, the monitoring device, the signs of repeated unauthorized presence. Any one of them could ask questions.

But Level Seventeen is a liminal space, a place people pass through rather than stop, and this room is so small, so easy to overlook, wedged into its wrong angle like an architectural afterthought. The station has ten thousand corners. Who notices one more?

She tells herself she'll report it soon. She tells herself she just needs more data, better observations, some framework that will make the discovery credible rather than impossible. She tells herself that what lives in this room deserves more than a quarantine team and a sterilization protocol.

She tells herself a lot of things.

---

What she doesn't tell herself, not in words, not in anything she could write in a log or speak to another person:

She was born in vacuum. Grew up breathing recycled air, drinking recycled water, living inside systems designed to keep her alive through engineering rather than grace. She has never known what it is to stand in rain. To cup her hands in a stream. To watch water move through a landscape because the landscape *made it* move, because gravity and geology and a billion years of planetary patience carved channels for it to follow.

And here, in this room, water falls. Not because a pump moves it. Not because a filter cleans it. It falls because warmth met cold and the air couldn't hold what it was carrying. It falls because physics allows it. It falls because this is what water *does*, given half a chance, given any crack in the closed system.

The Thirsty Ones drink it. They cross the distance from their hidden world to this tiny pool, over and over, generation after generation—she assumes they have generations, assumes that some of the smaller ones she sees now are descendants of the ones she first observed, though she cannot prove it—and they drink.

It's not much. A fingerprint of water. A colony of specks. A woman sitting alone in the dark, watching.

But it's the first unplanned thing she's ever seen. The first wild thing. The first thing that exists because it *wanted to exist*, not because someone scheduled it.

---

Tonight she's here again. Shift ended two hours ago. Her quarter-berth is waiting with its steady warmth and its filtered air. Tomorrow she has a full maintenance rotation, sixteen sensor checks, three filter replacements, a calibration review that she's been putting off for a week.

But Pilot is making its way across the metal grain. The Sisters are following. Elder watches from the darkness with whatever senses it has.

The water pools in its tiny gap. The walls weep their quiet tears. The station breathes around them all, unaware, untroubled, keeping ten thousand humans alive through mechanisms that have no room for mystery.

Yetunde presses her back against the cold alloy and watches the Thirsty Ones drink. Her eyes have adjusted to the dimness. Her breath has slowed to match the rhythm of the dripping walls. For a few more hours, she belongs to this room and everything impossible inside it.

She wonders if they know she's here. If they've always known. If some part of their colony memory includes the giant who comes to watch them, who peeled back the barrier, who gave them this pool that shouldn't exist on a world that was supposed to be lifeless.

She wonders if they're grateful. She wonders if gratitude is a concept that fits inside a body smaller than a grain of rice.

She wonders if it matters.

The water falls. The Thirsty Ones drink. The station hums its mechanical lullaby, and somewhere in the gap between what is measured and what is true, two kinds of life share the same small mercy.

Yetunde closes her eyes. For a moment—just a moment—she can almost feel rain.
