**The Warden**

She has no name she remembers, though she must have had one once—must have been given one by a mother or father in some village that existed when the cistern was new. Now she is simply the one who stays.

She lives in a hollow she has worn into the eastern curve of the wall, where the original builders left a maintenance alcove that she has deepened over years with a sharpened stone. The alcove is perhaps four feet deep now, lined with dried moss that she replaces when it grows too damp, though *too damp* is a relative concept in a place where the air itself is water. She sleeps curled on her side, knees drawn up, and the pale crickets have learned to avoid the space where her body rests. They flow around her alcove like water around a stone, their clicking uninterrupted.

She is old. She knows this without knowing her age—knows it in the way her joints ache when the groundwater pressure shifts, in the cloudiness that has crept into her left eye, in the loose teeth she probes with her tongue during the long hours between tasks. Her skin has taken on the pallor of the cistern's creatures, though she is not translucent like them. Not yet. Her hair, once dark, has gone white and hangs in ropes she does not cut because she has nothing sharp enough and because it no longer matters. She wears what remains of a rough-woven garment that has fused with accumulated mineral deposits into something more like armor than cloth, stiff and salt-white, cracking when she moves.

Her eyes have adapted. She sees well in the phosphorescent glow, can track the movement of individual amphibians in the basin, can spot the pale scavengers at the chamber's edge even when they try to hold still. In full daylight—when she climbs the shaft to check the grate, to clear debris, to look at the sky—she must shield her face and squint against brightness that feels like violence.

She does not remember why she came here. She remembers *that* she came—remembers descending the shaft when her body was young and limber, remembers the fear and then the wonder and then the slow settling into routine. There was a reason. A duty, perhaps, or a punishment, or a hiding. The cistern has washed the reason away and left only the staying.

---

**Her days:**

She wakes when the light through the shaft shifts from grey to pale gold—though *gold* is a word she no longer uses, has perhaps forgotten. She wakes and lies still, listening to the chorus, checking its patterns. She has learned to hear illness in the cricket-song, can detect when a population is stressed or when something new has entered the water. The sounds are a language she reads without understanding, the way one reads weather in the ache of old bones.

She drinks from a seep in the wall near her alcove, cupping her hands beneath it, waiting for them to fill. The water tastes of limestone and iron and something faintly sweet—the mineral signature of this particular groundwater, as familiar to her as her own breath. She does not remember what other water tastes like.

She eats the amphibians.

This is her primary task, the thing that keeps the basin from overflowing with pale bodies, the thing that maintains the equilibrium she has come to understand is sacred. She wades into the shallows at the basin's edge—the water cold enough to make her gasp, even after all these years—and stands motionless until the amphibians forget her presence. Then her hands move, quick and certain, and she catches them. Two or three per day. She kills them with a sharp blow against the stone rim, a mercy she taught herself early when she could not bear their struggling. She eats them raw because she has no fire, has not had fire since the smoke drove the crickets into a frenzy that lasted for days and disrupted the entire web of feeding and being fed upon.

The flesh is cool and faintly gelatinous. It no longer disgusts her. It is simply what she eats.

---

**The scavengers know her.**

The many-legged pale things at the chamber's edge—she calls them *the long ones* in the part of her mind that still forms words—have learned to wait for her leavings. When she finishes an amphibian, she leaves the bones and organs she cannot digest in a specific spot at the base of the eastern wall. The long ones emerge to claim them, and she watches, and they watch her watching, and there is something in this exchange that approaches relationship. Not trust. Not affection. But a mutual recognition, a negotiated coexistence.

She tried to catch one once, years ago, when hunger drove her to experiment. It bit her with mandibles she hadn't known it possessed, and the wound festered for weeks, wept a pale fluid that smelled of rot, nearly killed her. She learned. The long ones are not food. The long ones are neighbors, and neighbors have boundaries.

---

**She maintains the cistern.**

This is the duty she has assigned herself in the absence of remembered purpose—or perhaps this was always the purpose, and she simply cannot recall being told. She clears debris from the drainage channels with her fingers, working free the accumulated silt and dead matter that would otherwise clog the flow. She checks the mortar between stones, pressing her palm against the spongy places, listening for the subtle sounds of structural stress. When she finds a crack that is growing, she packs it with a paste she makes from calcium deposits scraped from the walls, mixed with her own spit. It is not proper mortar. But it holds.

She climbs the shaft twice a month—a count she keeps by scratching marks into the stone of her alcove, though she has lost track of how many cycles the marks represent. The shaft is narrow and the handholds are worn smooth, and each climb is harder than the last. At the top, a iron grate blocks the opening, and she clears the leaves and branches that accumulate there, works her fingers through the bars to push away the larger debris. Once she found a dead bird on the grate, and she brought it down and ate it, and the taste was so different from amphibian flesh that she wept, though she could not have said whether from joy or grief.

She looks at the sky when she climbs. Trees she does not recognize. Clouds. Sometimes stars, if she climbs at night, though night climbs are dangerous and she rarely risks them anymore. The world above is still there. She confirms this and descends.

---

**She talks to herself.**

Not constantly. Not madly. But words still live in her, and sometimes they need releasing. She narrates her actions in a voice gone rough from disuse: *Now we check the eastern seep. Now we count the snails.* She recites fragments of songs she once knew, the melodies dissolved but the rhythms preserved, nonsense syllables that might once have been words. She speaks to the amphibians as she catches them—*Thank you, small one. I'm sorry, small one*—because the ritual of acknowledgment seems important, though she cannot say why.

Sometimes she speaks to the cistern itself. Asks it questions. *Are you well today? Is the water sweet?* The cistern does not answer, but she listens to its sounds—the seeping, the dripping, the slow geological breath of stones under pressure—and she interprets. *Yes. The water is sweet. The system holds.*

---

**She is not alone.**

There is another.

She became aware of him—*him*, she has decided, though she has never seen clearly enough to confirm—three winters ago. Or four. Or ten. Time has grown unreliable. She noticed that her scratched marks in the alcove wall had been altered. New marks, in a different pattern, appearing while she slept or while she was deep in the drainage channels or while she was climbing.

She was terrified at first. Hid in her alcove for days, watching the shadows, eating nothing. The long ones grew agitated at the disruption of routine. The crickets' song shifted into a register she associated with danger.

Then she found a gift.

A smooth stone, rounder than any in the cistern, placed deliberately at the edge of her alcove. A river stone, maybe. From outside. Placed where she would find it, placed carefully, placed by hands that were not hers.

She keeps it in a fold of her calcite-stiff garment. She touches it when she is afraid.

---

**She has seen him only twice.**

Once: a shape at the far edge of the chamber, moving along the wall with the same careful gait she uses, the gait of someone who has learned to navigate wet stone in darkness. Tall. Thin. Pale. Gone before she could call out—and what would she have called? She has no name for herself, let alone for him.

Once: a reflection in the basin's surface, bending to drink at the opposite edge while she stood frozen in her alcove. The light was wrong, too dim, and the water's movement distorted everything, but she saw *hands*. Human hands. Scarred and weathered and alive.

She left a gift in return. A snail shell, particularly beautiful in its spiral geometry, placed at the base of the western wall where she does not normally go. It vanished within a day.

They have not exchanged more. They have not spoken. Perhaps he is afraid of her. Perhaps he is simply parallel—another maintainer, another warden, tending his half of the cistern while she tends hers. Perhaps the cistern requires two, has always required two, and she simply didn't know.

She wonders if he was here first. Wonders if he watched her descend all those years ago, watched her adapt, waited for her to become safe. Wonders if he is as old as she is, or older, or whether time works differently for him somehow.

She wonders if he is real.

But the marks appear. And the stone is smooth and round in her hand. And she is less lonely than she was, even without words, even without contact, even with only the knowledge that somewhere in the darkness, another human breathes.

---

**She will die here.**

She knows this. Has accepted it. Has, in some ways, looked forward to it—not eagerly, not with despair, but with the calm recognition that the cistern will need her body as it has needed the bodies of countless creatures before her. She will die and the long ones will come for her, and what they leave will wash into the drainage channels and feed the fungal bodies and the moss and eventually the water itself. She will become part of the equilibrium she has spent so long maintaining.

She hopes—though *hope* feels like too strong a word—that the other one will take over her half. Will check her seeps and clear her channels and catch the amphibians in the shallows where she caught them. Will talk to the cistern in whatever voice he has. Will know that she was here.

She hopes the cistern will remember her.

In the phosphorescent glow, in the layered sound, in the cool breath of air that is more water than air, she curls in her alcove and listens to the system she has become part of. The clicking shifts and settles. The amphibians float motionless beneath the surface. The long ones move in the shadows at the edge of sight.

She is the warden of a place that no longer needs human wardens. But she stays anyway, because the staying has become who she is, and because the cistern—against all reason, against all the evidence of its blind and indifferent ecology—feels like home.
