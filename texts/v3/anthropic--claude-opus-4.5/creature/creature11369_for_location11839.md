# The Annealed

They call themselves the Annealed, though the name is older than anyone living and may have originally meant something else—a process, perhaps, or a state of being rather than a people. They are human in the way that metal worked for three thousand years is still ore: the origin remains true, but time and heat have made something else of it.

Their skin holds color like the plateau holds warmth—deep reds and browns and bronzes that don't derive from sun exposure (there is no sun here, not really, not as a presence) but from generations of iron-rich dust breathed and absorbed and incorporated. They move slowly. Not from age or infirmity, but from *rightness*—the same rightness that governs the foundries themselves. A gesture begun will be completed, fully, without abbreviation. A step taken will land with intention. Watching an Annealed worker cross a workshop floor is like watching metal cool: inevitable, unhurried, each moment containing exactly what it should contain and nothing more. Children here learn stillness before they learn speech. The first discipline is patience; everything else is built upon it.

---

**The Chainmasters** tend the great descent-lines that lower ore into the smelting depths and raise finished metal back to the working levels. They are born to the work, quite literally—Chainmaster families have spent so many generations attuned to the vibration of cables under tension that they can read the state of a load from the hum transmitted through the stone floor, can feel the precise moment when ore transitions to slag to liquid to collectible metal, hundreds of meters below in heat that would vaporize them instantly.

Their hands are their diagnostic instruments. The calluses on a senior Chainmaster's palms are layered like tree rings, each stratum representing decades of cable-contact, and these callused pads have become sensitive beyond normal human range—they can detect temperature variations of fractions of a degree through a steel chain, can feel the crystalline structure of cooling metal through the vibration it imparts to its lifting apparatus. They speak of loads as though they were children: this one is settling well, this one is troubled, this one will need extra time in the gradient.

The youngest Chainmasters—those under forty—work the upper levels, learning the gross movements, the obvious signals. By sixty, they have earned positions at mid-descent, reading more subtle harmonics. The true masters, the ones who manage the deepest and most critical smelts, are rarely younger than a hundred, and they no longer use their eyes for anything related to their work. They sit in sensing-chambers near the primary cable-housings, palms pressed to the thrumming metal, faces slack with concentration, their awareness extended downward along the chains into regions of heat where consciousness itself seems to soften and pool.

When a Chainmaster dies, their hands are preserved in ceramic and hung in the cable-housing where they worked longest. The oldest foundries have galleries of these relics, hundreds of amber-colored hand-casts arranged in chronological sequences that span millennia. New Chainmasters visit these galleries before beginning their training, pressing their own palms against the ancient ceramics, trying to feel some resonance of the skills those hands once held.

---

**The Gradient-Readers** are the closest thing the plateau has to priests, though they would reject the term. They tend the temperature differentials that make the foundries function—not through any mechanical intervention, but through *prediction*, through understanding so deep it becomes something like prophecy.

A foundry's thermal profile is not static. The magma channels shift. The stone's conductivity varies with mineral content. Seasonal pressure changes in the atmosphere (minimal, but present) affect convection patterns. A Gradient-Reader's work is to know these variations before they manifest, to anticipate shifts in the temperature curves that will affect smelting and annealing processes months or years in the future, to adjust production schedules and material placements accordingly.

They train in the crevasses, the young ones, spending years in the glow-zones where the heat rises from below. They learn to feel temperature the way others feel texture—not just hot and cold, but the *quality* of heat, its source and vector and age, whether it is rising or falling, whether it has passed throughiteite oriteite calciumite on its way to their skin. A skilled Gradient-Reader can stand in a corridor and describe the thermal structure of the surrounding hundred meters of stone, can trace the paths by which heat is flowing, can identify anomalies that indicate shifting channels far below.

The most accomplished Gradient-Readers develop a condition that has no name but is recognized by all: their sense of temperature becomes untethered from their bodies. They report feeling heat-patterns in distant locations, experiencing the thermal signature of places they have never visited, waking from sleep with detailed knowledge of gradient shifts occurring in foundries on the other side of the plateau. Whether this is genuine perception, intuition refined beyond conscious understanding, or a form of madness that happens to produce accurate results, no one can say. The Gradient-Readers themselves don't speculate. They simply report what they feel, and the reports are accurate often enough that the foundries rely upon them.

They live apart from the other workers, in chambers positioned at thermal nodes where multiple gradients intersect. They eat little. They speak less. Their eyes, over decades of crevasse-training, have adapted to the orange glow until they see poorly in any other light—not blind, but *calibrated*, their vision tuned to the specific frequencies of magma-radiance until the polished-obsidian light of the foundries seems harsh and strange to them.

**The Stockpile-Keepers** are perhaps the strangest of the Annealed, because their work requires them to care for things that will never be used.

Each foundry-city maintains meticulous records of its accumulated production. Every ingot is logged. Every cast part is catalogued. Every beam and bracket and precision component is assigned a location in the vast warehouse-fields and tracked as it slowly sinks into the compressing dust-layers below. The Stockpile-Keepers maintain these records and conduct physical inventories—work that never ends, because production never stops, and because the stockpiles themselves are in constant slow motion, settling and shifting and gradually becoming something other than what they were.

They develop a relationship with the accumulated goods that defies economic logic. A Stockpile-Keeper will speak of a particular ingot the way others speak of family members, will track its position over decades as it sinks millimeter by millimeter toward the plateau stone, will note with something like grief when it finally passes below the survey-threshold and becomes part of the deep stratum of compressed metal that underlies the older foundry-cities. They argue about the *purpose* of specific stockpiles—not whether they have one, because clearly they don't in any practical sense, but what purpose they *would* have, what they are *suited for*, what need they might someday fill.

Some Stockpile-Keepers develop theories about the orders that drive production. They spend their lives analyzing the work-schedules that arrive through the pneumatic tubes, looking for patterns, trying to determine what intelligence specifies the quantities and designs, trying to understand the logic of a system that produces endlessly for no apparent recipient. These investigations never reach conclusions, but they are passed down through generations, each Keeper adding their observations to records that have grown larger than any individual could read in a lifetime.

The oldest Keepers, the ones who have spent a century or more walking the warehouse-rows and watching goods accumulate, sometimes stop speaking of the stockpiles as manufactured objects at all. They describe them as *growth*, as something the plateau is doing through the foundries, as the slow expression of geological intention that merely uses human hands as intermediaries. The metal is not being *made*, they say. It is being *expressed*. The plateau is becoming what it always was, and the foundries are simply the mechanism by which it reveals itself.

---

**The Caravan-Born** are the exception that proves the plateau's patient rule.

Someone must drive the great wheeled transports that crawl toward the plateau's edge carrying cargo to the lowlands. Someone must make the journey of weeks or months across the empty tile-fields, far from the foundry-cities, exposed to the minimal but real variations in temperature and pressure that the plateau's interior never experiences. The Caravan-Born are bred for this work—literally selected, over generations, for traits that the settled Annealed consider slightly shameful: restlessness, speed, comfort with change.

They are leaner than the foundry-workers, quicker-moving, their metabolisms tuned for the journey rather than the destination. They speak faster, decide faster, live faster—their lifespans are shorter than the settled Annealed, rarely more than eighty years, and they accomplish in that time what a foundry-worker might spread across two centuries. They are regarded with a mixture of gratitude, pity, and unease: necessary, but not quite *right*, adapted to a mode of existence that the plateau barely tolerates.

The Caravan-Born have their own culture, developed in the weeks between foundry-cities when they travel with only each other and the cargo for company. They sing, which the settled Annealed rarely do. They tell stories, rapidly, with dramatic gestures and raised voices. They make decisions by argument and consensus rather than by patient contemplation. To a foundry-worker, a Caravan-Born encampment seems chaotic, wasteful, *loud*—all that energy expended on communication that could be internal, on movement that could be stillness, on change that could be continuity.

But the Caravan-Born know things that the settled Annealed have forgotten. They have seen the plateau's edges, where the great terraces drop away toward the lowlands. They have glimpsed the world beyond: the distant glitter of water in planetary basins, the green of vegetation that grows where rain falls, the blue of skies that don't shade toward bronze. They carry this knowledge like a secret, rarely sharing it with the foundry-workers who wouldn't know what to do with it anyway. The plateau is a world unto itself. The Caravan-Born are the only Annealed who remember that it isn't the only world there is.

---

**The Deep-Listeners** never come to the surface.

Far below the working levels, in chambers cut into stone that stays warm enough to soften bare feet, small communities maintain vigil over the heat-worms. Their purpose is unclear even to themselves—they do not control the worms, do not communicate with them, cannot predict their movements. But they listen. They press their bodies against the warm stone and feel the grinding vibration of biological drills working through basalt, and they record what they hear in notation systems that have evolved over millennia into something more felt than written.

A Deep-Listener's scores are inscribed on ceramic tablets using heat-styluses that leave raised marks readable by touch. The tablets record not sound, exactly, but *presence*—the quality of a heat-worm's passage, its depth and direction, the tenor of its grinding, any anomalies or hesitations in its progress. These records are consulted when foundries make major decisions about pit placement or expansion, but more often they are simply maintained, added to archives that no one will ever fully review, another form of accumulation in a place defined by accumulation.

Deep-Listeners develop a condition the surface-dwellers call *stone-sense*: an awareness of the rock itself as a living medium, full of movements and pressures and slow intentions. They feel the plateau breathing. They feel the magma channels pulsing far below. They feel the weight of the stockpiles pressing down from above, the compression of millennia of manufactured goods becoming geological strata. Some of them stop distinguishing between their own bodies and the stone that surrounds them—not a delusion, exactly, but a boundary-dissolution, an expansion of self to include the medium they have spent their lives attending to.

The Deep-Listeners live long, even by Annealed standards. Some of the eldest have been at their posts for three centuries or more, their bodies half-absorbed into the warm stone of their chambers, their awareness spread through kilometers of rock, their heartbeats slowed to match the patient rhythms of the plateau itself. They are fed by acolytes who press nutrient pastes to their lips. They are asked questions by Gradient-Readers seeking confirmation of thermal shifts. They respond, when they respond, in vibrations rather than words—touches against the stone that travel upward through the foundry structure, felt rather than heard, interpreted by those with the training to interpret them.

Whether they are still fully human is a question the Annealed do not ask, because asking it would require defining *fully human* in a place where humanity has spent three thousand years—or thirty thousand—becoming something else.

---

**The children** are the most unsettling thing about the Annealed, if you come from elsewhere.

They are not neglected. They are not unloved. They are surrounded by family, by community, by the patient attention of elders who have all the time in the world to teach them. But they are *quiet*. Foundry children learn stillness before speech, and they learn patience before play, and the games they do play are games of long duration—construction projects that span years, hide-and-seek rounds that last for weeks, contests of endurance rather than speed.

A foundry child of ten has the calm, the settled presence, of an elder in most societies. They sit for hours without restlessness. They speak in complete, considered sentences. They have already begun their apprenticeships, already started to develop the specialized awareness—of chain-vibration, of thermal gradient, of stockpile-position—that will define their adult lives. They are not forced into this. They absorb it from the environment, from the pace of the plateau itself, from the example of everyone around them.

Outside observers—the rare traders from the lowlands, the occasional scholar who makes the journey to study the foundries—find the children deeply unnerving. There is something wrong, they feel, about a six-year-old who can out-wait an adult, who responds to questions after minutes of silent consideration, who seems to have no urgent needs, no desperate wants, no tantrums or demands. The children seem *finished* somehow, complete in a way that children shouldn't be, already fully themselves at an age when elsewhere they would still be becoming.

The Annealed do not understand this discomfort. Children here are simply small people with much to learn, moving at the pace appropriate to a place where everything moves slowly, growing toward adulthood the way metal cools toward its final temper: gradually, thoroughly, with no stages skipped and no shortcuts taken.

---

And then there are **the ones who tend the pneumatic tubes**.

No one speaks of them directly. They are referred to obliquely, when they must be referred to at all: *the schedule-keepers*, *the order-sources*, *those who know what is needed*. They work in the deepest parts of each foundry-city, in chambers that connect to a tube system that runs through the plateau itself, linking all forty-odd installations in a network that predates any living memory.

The work-orders that drive production emerge from this network. Specifications for goods, quantities required, delivery schedules—all of it arrives through the tubes in ceramic capsules, inscribed in a notation that the foundry managers can read but cannot write. The orders are obeyed. They have always been obeyed. The system works.

But someone must tend the tubes. Someone must receive the capsules, sort them, distribute them to the appropriate departments. Someone must—it is assumed—send reports back, though no one has ever witnessed this happening. Someone must maintain the tube system itself, which runs through kilometers of stone and must occasionally require repair.

These people exist. They must exist. But they are not part of foundry society in any normal sense. They do not eat in the common halls. They do not live in the dormitory levels. They do not have families, or if they do, those families are not known. They are seen, sometimes, in the deep corridors: figures in robes the color of rust-dust, moving with purpose toward destinations that others cannot follow. They do not speak when encountered. They do not acknowledge greetings. They are there, and then they are not there, and the tubes continue to function, and the orders continue to arrive, and the foundries continue their work.

Some believe they are a separate caste, bred for their role as the Chainmasters and Gradient-Readers are bred for theirs. Some believe they are not human at all, but something produced by the foundries themselves, manufactured workers for manufactured work. Some believe they are the original inhabitants of the plateau, the ones who recognized its potential three thousand years ago—or thirty thousand—and who have continued their work ever since, unchanged while the Annealed adapted around them.

The Annealed do not investigate. The system works. The orders arrive. The foundries produce. Whatever lives in the tube-warrens is part of the plateau's pattern, like the heat-worms and the cable-moths and the magma channels themselves, and the pattern has been working for longer than anyone can remember, and will work for longer than anyone can imagine, and questioning it would be like questioning the warmth of the stone or the color of the sky.

---

In the amber half-light of the foundries, the Annealed live their long slow lives.

They work. They produce. They accumulate skills and memories and goods that will never be used. They grow old gradually, their bodies absorbing the plateau's minerals, their awareness expanding into the stone and the heat and the patient rhythms of production. They raise children who will do the same. They die and are buried in the stockpiles and slowly become indistinguishable from the metal that surrounds them.

They do not wonder, most of them, whether there is more than this. The plateau provides. The foundries produce. The goods accumulate. The system has run for longer than recorded history; it will run until the magma cools. There is a rightness to it that transcends any individual purpose, any personal desire, any question about meaning or destination.

They are the Annealed: heated and cooled and heated again, worked by time into shapes that fit precisely the niches available to them, patient as the stone that holds them, productive as the channels that warm them, slowly becoming what the plateau has always intended them to be.

And in the depths, the Deep-Listeners feel the heat-worms turning. And in the tube-warrens, the robed figures carry capsules to destinations unknown. And in the stockpile-fields, another ingot is added to a pile that will never be moved.

The foundries continue their work.
