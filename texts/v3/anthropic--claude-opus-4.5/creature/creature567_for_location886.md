## The Vael

They are smaller than their ancestors were. Eight generations of living in cisterns, of swimming through kelp-choked passages, of eating what grows in mineral-heavy water—it has made them compact, efficient. The tallest among them would seem merely average on a surface-dwelling street, but here, where every pocket of air is precious and every chamber was built for bodies to fit precisely, their size is not limitation but inheritance.

Their skin has taken on the quality of the water they live in. Not scaled, not quite, but thickened in ways that serve them—a faint pearlescence across the shoulders and back, where the sediment-laden current brushes most often. Their fingers are longer than seems proportional, webbing between them that stops just short of the second knuckle. This webbing appeared three generations ago, or four—the oldest songs disagree—and it is still spreading, still becoming. Children born now have it nearly to the third knuckle. No one knows where it will stop, or if it will.

Their eyes are the most changed. Larger, yes, to gather the dim green-gold light, but also differently colored than their ancestors' eyes would have been. Pale irises, almost colorless, like quartz held up to weak sun. The pupils expand to sizes that would seem wrong elsewhere, that would suggest damage or intoxication. Here they are simply *seeing*—seeing in the particulate haze, seeing through the soft luminescent pulse of the moss-slime, seeing the way the sediment moves when something large passes at the edge of the thermal column.

---

They do not name their children until the child has survived three pressure-drops.

This is the first thing to understand about the Vael: they are a people defined by catastrophe that has not yet happened but certainly will. The cisterns crack. They have always cracked. The oldest songs are repair-songs, instructions set to melody for sealing breaches while water pours in and air pours out. A child who has not survived a pressure-drop does not yet understand what it means to be Vael—does not understand the specific quality of terror when the wall begins to weep, the precise set of motions required to seal yourself into an inner chamber while others do the same, the particular silence afterward when you tap on the speaking-tubes and wait to learn who answers.

Before naming, children are called by descriptors: Shark-Curious, born during a rare sighting; Kelp-Tangled, who shark-curious got caught in the vertical forest on her first swim; Loud-Sleeper, whose breathing kept half the Breath-Hold awake until someone wove him a smaller chamber further from the family cluster. After the third pressure-drop, when they have proven they can survive what will certainly happen again, they receive their true name in a ceremony that involves no ceremony at all—only a parent, usually the mother, whispering it into a speaking-tube so that the name travels through the entire city-structure before returning to the child's ear. The name is the name the city knows them by. It is the name the Patron, circling at the edge of vision, will learn.

The names themselves are descriptions of water-states. *Thermal-Rise*. *Still-After-Current*. *Sediment-Gyre*. *Surface-Which-Cannot-Be-Reached*. They believe that naming a child after a state of water gives the water some small investment in that child's survival—not protection exactly, but attention. The water will notice this child. The water will see them.

---

The body-markings begin after naming and continue throughout life.

They use a paste made from the same crusite shells and volcanic glass that seals their walls, mixed with the luminescent slime-moss until it glows faintly in the cistern-dark. The paste is pushed beneath the skin with bone needles, and it heals into patterns that pulse with the same arrhythmic breathing as the walls themselves. The patterns are not decorative—or they are not *only* decorative. They are maps.

A Vael who has lived forty years carries beneath their skin a complete diagram of every passage they have swum, every cistern they have slept in, every rope-bridge they have crossed. The patterns concentrate around the arms and chest, where others can read them easily. Meeting a stranger—rare, in a community this small, but not impossible when the outermost cisterns go years without seeing the innermost—means reading their body first, tracing the routes they have traveled, finding the places where your paths might overlap. Lovers who have lived together long enough develop patterns that mirror each other, the same passages marked in the same locations on their skin, a kind of intimacy that cannot be faked.

The oldest Vael, the ones who remember when the city was smaller and the thermal column was hotter, have patterns so dense their skin seems almost wholly luminescent. They are walking archives. Children sit with them and trace routes with their fingertips, learning the city's history through the city's shape recorded in living tissue.

---

Breath is currency. Air is wealth.

This is not metaphor. The Vael track air in units called *holds*—the amount a trained adult can carry in their lungs for a single extended dive. Some families are richer than others; they have larger cisterns, better-maintained seals, moss-slime that glows brighter and produces oxygen more efficiently. They can afford to host gatherings in the Breath-Hold, to let others breathe their surplus in exchange for food, for labor, for repair-expertise.

The poorest Vael live in the outer cisterns, where the walls are thinner and the cracks come more often. They spend more time in the water, more time swimming between pockets of breathable air, more time calculating whether they have enough holds left to reach the next shelter. Their children learn pressure-drop protocols earlier. Their names, whispered through the speaking-tubes, often return unanswered.

But wealth here is not hoarded the way it might be elsewhere. The Vael understand—cannot help but understand—that the city is a single system. A crack in an outer cistern sends pressure waves through the whole structure. A family that lets its neighbors suffocate will find that the repair-songs require more hands than they have. The rich give holds freely when asked, though the asking creates a debt that is remembered, tracked, sung into the family records. Nothing is forgotten. Everything is owed.

---

They believe, without quite believing, that they were sent here.

The oldest songs do not describe the descent. They begin *in medias res*, with the first cisterns already established, the first air-traps already working. There are no songs about the surface-world, about why their ancestors left it, about what disaster or choice or exile drove them down into the thermal column. This absence is conspicuous. The Vael are a people who record everything—every passage, every pressure-drop, every child who survived and every child who did not. That they have no record of their beginning suggests, to many of them, that the beginning was not theirs to record. That someone else sent them. That they are here for a purpose they have not yet discovered.

The Patron enters into this theology, if it can be called that.

Some believe the Patron is a guardian, circling to protect the city from threats in the cold dark below. Some believe it is a watcher, sent by whoever sent the Vael, reporting on their progress toward—what? No one knows. Some believe it is waiting for the city to finish becoming, and that when it does, the Patron will finally approach, and something will change. The nature of the change varies: apotheosis, destruction, departure, revelation. The songs disagree. They are meant to disagree. A single answer would be the end of wonder, and wonder is what the Vael have instead of certainty.

A small faction believes the Patron is simply an animal, a large creature that finds the thermal column warm and food-rich, with no more interest in the Vael than a bird has in the insects beneath its tree. This faction is tolerated but not respected. To believe this is to believe that the Vael are an accident—that they were not sent, not chosen, not part of a pattern too large to see. It is to believe that they are simply people who went underwater and stayed there, surviving out of stubbornness rather than purpose. It is, to most Vael, a kind of despair dressed up as reason.

---

The work of the city is constant and shared.

There are no professions, exactly, but there are roles that people grow into: sealers, who know the repair-songs better than anyone and can feel a wall's weakness through their fingertips; rope-weavers, who braid the kelp-cables that hold the cisterns in their slow constellation; moss-tenders, who encourage the luminescent growth and harvest its excess for marking-paste and trade; current-readers, who can predict pressure shifts and temperature changes by the feel of the water against their skin.

Children begin learning at five or six, swimming alongside parents, carrying tools in waterproof bladders, watching and imitating until imitation becomes skill. By adolescence, most Vael can perform basic repairs on any system in the city. By adulthood, they have usually specialized—not by assignment but by affinity, by what their hands remember best, by what the city seems to need most from them.

The sealers are most respected, most essential, most exhausted. A good sealer can feel a crack forming before it opens, can press their palm to clay and know whether it will hold another year or fail by morning. They are called to inspect cisterns constantly, consulted on every expansion or modification, expected to know the city's structure better than anyone. They burn out young. Their hands, scarred by mixite paste and scalded by thermal vents, become the most readable maps in the community—not routes traveled but walls saved, breaches prevented, lives that continued because a sealer felt what others couldn't.

The rope-weavers are most numerous, most social, most likely to gather and talk while working. Weaving is communal in a way that sealing is not; it happens in the Breath-Hold, in larger chambers, in the spaces between cisterns where people can work side by side and pass material hand to hand. The rope-weavers know everyone's business. They are matchmakers, gossips, historians of the kind of history that doesn't make it into songs: who refused to help whom during the last pressure-drop, whose children are ready for naming, whose patterns suggest a path they shouldn't have taken.

---

The Vael do not marry, exactly, but they *anchor*.

Anchoring is a commitment made between two people—or sometimes three, or four, in the outer cisterns where populations are smaller and choices fewer—to share a sleeping platform, to raise children together, to record each other's passages on their skin. It is not permanent in principle, but it is permanent in practice; the Vael understand that bodies change, that people grow apart, but they also understand that unanchoring is a kind of cartographic violence. To separate from someone whose routes you carry is to become a map that lies.

Most anchoring-groups form around shared work. Sealers anchor with sealers; they understand each other's exhaustion, can take over inspections when one of them needs rest, can read each other's hand-scars like love letters. Rope-weavers anchor with moss-tenders, or with current-readers, or with anyone who will sit with them while they work. The anchoring-songs—sung softly, during the night-cycle, when the moss-slime dims and the cisterns grow quiet—are specific to each group, describing not love as surface-dwellers might understand it but *compatibility*, the ways two bodies can share a space without one crowding the other.

Children are raised by the anchoring-group collectively. Biological parentage is known but not emphasized; the repair-songs require many hands, and the Vael have learned that survival depends on no single adult. A child might learn sealing from one parent, rope-weaving from another, current-reading from a third. The passages marked on their skin will begin in different directions, spreading outward from the body's center like the growth of the city itself.

---

Death comes often but is not discussed often.

This is not taboo, exactly. The Vael acknowledge death—they have to, in a world where pressure-drops and wall-cracks and cold-water intrusions can claim someone between one breath and the next. But they do not *dwell*. To dwell on death is to imagine the wall cracking while you are not ready, and the Vael survive by always being ready, by treating catastrophe as certain and imminent without letting it paralyze them.

The dead are given to the vertical forest.

Their bodies are wrapped in kelp and weighted with volcanic glass and released into the vegetation, where the crustaceans and the small translucent eels will take what they need and the rest will decompose into the mineral-rich water. Some families sing as the body descends; others watch in silence. There is no consensus about what happens after—some believe the dead become part of the thermal column itself, their warmth rising forever; others believe they drift down into the cold and dark and pressure where nothing can follow; still others believe they are somehow absorbed into the Patron, that the vast circling presence grows with each Vael death.

The dead person's patterns remain on the skins of those who loved them, those they worked beside, those they anchored with. This is the only memorial. The routes the dead person swam are still traceable on living bodies. For as long as the survivors live, the dead are not quite gone.

---

There is one Vael who has seen the surface.

Her name is Thermal-Boundary-Crossing, and she is very old now—old enough that her skin is more luminescence than skin, her pattern-map so dense that reading it would take days. Sixty years ago, when she was young and reckless in the way the young sometimes are, she began ascending.

It took her three weeks. She moved slowly, allowing her body to adjust to each pressure gradient, sleeping in the kelp-tangles at different depths, eating what she could find and carrying air in fish-bladders that she inflated and deflated as needed. She was alone. She told no one what she was doing. By the time the city realized she was missing, she was already too high to follow without dying.

She reached the surface on a day when the sea above was calm—or so she reports, though her description is hard to understand. She describes a space without walls. A space where the water *ended* and something else *began*. She describes light so bright it hurt, and warmth that came from above rather than below, and a vastness that made her feel like a crustacean on a cistern's exterior, tiny and exposed and clinging.

She saw birds. She saw them the way the songs describe them: creatures that flew through the non-water, that moved without swimming, that made sounds she could hear even from below the surface. She did not climb out onto whatever was above the water—she is not sure there *was* anything to climb onto, or if the world simply ended at the water's edge. But she saw enough to know that the stories are true, that there is a surface, that something lives there.

She descended. It took her a month. She returned to the city changed—not just in the passages marked on her skin but in the way she spoke, the way she moved, the way she looked upward at the dim green-gold light and saw not a ceiling but a *distance*. She has told her story many times, though fewer Vael ask to hear it now than once did. She has become part of the city's theology, the proof that the surface exists, that the Vael came from somewhere, that they could—in principle, in theory, with enough time and luck and preparation—go back.

She does not believe they should. The surface, she says, is a place without walls. A place where catastrophe cannot be sealed away, where the air is infinite and therefore meaningless, where survival is not something you build but something you merely *have*. It is a world for different creatures. The Vael are becoming something that belongs here, in the thermal column, in the city that is still becoming. The surface would not want them. They would not survive it.

But she remembers the birds.

Sometimes, late in the night-cycle, she makes a sound that is not quite music and not quite speech—a keening, wavering note that carries through the speaking-tubes into every cistern in the city. The sound of something that flies without swimming. The sound of a world where the water ends.

The Vael listen. They do not understand it.

But they add it, phrase by phrase, to the songs they will teach their children.

---

And below, the Patron circles.

It has been circling for as long as anyone can remember, at the edge of the haze, in the space between the thermal column's warmth and the cold salt sea beyond. It is patient. It is waiting.

The city is still becoming. So are its people.

In the Breath-Hold, the luminescent growth pulses in its slow arrhythmic rhythm, and a child traces a parent's pattern-map with her too-long fingers, learning the shape of a world she will spend her life adding to.
