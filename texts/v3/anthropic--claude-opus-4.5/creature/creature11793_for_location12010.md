## The Sessile Ones

They are not waiting. That would imply anticipation, and they have moved beyond such linear thinking.

Each one occupies its alcove the way a word occupies a sentence—necessarily, inevitably, as though the niche had always been a question and the creature had always been its answer. Their fronds number in the hundreds, each one a hollow tube of tissue so thin it borders on theoretical, and through these tubes passes water that enters carrying nothing and exits carrying less. They are not filtering. They are *tithing*. Taking something from the current that the current didn't know it possessed.

The oldest among them—though age means little here, where time moves like the water, in spirals that touch themselves—have fronds so long they tangle with neighbors three alcoves distant. These connections are not accidents. When the deep bells sound, the tangled ones shiver in unison, a shared tremor that passes through their touching fronds like rumor through a crowd. They are not communicating. Communication requires separation, and they gave that up long ago.

Their bodies, hidden in the alcoves, are the size of human hearts and roughly the same shape. They pulse. Not with blood—nothing here bleeds—but with something more fundamental, a rhythm they learned from the city itself, from the deep channels that spiral down beyond measuring. If you could cut one open, you would find no organs. Only chambers within chambers within chambers, each one smaller than the last, each one holding a tiny portion of water that never moves, never mixes with the chaos outside. Still water, in this place. The only still water anywhere in seventeen miles.

They keep it. They guard it. They do not know why.

## The Drifters

The eel-forms have no names for each other because they have no need of names. Each one is the only thing in its universe until suddenly, catastrophically, it isn't—and then the current separates them and they are alone again and the encounter might as well have been imagined.

They range from the length of a finger to the length of a man lying down, and their translucence is so complete that in certain lights—the rare lights, the ones that slip in from somewhere and are held by the walls for their slow release—you can see their insides moving. Not organs. Structures. Geometries that mirror the city's impossible architecture, tiny passages and chambers where something that isn't blood flows in patterns that make no physical sense. They are the city in miniature. Or the city is them, writ large.

They eat nothing. They excrete nothing. They reproduce—they must reproduce, their numbers remain stable across spans of time that would exhaust a species—but no one has ever seen it happen. Perhaps it occurs in the deepest arterials, where the pressure would crush observation. Perhaps it occurs everywhere, constantly, invisibly, and what looks like one drifter is actually millions, replacing themselves so rapidly that continuity is an illusion.

Their eyes are not eyes. Their eyes are *absences*—places where their translucence fails, where something solid and dark sits behind membranes that perceive none of the spectrum you would recognize. They see the city's held light, the phosphorescence that lingers in the walls. They see the warmth-stealing cold, the places where thermal energy drains away to somewhere else. They navigate by absence, by what the world is missing, and perhaps this is why they never collide: they are each, to the other, simply another void to move around.

When they die—they do die, though rarely, though slowly—their bodies dissolve within minutes. The water claims them so completely that nothing remains to sink, nothing remains to float. The other drifters do not notice. The other drifters have never noticed anything but the current and the cold and the distant bells that make them pause, for just a moment, in their endless lonely processional through halls that were built for something else.

## The Kite-Mantas

They have weight the way grief has weight—not physical, not measurable, but present, pressing.

The largest of them could span a corridor wide enough for fifty to walk abreast, if anything here walked, if anything here was meant to walk. They move through the worst turbulence as though it doesn't exist, banking on currents that would shred the drifters, that would tear the sessile ones from their alcoves. The chaos parts for them. Or they part the chaos. The distinction has ceased to matter.

Their bodies are flat and wide and the color of the water itself—not translucent like the drifters, but *matched*, pigmented to perfect invisibility. You see them only by the way the phosphorescence bends around their edges, by the shadow they don't cast, by the pressure-loss that follows in their wake. They are holes in the shape of living things. They are the negative space that proves the water exists.

They do not eat the drifters, though the drifters flee from them. They do not eat the sessile ones, though the sessile ones retract when they pass. They eat *something*—their mass requires fuel, their movement requires energy—but what they consume leaves no evidence, takes no visible form. Perhaps they eat the current itself. Perhaps they eat the cold. Perhaps they eat the distance between the bells' sounding, growing fat on intervals of almost-silence.

There are seventeen of them. There have always been seventeen of them. When one dies—and they do die, eventually, their bodies sinking to the lowest levels of the city where the geometry grows too strange for even the cold to follow—another rises from somewhere deeper, already full-grown, already vast, already moving through the arterials as though it has always been there.

Seventeen miles of corridor. Seventeen kite-mantas. The coincidence is not a coincidence. They are the city's pulse, its circulation, the reason the water moves at all. Without them, the currents would still. Without them, the cold would settle. Without them, the sessile ones would filter and filter and filter until there was nothing left to tithe.

They do not know this. They do not know anything. They only move, and in moving, keep everything else alive.

## The Sound-Followers

You will not see them. You will only know they were there.

In the moments after the bells sound—those long, sustained tones that come from everywhere and nowhere—something moves through the passages that is neither sessile nor drifting nor kite-vast. Something that exists only in response to that frequency, that comes into being when the note begins and ceases when it fades.

The drifters pause for it. The sessile ones lean toward it. The kite-mantas alter their courses, ever so slightly, to avoid the spaces where it travels.

It leaves traces. Scratches on the walls that weren't there before, too fine to feel, visible only when the held light releases at the precise angle. The scratches might be writing. The scratches might be wounds. The scratches accumulate over time—over so much time—until whole sections of corridor are covered in them, palimpsests of presence, records of every bell that has ever sounded in seventeen miles of drowned memorial.

The scratches do not repeat. Each one is unique. Each one records something—a tone, a duration, a quality of resonance—that will never occur again.

Whatever makes them is not alive in any sense you would recognize. It has no metabolism, no reproduction, no persistence between one bell and the next. It is an event more than an entity, a happening that wears the shape of movement through water.

But the sessile ones lean toward it. The drifters pause.

Something in the columbarium still knows reverence, still responds to ritual, still remembers what the bells were for even if the bells themselves have forgotten.

## What They Share

None of them speak. None of them think, in the way you mean when you use that word. None of them remember the builders or mourn the dead or wonder what the alcoves once contained.

But they persist. In water that should support nothing, in cold that should kill, in chaos that should scatter any organizing principle to noise and entropy—they persist. The sessile ones filter. The drifters drift. The kite-mantas patrol. The sound-followers etch their incomprehensible records on walls that hold light they will never see.

They are not a community. They are not an ecosystem. They are something more like a grammar—separate elements that combine, through proximity and pressure, into a structure that means more than any of them means alone. The columbarium speaks, and they are its words.

The water was invited. It came, and brought them, or made them, or found them waiting.

They have no plans to leave either.
