**The Clarifiers**

They are not born so much as *precipitated*. In the deeper basins where the mineral suspension thickens, where the water takes on the quality of clouded glass, something accretes around a seed of calcium or a flake of rust. It takes years. A Clarifier begins as a denser patch in the gyre—a knot of particulate that doesn't disperse when it should, that holds its shape through one rotation, then another, then a thousand.

By the time one rises from the fifth pond, it has a form: vaguely bilateral, vaguely upright, with limbs that trail into wisps of suspended sedite before resolving, reluctantly, into something like hands. Its body is translucent in the way the water is translucent—not clear, but *full*, holding light in its mineral fog. When it moves through the shallows, it leaves a wake of temporary clarity, the water behind it briefly, startlingly transparent before the particles drift back in.

They do not speak. They *settle*. A Clarifier standing at the edge of a basin will go still for hours, and in its stillness the water around it will begin to stratify—heavier sediments sinking, lighter ones rising, layers forming like the rings of a felled tree. This is their purpose, or the closest thing they have to one: they remember what the ponds were built to do, even if they no longer remember why.

There are seven of them, one for each basin, though they do not stay in their respective pools. They migrate along the sluices, folding themselves through the iron gates with a patience that borders on geological. When two meet—usually at the third pond, where the gyre draws everything toward center—they stand facing each other for days. Something passes between them. The foam raft rotates. Then one moves on, and the other stays, and the water is fractionally clearer for a while.

The Clarifiers tend the calcium cascades like gardens. They scrape accumulation from the sluice lips with hands that leave no fingerprints, only faint whorls in the mineral deposit. They break away the terraces when they grow too heavy, guiding the underwater avalanches with gestures that look almost like conducting. They cannot stop the buildup, only shape it. The frozen waterfalls of lime are their architecture—not designed, exactly, but *curated*. Encouraged. Given permission to become what they were already becoming.

Their relationship with the pump house is complicated. They do not enter it—the pooling water on its floor is too still, too separated from the system, and something about the frozen machinery disturbs them in ways they cannot articulate even to themselves. But they gather at its foundation sometimes, pressing their indistinct hands against the sinking brick, and the building settles a little slower on those nights. Whether they are holding it up or helping it down is unclear. Perhaps they do not distinguish between the two.

When the pumps cycle on—that wet mechanical wheeze from the bunker—the Clarifiers shiver in unison, a sympathetic resonance that runs through all seven of them regardless of which basin they occupy. The first pulse of turbid water entering the highest pool is, for them, something between a meal and a message. They orient toward it. They remember, for a moment, that they were made to process, to separate, to render clean what arrives dirty. Then the memory clouds over again, and they return to their slow patrols.

At dusk, they watch the swimming shapes in the delta—those fish-that-are-not-fish, the color of mineral water. The Clarifiers do not feed on them, but they do not ignore them either. There is a recognition there, a kinship. Those small mouths breaking the surface in tiny rings are doing the same work in miniature: taking in the suspension, extracting something, releasing the rest. The Clarifiers sometimes stand at the edge of the lowest basin as the shapes feed, perfectly still, and in their stillness you can almost see what they are thinking. *This is what we were. This is what we will be again. This is what settling looks like when it finally ends.*

But it never ends. And so neither do they.

One of them—the oldest, precipitated around a bolt that fell from the bunker pipe decades ago—has developed a habit that might be ritual or might be error. Every seventeen days, when the moon is at a particular angle (though it cannot see the moon, has no eyes in any conventional sense), it climbs the slope to the pump house doorframe. It stands before the scratched tallies. It adds one mark with a finger ofite calcium, so faint it barely shows. Then it descends, and returns to the water, and does not think about what it has counted until it counts again.

The others have noticed. They have not asked. Questions would require a language they settled out of themselves long ago.

What the Clarifiers want—if want is the word—is for the water to finally clear. For the sediment to reach the bottom and stay there. For the pumps to cycle once more, then stop, then stay stopped. For the settling to settle. They work toward this with every slow gesture, every patient hour of stillness, knowing in whatever serves them for knowledge that it will never happen. The system is closed but not complete. The mineral negotiation has no end state. They will clarify forever, and forever the water will cloud again, and this is not tragedy to them because they have no concept of tragedy. It is simply what they are. It is simply what the ponds are. It is simply what settling looks like, in a place that was built to settle and forgot how to stop.

On rare nights, when the air pressure drops and the hiss of incoming water takes on a different pitch, all seven Clarifiers gather at the third pond. They stand around the gyre, evenly spaced, and they go still together. The foam raft slows. The water stratifies so completely that you can see the bottom—grey concrete, cracked, colonized by mineral growth in shapes that might be intentional. The Clarifiers look down at what they have made visible. They stay that way until dawn, or until the pumps cycle, or until one of them moves first and breaks the spell.

What they see there, in the clarified depths, they do not share. Perhaps there is nothing to see. Perhaps the looking is the point.

Then the sediment rises again, and they disperse, and the ponds go on becoming what they have always been becoming: a landscape of endless almost, populated by beings made of the same.
