## The Tenders

They are not moths, though the first glimpse suggests it — that papery flutter, those too-many limbs folding. They are not hands, though they grip and weave and shape with an intelligence that is undeniably manual. They are something that evolved in darkness and then found this place, this slow blue light, and decided it was enough.

There are perhaps forty of them. Perhaps sixty. They don't hold still long enough to count, and they look so much like the roots they tend that your eye slides past them, registering movement but not presence. Their bodies are segmented, pale where they press against ice, bruising to violet where they cluster in the warmer nodes. They have no eyes that you can identify — no heads, really, in any familiar sense — but they navigate the root-maze with absolute certainty, never colliding, never hesitating, as if the architecture itself tells them where to go.

They communicate through touch. You see it once you know to look: two of them meeting on a walkway of braided root, pausing, their forelimbs extending to brush against each other in patterns too quick and subtle to parse. A conversation that lasts three seconds. Then they part, each continuing in a new direction, their paths altered by whatever passed between them. The whole city is threaded with these encounters. Information moving through the population like blood through capillaries.

---

The ones who work the growing edges are larger than the others, their limbs thicker, their movements slower and more deliberate. They press themselves against the reaching tendrils and stay there for hours, their bodies warming the growth-tips just enough to encourage extension. Sometimes they seem to *guide* — a limb positioned just so, redirecting a root's blind reach toward a gap that needs closing. But it's not construction, not exactly. More like suggestion. More like parenting.

They eat the dead growth. This is the closest thing to violence you witness: a tender descending on a section of pale, papery root-matter, its mouthparts (if that's the word) working with industrious patience, reducing architecture to nutrient slurry that it then — you watch, fascinated and uneasy — regurgitates into the living walls. The city consumes its own corpse. The tenders are its gut.

The small ones stay near the center, near the warmth-nodes where the air shimmers. Juveniles, you think, though their bodies show no obvious immaturity — just scale, just caution. They move in clusters of three or four, practicing the touch-language on each other, sometimes getting it wrong in ways that make the adults intervene with a gentle redirecting limb. They are learning the city. Learning how to speak it, how to read its needs, how to be part of its metabolism.

---

One of them finds you on your second day.

You've been still for hours, breathing shallow in the cold, watching a new chamber take shape. You don't hear it approach — they make no sound, these creatures, or none that registers above the glacier's groaning — but suddenly it's *there*, a body's length away, its forelimbs extended toward you in what might be greeting or threat or simple investigation.

It touches your hand. The contact is dry, cool, textured like bark. Its limbs move across your skin in patterns you can't interpret, asking questions you have no way to answer. You stay frozen, afraid to breathe, and it continues its examination with what feels like patience, mapping the alien geography of you.

Then it withdraws.

It doesn't flee, doesn't recoil. It simply folds itself back into the root-wall and resumes whatever task you interrupted. You're not food. You're not threat. You're not, apparently, interesting enough to report.

But that night — or what passes for night here, when the glacier-light dims to near-black — you feel them moving around you. Not approaching. Just... aware. The city has noted your presence. The tenders are deciding what you mean.

---

They have no names. They have no leaders. They have no *selves*, perhaps, in any way you'd recognize — just roles, just functions, just the endless collaborative project of keeping the city alive. But watch long enough and you begin to see individuals anyway. This one moves with a slight hesitation, favoring one side. That one tends the same section of wall obsessively, returning to it again and again. The juveniles have preferences, clusters they stay near, adults they follow.

Something like personality emerges from pure function. Something like love might live in the way they touch each other, in the three-second conversations, in the patient warmth they offer the growing roots.

They are not building a city. They are *being* a city. The architecture and the architects have grown so entangled that the distinction no longer holds. When the roots reach and weave and fuse, it is because the tenders have made it possible. When the tenders move and feed and speak, it is because the roots have given them a world to move through.

You are inside something's idea of shelter.

You are inside something's idea of *themselves*.
