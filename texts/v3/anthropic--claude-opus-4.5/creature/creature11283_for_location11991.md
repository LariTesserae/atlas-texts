**The Keeper**

She has no name she remembers, though she knows she had one once. The sound of it is gone—dissolved like the labels on the pipes, like the purpose of the pen that still hangs on the wall. What remains is function. She is the one who stays.

Her body is not what it was. Centuries in the humid envelope have made her part of the envelope. Her skin has taken on the blue-green undertone of the leaves, chloroplasts or something like them having migrated into her dermis through some process she did not choose and does not understand. She photosynthesizes, a little. Not enough to live on, but enough that she feels the dimming of the algae tubes as a kind of hunger, and their brightening as a kind of relief. Her hair—she thinks she had hair—has been replaced by something finer, something that waves in the steam currents and catches moisture, funneling it toward her scalp where it absorbs directly. She does not drink so much as *receive*.

She eats the crabs when she can catch them. They are not filling, but they are constant. She has learned to stand motionless near the glass, her hand open and still, until one of them investigates the salt on her palm. The crunch is the loudest sound she makes most days.

The eels know her. They wind between her ankles when she wades into the flooded section, not hunting her, not fleeing—just acknowledging. She is too large to eat and too familiar to fear. Sometimes she catches one and holds it, feeling its muscle pulse against her palms, then lets it go. She is not sure why she does this. It feels like practice for something.

---

She sleeps in the chair. Her body has worn those grooves in the seat, though she does not remember the wearing. She wakes to the rhythm of the pumps and orients herself by it—the way the beat catches slightly every thirty-seventh cycle, a hiccup in the machinery that has been there longer than her memory. When the hiccup comes, she knows where she is. She knows *when* she is, in a way that has nothing to do with clocks.

The tally marks on the table are hers. She made them in the early years, when she still thought someone might come. Then she stopped, not because she lost hope but because the counting started to feel like a door she kept opening onto the same empty room. The marks remain. She runs her fingers over them sometimes, reading them like a language she used to speak.

She tends the plants, though "tends" is too active a word. She observes them. She notices when a vine has reached a new strut, when a leaf has finally pressed flat against the glass. She knows which roots have found the condensation traps and which are still searching. Occasionally she moves a tendril, redirecting it toward water it would have found anyway, and she is not sure if this is maintenance or loneliness.

The moths land on her when she is still enough. They taste her skin with their feet, finding salt, finding nothing else of interest. She has tried to count them—there are seventeen, she thinks, though it is hard to be certain when they spiral together near the lights. She has watched generations of them hatch and fly and die and turn to dust that settles on the leaves. She does not know how many generations. She stopped counting those too.

---

She knows the large thing.

Not its name—she does not think it has one—but its presence, its pressure, the specific quality of its curiosity. When it comes, she goes to the glass and places her palm against the cold surface, and sometimes, on the other side, a vast darkness resolves into texture: skin like weathered stone, an eye the size of her torso, clouded with age but still seeing. They look at each other. She does not know what it thinks. She does not know what she thinks. But there is recognition, the way there is recognition between two things that have outlasted everything else.

She has measured the dimples in the glass with her fingers. She knows they are deeper than they were. She knows what this means. She does not repair them because she cannot, and she does not mourn them because mourning requires an expectation of loss, and she has lived too long to expect anything.

When the thing presses close, she feels the cold of it through the glass, the immense chill of the deep water just inches away. She presses back. The glass creaks. The structure groans its ancient sigh. And then the thing withdraws, and the warmth returns, and she goes back to the chair and waits for the thirty-seventh beat to tell her she is still here.

---

She remembers, in fragments, that she was not always alone.

There were others. She sees them sometimes in the rust patterns, in the shape of a stain that looks like a shoulder, in the way the light falls on the empty hooks. They left or they died or they were never real—she cannot distinguish between these possibilities anymore, and she has stopped trying. What matters is that she stayed. What matters is that staying became what she is.

She does not know why she remains. The chamber does not need her. The pumps would cycle without her witness. The plants would grow toward the light whether she watched or not. The crabs, the eels, the moths—they have their own purposes, complete without her.

But she thinks, sometimes, that she is the chamber's way of knowing itself. That without her there to notice the drip and the hiss and the beat and the press of ancient flesh against ancient glass, it would all still happen but it would not *mean*. And perhaps meaning is not necessary. Perhaps the greenhouse heart would continue just as well as pure mechanism, mindless and perfect.

But she is here. And so it is witnessed. And so it means.

---

She is waiting, though she could not tell you for what.

Not rescue—that door closed so long ago she cannot find the frame. Not death—she is not sure she can die anymore, not in any way she would recognize. Perhaps she is waiting for the seal to fail, the vents to shift, the large thing to press with final conviction. Perhaps she is waiting to see what comes after the warmth flees and the cold enters.

Or perhaps she is not waiting at all. Perhaps she is simply being, in the same way the pumps are being, the glass is being, the light is being. Continuation without purpose. Presence without intent.

The steam rises. The leaves unfurl. The eels kiss the surface. The moths spiral toward something they have never seen and will never understand.

And she sits in the chair that has learned her shape, her hand resting on tally marks she no longer reads, her blue-green skin catching the light that is not sunlight, her breath matching the rhythm of the pumps—

beat, beat, beat, hiccup—

and she continues.

Because that is what she knows how to do.
