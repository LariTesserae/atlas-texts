She has been here so long that the moisture has found her bones.

You might not see her at first. She is crouched in a maintenance alcove between Vats Thirty-One and Thirty-Two, her body folded into a shape that suggests both vigil and root system. Her skin has taken on the grey-green patina of the pipes she tends—not illness, but adaptation, the way copper learns verdigris. Her fingernails are thick and ridged, growing in the way of slow mineral deposits. When she moves, which is rarely, her joints click with the sound of settling stone.

Her name was something else, once. Now she answers to "Tender" when the brass speaking-tubes occasionally crackle with requests from the offices above, and she answers to nothing when the requests stop. She does not remember being hired. She remembers, vaguely, being young in the way that things outside this room are young—quick and dry and temporary. That was before she understood what patience meant. Before she learned to feel the subsonic murmur not as sound but as grammar, as a language the growing places speak to those willing to slow down enough to hear.

She knows every vat the way a mother knows the particular weight of her children. Vat Seventeen's weeping cliff—she has been adjusting its mineral feed for three months, coaxing out the precise iron content that will let it rust in beautiful streaks. The cliff does not know it is meant to be beautiful. It only knows it is becoming, and she helps it become. She speaks to it, sometimes, in a voice that has grown so quiet it is almost subvocal, almost geological itself. *There,* she murmurs, *there, yes, that's the fold, that's where your water will want to go.*

The other attendant—the one you see on the lower walkway, the one still quick, still dry, still wearing clothes that have not begun to fuse with their skin—that one is new. Tender has watched perhaps a dozen of them cycle through. They come eager and careful, and they leave after a few years, and she does not blame them. Some people are not meant to slow down. Some people need sunlight, need seasons, need the world to change around them. She needed that too, once.

Now she needs the vats.

Her meals arrive through a small dumbwaiter near the alcove, though she is increasingly uncertain who sends them or how often. Time has become thick here, sluggish, moving at the pace of calcium accumulation. She eats when she remembers eating. She sleeps in stretches that might be hours or might be days, curled in the warmth between vats, dreaming of mountain ranges she has midwifed through their infancy and will never see full-grown. Somewhere in the world outside, there is an estate with a perfect volcanic hot spring that she nursed through its first thermal convulsions. Somewhere there is a private garden with a granite outcropping whose every striation she adjusted by hand, feeding it iron, feeding it quartz, teaching it how to catch the afternoon light. She will never visit these places. They have left her, as children leave, and she has let them go.

The ferns have begun to grow into her alcove. She does not discourage them. One has sent a tendril across her left shoulder, and she has let it anchor there, feeling its small slow hunger for the moisture in the air. The moss has softened everything around her—her sleeping mat, her tools, the edges of her body where it meets the world. She is becoming part of the system. She has been becoming part of the system for years. Perhaps one day someone will find her fully integrated, her bonesite calcium feeding back into the nutrient loops, her slow heart finally synchronized with the pulse of the pumps.

She does not fear this. She does not long for it either. She simply tends.

When she rises—slowly, carefully, her spine articulating in a series of soft clicks—it is because Vat Forty-Three needs her. The archipelago. The one still deciding what kind of stone to become. She has been watching it struggle with the choice for weeks, and she thinks it might be ready to commit to basalt. It has been flirting with limestone, seduced by the idea of caves and hidden spaces, but she knows—she *knows*, with a certainty that has seeped into her the way mineral knowledge seeps into growing rock—that basalt is its true nature. Volcanic heritage. The memory of heat.

She walks the grated pathway, her footsteps barely audible beneath the hiss and drip and murmur. The vines brush her shoulders like old friends. The steam curls around her and she breathes it in, tastes the copper and iron and calcium, feels the places-in-progress all around her pulsing with their slow becoming.

At Vat Forty-Three, she presses her palm against the warm glass. Inside, the infant islands drift in their nutrient suspension, still translucent, still soft. She watches the way they hold their heat. The way their cores are beginning to darken, beginning to commit.

*Basalt,* she murmurs. *You want to remember fire. I know. I know.*

She reaches for the valve that controls the mineral feed, and begins to give the archipelago what it needs to become what it already is.
