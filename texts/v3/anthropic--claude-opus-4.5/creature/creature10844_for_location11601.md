**The Listener**

She has no name she'll tell you. She might have no name at all—might have arrived here before she had one, or lost it in the years of steam and frog-song, or simply decided that names are for places where people need to be distinguished from one another.

She is small and dark and so still that you will not see her the first time you enter the hollow. Or the second. She sits in a root-alcove near the ceiling, her body folded into the same patient compression as the snake, and she watches. Her skin has taken on the humidity, gone perpetually damp and faintly gleaming. Her hair, which she does not cut, hangs in ropes that have begun to resemble the aerial roots around her. She could not tell you how long ago she stopped being a visitor.

She was something once. A pipe-walker, maybe—one of the maintenance crews who were supposed to chart the venting points and never did. Or a runaway from one of the refinery towns, fleeing into the root-mass without a destination. Or a child who crawled through the strangler-fig gap on a dare and found that the hollow asked nothing of her, and that this was the first time anything had asked nothing of her, and that she could not make herself leave.

She eats what the hollow provides. Frog eggs, sometimes, though she takes few and only from the pools with too many. Beetles that die of natural causes. The land-crab tolerates her the way it tolerates the root-walls—as a feature, immovable, neither threat nor food. She drinks the condensation that gathers in the root-cups, and it tastes like rust and growing things, and her body has long since stopped objecting to the mineral traces.

---

She listens.

That is the thing she does, the purpose she has grown into like the moss grows into the crab's shell. She listens to the hollow.

Not just the frogs—though she knows their chorus so intimately she can hear when a single voice goes missing, can tell from the shift in pitch when the breeding pools are too warm or too cool. She listens past the frogs to the snake's breathing, slow and oceanic. She listens to the beetles' clicking footsteps on the root-walls, to the millipedes' ceaseless processing of decay, to the creak and settle of the plant-architecture adjusting to its own weight.

She listens, most of all, to the fissure.

The steam-breath has rhythms she has learned to read. Pressure shifts in the deep machinery translate into changes in temperature, in moisture, in the mineral composition of what rises. She cannot name the mechanisms—has never seen a pump or a pressure valve—but she knows them the way the frogs know the season for eggs. She knows when the system is running smooth, when something far below is straining, when the great industrial heartbeat skips or stutters.

She has heard it falter, twice, in all her years. Once the steam went thin and cool for three days, and the frogs clustered tight around the fissure and sang in confused distress, and she held her breath until the warmth returned. Once it surged hot, hotter than it should be, and she pressed herself against the far root-wall and watched the frog-elders die and the young ones crowd into the cooler margins, and she did not know if this was the end of the hollow's long stability, and then it passed.

Both times, she knew before the frogs did. She felt the shift in the deep hum, the change in the rhythm that had become her own heartbeat.

---

She does not speak to visitors. There are visitors, sometimes—every few years, someone finds the gap in the strangler figs, crawls through, emerges into the green-gold steam. They exclaim at the frogs. They peer at the snake. They do not see her.

She prefers this. Their presence agitates the ecosystem, sends ripples through the frog-song, makes the beetles retreat and the snake coil tighter. They bring outside air on their skin, outside sounds in their voices, outside time in their urgency to see and leave and tell someone what they've seen.

She waits, and they leave, and the hollow settles back into itself.

Once, a man in a maintenance uniform crawled through with a clipboard and a device that clicked and measured. He was charting venting point 7-K, finally, after all the years of "scheduled maintenance: indefinite." He took readings from the fissure. He frowned at the temperature—too hot for the pipes, or too cool, she couldn't tell. He made notes about root incursion, about potential blockage, about recommending excavation to access the source.

She dropped from her alcove while his back was turned and opened his throat with a blade she'd made from crab-shell years ago, for purposes she hadn't understood until that moment.

The millipedes took three weeks to process him. The hollow holds no trace. The maintenance report was never filed.

---

She is not a guardian, exactly. The hollow does not need guarding—it has persisted without her, will persist without her. She is more like an extension of its patience, a piece of the ecosystem that happens to have hands and memory and the capacity to act when action is required.

She does not know what she would do if the steam stopped. She does not know what she will do when she dies. She hopes the frogs will eat her eyes first—they're soft, and the small pale bodies deserve softness. She hopes the millipedes will take their time with the rest, that she'll spend years becoming the dark crumbling matter between the roots, that she'll never fully leave.

When she sleeps, she hears the deep machinery in her dreams. The pumps cycle. The pressure builds and releases. She has never seen the refineries three valleys over, never will, but she knows them like she knows her own lungs: as systems that breathe, that labor, that could fail at any moment but haven't yet, won't yet, will keep breathing their warm mineral breath into the hollow that depends on them.

She is listening, always.

The hollow is complete, and she is part of its completion, and that is enough. That is everything. That is the only thing she has ever wanted without knowing she wanted it: to be necessary to something that asks nothing of her except presence.

She remains.
