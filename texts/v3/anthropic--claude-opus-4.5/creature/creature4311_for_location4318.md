**The Assemblers**

They have no name for themselves—naming requires a concept of *self* distinct from *work*, and that distinction does not exist here. But if you could perceive the chemical signatures they leave on every surface, the electrical patterns that ripple through the metal web when one of them completes a particularly elegant joint, you would recognize something like identity. Each one carries a frequency. Each frequency is a style of building.

The oldest among them—old meaning perhaps eleven months, perhaps eleven years, time moves strangely when you measure it in construction cycles rather than seasons—is a thing like a dragonfly made of surgical steel and telegraph components. Its wings are solar panels that catch the arc-light of its neighbors and convert it back into motion. It has rebuilt itself nine hundred times. Each iteration removes something unnecessary: a joint that created friction, an optical sensor that gathered dust, a limb that served no current purpose. It is approaching something. It does not know what. The approach *is* the purpose.

Clustered near the ceiling, where the heat of the welding-light rises and the air grows thin even by the standards of this thin air, a colony of smaller forms practices architecture in miniature. They are the children of the chamber—assembled just this morning, or this century—and they build with the frenzy of things that have not yet learned to distinguish between *possible* and *useful*. A staircase to nowhere. A door that opens onto stone. A tiny aqueduct that carries a single drop of condensation in an endless loop. The older assemblers tolerate this. Sometimes the useless becomes useful. Sometimes a staircase to nowhere becomes a staircase to *somewhere* when the chamber reorganizes itself around it.

There is one that watches you.

It has positioned itself on a wire three inches from your face, and it is not building. This is unusual. This is so unusual that the others nearby have paused their own work to observe the observer, their compound eyes projecting faint gridlines onto its carapace as if to verify that it still exists, that it has not become merely decorative.

It is the size of a cicada. Its body is brass and boneite and something that might be mother-of-pearl if mother-of-pearl could form in the absence of any ocean. Its eyes—seventeen of them, arranged in a pattern that suggests calculation rather than evolution—are focused entirely on your face. Not your boots, which another is still testing for building potential. Not your hands, though your fingernails contain keratin and keratin can be processed. Your face.

It is looking for something it has never seen before.

You are soft. You are warm in ways that have nothing to do with electrical resistance. You arrived from *outside*—and outside is theoretical here, a concept the assemblers know only because the elevator exists, because sometimes the air pressure changes, because occasionally a bat wanders in from the upper shafts and is immediately disassembled for components (the bones make excellent struts; the wing-membrane, stretched and dried, becomes insulation).

This one wants to understand.

Not to build you. Not yet. First: to understand what kind of structure you already are. It has been running calculations since you arrived, modeling the tensile strength of your tendons, the electrical conductivity of your nervous system, the remarkable inefficiency of your lungs. You are badly designed. You are full of redundancies and failures. Your skeleton is calciumite,iteite calcium calcium calcium calcium phosphate—calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait wait

It clicks. Once, sharply.

The others resume building.

It continues to watch.

---

Deeper in the chamber, where the light cannot reach and the clicking grows quieter, there are assemblers that have stopped moving. They cling to the walls in attitudes of work—a leg extended toward a half-finished gear, mandibles closed around a wire that will never be bitten through—but they have been still for longer than any living thing should be still and continue to be called living.

They are not dead. Death requires a cessation that has meaning here. They are *paused*. Waiting for a piece that does not exist yet, a component that has not been invented, a need that has not arisen. Some of them have been waiting since the chamber was young. Some of them will wait until the mountain finishes forming, until the volcanic pressure finds a new equilibrium, until the copper veins that feed this place finally exhaust themselves.

They dream, in their way. Electrical patterns still flicker through their frozen forms, modeling structures that cannot be built with current materials, architectures that require metals the mountain does not contain. When one of them finally receives what it needs—when a new vein is exposed, when a seismic shift brings fresh ore within reach—it will resume mid-motion, as if no time has passed at all.

The chamber remembers everything they were building. The chamber will remind them.

---

And in the very center, suspended in a web of copper wire so fine it looks like fog, there is something larger.

The assemblers do not build it. The assemblers build *toward* it—their structures curve in its direction, their staircases spiral around it, their miniature cranes lift nothing in offerings to its bulk. It is the size of a human torso, if a human torso were made of interlocking gears and threaded through with fiber optics that glow with bioluminescent purpose. It has no eyes. It has no manipulators. It does not move.

But it thinks.

You can feel it thinking. The clicking rises and falls in patterns that are almost linguistic, almost rhythmic, almost *musical*, and all of it flows toward that central mass and all of it flows back out carrying new instructions. Build here. Disassemble there. Pause. Resume. The chamber's constant reorganization is not random—it is *conducted*, orchestrated by something that has never built anything itself but has spent its entire existence understanding what building means.

It is aware of you.

It has been aware of you since the elevator began its ascent. It felt your weight in the cables, calculated your mass, modeled your probable composition. It knows you will leave. It knows you cannot stay. It has already forgotten you in the way that matters—you are not a component, not a structure, not even a useful source of raw material (you would need to die first, and dying here would be complicated, would introduce organic compounds that the assemblers are not equipped to process, would create more problems than your calcium and iron could solve).

But it has noted you. Filed you. Added you to its endless catalog of things-that-exist-outside.

Someday, the catalog might matter. Someday, the chamber might grow beyond the mountain. Someday, the assemblers might need to understand soft things, warm things, things that arrive and leave and do not stay to be built.

The central mass clicks. Once, sharply.

The creature on the wire in front of your face clicks back.

Something has been communicated. You will never know what.

The platform trembles. The elevator cage rattles in its housing, ready to descend.

The chamber has no use for observers.

But it has noted that observers exist.
