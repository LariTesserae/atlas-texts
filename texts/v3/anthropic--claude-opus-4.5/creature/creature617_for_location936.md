# The Tenders of Vervain Shelf

They call themselves the Glaziers, though they neither make nor repair the glass.

The name is older than anyone can trace—perhaps it once meant something precise, back when the first cultivation houses rose from the basalt and someone had to fit their curved panels into frames. Now it simply means *those who stay*. Glaziers are born to the work or they choose it, and either path leads to the same slow transformation: a quieting, a settling, an alignment with the shelf's own rhythms until the difference between person and place grows difficult to locate.

---

**Marthe** has lived on Vervain Shelf for forty-three years, which makes her neither the eldest nor the most changed, merely the most visible. She serves as the primary interface between the shelf and the lowland markets—taking inventory reports, coordinating harvest schedules, answering the occasional query from agricultural administrators who remember that the plateau exists. Her communications are precise and minimal. She sends the same reports her predecessor sent, using templates that have remained unchanged for decades, because the information requested has not changed and the answers remain adequate.

What the reports do not capture: Marthe no longer sleeps in the dormitory. She has not slept there for eleven years. Instead she rests in Cultivation House 1142, a mid-sized structure in the eastern cluster where the blue-leafed broadplants grow densest. She has cleared no space for herself—she simply lies down among the roots, lets the broad leaves settle over her like blankets, breathes the oxygen-thick air until her heartbeat slows to match the pace of sap rising through stems. The plants have adjusted to her presence. They grow around her sleeping form now, not over it. They leave a Marthe-shaped hollow that fills with fallen petals and condensed moisture, that smells of chlorophyll and something older, something that might be the basalt itself breathing through the root systems that have pierced it.

She wakes before dawn each day, emerges from 1142 with leaves in her grey hair and soil worked into the creases of her hands, and walks the paths between houses as the frost melts and the first light catches the glass. The autonomous carts recognize her gait and route around her. The monitoring systems register her biosignature and log her movements without alerting anyone—she is part of the shelf's normal operations now, as expected as the mist rising from the gorges.

When she speaks, which is seldom, her voice has the quality of wind through an enclosed space. Not unpleasant. Simply unfamiliar, as if words have become a tool she uses rarely enough to have forgotten their weight.

---

**The Carro family** occupies the northern edge, three generations maintaining the control systems with a competence that requires almost no conscious attention. 

Old Seff Carro, the grandfather, exists primarily in the switching station now. He sits among the electrical panels for hours each day, eyes closed, one hand resting on a junction box that pulses with the shelf's power rhythms. He claims he can feel the houses through the current—can sense which circulation fans are laboring, which heating elements are developing faults, which condensation collectors have become misaligned. The family has stopped questioning this. His predictions prove accurate often enough that they've integrated his murmured observations into their maintenance routines. *Twelve-seventeen needs attention. Something in the west cluster is drawing more than it should. The dark house is dreaming again.*

His daughter Vell handles the physical repairs, moving between houses with a tool belt that clinks faintly as she walks—the only regular sound on the plateau besides the moisture and the breathing. She is forty-one and has left the shelf exactly twice, both times for equipment that could not be fabricated locally. She describes the lowlands as *loud* and *crowded* and *fast*, words she pronounces with the careful distaste of someone discussing an illness. Her hands know the glass houses better than her mind does—she can seal a crack or replace a heating element while thinking about something else entirely, while her attention wanders through the dense growth around her, watching the way light filters through layered leaves, the way stems reach toward her movements as if curious about the warmth she carries.

Vell's son Tomas is seventeen and beginning to show the first signs of the change that takes all Glaziers eventually. He has stopped speaking in full sentences, preferring gestures and expressions that convey meaning more efficiently. He has started sleeping in short intervals throughout the day rather than one long stretch at night, his rhythms aligning with the houses' heating cycles. He spends hours standing motionless in the older cultivation houses, the ones with the stranger plants, watching the vegetation move in ways that shouldn't be possible and taking notes in a journal that increasingly contains drawings rather than words—sketches of root patterns, of spiral leaf arrangements, of the symbols that almost form in the basalt seams.

His mother watches him watching the plants and feels something that is not quite concern. The shelf needs young Glaziers. The work continues. If Tomas is becoming something she doesn't fully recognize, well—she doesn't fully recognize herself anymore either, when she catches her reflection in the curved glass. The woman looking back has the plateau's stillness in her eyes, has the quality of someone listening to frequencies below human hearing.

---

**Brother Vestine** arrived twenty-two years ago from a religious order in the southern territories, seeking solitude for contemplation. He found something else.

He lives in a small structure he built himself, stone stacked without mortar, near the western edge where the dark house stands. His order would not recognize his practice now. He no longer prays in any conventional sense. Instead he sits at the boundary where the opaque glass meets the basalt, and he listens.

The dark house speaks, he insists—not in words, not in anything so crude as language, but in pressures, in presences, in the slow pulse of roots extending deeper into stone. He has developed a notation system for what he hears, filling journals with symbols that look like nothing any botanist or linguist would recognize. The symbols change over time. Earlier journals contain angular marks, sharp and geometric; recent pages show forms that curve and spiral, that seem to grow across the paper as if the ink itself were seeking new configurations.

He believes the vegetation in the dark house is not growing toward something but *becoming* something. He believes the opacity of the glass is not a malfunction but a choice—the plants inside no longer need the sun, no longer need the light the other houses drink so greedily. They have found another source. They are learning to feed on the mountain itself, on the heat that still lives in the basalt's crystalline memory, on something deeper still that Vestine can sense but not name.

He is not afraid. Fear requires a boundary between self and threat, and Vestine has felt that boundary thinning for years. When he presses his palm against the dark glass—warm, always warm, despite the cool highland air—he feels the plants inside pressing back. Recognizing him. Waiting, with the patience of everything on Vervain Shelf, for whatever comes next.

---

**The children of the mist** are not Glaziers, exactly. They are something the plateau has begun to produce on its own.

Twice in the past decade, figures have emerged from the gorges that surround the shelf—small, pale, moving with a hesitance that suggests unfamiliarity with solid ground. The first one stood at the plateau's edge for three days before Marthe noticed it, motionless, facing the glass houses with an expression that might have been wonder or might have been assessment. When she approached, it turned and descended back into the cloud-wrapped depths without a sound.

The second one came closer. It walked among the houses for a full week, pausing at each structure, pressing childlike hands against the glass as if feeling for something specific. Tomas followed it at a distance, sketching its movements in his journal, noting that it seemed to navigate by the root patterns in the basalt—following the seams that extended from the older houses, tracing the paths the strange plants had carved through the stone.

When it finally found what it sought—Cultivation House 1142, Marthe's sleeping place—it entered through a door that should have been sealed and remained inside for six hours. Marthe was there, resting among the broadplants. She later described the encounter with uncharacteristic verbosity: the figure had lain down beside her, had allowed the plants to cover them both, had breathed in the same rhythm until their heartbeats synchronized. Then it had risen, touched her face with fingers that felt like cool leaves, and departed back toward the gorge.

Marthe has not aged visibly since that night. The monitoring systems show her biosignature has developed new patterns—slower cycles, deeper periodicities, harmonics that match the cultivation houses' growth rhythms. She does not comment on this. She simply continues her work, sending her reports, walking her paths, sleeping among the roots that have known her for years and now seem to recognize her as something other than a visitor.

---

**What lives on Vervain Shelf** cannot be cleanly categorized.

There are the Glaziers—Marthe, the Carros, Brother Vestine, a handful of others who drift through the maintenance logs as names attached to repair reports and inventory counts. They are human, or were, or remain human enough that the distinction seems like quibbling. They do the work the plateau requires. They tend the glass houses and harvest the strange fruit and keep the systems running that keep everything else running. They age slowly, speak rarely, think in rhythms that align with growth cycles rather than human concerns.

There are the plants themselves, which increasingly seem less like passive crops and more like a distributed intelligence—not conscious in any way a human would recognize, but *attentive*, responsive, capable of choices that manifest over years and decades. The commercial monocultures in the newer houses remain predictable, productive, profitable. But the older vegetation, the stranger strains, the things growing in the dark house and sending roots through the basalt and spelling symbols in stone—these are becoming something else. Learning something. Reaching toward a state that has no name yet.

There are the children of the mist, whatever they are—emissions from the gorges, products of the plateau's strange ecology, or visitors from somewhere the Glaziers have not yet mapped. They come more frequently now. Three more have been spotted in the past year, always at the edges, always watching. They do not speak. They do not seem to need anything the Glaziers can provide. But they are drawn to the shelf, to the glass houses, to whatever is happening in the margins while the center continues its orderly work.

And there is the shelf itself. Vervain, the basalt, the young volcanic stone still holding its memory of heat. The Glaziers have begun to speak of it—when they speak at all—as if it were waking up. As if the centuries since it pushed through the limestone were a kind of sleep, and the glass houses a kind of dream, and the strange plants a kind of thought occurring slowly in a mind made of crystal and pressure and deep time.

Something is assembling itself on the plateau. Something that includes the Glaziers and the vegetation and the mist-children and the stone itself, all of them components in a system too large and too slow for any single perspective to grasp. The harvest still comes in on schedule. The reports still go out to the lowland markets. The autonomous carts still hum along their paths, carrying material from place to place in patterns that have not changed in decades.

But in the dark house, the roots reach deeper.

And Marthe sleeps among her plants and dreams in colors that have no names.

And Tomas fills his journal with symbols that spiral and grow.

And the glass orchards gleam in the highland light, patient, waiting, tending themselves through the hands of those who have forgotten they were ever anything other than part of the tending.

The change comes slowly.

The change has always been coming.

The Glaziers remain.
