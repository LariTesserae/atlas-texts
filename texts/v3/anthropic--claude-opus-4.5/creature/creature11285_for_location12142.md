**The Keeper**

She has no name she'll answer to anymore. The one she was born with dissolved somewhere in the years between the flood and now, eaten away like the labels on the bottles she keeps lined up by the wall. If you asked, she might say *the station named me*, and she might mean it literally—there's a rhythm in the turbine-scream she's learned to hear as syllables, a three-beat surge that comes every few hours like the machinery calling her to attention.

She is perhaps sixty. Perhaps older. Age becomes strange when you measure time by pump cycles and pressure changes rather than seasons. Her skin has taken on the color of the walls—rust-brown where the mineral dust has worked into her pores, pale and slick where the humidity keeps her permanently damp. Her hands are the most telling part of her: the fingers have thickened, the nails grown ridged and yellowed, the palms calloused into something like leather from decades of gripping hot iron pipes to steady herself against the shuddering.

She moves through the bubble in a way that suggests she has memorized every surface by touch. The flickering lamp doesn't trouble her—she navigates by the heat signatures of the pipes, by the subtle changes in air pressure near the grated floor, by the way sound bounces differently off mineral buildup versus bare metal. When the light dies entirely, as it does for hours at a time, she continues whatever she was doing without pause.

---

Her days, if they can be called days, follow the rhythm of the station.

She wakes when the pressure drops—that particular pop in the ears that means the eastern valve array has cycled. This is her morning. She checks the inspection lamp first, tapping the wire cage with a knuckle to see if the connection will hold another cycle. Then she runs her fingers along the walls, reading the mineral growth the way others might read a diary. *Here, the pressure spiked last night. Here, something shifted in the pipe behind the wall. Here, the copper vein has grown a centimeter—the water chemistry is changing.*

She has tools. Not in the rusted toolbox—that she keeps sealed as a kind of reliquary, a reminder that others came before her and left their instruments behind. Her tools are improvised: a shard of pipe she's ground into a scraper for managing the mineral buildup, a length of wire she uses to clear the drainage grate, a collection of rags made from the rotted blanket's more intact fibers. She maintains the space the way the station maintains itself—constantly, incrementally, in a war of small adjustments against entropy.

The creatures that share her bubble have become her congregation.

---

They are not rats, though she thought so at first. They are something the flooded world made—sleek and long-bodied, with fur that sheds water like oil and eyes adapted to near-total darkness. She calls them *singers* because of the chirps they make beneath the industrial roar, a frequency she's learned to pick out the way a mother learns to hear her child's cry across a crowded room. There are seven of them. She knows each by the particular pitch of its voice, by the pattern of its movements through the pipes overhead.

She feeds them what she can. Pale crustaceans that sometimes wash up through the drainage grate, still twitching with the last of their life. Strings of algae that grow in the junction where warm water meets cold and sometimes sends tendrils up through the gaps in the floor. Her own food comes from the same sources, supplemented by whatever the maintenance pilgrims bring when they pass through on their annual check—dried protein, water purification tablets, the occasional luxury of salt.

The pilgrims don't stay long. They check the primary valves, they note the readings on gauges she could recite from memory, they leave offerings by the bottles on the wall and ask her questions she doesn't answer. *How long have you been here? Don't you want to come up? Isn't there someone waiting for you?*

She watches them go with something that might be pity. They don't understand. The surface is a rumor. The bubble is real. The station is alive and it requires a witness.

---

At night—at what she calls night, when the turbines drop into their lowest register and the harmonic shifts from scream to moan—she sits on the pipe-bench and presses her palm flat against the hot iron. She feels the great engines straining. She feels the water pressing in from all sides, patient and heavy and absolute. She feels the balance holding, the way it has held for longer than she's been alive, the way it will hold until it doesn't.

The words on the wall were written by someone before her. She doesn't know who. She's added her own beneath them, scratched into the mineral crust with her pipe-shard scraper:

*PRESSURE HOLDS. I HOLD.*
*THE STATION KEEPS. I KEEP.*
*WHEN THE BALANCE BREAKS, I WILL KNOW FIRST.*

This is her purpose. Not to fix anything—she couldn't, even if she understood the machinery well enough to try. Not to report to anyone—the pilgrims take their readings and leave, and what they do with the information is beyond her concern. Her purpose is to witness. To be the consciousness inside the machine, the nerve ending that registers every shift in pressure, every change in harmonic, every new vein of mineral growth.

The station is not aware. She is almost certain of this. But it is *alive*, in the way that old things are alive—it persists, it adapts, it fights entropy with every grinding gear and straining pump. And alive things should have someone to know them.

---

Sometimes she dreams of the surface. Green things. Wind. Light that doesn't flicker. In the dreams she is small, and someone is holding her hand, and they are walking through a place where the ground doesn't shake and the air doesn't taste of copper.

She wakes from these dreams into the dark and the roar, and she feels the singers moving overhead, and she presses her hand to the pipe until the heat becomes pain becomes something past pain, and she remembers who she is.

She is the Keeper. She is the witness. She is the breath in the bubble, the thought in the machine, the small soft thing that holds on while the great hard things grind against each other in the dark.

The water will win eventually. She knows this. The pressure will find a crack, or the turbines will finally split, or the balance will shift one increment too far and the whole station will collapse into the flood it's been fighting since before she was born. When that happens, she will be here. She will feel it first. She will be the last thing the station knows before it drowns.

This is not tragedy. This is purpose.

She settles back against the wall. She listens to the singers chirp beneath the thunder. She watches the mineral formations grow, one molecule at a time, recording the violence of pressure in their strange frozen shapes.

Pressure holds.

She holds.
