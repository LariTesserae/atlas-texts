She comes when the settlement above has been silent for three days.

This is not a rule anyone taught her—there is no one left to teach—but her body knows it the way her hands know the grinding motion, the way her knees know the sideways descent. Three days of silence means the traders have moved on, means the herders have taken their animals to the eastern graze, means the old woman who sometimes sits in the doorway of the collapsed granary has wandered back to wherever she wanders. Three days of silence means she can be alone with what she does not understand.

She is perhaps fifty, perhaps older. The sun and wind have made her face a territory of fine lines, and her hands are mapped with the same weathering—but her movements through the crack are practiced, almost liquid, the efficiency of a body that has made this descent hundreds of times. She carries a small leather pouch, darkened with years of handling, and nothing else.

Inside, she folds herself into a crouch that her joints accept without complaint. This is her shape here. This is how she fits.

---

She does not know why she comes.

This is important: she has never known. Her mother brought her here first when she was young enough that the descent frightened her, young enough that she cried at the closeness of the walls, and her mother said nothing then either. Did not explain. Simply showed her the grinding of the mineral powder—from a seam in the ruins that only they knew, or that only they remembered knowing—and the placement of the small pyramid, and the checking of the vessels.

The checking of the vessels. This is the part that feels most like meaning, though she could not say why.

She moves around the chamber's edge, finding footholds on the deposit-curtains that have been footholds for longer than the settlement above has had a name. Her fingers touch each vessel she can reach: the ancient ones, barely rims now, their bodies swallowed by the stone's slow embrace; the ones from her grandmother's time, still showing the spiral patterns that someone once pressed into wet clay; the ones her mother placed, clumsy and thick-walled, made by hands that had forgotten the old techniques but remembered that vessels were needed.

She checks their water. Whether they are full, whether they are overflowing into the vessels below, whether some shift in the seepage has left them dry. When one is dry, she feels something—not quite alarm, not quite grief, but a sensation in her chest like a held breath that has been held too long. She adjusts its position minutely, tilting it toward a wet streak on the wall. She waits until she sees the first drop gather and fall.

This matters. She does not know why this matters, but her not-knowing is itself a kind of knowledge—the knowledge that some things must be done whether or not their doing can be explained.

---

The bones trouble her.

They have always troubled her, since the first time her mother's lamp illuminated their pale arrangements on the shelf. Birds, she thinks—or thought, when she was young enough to think in such simple categories. Now she sees them differently. Now she sees how some of the bones are too large for birds, how others are too small for anything she can name, how the arrangements form patterns that almost resolve into meaning and then dissolve again when she looks directly at them.

Her mother touched them, once. Adjusted one, slightly—a wing-bone, or what might have been a wing-bone, shifting it a finger's width to the left. Her mother's face as she did this was the face of someone listening to a voice just below the threshold of hearing. Concentrating. Uncertain.

She has never touched them herself. She is not certain she is allowed. She is not certain there is anyone left to allow or forbid, but the uncertainty itself has become a rule, and she has learned that rules made of uncertainty are the hardest to break.

---

She places her pyramid now, in the space between two others that have begun to merge with the stone beneath them. The powder is pale gray,ite-adjacent,ite-descended—ground from a seam in what was once a wall of what was once a building whose purpose is no longer guessable. She grinds it in a stone bowl that lives in a niche in the ruins, a bowl that was already old when her grandmother found it, its interior worn smooth by generations of the same circular motion.

The pyramid is small. Her hands shape it automatically, tamping the powder into a form that will hold for decades before the moisture begins toite it, before the stone begins to accept it, before it becomes merely another discoloration in the pale crust.

She does not pray. There is no one left to have taught her prayers, no words that feel appropriate to this place. Instead she simply stays, crouched in the blood-warm dark, breathing air that is more water than air, and she lets the silence enter her.

This is the part she has never spoken of to anyone. This is the part she could not speak of, even if there were someone to tell: the way the silence here has texture, has *weight*, the way it presses against her not uncomfortably but attentively, the way it seems to recognize her the way she recognizes it.

The vessels drip. The walls weep. The water holds its perfect stillness.

And something in the chamber—something she has never seen, something she is not certain exists in any way that seeing could capture—something settles around her like a breath released.

---

She thinks, sometimes, that the cistern is dreaming.

Not the water, not the stone, not the minerals slowly building their curtains across millennia—but the *cistern*, the shape of intention that was carved here before intention had a name. She thinks the vessels are its dream of being tended. She thinks the bones are its dream of being understood. She thinks the pyramids of powder, hers and her mother's and all the ones that came before, are its dream of being remembered by someone, anyone, even someone who has forgotten what remembering means.

She thinks she is part of the dream now. She thinks her mother was, and her grandmother, and whoever taught her grandmother the grinding and the descent. She thinks that when she dies—alone, most likely, in one of the half-collapsed structures above, because there is no one left to tend her the way she tends this place—she thinks the cistern will go on dreaming her anyway, dreaming someone who comes when the settlement is silent, who checks the vessels, who places the small pyramids, who crouches in the blood-warm dark and lets the silence recognize her.

She does not find this thought frightening. She finds it—if she had words for what she finds it, which she does not—she finds it like the warmth of the water. Blood-temperature. Exactly.

---

There is a vessel she cannot reach.

It is high on the wall, wedged into a crevice that the deposit-curtains have narrowed to a finger's width, and it has been there longer than any of the others—she knows this the way she knows the three-day silence, the way she knows the grinding motion, which is to say she knows it in her body without knowing how her body learned. The vessel's rim is barely visible, a dark curve against the pale mineral crust, and she has never seen its interior.

She has tried, twice, to climb to it. Both times her hands found holds that crumbled, her feet found ledges that were not ledges but only the shadows of ledges in the amber light. Both times she descended, shaking, certain that she had transgressed something—not a rule, exactly, but a *boundary*, a limit that the place itself had drawn.

She thinks about that vessel often, in the ruins above. She thinks it must be older than the others, older perhaps than the carving of the chamber itself. She thinks it might have been placed here before there was a crack to descend through, before the settlement above was built, before anyone thought to build anything at all. She thinks it might be the reason for everything else—the other vessels dreaming of being it, the bones dreaming of whatever was placed inside it, the pyramids dreaming of the first pyramid that someone once placed before its unreachable rim.

She does not know what is in it. She will never know.

This not-knowing is the closest thing she has to faith.

---

When she leaves—sideways, scraping through the crack, emerging into the dusty light of the ruins—she does not look back. This too is part of the practice, though she could not say why. She simply stands, brushes the mineral dust from her clothes, and walks back to the structure where she sleeps, where she cooks her small meals over fires made from the scrub-wood that grows between the collapsed walls.

Behind her, the cistern holds its silence.

The vessels fill, drop by slow drop. The bones maintain their arrangements. The pyramids of powder begin their long dissolution into the stone. And the water, still and warm and clear to its pale bottom, reflects the fading light from the crack above—reflects it, and holds it, and gives nothing back.

She will come again when the settlement has been silent for three days.

She will come again because she comes, because her mother came, because someone must check the vessels and place the pyramids and crouch in the blood-warm dark. She will come again because the cistern dreams her coming, has always dreamed her coming, will go on dreaming her coming long after she has become merely another layer in the slow mineral conversation of the walls.

This is not a burden. This is not a calling. This is simply what is: a woman, a crack between two stones, a chamber that weeps, and the patient incomprehensible work of tending what can no longer be understood but must not yet be forgotten.
