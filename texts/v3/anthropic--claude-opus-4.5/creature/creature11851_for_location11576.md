**The Keepers of the Throat**

They are not a single species but a collaboration, though none of them would use that word.

---

**The Masons Above**

They have broad hands with splayed fingers, each digit ending in a pad of callused skin that can feel the grain of stone the way a reader feels the raised letters of a manuscript. Their eyes are set deep beneath heavy brows—protection from stone chips and the constant bright disk of sky they work beneath. When they chant, their voices come from somewhere lower than the throat, a resonance that begins in the chest cavity and emerges shaped by mouths wider than yours, with teeth worn flat from years of testing mortar with their tongues.

They live in the scaffolding itself, in hammock-nests slung between platforms, wrapped in felted moss harvested from the lower courses. They do not descend to the floor of the shaft—not out of prohibition, but because they have forgotten how. Their legs have grown strange from a life of climbing: shortened, bowed, the feet prehensile enough to grip rope and timber. If you watched one sleep, you would see them curl like a question mark, knees drawn up, fingers still twitching through the motions of setting stone.

Their children are born on the scaffolding and learn to climb before they learn to speak. The chant-language comes later, absorbed through the bones rather than taught, until a young mason can hold their part in the work-song without understanding what any word means. The meaning is in the rhythm. The rhythm is in the stone.

They eat what is hauled up in baskets from below: dense bread, dried fish, a paste of crushed seeds that they spread on everything. They drink the water that seeps through the walls, catching it in shallow bowls carved from leftover stone. They believe the water carries the mountain's intentions, that drinking it aligns their hands with what the stone wants to become.

They will not finish the tower. They know this. Their grandchildren's grandchildren might see the capstone set, or might not. The tower is not a project with an end; it is a way of being a mason. When one of them dies, the body is lowered to the floor of the shaft in a basket, and the water receives it, and eventually there is only the faint mineral outline of where a person once was, drawn in sedite on the bedrock. They consider this a form of completion.

---

**The Pale Frogs**

They came from the cloud forests on the lower slopes, carried in baskets of moss and fern used for packing between stones. The first ones were accidents. Now they are residents.

They are translucent in their youth, the shadows of their organs visible through skin that hasn't yet learned to hold pigment. As they age, they pale to the color of wet limestone, with eyes like drops of groundwater—clear, reflective, showing nothing of what moves behind them. They do not croak. They pulse. Their throat-sacs inflate and deflate in patterns that respond to the tower's acoustics, and if you watched a dozen of them for an hour, you would begin to suspect they are participating in the chant, adding a subsonic layer the masons cannot hear but perhaps can feel.

They eat the small soft things that breed in standing water: larvae, worms, the pale shrimp-like creatures that have colonized the deepest pools. They also eat the moss, though only the youngest growth, the part that tastes of chlorophyll and ambition. They are eaten in turn by the birds that sometimes dive through the aperture—kingfishers, or something like kingfishers, that have learned this shaft is a reliable hunting ground.

The frogs have no name for themselves. They have no names for anything. But they recognize the tower as a throat, and they live in it the way a song lives in a singer: temporary, necessary, already gone.

---

**The Boy Who Does Not Climb**

He is nine years old, or eleven, or some age that doesn't correspond to years the way you would count them. He has lived his entire life in the shaft, but he is not a mason's child. The masons found him in a basket at the base of the scaffolding, wrapped in a material none of them could identify—something like felt, something like membrane, something that dissolved when they tried to examine it. They raised him on the same food they eat, but he cannot digest the seed-paste, and the seep-water makes him sick. So he lives on the bread alone, supplemented by fern roots and the occasional frog he catches in the pools.

He cannot climb. His hands are wrong for it: long-fingered, graceful, the pads too soft to grip rough timber. His legs are long and straight and would be good for running if there were anywhere to run. So he stays at the bottom of the shaft, in the cold and the wet and the green, and he watches the sky-disk, and he waits.

For what? He cannot say. The masons sometimes lower baskets to him with questions scratched on slate: *Are you well? Do you need? What do you see?* He answers in a language none of them taught him, a language he writes in the sediment of the pools with his fingers, words that dissolve before anyone can read them. He is not unhappy. He has the frogs for company, and the sound of the chant, and the slow work of watching the moss creep upward, claiming another handspan of wall each season.

He talks to no one, but he is not silent. Sometimes, late at night when the masons are sleeping in their hammock-nests and the tower's voice fades to a whisper of trickling water, he opens his mouth and adds his own note to the chord. It is high and thin and human, and it rises through the shaft like something looking for a way out.

The masons hear it in their dreams. They think it is the tower, learning to sing on its own.

---

**What Will Come**

In the diagram on the wall, the one being slowly erased by seep-water, there is a room drawn at the top of the tower. It has windows facing the four directions, and in the center of the room, a shape that might be an altar or a chair or something that serves as both. The masons do not know what will live there. They are not building for a known purpose; they are building because the mountain asked them to, in the language of stone that wants to be shaped, water that wants to be channeled, height that wants to be achieved.

Something will come to live in that high room. Something is already on its way, has been on its way since the first chisel bit into bedrock, drawn by the sound of a throat learning to speak. It will arrive when the tower is ready, or before, or never. It will be the reason for all of this, or it will find a reason in what has already been built. The masons do not worry about this. The frogs do not worry. The boy at the bottom of the shaft worries, sometimes, in the way that water worries at stone—slowly, persistently, shaping something it cannot see.

For now, the tower breathes. For now, that is enough.
