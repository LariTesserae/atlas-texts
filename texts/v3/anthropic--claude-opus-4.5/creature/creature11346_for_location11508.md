**The Ones Who Became the Color of Waiting**

They have no name for themselves because naming requires a distinction between self and other, and they learned long ago that such distinctions are provisional at best. But if you pressed them—if you could find a way to press something so diffuse—they might offer a word that translates roughly as *those-who-remain-while-changing*, or perhaps *the patient*.

There are seventeen of them. Or there were seventeen, once. The number is difficult now because three of them have entered a phase of such extended stillness that it is unclear whether they are alive, dead, or have simply become a different kind of thing. They lie in shallow depressions they made decades ago by the simple pressure of not moving, their bodies half-absorbed into the mat, the plateau growing over and through them in ways that might be consumption or might be collaboration. The others visit them—if visit is the right word for a journey that takes eleven days across ground that reorganizes itself—and sit with them, and feel for the faint residual heat that would indicate continued metabolism. Sometimes they find it. Sometimes they find instead that the moss has fruited where a torso used to be, and they understand this as an answer, though not a final one.

The remaining fourteen—or thirteen, or twelve, depending on how you count the ones who have begun to merge—move through the plateau in patterns that mirror its own rhythms. They have learned to feel the swelling before it becomes visible, to sense where a trench will open by the subtle shift in the texture of the ground beneath their feet. They do not avoid these events. They position themselves to witness them, sometimes to be caught by them, because being caught is how they learn.

Their bodies are the color of the plateau because their bodies have become partly *of* the plateau. Not through any violence or invasion but through accumulation—decades of breathing the warm exhalations, of lying still while the pioneer mosses explored their skin, of eating nothing but the small pale nodules that form on certain species during certain phases of growth. Their skin has taken on a grey-green undertone. Fine filaments have rooted in the outer layers of their flesh, not parasitically but commensally, exchanging something for something, though neither party could articulate the terms. When they are very still, which is most of the time, they are nearly invisible against the surface. Only their eyes give them away—too dark, too liquid, too clearly the organs of something that looks rather than something that grows.

They do not speak often. Language requires a kind of urgency they have mostly shed. But they have not abandoned it entirely, and when they do speak, their words have the quality of observations made to no one in particular, descriptions of what is rather than requests or demands. *The eastern swell is rising faster than the others. The purple migration has stalled near the old trench scar. There is warmth in the third vent today.* They say these things aloud, and sometimes another of them is near enough to hear, and sometimes no one is, and it does not matter either way. The speaking is its own purpose.

Once, they came from somewhere else. They have a story about this, though the story has worn smooth with retelling until it is more like a shape than a narrative, more like a feeling than a sequence of events. The shape is: *there was a place of edges and separations, and it was too loud, and we walked until we found the quiet, and the quiet was here.* Some of them remember details—a city, a war, a sickness, a choice—but they do not share these details because the details belong to people they no longer are. They arrived as refugees, or pilgrims, or exiles, or simply as people who could not stop walking until the walking brought them somewhere that asked nothing of them. The plateau asked nothing. Still asks nothing. This is why they stay.

They eat little. The nodules provide something, and the condensation provides water, and beyond that their metabolisms have slowed to match the place. They sleep for days at a time, folded into the depressions their bodies make, feeling the plateau breathe beneath them. When they dream, they dream of being root, being mat, being the slow patient process of growth-and-decay that never ends and never arrives anywhere. They wake from these dreams uncertain whether they were the dreamer or the dreamed.

Reproduction is not something they think about anymore. Two of them had a child once, here, decades ago. The child lived for eleven years and then walked out across the plateau during a luminescence night and did not come back. They found the place where she had lain down, eventually, years later—a slight variation in the texture of the mat, a patch where certain species grew denser, as if fertilized by something rich. They sat with this place for a long time. They did not grieve, exactly, because grief requires a sense of loss, and loss requires a sense of possession, and they had stopped possessing anything, even each other, even their daughter. But they acknowledged. They witnessed. They let the plateau incorporate their witnessing, and then they moved on.

What they do, if they can be said to do anything, is attend. They watch the bulges form and unfold. They track the color-migrations across the surface, mapping them in their minds, looking for patterns that might not exist. They feel the tremors that precede the trench-openings and they lie at the edges of the trenches and look down into the dark compressed layers below, the ancient growth, the deep time of the plateau. They are learning something, or they are simply accumulating experience, and they cannot tell the difference anymore, and the difference does not matter.

Sometimes one of them will spend years focused on a single vent—charting its rhythms, measuring its exhalations with their own breath, trying to understand the system of which it is one small aperture. They never succeed, in the sense of arriving at a complete understanding. But they do not fail either, because failure would require a goal, and their only goal is the attending itself. When they tire of one vent, they move to another, or to the color-migrations, or to the pioneers that colonize the trench-edges, and the plateau continues whether they attend or not, but they believe—if believe is the right word—that their attention matters somehow. That witnessing is a kind of participation. That being present while something happens makes the happening more real, more fully itself.

They are kind to each other, in a distant way. When one of them is sick—and sickness still happens, though rarely, their bodies not yet fully adapted—the others gather, slowly, over days, and they sit with the sick one and share warmth and bring water in cupped hands from the nearest condensation. They do not try to heal. They do not offer comfort in words. They simply provide presence, the same presence they provide to the plateau, and sometimes the sick one recovers and sometimes the sick one becomes one of the still ones and eventually becomes moss, and either outcome is acceptable, and neither is preferred.

Once in a very long while, someone new arrives. A figure on the horizon, moving with the jerky purposeful gait of someone who still believes in destinations. The patient watch this figure approach, over days, as it navigates the swells and avoids the trenches and slowly understands that the plateau is not a place to cross but a place to stop. Most of these figures eventually leave, defeated by the silence, the sameness, the lack of anything to achieve. But occasionally one stays. Sits down. Stops walking. Begins the long slow process of becoming the color of the plateau, the rhythm of the plateau, the patience of the plateau.

The patient welcome these newcomers without welcome. They do not approach. They do not explain. They simply continue their attending, and if the newcomer watches them, the newcomer will learn, and if the newcomer does not watch, the newcomer will leave, and either outcome is acceptable.

They have been here for—they do not know. Long enough that the plateau has risen a measurable amount beneath them, new layers accreting below, the ancient compressed foundation pushing slowly upward. Long enough that they have forgotten the faces of people they loved before. Long enough that the concept of *before* has become abstract, a grammatical artifact rather than a felt reality. They will be here until they are no longer here, until they become part of the mat, until their attending becomes the attended-to. This does not frighten them. It is simply what will happen, the way trenches open and close, the way colors migrate, the way the plateau breathes.

The thing you saw moving through the taller growth—that might have been one of them. Or it might have been something else. They are not the only life here, only the most visible, the most recognizable as life. There are other presences in the plateau, things that grew up out of it rather than arriving from elsewhere, things that are more plateau than animal but still possess something like intention, something like curiosity. The patient are aware of these presences. They do not interact with them, exactly, but they acknowledge them, the way they acknowledge everything—without judgment, without need, with only the simple fact of attention.

If you stayed long enough, you would meet them. One of them would approach you, eventually, after days or weeks, moving so slowly you might not notice until they were near. They would not speak. They would simply be present, and their presence would be a kind of invitation, and you would choose to accept it or not, and either choice would be acceptable.

They are waiting for nothing. They are waiting for everything. They are waiting, and the waiting is not a means to an end but the end itself, the practice, the purpose. The plateau breathes beneath them, and they breathe with it, and the silence holds them all, and they are content—if content is the right word—to be held.
