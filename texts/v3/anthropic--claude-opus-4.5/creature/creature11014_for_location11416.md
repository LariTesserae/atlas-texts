**The Keeper**

She was not born here. No one is born at seventeen thousand feet, in the noise, in the steam. But she has been here long enough that the distinction no longer matters.

She came as a girl of fourteen with her father, who was the last contracted maintenance engineer, in the final year anyone pretended the station would be staffed. That was 1987. Her father stayed three months, wrote his reports, noted that the turbines required no intervention and that intervention might in fact be impossible without equipment no one would haul to this altitude, and prepared to leave. She did not prepare. She had found the pale crustaceans in the warm pools. She had begun naming the orchids.

Her father descended alone, intending to return for her in a week with warmer clothes and proper boots. The road washed out that autumn and did not reopen until spring, and by spring she had stopped expecting him, had stopped expecting anyone.

---

She is perhaps fifty now, though she does not count. Her hair is the gray-white of the mineral deposits on the catwalk railings, and she wears it in a single braid that reaches her waist, stiff with calcium and copper salts from the water she washes in. Her skin has the texture of the landscape: weathered, seamed, holding color in unexpected places where the minerals have left their mark. A faint green trace follows the veins of her forearms. Her lips are perpetually cracked. Her eyes are the pale brown of the exposed basalt, and they have the same quality of seeming to look through rather than at.

She is small—the altitude has kept her that way, the thin air unwilling to support excess. Her movements are slow, deliberate, conserving oxygen, conserving heat, conserving the energy that food provides only grudgingly at this height. She has learned to work in the rhythm of the turbines, to time her efforts to their four-minute rotations, to rest when they rest in their momentary pause at the bottom of each cycle.

Her hands are extraordinary. Thick-knuckled, scarred, the fingers slightly curved even at rest as if perpetually gripping some tool. The nails are short and rimmed with permanent black—not dirt, but iron oxide worked so deeply into the keratin that it has become part of her. These hands know every pipe, every valve, every seam in the building's failing body. They know which leaks to ignore and which to pack with moss and mineral paste. They know how to coax warmth from the turbine housings on the coldest nights, which spots stay warm longest, which metal will burn and which will only comfort.

---

She lives in the space beneath the catwalk, in a nest she has built over decades from materials the mountain and the machine have provided. The foundation is a platform of copper pipe segments, lashed together with wire stripped from the building's defunct electrical system, raised just above the warm water that covers the floor. On this she has layered insulation: first the dried stems of the ridge grasses, then blankets of moss that she harvests from the exterior walls and dries on the sun-warmed southern face of the cistern, then a final covering of plastic sheeting salvaged from equipment that arrived in the 1970s and never left.

The plastic has grown brittle, cracked in places, but she patches it with a paste made fromiteite crystallite shavings and the sticky sap of the round-leafed vine. The repairs hold. Everything here holds longer than it should, preserved by some quality of the air, the water, the isolation.

Her possessions are few and strange. A metal cup, handleless, its rim worn smooth by her lips. A knife that was once part of a multi-tool, now just the blade, wrapped in leather she does not remember acquiring. Three glass jars that held something once—chemicals, perhaps, or samples—now holding dried orchid petals, crustacean shells, and a collection of small bright stones she has picked from the cliffs over the years. A single book, so water-damaged that its pages have fused into a solid block, the cover still barely legible: *Operación y Mantenimiento de Sistemas Hidráulicos*. She cannot read it, has never been able to read it, but she keeps it because it was her father's, because it is a made thing, because it reminds her that there are other places where people write words and bind them.

---

She eats what the ridge provides, which is not much. The bromeliad rosettes collect water rich in minerals that sustain a thin film of algae; she scrapes this film and eats it, a green paste that tastes of copper and electricity. The floating plants on the cistern are edible in small quantities—she learned this through careful experimentation, through days of stomach cramps and worse, through the slow narrowing of what her body would accept. The tiny white flowers are sweet if taken before they wilt, bitter after. She times her harvest to the flowers' strange accelerated rhythms, visiting the cistern four or five times a day.

Once every few months, when the mist clears and the vertigo of the view does not grip her too tightly, she descends the ridge's southern face for perhaps a hundred meters, to a shelf where tough grasses grow and, occasionally, a small rodent can be trapped. These trips exhaust her. She returns with shaking legs and sleeps for a full day afterward, curled in her nest, the turbine's hum her lullaby.

She has tried to count the days, the years, but the numbers slipped away from her long ago. Now she marks time by the growth of the crystallite formations—this one was at her knee when she arrived, now it reaches her shoulder—and by the slow collapse of the roof, which has sagged another handspan since she first stood beneath it. She thinks perhaps twenty years have passed. She thinks perhaps forty. The distinction matters less than the drip of water from the southeastern pipe, which has slowed slightly and which she monitors with the attention another woman might give to a lover's breathing.

---

She speaks, still, though there is no one to speak to. She speaks to the turbines, naming them: the eastern one is *La Vieja*, the old woman, because it groans the loudest; the middle one is *El Dormido*, the sleeper, because it sometimes pauses an extra half-second at the bottom of its rotation, as if dreaming; the western one is *El Joven*, the young one, though it is no younger than the others, only quieter, its bearings still somehow smooth after all these decades.

She speaks to the crustaceans when she sees them, apologies mostly, for disturbing their slow patrols across the flooded floor. She speaks to the lizard on the cistern, when it appears, asking it questions about the lowlands it must have come from, though she knows it was likely born here, knows nothing of anywhere else.

She speaks to herself, a running commentary on her actions that serves no purpose except to prove that language still lives in her, that she has not yet become entirely a creature of the place. *Now I check the eastern seal. Now I eat. Now I rest.* The words are Spanish, mostly, though English phrases surface sometimes, remnants of a childhood she barely remembers, and other sounds that might be words in no language at all, invented to describe things that exist only here.

---

Her days have a shape, though it is not the shape of days elsewhere.

She wakes when the light through the roof-holes shifts from gray to gold, which varies with the season in ways she has learned to read. She drinks from the cleanest of the overflow channels, the one she has lined with smooth stones to filter the heaviest minerals. She checks the turbines, not because she can fix them if something fails but because the checking is a ritual, a proof of attention, a promise she made to no one but keeps anyway.

She walks the exterior of the building, slowly, noting changes: a new crack in the concrete, a spray of orchids where there were none yesterday, the progress of the copper-colored stain down the northern wall. She touches the walls as she walks, her palm flat against the warm iron, feeling for vibrations that might signal trouble, though she no longer remembers what trouble would feel like, what she would do if she found it.

She tends the mosses on the roof, pulling away dead growth, spreading spores to bare patches with her fingertips. She is not sure the mosses need tending. She tends them anyway.

In the afternoon, when the sun is most direct and the building's shadow retreats to a thin line on the eastern side, she sits in that shadow and looks at the sky. She does not think, exactly. She lets the noise of the turbines fill the space where thought would be, lets the vibration travel up through the stone and into her bones, lets herself become for an hour or two just another thing the mountain is doing.

---

She has been sick, sometimes. Fevers that lasted days, that left her delirious in her nest while the turbines turned and turned and did not notice. Cuts that festered in the mineral-rich water until she learned to pack them with the crystallite paste that burns but somehow heals. Once, a fall from the catwalk that broke something in her wrist—she still cannot grip strongly with her left hand, and the bones there make a sound like small stones clicking when she rotates them.

She has thought about leaving. In the early years, she thought about it constantly: the path down, the road that must have been repaired by now, the villages that must still exist in the valleys below. But the thinking was always abstract, always *someday*, and someday never gathered enough weight to move her. Now she does not think about leaving. The thought has become as foreign as the thought of flight, of breathing water, of being someone other than who she is.

She belongs to the ridge. The ridge, perhaps, belongs to her—or they belong to each other, or the word *belong* means nothing here, where everything is simply what it is, turning, growing, wearing away.

---

There are things she knows that no one else knows.

She knows that the turbines do not turn at a constant speed, that their rhythm varies with the pressure from below in patterns that correlate somehow with distant weather, with storms she cannot see, with earthquakes too small to feel. She has learned to read these variations the way a sailor reads the sky. When *La Vieja* slows by a quarter-second per rotation, rain is coming, though rain at this altitude is rare, is precious, is collected in every vessel she owns.

She knows that the orchids bloom in response to the turbines' vibration, that certain frequencies trigger flowering in species that would otherwise remain dormant. She has tested this, tapping on pipes at different pitches, watching for the slow unfurling that follows. She does not understand why. She understands that it happens.

She knows that the crustaceans are not one species but three, distinguishable by the shape of their tails and the patterns of their movement, and that the three species avoid each other with an etiquette she finds touching, a small politeness in the warm dark water.

She knows that something lives in the cistern, below the floating plants, something larger than the crustaceans, something she has glimpsed only twice in all her years here: a pale shape rising toward the surface and then thinking better of it, sinking back into the depths where the water is hot enough to hurt. She does not know what it is. She does not try to find out. Some things are allowed their privacy.

---

She will die here. She knows this the way she knows the building's sounds, the way she knows which stones are stable and which will shift under her weight. It is not a sad knowledge. It is simply true, like the altitude, like the noise, like the steam.

When she dies, the turbines will keep turning. The mosses will grow over her nest, her belongings, her body. The crystallite will accumulate. The orchids will bloom and wilt and bloom again at their accelerated pace, and the lizard will bask on the cistern, and the thing in the depths will rise and sink and rise again.

The ridge will continue its slow tilt toward the southern cliff. The building will settle further into its foundation, and someday—not soon, but someday—the turbines will stop, and the water will find other paths, and the warmth will fade, and the delicate ecosystem that exists only because she has existed to tend it will contract and simplify and eventually disappear.

But not yet. Now she is here, her breath visible in the morning cold outside the building's warmth, her hands checking the eastern seal as they have checked it ten thousand times before, her voice naming the turbines, greeting the day, continuing.

*La Vieja. El Dormido. El Joven.* 

She is the fourth turbine, the one made of flesh, turning in her own slow rhythm, driven by pressures she did not create and cannot control, patient, persistent, still.
