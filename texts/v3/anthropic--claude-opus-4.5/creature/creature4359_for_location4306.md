## The Swarm-Tenders

They have no queen. This is the first thing to understand, and the last thing visitors grasp: there is no center, no throne, no single mind dreaming the bower into being. There are only the ten thousand, and the ten thousand are only the work.

Each beetle lives perhaps three months. In that time, it will carry its own weight ten thousand times over. It will drink from the pool on seventeen thousand occasions, always at the edge, always in its turn. It will taste the moss with organs clustered beneath its mandibles and know, in some way that is not knowing, whether this patch should be stripped today or left to grow another season. It will communicate through chemical trails that evaporate within minutes, through vibrations passed body-to-body faster than thought, through simple presence—the pressure of many legs moving one direction that draws others to follow.

They are born in the deep moss, in chambers no larger than a thimble, where eggs are laid not by any special mother but by any beetle old enough and fed enough to produce them. The larvae eat moss and fungus and the bodies of the dead, all of it already partially digested by the adults who chew it and pass it down. By the time a new beetle emerges, it has already consumed its ancestors. It carries them in its small body, in the minerals of its soft carapace, in the bacteria that colonize its gut. The bower is made of the dead transformed endlessly into the living.

---

What do they want? The question dissolves when you watch them long enough.

They want the moss to be *right*—the moisture content precise, the pH within tolerances no instrument could measure more accurately than their bodies do. They want the fungi to fruit in the dark places, want the pool to remain clear, want the light through the crack above to fall where it has always fallen. They want the work to continue.

But "want" is wrong. A river does not want to reach the sea. They simply *do*, and the doing maintains the bower, and the bower maintains them, and the cycle continues as it has since before the stones were laid, since before the mountain lifted this hollow into the clouds.

They experience something, surely. When the swarm pulses and half of them freeze while the others race along invisible paths, something is happening that might be called feeling—a wash of chemical signal that floods their small neurons, that makes their legs move or stop, that draws them toward the disturbance or away from it. Is it fear? Excitement? The swarm-equivalent of a gasp?

You cannot know. They cannot tell you. Perhaps the feeling is so different from anything you have felt that no human word could hold it—a sensation of *many-moving-as-one*, of *pattern-Loss-pattern-restored*, of being a single cell in a body that has no brain but somehow thinks.

---

The oldest among them—those who have survived two months, nearly three—move more slowly now. Their carapaces have darkened, taken on the exact shade of the shadow-moss in the bower's deeper reaches. They spend more time near the pool, drinking longer, carrying smaller loads. The younger beetles flow around them without impatience. There is no retirement here, no elder-care. The old simply do less until they do nothing, and then they are food, and then they are moss, and then they are new beetles carrying fragments of leaf in mandibles that once belonged to their own bodies.

When one dies in the open, the others do not mourn. They investigate—mandibles tasting the chemical signature of death—and then they begin to dismantle. Legs are carried one direction, carapace another. The soft interior goes to the larvae in their thimble-chambers. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is remembered in any way you would recognize as memory.

And yet.

The patterns persist. The pool has been kept clear for longer than any beetle has lived, longer than a thousand generations of beetles have lived. The fungi have been cultivated in the dark places since before the old masonry was laid. Someone taught no one, and yet everyone knows: *this is how the bower is kept*.

---

When clouds cover the sun and the light dims, they slow, clustering together in masses that conserve heat. In these moments, pressed carapace to carapace in the cooling moss, they share something that might be called communion—chemical signals passing freely between bodies, vibrations humming through the group like a single sustained note. What information moves in these exchanges? Population counts, perhaps. Resource assessments. The location of new growth, of failing moss, of places where the work is needed.

Or perhaps nothing so organized. Perhaps they simply *are together* in these moments, many bodies briefly becoming one body, the boundary between self and swarm dissolving in the dim light until the sun returns and they scatter again into individuals.

They have no language. They have no stories. They do not know that the old masonry was laid by human hands, do not know that humans exist, do not know that *they* exist in any way that could be called knowing. They are only the work, and the work is only them.

---

And yet the bower is beautiful.

Not to them—they have no eyes that see beauty, no minds that frame it. But beauty emerges anyway, the way crystals emerge from solution, the way river deltas branch into patterns that mathematicians find sublime. The seventeen shades of moss, the ferns like green smoke, the scattered constellation of bioluminescence in the deep places—all of it has been tended into being by creatures who tend without intention, who create without knowing they create.

The swarm does not know the bower is beautiful. The swarm does not know the bower at all.

There is only the work. There is only the carrying and the stripping and the laying down, the drinking and the mating and the dying, the pulse when something changes and the settling when it is resolved. There is only the pattern, which is no one's pattern, which belongs to everyone and no one, which will continue until the mountain itself crumbles and the crack closes and the last beetle dies in darkness with no one to carry its body to the larvae.

Until then: the whisper of thousands of small legs. The click of mandibles passing objects one to another. The clear pool. The patient moss. The light falling where it has always fallen.

The work continuing, as it does, as it will, as it has since before the stones were laid, tended by those who do not know they tend, kept by those who do not know they keep, perfect and purposeless, asking nothing, answering nothing, simply *being* what it is.

Ten thousand bodies the size of your thumbnail. Ten thousand lives too brief to name. Ten thousand fragments of a mind that exists only in the spaces between them, thinking thoughts no single beetle could hold, dreaming the bower into being one carried leaf at a time.
