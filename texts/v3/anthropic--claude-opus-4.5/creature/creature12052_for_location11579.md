**The Tenders of the Necessary Distance**

They are born in the towers but do not belong to them—that is the first lesson every child learns, sometimes before they learn to speak. The towers work, and work requires service, but service is not ownership. The families who live in Qel-Maroth exist in the spaces the machinery leaves: the narrow rooms between pipe runs, the platforms suspended over cycling engines, the alcoves where the heat is merely uncomfortable rather than lethal. They have lived this way long enough that their bodies have adapted in small ways—skin that flushes dark when overheated rather than fair, pupils perpetually widened against the amber gloom, a particular efficiency in their sweat glands that borders on the excessive. They are always damp. They do not think of this as unpleasant.

Each tower holds between forty and two hundred people, depending on its function and the complexity of its processes. They organize themselves not by family name but by **duty-cycle**—those who share the same eight-hour period of wakefulness, the same maintenance routes, the same responsibility for particular subsystems. A child might see their birth-mother twice a week if their cycles align poorly. This is neither tragedy nor design; it is simply how the towers divide consciousness across time, ensuring that every process has witnesses at every hour.

The work itself is interpretive. No one living remembers building the towers or installing their machinery. What remains are patterns: this gauge should read within this range, this valve should turn this far at this interval, this sound means adjustment and this sound means disaster. The Tenders have developed a vocabulary of over six hundred specific words for mechanical timbre—the difference between a healthy bearing and one three weeks from failure, between steam venting correctly and steam venting slightly too wet. Children learn these distinctions before they learn to read, pressing their ears to warm pipes while adults quiz them.

They do not read much. What texts exist are maintenance logs going back generations, each entry adding to an accumulated interpretation of what the towers want—not literally, not superstitiously, but in the practical sense that a gardener speaks of what soil wants. *Tower Eleven wants more cooling in its upper third during the warm months. Tower Eleven resents interruption to its southeastern pump system; work quickly if you must work there at all.* These notes are written in a compressed shorthand that takes years to fully learn, dense with implications and cross-references to other entries, other generations' observations. A senior Tender might spend their entire rest period reading through a single subsystem's history before making a decision about an unusual reading.

**The young**, when they are young enough, sometimes play between the towers.

This is tolerated until approximately age ten. The plateau is not precisely dangerous—the pale scuttling things avoid anything warm-blooded, and the wet stone offers sure footing to those raised on it—but the crossing requires something that adults have learned to ration: *the willingness to be alone in the middle*. Children chase each other between drainage channels, shrieking in the sound-swallowing air, daring each other to stop in the exact center between two towers where neither can be reached quickly. They play games of breath-holding in the thickest steam. They give names to the scuttling things and claim to have befriended particular individuals, though the creatures show no sign of recognizing anyone.

Then something shifts. Usually around the first year of assigned duty-cycles, sometimes later for the stubborn or the strange. The distances begin to *weigh*. The amber emptiness between towers becomes legible as absence rather than space, and the thought of crossing it—of being that far from the throb of machinery, that far from walls that breathe condensation, that exposed beneath the sourceless light—becomes uncomfortable, then anxious, then nearly unthinkable. Adults cross when they must. For repair exchanges between towers, for the seasonal census, for the rare instances of marriage or permanent transfer. But they cross quickly, in groups when possible, speaking loudly to fill the muffling air, and they do not look too long at the middle distance.

**Marriages** happen perhaps a dozen times per generation across all of Qel-Maroth. The logistics discourage them: to unite duty-cycles across towers requires months of negotiation, since each tower must adjust its staffing to accommodate loss or gain. More significantly, it requires someone to make the permanent crossing—to leave the tower where they know every pipe's voice, every gauge's acceptable range, and begin again as a novice in a machine they do not understand. Some towers have not sent or received a marriage partner in living memory. Their gene pools grow concerningly narrow; they know this; the knowledge does not make the crossing easier.

What they do instead is **call**.

On clear nights—clear meaning merely *less obscured*, the haze thinning enough that four or five towers become visible from any given point—they climb to their rooftops and sing. Not words. Not exactly music. A kind of pitched humming that carries better through the saturated air than speech, rising and falling in patterns that each tower elaborates independently. The songs contain information: we are well, we are struggling, we have someone of marriage age, we need a particular spare part, we remember you from last season's census. But mostly they contain presence, the assertion that other towers exist, that the lights in the distance are not phantoms or reflections, that Qel-Maroth is a place with people in it rather than a collection of machines that happen to be observed.

Children learn the songs before they learn to fear the crossings. In this way they carry something of the connection forward even after it becomes viscerally difficult—a muscle memory of reaching out, of being heard, of hearing back.

**The question of purpose** does not trouble them the way it might trouble visitors.

Occasionally someone arrives from elsewhere—traders from the lowland routes, scholars interested in the plateau's geology, engineers who have heard rumors of the towers' peculiar perpetual function. These visitors always ask: *what do the towers make? What is all this for?* The Tenders find the question mildly embarrassing, the way one might be embarrassed by a guest who asks why you breathe. The towers transform, they explain. They take in water and heat and return water and heat in different configurations. They require maintenance to continue transforming. The maintenance requires people. The people require the towers for shelter from the plateau's raw conditions. The cycle sustains itself. What more purpose could be needed?

The visitors sometimes press: *but surely it began for a reason. Surely someone built this to accomplish something.* The Tenders agree that this is likely true. They do not know what that something was. They cannot know—the machinery is too complex, its functions too interwoven, its outputs (if any) feeding into systems beneath the plateau that no living person has ever accessed. The question of original purpose is like the question of what the first Tenders intended their great-great-grandchildren to feel. Interesting, perhaps, but not actionable. The towers work now. They work in particular ways that have been learned through generations of patient observation. That knowledge is the inheritance, not the unknowable intentions of vanished builders.

Some of them pray. Not to the towers, not exactly—but not *not* to the towers either. A kind of focused attention before beginning a difficult repair, a murmured thanks when a process stabilizes after days of worry. They do not have priests or organized theology. What they have is the sense that the machinery's continued function is not guaranteed, that it operates in some space between pure mechanism and something more responsive, and that acknowledgment of this uncertainty is simply politeness. The towers have not stopped working in any recorded generation. This does not mean they cannot stop. This does not mean they will not, someday, want something other than what they want now.

**The pale scuttling things** are called *keleth* in the Tender vocabulary—a word that means roughly "those-who-are-not-us-but-are-also-here." They range in size from a clenched fist to a crouching child. Their ceramic shells are not quite the same ceramic as the towers, but close enough to suggest relationship. They emerge from vents in the plateau's surface during the cooler hours, moving between drainage channels in their characteristic bursts-and-freezes, apparently feeding on mineral deposits or perhaps on something smaller and less visible. They avoid the Tenders absolutely. If approached, they freeze; if approached further, they flee toward the nearest vent with startling speed. No one has ever caught one, though children sometimes try.

The Tenders leave them small offerings. Not food—the keleth do not seem to eat anything organic—but objects: worn-out gaskets, cracked ceramic fragments, bolts too corroded to serve their function. These are placed at the edges of tower territories, where the low boundary walls meet the open plateau. By morning the offerings are always gone. Whether the keleth take them, or the drainage system claims them, or something else entirely occurs, no one can say. The practice continues regardless, has continued for longer than anyone can remember beginning it. It costs nothing. It acknowledges that the plateau is shared. That seems enough.

**In the amber twilight**, which is all the light they know, the Tenders go about their duty-cycles with the calm attention of those who have found their fit. They listen to pipes. They read gauges. They adjust valves by fractions, note the results, record their observations in the growing logs. They raise children who will do the same, who will learn the same six hundred words for mechanical timbre, who will play in the wet emptiness until they cannot anymore, who will climb to the rooftops on clear nights and add their voices to the songs. They do not think of themselves as trapped, though a visitor might see it that way. They do not think of the towers as prisons or saviors.

They think of them as the place where the work is, and themselves as the ones who do it, and the distance between each spire as the necessary space that keeps the whole system from becoming something else—something crowded, something less careful, something that might forget to listen for the particular groan that means a bearing is three weeks from failure.

They are alone together, each tower an island, all of them sweating in the endless amber dusk, and they would not know how to be any other way.
