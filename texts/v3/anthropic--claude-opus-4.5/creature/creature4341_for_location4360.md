**The Keeper of the Unfinished Turn**

She has no name she answers to anymore, though the pumping station logs from forty years back would show a Maren Vos, apprentice hydro-engineer, third class, assigned to Cistern Maintenance Division. The records would show her dismissed after seven months for "temperamental unsuitability," which was the foreman's way of saying she spent too long in the deep places and came back speaking of measurements that weren't on any gauge.

She lives in the space between the second and third columns from the eastern entrance, in a hollow she did not carve but which was waiting for her when she returned, twenty years after her dismissal, following a path through the service tunnels that she could not afterward have retraced. The hollow is not visible unless you know to look for it—a place where the mineral deposits have built up over centuries into something like a shelf, something like a bed, something like a cocoon if cocoons were made of patience and dissolved calcium. She has lined it with rags that were once engineer's coveralls, though the fabric has taken on the character of the pale growths, gone soft and strange and faintly luminescent in the right darkness.

She is old now, or old enough. Her hair is the white of the mineral crusts, and whether this is age or adaptation she does not know and has stopped asking. Her fingers have developed a permanent curvature from decades of running along channel-edges, reading the water's intentions by touch. The cold has settled into her bones so thoroughly that she no longer feels it as cold—only as presence, as company, as the particular embrace of a place that has decided to keep her.

---

She eats the pale growths, when she eats. They taste of nothing and everything, of mineral and time and the faint organic residue of a city's slow decay filtering down through stone. She drinks from the channels directly, lowering her face to the water like an animal, like a supplicant, like someone who has forgotten that cups exist or has decided they were always unnecessary. The water is so cold it should burn, but her throat has adapted, or surrendered, or been claimed.

The beetles know her. This is not anthropomorphism; this is observation. They climb her when she is still, which is often, processing across her mineral-stained clothing and up her arms and over her face with the same careful deliberation they bring to the columns. She has learned to read their movements as a kind of language—not words, nothing so crude, but patterns that tell her when the pressure above is changing, when the channels will reroute, when the breathing will shift from expansion to contraction. In return, she offers them her stillness, her warmth—what little of it remains—and occasionally a fragment of pale growth held between fingers that have not trembled in years.

---

She knows about the brass mechanism. She has been watching it since the bootprints appeared, since the stranger descended and fiddled and cranked and then—loss of nerve? sudden errand? the chamber's subtle discouragement?—abandoned their purpose half-completed. She has not touched it. She has learned that the chamber considers new arrivals in its own time, and to interfere with that consideration would be rude, would be presumptuous, would be a violation of the hospitality that has kept her alive in a place where no human should be able to survive.

But she watches. In the dim flicker of the three remaining lamps, from her hollow between the columns, she watches the mineral deposits creeping up the brass feet, the pale tendrils extending their exploratory welcome, the slow incorporation of another foreign object into the chamber's long memory. She wonders what it measures. She wonders if whoever left it will return to finish their half-turn of the crank. She wonders, with the idle curiosity of someone who has let go of urgency, whether the chamber will allow the completion or whether it will simply absorb the mechanism whole, brass and glass and gears becoming another feature of the terrain, another thing the stone is remembering poorly.

---

She speaks, sometimes. Not to anyone—there is no one—but to the chamber itself, to the water, to the eroded figures in their hooded robes. Her voice has gone strange from disuse, from the particular acoustics of the space, from years of pitching her words to harmonize with the whisper of the channels rather than cut across it. She tells the columns about the city above, about the changes she intuits from the vibrations of footsteps, from the shifting mineral content of the water, from the subtle variations in how the pumping stations pulse. She tells them about the winters that are colder now than when she first came, about the foundry soot that tastes different in the water than it did a decade ago, about her theory that the city is slowly dying and does not know it yet.

The columns do not respond. But their conversation—the one that will take centuries to complete—seems to include her now. She has become part of their patient audience, part of the chamber's consideration, part of whatever the breathing means.

---

Once, perhaps five years ago or perhaps fifteen—time has become approximate—she saw someone else. A child, descended through a passage that should have been sealed, standing at the edge of the lamplight with eyes that reflected the water's gleam. They stared at each other for a long time, the old woman in her hollow and the child at the threshold, and then the child turned and fled back up into the world where people lived properly, with fires and food and names that others used.

She did not follow. She thought about it—felt something that might have been longing, might have been memory, might have been the last ember of her temperamental unsuitability flaring up—but she did not follow. The chamber had breathed in at that moment, contracted by distances too small to see, and she understood this as a question: *are you staying?*

She stayed.

---

The engineers will come eventually. Someone will authorize the maintenance expense, or the pumping stations will fail, or the city's thirst will drive them down into the deep places again. She knows this. The chamber knows this. The columns are having a conversation about it that will conclude long after it matters.

When they come, they will find her, or they will find what remains of her, or they will find nothing at all—just a hollow in the mineral deposits that seems almost shaped for a body, just a scattering of beetle shells, just a disturbance in the pale growths that might be footprints or might be the chamber's own slow rearrangement of itself.

She does not worry about this. She has stopped being someone who worries.

In the dim light, she runs her fingers along the nearest channel and feels the water's cold intention, its mineral patience, its long memory of being snow and then being rain and then being mountain-tear and then being here, with her, in the Breathing Room that does not appear on any map anyone remembers to consult.

The chamber exhales. She exhales with it.

Above, the city is cold and high and old, and it has forgotten her as thoroughly as it has forgotten the cistern, and she finds this—she finds everything, now—good.
