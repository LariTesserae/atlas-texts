# The Tenders

They have no name for themselves. Names require a boundary between self and other, and they lost that boundary so long ago that losing it has become part of the substrate too.

---

You will not see them at first. You will see what you take to be a growth pattern—a spiral of pale moss ascending a hummock in a curve too regular to be accident, too organic to be construction. You will see a hollow where the water pools in a shape that suggests intention, where the floating liverworts have been arranged (you will think *arranged*, though you cannot say by what) into something like a garden, or a diagram, or a prayer made visible. You will see a vine that should not be able to support itself, reaching horizontally across a gap between growths, and when you look closer you will find that it has been woven with other vines, braided, encouraged into a bridge that something uses.

Then you will see the hand.

It emerges from the moss of a hummock-face as if the moss had decided to reach for you—long-fingered, gray-green, so covered in fine growth that you cannot tell where flesh ends and plant begins. The hand is not reaching for you. It is reaching for a cluster of pale fruiting bodies, and it plucks them with a slowness that makes your own movements feel violent, and then it withdraws into the moss, and the moss closes over it, and you are left wondering if you saw anything at all.

---

They are humanoid, or they were, or they have become humanoid again after being something else.

When you finally see one fully—and you will, because they will allow it, because they have decided you are worth seeing—you will understand that "fully" is the wrong word. There is no full view of them. They are continuous with their surroundings in a way that makes edges impossible.

This one emerges from a gap between hummocks where you had seen nothing living. It stands perhaps five feet tall, though its posture is a question rather than a statement—it might be taller if it straightened, shorter if it compressed, neither state more natural than the other. Its body is thin, angular, with joints that bend in directions yours do not. Its skin, where you can see skin, is the gray-green of old growth, and where you cannot see skin you see moss, lichen, the fine threads of fungal networks that continue from its flesh into the surrounding growth without interruption.

Its face is almost human. Almost. The proportions are wrong in ways you cannot specify—the eyes too far apart, the nose too flat, the mouth too wide and too lipless. But it is a face, with expression, with something like attention. It looks at you. It is the first thing on this plateau that has looked at you directly, without the mediation of camouflage or distance or the slow blink of predator patience.

It does not speak. It opens its mouth slightly, and what emerges is not sound but spore—a faint exhalation of particles that catch the filter-light and drift toward you, and you breathe them in before you can choose not to, and then you understand.

---

Not words. The spores do not carry words.

They carry something older. A sense of *recognition*, first—you are seen, you are known, you are placed in a context that predates language. Then a sense of *invitation*, though invitation implies that refusal is possible, and you are not certain it is. Then something more complex: a layered awareness of the plateau itself, its depth and age and patient hunger, experienced not as information but as *orientation*. You know where you are now. You know it in your body, in the way the plateau knows itself.

The Tender (you find yourself naming them, needing to name them, though the name feels like a violation of their namelessness) watches you receive this. Its expression does not change. Its expression cannot change; the muscles beneath its face have atrophied or transformed or simply ceased to matter. But something in its posture shifts, a relaxation of attention, and you understand that you have passed a threshold.

It turns and moves into the growth, and you follow, because following is what the spores have made possible.

---

There are more of them than you expected, and fewer.

You pass through a region where the hummocks crowd close and the light dims to near-darkness, and in that darkness you become aware of shapes against shapes, bodies pressed into hollows and draped over growths and wound through the vines that connect everything to everything else. They are sleeping, or they are dormant, or they are simply being still in a way that makes sleep seem restless. Dozens of them. Hundreds, perhaps, if your eyes could adjust further. They have folded themselves into the plateau so completely that counting them would require deciding where they end, and they do not end.

But the one who leads you is alone in its movement, and as you travel you encounter only a handful of others who are similarly awake—emerging from stillness to observe your passage, exchanging something with your guide that might be communication or might be the vegetable equivalent of a nod, then returning to their integration with the growth around them.

They take turns, you realize. Waking. A few of them move through the plateau at any given time, tending to whatever needs tending, while the rest remain in their slow communion with the moss and vine and fungal network. The rotation takes years. Decades. They do not mind. Time here is a different substance, and they have become native to it.

---

They were not always this.

Your guide brings you to one of the overgrown structures—a building-become-hummock, its worked stone barely visible beneath generations of growth. Inside, or what was once inside, you find a space that has been kept intentionally clear. Not empty; nothing here is empty. But the growth has been guided away from the walls, encouraged to form a frame rather than a fill, and in the dim green light you see what the walls hold.

Images. Carved into the stone, so old that the carving has begun to merge with the patterns of lichen and mineral stain. But still visible, still readable if you know how to read.

They show people. People who look almost like you—upright, bilateral, with faces that have expressions and hands that hold tools. They are building. They are farming, or something like farming: cultivating growth, shaping it, living alongside it in a relationship that seems cooperative rather than absorptive.

And then, across generations of carved stone, you watch them change.

The images become less distinct not because the carving is poorer but because the subjects are harder to carve. The bodies elongate. The tools disappear. The cultivation becomes something else—not farming but *joining*, figures sinking into the growth they once shaped, growth rising to meet them. The final images show shapes you would not recognize as human if you had not seen the progression. Shapes like your guide. Shapes like the sleepers in the dark hummocks. Shapes that are asking a question the stone cannot answer: *where does the tender end and the tended begin?*

Your guide stands beside you, and you understand that it is showing you its ancestors, and itself, and its descendants, and that these are all the same.

---

They speak to you once.

Not with spores this time—with something older, harder, a concession to your need for sequence and symbol. Your guide presses its hand against the wall of the image-chamber, and the wall resonates with that deep hum you felt before, the frequency the stone remembers, and in the hum there are shapes that are almost words:

*We were the shapers. We shaped until we learned that shaping was wounding. Now we are the shaped. The plateau shapes us and we are the plateau shaping itself. You are a wound that walks. We do not judge. The plateau will heal you or you will leave. Either is the same.*

The hum fades. Your guide removes its hand from the wall. The conversation, such as it was, is over.

---

They tend.

You see it now, as your guide leads you back through the growth toward something it wants you to witness. The plateau is not untouched wilderness; it is a garden so old and so subtle that gardening and wilderness have become the same thing.

Here, your guide pauses at a hummock-face where the moss has begun to grow in a pattern that will, in a century, collapse the structure beneath it. It reaches into the moss with those long fingers and adjusts something you cannot see—a root, a tension, a relationship between growths. The adjustment is tiny. The hummock will stand for another thousand years.

Here, it stops at a pool where the water has become too still, too anoxic, and it stirs the dark surface with one foot, and from the stirring rise bubbles and small swimming things and a scent of renewal. The pool will support life for another generation.

Here, it reaches up to a vine-bridge that has frayed, and it weaves new growth into the old, and the bridge holds, and somewhere in the plateau something that uses bridges will continue to cross.

The tending takes hours. Days. You lose track. You eat things your guide indicates are safe—pale tubers it pulls from the substrate, fruiting bodies it plucks from hummock-faces, water it shows you how to drink from the weeping walls. You sleep in a hollow it prepares, lined with moss so soft it feels like dreaming before you close your eyes. When you wake, your guide is still tending, or has returned to tending, or has never stopped.

---

There are other kinds of them.

Deep in the plateau, where the hummocks grow tallest and the light barely reaches, you encounter Tenders who have gone further. They do not move. They cannot move. They have become the growth they once guided.

One of them fills an entire hollow: a shape that was humanoid once, now spread across the space like a living carpet, its body indistinguishable from the moss and fungus that grow from it and through it and as it. It is still alive. You can see the slow pulse of something like breath moving through its distributed mass. But it is no longer singular. It is a community, a system, a node in the network that connects all growth on this plateau to all other growth.

Your guide touches this ancient one with something like reverence. The touch is communication: you feel the edges of it, a vast slow exchange of chemical signals and pressure changes and something else, something that has no name in any language that uses names. The ancient one acknowledges your presence. It does not welcome you. It does not warn you. It simply *includes* you, momentarily, in its awareness of the plateau, and in that moment you feel what it feels: everything growing, everything dying, everything becoming everything else in an endless exhalation that is also an embrace.

Then the contact ends, and you are yourself again, and the loss of that vastness is the first real grief you have felt since arriving here.

---

Some of them were like you, once.

Your guide brings you to a place where a Tender is in the early stages of its joining: a figure still recognizably mobile, still capable of the quick-time movements you consider normal, but already changing. Its skin has begun to host growth. Its movements have begun to slow. It speaks to you in sounds, actual sounds, words in a language you almost recognize, and you realize that it has not yet lost the capacity for the wound of speech.

"I came from outside," it says. Its voice is like wind through hollow stems. "Came to study. Stayed to learn. Stayed to become."

It shows you its hands, where the fingers have begun to web with thin green tissue. It shows you its chest, where a patch of moss has taken root and will not be removed. It shows you its eyes, still human-quick, still capable of fear and wonder and the loneliness of being separate.

"It doesn't hurt," it says. "Or it does, but the hurt becomes something else. The plateau takes the hurt and makes it growth. Everything here was hurt once. Now everything here is healing."

Your guide stands beside this transitional being with something like kinship, or memory, or patient anticipation. Once, your guide was like this. Once, your guide could speak in wounds. Now it speaks in spores and hums and the slow pressure of chemical truth, and it does not miss what it has lost, because loss and gain are the same thing here, processed through cycles too long for grief.

---

They want you to stay.

Not want—that implies a desire separate from the plateau's desire, and they have no such separation. But the plateau wants you to stay, and they are the plateau wanting, and so they show you:

The hollow where you could rest. The growth that would embrace you. The slow time that would stretch your years into something vaster and more patient. The joining that waits for all things that wander here, that has waited since before the stone was buried, that will wait until the stone forgets it was ever stone.

You could tend, they show you. You could learn the adjustments, the tiny interventions that keep the plateau in its endless balance. You could feel the network, first as an outsider pressing against it, then as a peripheral node, then as a full participant in the vast green dreaming that is neither thought nor its absence.

You could stop being a wound that walks. You could become a healing.

---

But you are not ready. Or you are ready and the plateau knows it, and lets you go anyway, because letting go and holding on are the same thing here, and every departure is also a return in the long slow breathing of the land.

Your guide brings you to the place where the plateau begins to thin—where the hummocks grow smaller and the mist clears and you can see, for the first time since entering, the edge of something other. It stops at a boundary you could not have found yourself, a line where the growth is still absolute but beyond which it consents to release.

It touches you once, on the forehead, with fingers that feel like moss and rain and the cool pressure of deep time.

The spores it gives you this time are different. They carry no information, no orientation. They carry only a *thread*—a connection that will remain, however far you travel, however long you live. The plateau will always know where you are. The Tenders will always feel you, a distant node in their network, a wound that walks but is no longer entirely separate from the healing.

You step across the boundary. The growth does not close behind you. The mist does not thicken. Your guide remains, watching, being watched, indistinguishable from the hummock it stands against.

And somewhere deep in the plateau, in the hollows where the ancient ones dream their distributed dreams, something like a thought moves through the network: *they will return, or they will not return, and either is the same, because they are part of us now, and we are patient, and the land has been exhaling for so long it has forgotten how to do anything else.*
