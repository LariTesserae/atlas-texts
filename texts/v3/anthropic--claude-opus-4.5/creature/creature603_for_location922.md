**The Kessler Family**

They came three generations ago, when the last legitimate salvage crews pulled out and the district fell below the threshold of official notice. Great-grandmother Mira was a furnace-tender's daughter who remembered the smelters running, who knew which buildings had basements that stayed dry and which chimneys drew clean. She brought her husband and her sister and her sister's three children, and they claimed the old assay office on Foundry Road—stone-built, small-windowed, with a cellar that tapped into the thermal veins just enough to stay warm without cooking.

Now there are nineteen Kesslers, though they don't all use the name, and they don't all live in the assay office. They've spread into the adjoining structures, knocked careful passages through walls, created a warren that spans half a block. The youngest is eight months old, born in the back room where Mira herself died six years past. The oldest is Mira's sister Colm, seventy-three now, who sits by the window that faces the river and watches the steam rise and rarely speaks except to say that the salamander-things are closer this year than last.

She's right. The family tracks them.

**Old Piotr** keeps the maps—hand-drawn on the back of salvaged ledger pages, updated nightly. He marks the dens with red ink made from rust and rendered fat, marks the hunting paths with orange, marks the places where the big ones have been seen with a symbol of his own devising that looks like a closed eye. Piotr is forty-six and walks with a limp from the time he fell through a factory floor and spent the night in a basement with two juveniles circling above, too cautious to descend, while he lay still as death and listened to them churr. He does not go out anymore. He reads the reports from those who do, and he draws his maps, and he knows the district better than anyone alive.

**The twins, Seff and Senna**, are the primary scouts. Seventeen years old, born in the district, never lived anywhere else. They move through the ruins with a silence that the salamander-things themselves might envy—bare feet calloused thick enough to handle the hot cobblestones, bodies thin and flexible enough to slip through gaps that would stop anyone from the city above. They know the sound that means a roof is about to give way (a particular creak, a settling groan) and the smell that means a thermal vent has shifted (sharper, more sulfurous, a warning to move). They communicate with each other in a system of hand signals and birdcalls that the family has developed over three generations, and they never go out alone.

No one goes out alone.

**What they eat**: The beetles, roasted. The pale crabs, boiled in water from the deeper pools where the mineral content runs lower. The long thin not-rats, when they can be caught in sufficient numbers and when nothing else is available—the meat is stringy and tastes of copper, but it keeps you alive. Occasionally the twins bring back something from the edge of the district, traded with the scavengers who work the boundary zone: hard bread, dried fruit, salt. Once a year, in winter when the haze thickens and the salamander-things den deep in the warmest chimneys, old Piotr's son **Harek** makes the journey up to the city proper to sell what the family has collected—salvaged copper, old iron fixtures, the occasional intact tool—and returns with medicine, cloth, things the district cannot provide.

**What they know**: They know the salamander-things are getting bolder. The big ones used to stay near the river; now they hunt as far as the stamping mill, and last month Senna saw one on the roof of the building across from the assay office, just watching. They know the beetle population has shifted north, following some change in the thermal patterns that old Colm says she can feel in her bones. They know a new family tried to settle in the district eight months ago—four adults, two children, city people who thought they could make a go of salvage work—and they know what happened to them, because Seff found what was left.

They did not intervene. You learn, in the district, what is yours to prevent and what is not.

**The children**: There are six of them young enough to still be learning. They play in the interior rooms, where the walls are thickest and the sounds from outside are muffled. They learn the hand signals before they learn to read. They practice freezing—going absolutely still, controlling their breath, becoming part of the furniture—because someday their lives will depend on it. The game they play most often involves one child making the churring call of a salamander-thing while the others hide, and it is not really a game at all.

**Ama** is fifteen and has decided she will leave. She has told only her mother and one of the twins (Senna, who understood; Seff would have argued). She has been saving—a coin here, a small salvageable item there—for two years. She has studied the route to the city, memorized the safe passages through the boundary zone, calculated what she will need to survive the first month while she finds work. She does not hate her family. She does not resent the district, exactly. But she has seen the lights on the hills above, and she has done the mathematics of life expectancy down here, and she has made her choice.

She will go in spring, when the salamander-things are most active and the family's attention is focused outward. She has not yet found a way to say goodbye that does not feel like betrayal.

**What they believe**: Mira taught them that the district gives and takes in equal measure. The heat that keeps them warm also draws the predators. The isolation that protects them from the city's laws also means no help will come. The industrial bones that shelter them also, slowly, poison them—old Colm's tremors, the Kessler cough that takes the lungs of one in four, the children who come too early or not at all. You accept the terms. You learn the rules. You survive another day, and another, and eventually you are part of the district yourself, no more separable from it than the rust from the iron.

At night, when the family gathers in the main room of the assay office—nineteen souls in a space meant for a dozen, but warm, and safe, and theirs—old Colm sometimes tells stories about the smelters running. The glow of the furnaces, the clang of metal, the workers moving in their hundreds through streets that are empty now. She tells it like a fairy tale, like something that happened in another world. The children listen, and do not quite believe, and look out the narrow windows at the amber haze and the dripping walls and the distant gleam of salamander eyes.

This is the world they know. This is home.

**And in the deepest basement**, below even the thermal tap that heats the assay office, there is a door that Mira sealed three generations ago with brick and morite and a warning scratched into the frame: *Do not open. Do not ask.* 

The family does not open it. The family does not ask. Occasionally, on the quietest nights, when even the salamander-things are still, someone will report hearing something from below—a tap, a scrape, a sound like breathing. The family has agreed, unanimously, across all nineteen members, that this is the settlement of the old building, nothing more.

The maps that old Piotr keeps do not include the basement.

Some things you do not draw.
