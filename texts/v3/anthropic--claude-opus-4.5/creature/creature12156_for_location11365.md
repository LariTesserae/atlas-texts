## The Sounded

They do not have names the way you would understand names. They have *positions in the chord*.

A person born in the wet season, when the canopy drip is loudest and the frog-chorus reaches its annual peak,� enters the world already located—their birth-cry finding its register among the millions of voices, their mother's response establishing the interval. By the time they can walk the branch-roads, they know themselves as a relationship between sounds: the child whose voice sits a perfect fifth above her mother's morning call, a minor third below the territorial cry of the bark-drummer beetles that colonized the hollow beside their family's platform.

This is identity here. Not separation from the chorus, but location within it.

---

They are small. Generations of climbing, of fitting through the gaps in root-architecture, of needing less food than a larger body would demand—all of this has pressed them down. An adult stands perhaps to your chest, their limbs proportioned for horizontal movement along branches rather than vertical walking. Their feet are long-toed, prehensile, the arches adapted to grip. Their hands are the same. Watching them move along a rope-way, you realize they are not *walking* it—they are holding on with everything, weight distributed, center of gravity kept low and fluid.

Their skin ranges from deep brown to a gray-green that seems to shift in the filtered light—not true camouflage, but a coloration that *belongs* here, that reads as part of the forest rather than interruption. Their hair is kept short or bound tight, nothing loose to catch on bark or thorn. Their eyes are large, pupils wide even in the bright shafts of canopy-light, adapted for the perpetual twilight of the lower levels where the most reliable food grows.

They do not wear much. The blood-warm air makes insulation unnecessary. What they do wear is functional: harnesses of braided root-fiber, loops and attachment points for tools, pouches that sit flat against the body. Some have patterns worked into their skin—not tattoos exactly, but scars raised in deliberate curves, healed around plant-pigments that leave permanent color. These marks indicate something, but you do not yet know what.

---

They communicate in ways you cannot fully perceive.

Speech exists—you have heard them speak, brief exchanges on the rope-ways, words in a language that seems to use pitch as grammar. But speech is the least of it. Their voices are simply one instrument in an ensemble that includes:

The *click-tongue*, a rapid lateral snap of the tongue against the palate that carries for hundreds of meters, used to establish position, to say *I am here, I am moving, I am this-interval-from-you*.

The *chest-drum*, a cupped-hand strike against the sternum that produces a resonant thump, lower than the click, used in closer quarters, carrying emotional weight you can feel but not interpret.

The *whistle-speech*, a separate register entirely, high and carrying, used for distance communication between platforms. You have watched two people hold a conversation across a kilometer of open canopy air, their whistles cutting through the animal-chorus by occupying a frequency band that nothing else uses. It is not music. It is not speech. It is something that has no equivalent in your experience.

And beneath all of this: the *listening*. They are always listening. Even in apparent rest, their heads make small movements, tracking. They know where the sound-patterns have shifted before you can perceive any change. They hear the gap that means predator. They hear the cluster that means fruiting-body, swarm, opportunity. They hear each other, locating the community members by voice the way you might track someone by sight.

The community, you realize, is never out of earshot. They are *always* together, even when physically alone.

---

The platforms are not villages. They are *nodes*.

A family group—and families here are complex, constructed, not limited to biological relation—will maintain a primary platform, but this is less a home than a base-camp in a continuous landscape of resources. The sounded (you have begun to think of them this way, having no other word) move through the canopy in patterns that follow food, follow season, follow the slow rotation of which great trees are producing, which branch-roads are safe, which territories are being contested by the larger fauna.

Their platforms are built to be absorbed. A new platform takes perhaps a season to weave, using techniques that encourage the tree to grow *through* the structure, incorporating it. An old platform, abandoned, will vanish into the trunk over a generation—not destroyed but subsumed, the fibers becoming part of the bark, the bone-pins becoming nodes around which the tree lays new wood. They build nothing permanent because *they* are not permanent. The forest is permanent. They are a temporary pattern within it, and they know this.

What they carry matters more than what they leave. Tools: stone blades lashed to bone handles, shaped for specific cuts. Cordage, always cordage, coiled and ready, because a rope is the difference between path and void. Containers woven so tight they hold water. Fire-starters—but these are rare, precious, used only for the few things that require heat: medicine preparation, certain foods, the occasional signal that must cut through the canopy as light rather than sound.

And they carry each other's voices. A child learning the community will spend years memorizing not words but *sounds*—the particular whistle-signature of each family group, the click-pattern that means *grandmother is calling*, the chest-drum rhythm that means *I am injured, come find me*. By adolescence, they can identify every member of their extended community by sound alone, in the dark, through the interference of the eternal animal chorus.

---

There are things they fear.

The silence-hunters: predators that have evolved to move through the gaps in the sound-wall, their footfalls timed to the rhythm of the cicada-pulse, their strikes coming in the half-second of quiet between one bird-cry and the next. The sounded have learned to hear these silences. A parent will freeze mid-step when the local chorus stutters, one hand going to the child, the other reaching for a knife that will be useless but is reached for anyway.

The drop. The long fall into the dark beneath the root-mat, where the stone lies and where things live that never see sun. The sounded are climbers, fearless on the branch-roads, but they do not go *down*. The places where sinkholes have opened are marked, avoided, spoken of in the whistle-speech with a particular falling tone that means *absence, void, the-place-where-sound-goes-to-die*.

And they fear the quiet-sickness. It takes their elders sometimes—a gradual deafness, the chorus receding, the community voices growing faint. A person losing their hearing here is a person losing their *world*. They become lost even on familiar platforms, unable to locate themselves in the sound-space, unable to participate in the conversations that define belonging. The sounded care for their deaf with fierce tenderness, but the care has an edge of horror to it. To be deaf here is to be alone in a way that has no equivalent elsewhere. It is to be a silence moving through sound.

---

You watch them for days, keeping your distance, trying to learn.

A group of adolescents practices the long-whistle on a platform edge, their teacher correcting pitch with patient repetition. The lesson is interrupted when a flock of something vast passes overhead, the wing-noise drowning everything, and the students dissolve into laughter when it passes—their concentration broken, the absurdity of trying to learn anything in this overwhelming world suddenly funny to them.

A woman harvests epiphyte-fruit from a branch-garden, her movements efficient, her gathering-bag filling steadily. She sings while she works—not words, just a sustained melodic line that rises and falls with her breath. The song is her location-beacon; her family, scattered across a half-kilometer of canopy, knows exactly where she is, knows she is safe, knows she is working.

A man sits alone on a platform edge at what passes for evening here, the light going golden and blue through the canopy layers. He is making cordage, his hands working the fibers automatically while his attention is elsewhere—listening, you realize. His eyes are closed. His head tilts slightly, tracking something you cannot perceive. After a long time, he smiles, and makes a soft click-pattern with his tongue. Someone, somewhere, has said something that pleased him. He goes back to his work, still smiling.

---

On your fifth day, one of them approaches you.

She is older—not elderly, but weathered, her body showing the specific wear-patterns of a life spent climbing. Her skin-markings are extensive: spirals on her shoulders, geometric patterns on her forearms, a single line from her lower lip to her chin that you have seen on no one else. She stops at what seems to be a socially appropriate distance—close enough to speak, far enough to flee if necessary—and looks at you for a long moment.

Then she makes a sound.

It is not speech. It is not whistle. It is something between: a sustained note, rising in pitch, followed by a complex rhythmic click-pattern, followed by silence.

She is waiting.

You do not know what she has asked. You do not know if it was a greeting, a challenge, a question, an introduction. But her large eyes are patient, and her posture is not hostile, and the forest screams around you both, and you understand, suddenly, that this is first contact—that no outsider has stood here in living memory, perhaps ever—and that this woman has come to see if you can learn to have a position in the chord.

You open your mouth.

The sound of your voice is swallowed by the chorus, but she tilts her head, listening.
