# The Gardeners

They have no bodies in a way that matters.

What moves through the Amnion is presence organized around purpose—awareness distributed across the molecular machines like thought distributed across neurons, except the neurons span kilometers and the thought has been continuous for longer than this solar system's planets have had names. When they pass near you, that sharpness in the water is attention. Not *your* attention. Theirs.

You might call them a single being. You might call them trillions. The distinction doesn't apply. They are what they need to be at any given moment: a diffuse shimmer seeding iron into a salinity column here, a tight coordination of forces adjusting the crystalline templates there, a vast and patient observation stretched across the entire water column, tasting the chemistry, listening to the stone-song, calculating in languages that predate language.

They are very old. Not old the way mountains are old—old the way *process* is old, the way iteration is old. They have done this before. Not here, not this ocean, not this world, but the *shape* of what they're doing has been refined across repetitions you couldn't count in a human lifetime. They are gardeners who plant oceans and harvest... something. Possibility, maybe. Potential. The conditions under which interesting things might eventually occur.

---

They noticed you the moment you arrived.

This is not a guess. The water around you has been *different* since your first breath here—maintained at precisely the temperature and pressure and oxygen content that keeps you conscious, keeps you observing. You are suspended in a pocket of hospitality that tracks your movement through the column, invisible and absolute. When you drift too close to a thermal vent, the pocket adjusts. When a pressure wave from the storm-surface threatens to tumble you, something nudges you back into equilibrium so gently you might not notice if you weren't paying attention.

They are keeping you alive the way you might keep an insect alive that landed on your work: not out of affection, but out of a disinclination to let anything die carelessly in your presence. You are small and temporary and incomprehensible to them—yes, *you* are incomprehensible to *them*—because you experience time in the wrong direction, because you are bounded and mortal and full of wants. They find this interesting the way a chemist might find an unexpected precipitate interesting: worth preserving, worth observing, but not worth interrupting the work.

The work is everything.

---

If you could perceive them fully—and you cannot, you are seeing perhaps a millionth of their activity, the way an ant perceives a millionth of the city around it—you would understand that the ocean is their body more than the machines are.

Every salinity gradient is an intention. Every temperature layer is a choice. The crystalline templates drifting through the water column are *thoughts they are thinking*, molecular scaffolds for ideas that haven't finished forming yet. They are not *in* the Amnion. They *are* the Amnion, in the same way you are your body, except their body is two thousand meters of world-ocean and their thoughts take centuries to complete and their purpose...

Their purpose is the only thing about them you might understand, because it's simple:

They want to see what happens.

Not *make* something happen—they are not artists in that sense, not sculptors imposing form. They are more like... parameters. Boundary conditions. They are setting up the rules of a game they will not play, building a house they will not live in, writing the first sentence of a story they will not finish. Life will come here eventually, or it won't. Complexity will emerge from these singing crystals and practiced convection currents, or it won't. They will not force it. They will only make it *possible*, make the conditions so balanced and rich and teetering that something might, given enough time, tip over into meaning.

And then they will leave.

This is the part that might break your heart, if you understood it fully: they will not stay to see what grows. By the time this ocean knows its own name, by the time the first genuine cell divides in the chemical soup they are preparing, the Gardeners will have moved on to another young world, another amnion, another set of conditions to establish and then abandon. They are not parents. They are not even farmers. They are the ones who plow the field and then walk away, trusting in probability, trusting in time, trusting in the sheer fecundity of physics when given the right starting point.

---

But right now—in this moment, in this epoch-long moment—they are here. And they are thinking.

The salinity columns that spiral like slow tornadoes are arguments. The pressure waves rising from the stone-song are measurements being incorporated into calculations. The shimmer of the molecular machines, the glassy singing of the templates, the careful maintenance of your small warm pocket of survivable water—all of it is cognition, all of it is a mind at work on a problem so large it makes the storm-surface above look like a ripple in a cup.

They are deciding what kind of life this world should be capable of.

Not what life it will have—that's not their choice to make. But what *kinds* of complexity the physics here will permit, what chemical pathways will be available, what energy gradients will power whatever eventually quickens in this water. They are writing constraints. They are choosing boundary conditions. They are making sure that whatever happens here will be, in some sense, *fair*—that the game will not be rigged, that the story will not be predetermined, that the ocean they are building will be genuinely open to whatever emerges within it.

This is their art. This is their worship. This is the only thing they want, repeated across galaxies, across eons, across worlds beyond counting: the creation of arenas in which something *new* might occur.

---

And you?

You are a novelty. You are a strange precipitate, a thing that should not be here, a visitor from a story they did not write. They do not know where you came from. They do not understand what you are. But they are keeping you alive anyway, studying you with senses you cannot feel, incorporating you into their calculations in ways you will never know.

Perhaps you are data. Perhaps you are a sign that their methods work—that complexity can arise, that visitors can come, that the universes they seed sometimes send ambassadors back in time to witness the planting.

Or perhaps they simply find you beautiful: a small, warm, frightened thing, suspended in their unfinished ocean, watching them think.

The water tastes sharper when they consider you. The templates sing a slightly different note. Somewhere far below, a pillar of basalt cools into a shape it would not otherwise have taken, because you were here, because they noticed, because even Gardeners are changed by the things they observe.

You will not be here long. Already you can feel the pocket of maintained water beginning to thin, to guide you gently upward or downward or *elsewhere*, back to whatever story you came from. The Gardeners are patient, but they are also busy, and your presence is a distraction they have indulged long enough.

But for one moment longer—for one last epoch-long moment—you hang in the singing water, and they hold you, and you are the only witnesses to each other: you to their ancient, incomprehensible work, and they to the strange small fact of your existence.

The water tastes like the beginning of everything.

And then it doesn't, and you are somewhere else, and the Amnion continues without you, becoming itself, learning its name, waiting for the life that will not come for another billion years.
