**The Midwives of Stone**

They call themselves nothing, of course—names require sound, and sound here belongs to the city. But visitors who stay long enough to learn the gesture-language eventually piece together the concept: *those-who-ease-what-comes*.

They are the oldest residents, or rather, they have been here longest, which isn't the same thing. Their bodies have adapted across generations to the particular demands of this place. Their skin has thickened and paled, taking on a greyish translucence that rhymes with the salamander-things, though they remain unmistakably human in shape. Their fingertips have developed a sensitivity that borders on the diagnostic—they can press a palm to rising stone and feel, through vibration and heat and some quality they have no gesture for, where the next fissure will open, where the next doorway-bubble will form.

This is their work: anticipating the city.

They move through the emergence zones in small groups, never alone, reading the rock with their hands. When they find a place where a new building is about to breach, they begin preparing—redirecting foot traffic with simple rope barriers, guiding the channel-creatures to move their bulk elsewhere, sometimes spending hours with their hands flat against a bulging section of ground, as if calming something about to be born. The stone doesn't respond to them exactly, but things go more smoothly when they're present. Fewer people caught in sudden floods. Fewer creatures crushed by unexpected shifts. The city emerges regardless, but the midwives make the emergence livable.

**The Harvesters**

A different population entirely, though they've interbred enough with the midwives over centuries that the line blurs. These are the ones who follow the grey slug-seals, who have memorized the migratory patterns of creatures that barely seem to move. They know which animals produce the clearest secretion, which yield the strongest when dried, which are approaching the end of their long lives and will soon sink into the warm pools to dissolve into something the city reabsorbs.

The harvesters work in silence like everyone else, but their silence has a different quality—attentive, patient, almost tender. They approach the slug-seals slowly, hands visible, movements telegraphed. The creatures don't react, don't seem to notice, but the harvesters behave as if they might. There's a ritual quality to the scraping of secretion from mottled flanks, the way harvesters bow slightly before and after, the specific gestures they make to each other that seem to mean something like *gratitude* or *acknowledgment*.

Some harvesters specialize further. A subset works exclusively with the bioluminescent crawlers, collecting the faint residue they leave on vertical surfaces, concentrating it into something that can light a dwelling for weeks. This work happens at night, when the creatures glow brightest, and the harvesters who do it have developed a nocturnal rhythm that sets them apart. You can identify them by their squint in the dim daylight, the way they move more surely when the glow rises.

**The Cartographers**

Every generation, the city becomes partially illegible. Streets that were thoroughfares become dead ends; buildings that stood alone find themselves merged into larger masses; entire districts vanish into the upward growth, their lower levels becoming basements, then sub-basements, then simply *below*. The cartographers don't map the city as it is—that would be futile—but rather as it *tends*.

They've developed a notation system written on sheets of dried secretion, symbols that indicate not locations but *vectors*: this area is rising faster, this channel is narrowing, this courtyard will fragment within a generation. They trade these tendency-maps to newcomers, to traders, to anyone who needs to plan beyond the immediate. The maps are always wrong in detail and always right in principle.

The cartographers congregate in the oldest risen district, where the buildings have grown tall enough to be stable, where the roar of emergence is distant enough to allow something approaching quiet—not true silence, but a reduction, a space where you can almost hear your own breathing. They've carved a meeting hall into stone that rose centuries ago, and they gather there to compare observations, to argue through gesture about which tendencies will dominate, to train their children in the particular way of seeing that their work requires.

There's a melancholy to them that the midwives and harvesters don't share. They're trying to hold something that refuses to be held. Every map they make is a record of loss as much as guidance—here is what was, here is what it became, here is what it's becoming. They do it anyway.

**The Listeners**

The strangest of the resident populations, and the smallest. Maybe two hundred in the entire city, spread across all districts, identifiable by the particular calluses on their palms and the way they press themselves against walls with their eyes closed.

The listeners claim—through gesture, through the patient work of conveying abstract concepts in a visual language—that the roar is not uniform. That beneath the overwhelming saturation of sound, there are patterns. Rhythms. Something that might be meaning.

Most residents find this claim somewhere between incomprehensible and irrelevant. The sound is the sound; it's landscape, it's weather, it's the texture of existence. What would it mean for it to *mean* something?

But the listeners persist. They've developed a practice of deep attention, pressing their bodies against the stone for hours, letting the vibration move through them, trying to differentiate the frequencies. They say—and this is where most people stop taking them seriously—that the city is not just emerging. That it's *speaking*. That the geological process driving everything upward has a kind of intention, or if not intention then something adjacent, something that could be understood if only they could learn to parse the grammar of the roar.

They've been saying this for generations. They haven't decoded anything yet. But their children take up the practice, press their palms to new-risen walls, close their eyes, and listen.

**The Visitors**

They come from elsewhere—from dry lands, from quiet places, from cities that were built rather than born. They arrive via the trade routes that thread through the mineral flats, carrying goods the Warrens can't produce: dried plants, preserved foods, metal tools, textiles, wood. They leave carrying secretion-goods, the unique toolstock that hardens harder than anything else available, the concentrated bioluminescence that lights without fire.

Most visitors don't stay long. The roar drives them back within days, sometimes hours. They conduct their trades near the city's edges, in districts young enough that the sound is merely overwhelming rather than obliterating. They communicate through their own interpreters—people who've made the journey often enough to have learned the gesture-language, who can translate between spoken words and hand-shapes.

But some visitors don't leave.

Something in the city catches them. Maybe it's the warmth, the way the stone-heat soaks into bones that have known cold. Maybe it's the strange peace of a place where everything is always becoming—no finished state to defend, no completion to maintain. Maybe it's the creatures, the pale slow life that asks nothing and provides everything. Maybe it's the sound itself, which after the maddening first days becomes something like a heartbeat, so constant that its absence would feel like death.

These visitors become residents by degrees. They stop speaking first, not because they've forgotten how but because there's no point, no one to hear. They learn the gestures. They find work—there's always work in a city that never stops emerging. They develop the pale skin, the particular walk that navigates perpetually damp stone, the patience that comes from living in permanent impermanence.

Their children will be born here, will know no other sound, will press their hands to rising walls and feel the city continuing beneath their palms. And they will not find this strange.

**The Creatures**

They are not residents in the way people are residents, but they are not separate from the city either. The salamander-things, the slug-seals, the glowing crawlers—they emerged with the first buildings, or perhaps before. No one has seen them reproduce, yet their numbers stay constant. No one has seen them eat, yet they don't waste away. They occupy their niches with a completeness that suggests they grew into those niches, or the niches grew around them, or both simultaneously.

The residents treat them as furniture, as weather, as architecture. Step over the slug-seal, harvest its secretion, move on. Let the crawlers spiral up the walls, collect their glow, use it for light. Watch the salamander-things rest in spray-zones, pale bodies glistening, and feel no need to understand them.

But sometimes, in the dim hours when day and night blur into sameness, a resident will pause near a cluster of salamander-things and notice something. The creatures aren't just resting. They're oriented—all of them, facing the same direction, toward a section of ground that bulges slightly with tomorrow's emergence. They knew, somehow, where the city would continue. They were waiting for it.

And the crawlers, spiraling up new-risen walls in their slow columns—their patterns aren't random. They trace the stress lines in the stone, the places where fissures will form, the channels the water will take. They're mapping the city's tendencies in real-time, in bioluminescence, writing predictions across the architecture.

The slug-seals, blocking foot traffic with their bulk—they're always positioned where the growth is stable, never where it's active. They know which ground is safe to rest on. They've always known.

The creatures are reading the city. Or they're part of how the city reads itself. Or there's no difference between those things.

---

**The Children**

Born into the roar, they don't experience it as sound at all. It's simply the medium of existence, the way fish don't experience water. They learn the gesture-language before they learn that other places have a different language, one made of mouth-sounds and air-vibrations that somehow carry meaning. When traders arrive with interpreters, the children watch the interpreters' mouths move and find it bizarre—why would you make shapes with your face when your hands are right there?

They grow up mapping the city's tendencies in their bodies. A child raised in the eastern emergence zones will orient toward the high whistle without thinking about it, using it as north. A child from the central depths will navigate by the grinding undertone, will feel anxious when that frequency diminishes. They don't just live in the city; they're calibrated to it, tuned to its frequencies, shaped by its perpetual emergence the way trees are shaped by wind.

And they develop something the adults never quite manage: a relationship with the creatures that goes beyond harvest and coexistence. The children sit with the salamander-things for hours, mimicking their stillness, learning whatever it is the creatures know about where the city will grow. They ride on the slug-seals' backs when the creatures migrate through the channels, accepting the slow pace, going wherever the creature goes. They let the crawlers climb over their bodies at night, decorating themselves with borrowed light.

The adults watch this and feel something between concern and awe. The children aren't just adapting to the city. They're integrating with it. They're becoming part of whatever process has been driving this place since before memory, the slow warm mineral birth that never completes and never stops.

Maybe that's what the city wants. Maybe want is the wrong word.

Maybe the city is raising them the way it raises buildings—slowly, crookedly, already ancient-seeming the moment they emerge.
