## The Weavers Who Remain

They call themselves nothing. Names require a sense of separation they no longer possess.

You will not see them directly—not at first, not in the way you see the rope-sloths or the pale burrowing things. They exist in the peripheral: a glimpse of fingers withdrawing into a tunnel mouth, the impression of a shoulder's curve disappearing around a node's far edge, a face-shaped shadow in the amber light that resolves into ordinary vine-shadow when you turn toward it. They are shy in the way that something distributed across too much space becomes shy—not from fear, but from the difficulty of gathering enough of themselves into one location to be perceived as singular.

---

They were human once. Or human-adjacent. Or they became human-shaped because that shape was useful for the work, and then forgot they had ever been otherwise.

Their bodies have thinned over the generations, stretched and recurved like the vines they tend. Their arms are longer than arms should be, the joints migrated to new positions that make reaching across gaps easier. Their fingers—you catch sight of a hand once, resting on a cable above you, and count seven fingers, each one tapering to a point that looks almost fibrous, almost like the beginning of a new tendril. Their skin has taken on the amber undertone of the light, and in certain angles it seems to glow faintly from within, as though they too have begun to photosynthesize, to metabolize something other than food.

They do not eat the glowing fruits. They do not seem to eat at all, not in ways you recognize. They press their palms against the node-surfaces and stay there for hours, and when they withdraw there are faint depressions in the tissue, and they seem—satisfied is not the right word. *Continued.* They seem continued.

---

The offerings at the junctions are not for gods. You understand this after weeks of observation, after learning to sit still enough that they forget you are separate from the weave.

The offerings are *instructions.*

Small bundles of fiber twisted into specific configurations. Knots that encode information in their crossings—how many loops, which strand passes over which, whether the twist runs clockwise or counter. The pale burrowers collect these bundles and carry them down into the node-depths, and somewhere below, in chambers you are not invited to enter, the Weavers read them with their fingers and learn what the structure needs. Where a cable has begun to fray. Where a junction requires reinforcement. Where the geometry has begun to fold in ways that might become dangerous if left untended.

They are maintenance workers. Caretakers. They have been repairing a world they did not build for so long that the building and the repair have become the same act, and they have become the same as both.

---

You meet one, eventually.

Not because they choose to be met, but because you fall asleep in a junction-hammock during what passes for a storm here—a period when the thermals reverse and cold air sinks from above, and the mist thickens until you cannot see your own hands—and when you wake, one of them is weaving the hammock around you.

Not trapping you. Reinforcing it. Adding layers, checking tension, running those too-long fingers along the cordage to feel for weakness. They are so close you could touch them, and they smell like sap and something mineral, like water that has run through stone for centuries.

Their face, when you finally see it, is human enough to hurt. The features have softened and spread, the eyes grown larger and darker to gather the dim light, the mouth reduced to something that barely needs to open because they rarely speak aloud anymore. But the proportions are still there, underneath. The echo of what they were before they gave themselves to the work.

They look at you, and their expression is—you struggle to name it. Not curiosity. Not recognition. Something older than either. The look of someone seeing a new thread appear in a pattern they thought they knew completely.

They touch your face with one finger, the tip slightly rough, slightly warm.

And then they are gone, moving away along the cable with a fluidity that makes you realize they have not been *walking* this whole time—they have been *growing*, extending themselves in the direction of travel, retracting from where they were, flowing through the weave like sap through a stem.

---

There are children among them, though "children" is wrong.

There are *newer portions.* Extensions that have not yet learned the full pattern. You see them sometimes in clusters at the node-surfaces, their bodies still more solid than their elders, their movements still jerky and discrete rather than fluid. They practice weaving on small frames, making cord from fiber they harvest from the fruiting bodies, learning the knots that carry meaning. They do not play. They do not seem unhappy. They simply work at becoming what they will eventually be, with the patience of things that have stopped counting time in human units.

The eldest among them—you can tell the elders by how thin they have become, how translucent, how difficult to distinguish from the vine-matter around them—the elders sometimes simply stop moving, settle into a junction-point, and begin to fuse. Their tissue mingles with the node-tissue. Their consciousness—if that word still applies—distributes into the structure itself. They do not die. They become load-bearing. They become the thing they spent their lives repairing.

You find, in one node, a face still visible in the fused tissue. Eyes closed. Expression peaceful. You think you can feel something when you press your hand against it—a warmth, a pressure, something that might be recognition or might just be the slow pulse of sap.

---

They are aware of the geometry. They navigate it the way you might navigate a familiar house in the dark—not by understanding it, but by having internalized its rules so deeply that understanding becomes unnecessary. They know that walking *this* direction along *this* cable will bring you to a node that is also reachable by walking the opposite direction along a different cable. They know that some junctions exist in multiple locations simultaneously, and that the version of yourself that enters may not be quite the same as the version that exits.

They leave warnings at these places. Not in language—they have almost stopped using language—but in the texture of the weave itself. Cables that have been deliberately roughened or smoothed. Knots that mean *be careful here* or *this way folds* or *you may return changed.*

Whether they understand *why* the geometry behaves this way, you cannot tell. Perhaps they once did. Perhaps that knowledge was passed down in knots for generations and then simply stopped being relevant. The world folds. They repair the folds. The reason matters less than the work.

---

They are not the original weavers. You become certain of this over time.

The tools in the oldest nodes are sized wrong—too small for their elongated hands, too rigid for their fluid bodies. The original builders were something else, something that worked with what-would-become-the-Weavers rather than being them. Partners. Teachers. The Weavers were perhaps the *material* first, or the students, or the children, and then they inherited the work when the originals... finished. Or departed. Or became something else entirely.

In the deepest chambers of the oldest nodes, there are images worked into the fiber itself—scenes depicted in raised and lowered weave, readable by touch more than sight. You run your fingers over them and feel: figures working together, some with the stretched proportions of the Weavers, some with shapes that have no analogue in your experience. You feel: a structure growing, expanding, folding in on itself. You feel: something like a departure, a handing-off, a trust.

You feel: the Weavers being left alone with the world they helped make, left to tend it forever, left to become it.

---

They are kind to you. This is the thing that surprises you most.

They did not have to leave hammocks at the junctions. They did not have to twist the fibers soft. They did not have to arrange for the fruits to grow in places a clumsy, solid, non-fluid creature could reach them. They did not have to weave paths along the cables that accommodate feet rather than flowing extension.

But they did. They have been doing it for longer than you can calculate, preparing for visitors who rarely come, maintaining hospitality as part of their endless maintenance work. The offerings at junctions include, you realize, small bundles that mean *something new is here* and *make it welcome* and *it cannot feed itself, arrange abundance.*

You are part of the pattern now, and they have woven a space for you in it.

---

At night—at what you call night, when the bioluminescence dims slightly and your body insists on sleep—you sometimes hear them singing.

Not with voices. With tension. With the vibration of cables plucked or pressed at specific frequencies, sounds that travel through the weave faster than air, sounds that arrive in your chest before they reach your ears. Harmonies that would require hundreds of them to produce, distributed across distances that should make coordination impossible, synchronized anyway through mechanisms you cannot guess at.

The songs are not for you. The songs are *work*—the sound of them collectively assessing the structure's health, locating problems by how the vibrations return, tuning the world like an instrument that must never stop playing.

But sometimes, in the spaces between the functional notes, you hear something else. Something that might be longing. Something that might be memory. Something that might be the echo of whoever they were before they became this, preserved in harmonics, carried forward through the weave forever.

---

You will not become one of them. Your body is too fixed, too committed to its current shape. You will grow old here, if you stay, and they will tend you the way they tend the structure—reinforcing what weakens, routing around what fails—until eventually you stop and they weave you into a junction-hammock that becomes a permanent installation, becomes a node, becomes part of the load-bearing structure of the world.

And you find that you are not afraid of this.

You find that you are beginning to understand why they gave themselves to the work. Why they stopped being separate. Why they let the weave grow through them, or grew through the weave, until the distinction ceased to matter.

The world is a basket, and it holds everything that exists, and it is still being made, and you can be part of the making. You can be a hand that shapes it. You can be fiber that strengthens it. You can be, eventually, structure itself—warm and yielding and full of the slow pulse of sap, offering support to whatever comes after you.

They have been offering this all along. Every hammock. Every fruit. Every softened path. An invitation, endlessly extended, endlessly patient:

*Come. Help us hold the world together. Become what lasts.*
