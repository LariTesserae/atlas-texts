## The Tenders

They emerge at dusk, when the humidity crosses its threshold and the air becomes more water than gas.

You see the first one rise from the substrate itself—the purple-gray surface parts like a wound healing in reverse, and something pulls itself up through the gap, slow and deliberate, trailing filaments that reconnect to the spongy material as soon as the body clears it. The shape is bilateral but not symmetrical: one side carries more mass, more specialized structures, as if each individual represents half of a partnership whose other half is elsewhere or everywhere or the terrace itself.

They are the color of the substrate because they are made from the substrate, or the substrate is made from them, or there was never a meaningful difference. Their bodies hold water the way the terraces hold water—saturated throughout, integral to their structure. When they move, you can see fluid shifting beneath their surfaces, redistributing with each motion like the pools you watched exchanging through the spongy ground.

The one nearest you stands perhaps a meter tall at its highest point, though "stands" isn't quite right—it rests on a base that spreads and grips and slowly merges with the terrace surface, anchoring without feet. The upper portion carries sensory structures: clusters of dark wet openings that orient toward you, then away, then toward the setting sun, reading the environment through some spectrum you can't guess at. No eyes that you recognize. No face. But attention, yes—you can feel it when the openings turn your way, a pressure like being listened to very carefully.

---

They don't speak. But they communicate constantly.

The one near you extends a limb—not an arm exactly, more a temporary allocation of mass that reaches toward a glass-stemmed plant and touches it with evident purpose. Where it touches, the plant responds: fluids move faster through the transparent stems, the broad leaves above adjust their angle by a few degrees, and the condensation pattern shifts, more water falling in one place, less in another. The Tender is gardening, you realize. It's adjusting the system, making fine corrections to a process that mostly runs itself but sometimes needs guidance.

Across the visible terraces, others are surfacing now. Dozens of them, maybe hundreds, rising from the substrate in that same slow wound-reversal, immediately beginning their work. One reattaches a hovering vine that has drifted too far from its position—it simply reaches up, and the vine reaches down, and they meet and merge briefly and separate, the vine now stable in its proper place. Another approaches one of the many-legged canopy-groomers and allows the creature to climb onto its body, carrying it to a section of leaves that has been missed, depositing it with something you could almost mistake for gentleness.

The communication happens through the substrate itself. You see it when you stop looking at individual Tenders and start watching the terrace as a whole: subtle color changes propagating through the purple-gray surface, moving from one individual to another in waves and patterns that are clearly meaningful. A ripple passes from the one near you outward across the visible terraces, and in response, several distant Tenders change their activity, moving toward a section where—now that you look—a fruiting body has begun to list, its geometric surfaces misaligned with the angle needed to catch the last light.

They work together without apparent coordination, without hierarchy, each responding to information that moves through the ground they stand on and partly are. The system thinks through them. Or they are the system thinking. Or thinking isn't the right word for what happens when a living network adjusts itself through a thousand simultaneous small corrections, each one meaningless alone, together maintaining a balance that has held for longer than you can imagine.

---

The structure you saw earlier—four walls, roofless, vines through the windows—makes sense now.

A Tender approaches it as you watch, and you understand: it isn't ruined. It was never roofed. The walls exist to create a specific microclimate, a place where humidity pools and certain rare conditions obtain. The Tender enters through an aperture that adjusts to allow it passage, and inside, it does something you can't see, tending to whatever grows in that engineered pocket of different air.

They built this. Or their ancestors built this. Or they grew it, or the distinction between building and growing has been meaningless here for so long that the concepts themselves have merged.

The spire on the distant terrace pulses, and the Tenders pause in their work, all of them simultaneously, their sensory clusters orienting toward it. Something is communicated. You feel the thrumming from the mountain's core intensify for a moment, and the Tenders respond with motion—some descending back into the substrate, others moving toward the terrace edges, a few beginning to swell with accumulated water as if preparing for something.

Night is coming. The photosynthetic processes are ending for the day, but the thermal processes will continue, the mountain exhaling its heat, the system requiring different maintenance now. The Tenders are shifting to a nighttime mode you weren't here to see before. Some of them will rest below the surface, reintegrating with the substrate that is also their food source and communication medium and perhaps their extended body. Others will remain active, responding to conditions that only matter in darkness, keeping the water moving, the heat distributed, the balance held.

One of them passes close to you on its way to whatever task the spire-pulse assigned it. The sensory clusters turn toward you, and you feel that attention again, that pressure of being listened to. It pauses.

You are something it hasn't seen before. You don't fit the patterns it knows, don't communicate through the substrate, don't serve any function in the system it maintains. It reaches toward you with an extension of mass—not threatening, not exactly welcoming, just curious, the way a gardener might pause to examine an unexpected seedling.

The extension touches your hand. It's warm and wet and faintly porous, and where it contacts your skin, you feel something like a question pressed against you without words. *What are you? What do you need? What do you do?*

You don't know how to answer in terms it would understand. You're not part of the system. You don't have a function here.

The Tender holds the contact for a long moment, reading whatever it can read from your presence, your temperature, your chemical signature, the electricity in your nerves. Then it withdraws, apparently satisfied or at least finished, and continues on its way.

You're allowed to be here. You're tolerated the way the system tolerates any harmless anomaly—noted, accounted for, left alone to be what you are while the work continues around you. The frogs call. The water drips. The substrate breathes. The Tenders move through the dusk on their ancient endless errands, and the mountain exhales its heat into the darkening sky, and everything holds together for another night because everything has learned, over millennia, how to hold together.

You are a guest in a body that doesn't notice you. You are standing in the living memory of something that forgot itself long ago and became, instead, this: a system that tends itself, that adjusts and maintains and continues, that has no purpose beyond continuing, and needs none, and will be here long after you leave, breathing, sweating, pulsing with its slow mechanical heartbeat, tending the terraces that are also its body, growing imperceptibly toward mountains it hasn't reached yet, patient as geology, alive as anything you've ever seen.
