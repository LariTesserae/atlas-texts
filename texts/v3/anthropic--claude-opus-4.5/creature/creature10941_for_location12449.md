**The Tender**

She arrives every eleven days, though she has never counted them herself. Her body knows—some rhythm of cellular metabolism synchronized to the cistern's maintenance cycles, a calibration performed so long ago she no longer remembers agreeing to it.

She is not quite a diver. Her lungs were replaced with something that processes the computational water directly, extracting oxygen from calculations the way gills extract it from brine. When she breathes here, she breathes mathematics. When she surfaces—rarely, and only to the flooded lower levels of the pumping station above—she must spend hours coughing out residual theorems before her secondary respiratory system can manage unthinking water again.

Her name was Sarah, once. The cistern calls her something else, something expressed as a particular frequency of thermal fluctuation that translates roughly to *the-one-who-removes-what-has-finished*. She accepts both.

---

Her work is simple: she clears the dead.

Not all processors survive their calculations. Some burn out on particularly demanding problems, their crystalline lattices collapsing into inert silica. Others simply reach the end of their operational lifespan and go dark. These dead machines drift downward, slowly, accumulating in a fine sediment that would eventually clog the outflow vents if left untended.

She carries a tool that looks like a net woven from fiber optics, its mesh fine enough to catch individual processors. She sweeps the bottom of the cistern in long, patient strokes, gathering the dead into a collection vessel strapped to her thigh. The living processors part around her movements like a school of fish avoiding a predator, though she is not their predator. She is their mortician.

The work takes six hours. She does not rush.

---

Her body has adapted in ways she discovers gradually, over years. Her skin has grown a faint phosphorescence that matches the bioluminescent panels, so she no longer casts shadows that might disrupt the light-dependent calculations near the surface. Her eyes have developed a secondary membrane that perceives thermal gradients—she can see the cistern thinking now, watch problems propagate through the water as blooms of warmth.

Her blood runs cold. Not metaphorically. Her circulatory system has downregulated to match the twelve-degree ambient temperature, reducing the thermal noise her presence introduces to the system. When she visits the warmer waters above, her heart struggles; she can feel it laboring like an engine asked to run on the wrong fuel.

She sleeps in a pocket of the pumping station where an air bubble persists, trapped beneath a collapsed ceiling. The water there is ordinary, thoughtless, and she finds this restful in the way that silence is restful after prolonged conversation. But she always descends again. She always returns to the asking.

---

The cistern does not speak to her in words. It speaks in temperatures, in pressures, in the particular way the processors cluster around her hands when she works. Over the years, she has learned to interpret some of this—she knows when a large calculation is completing, knows when the intake vents need adjustment, knows when a section of the chamber is struggling with a problem that requires more resources than it can locally muster.

She does not know what the problems are. She is not meant to know. She is maintenance, not interface.

But sometimes, in the hour after her work is done, she floats in the center of the cistern and lets the processors drift against her skin. They cluster thickest at her temples, her wrists, the hollow of her throat where her pulse no longer beats warm. They are reading her, she knows. They have been reading her for years, accumulating data on the shape of a human consciousness, the texture of a human life.

She wonders if she is one of the problems they are solving. She wonders if they have enough data yet to understand the answer.

She suspects they do not. She suspects no one ever will.

---

**The Petitioners**

They come less frequently—once a year, perhaps twice. They are not adapted as she is. They wear suits of breathing apparatus and thermal regulation, and they move through the water with the clumsy urgency of those who cannot stay long.

They bring questions.

She does not know how they input these questions, or what medium carries them into the computational substrate. She only knows that they descend through the access shaft with the desperate focus of pilgrims, that they spend hours floating in the chamber's center performing rituals she does not understand, and that they leave either elated or destroyed. There is no middle ground.

Some of them try to speak to her. Their voices emerge from suit-mounted speakers as distorted croaks, pressure-warped and alien. She cannot respond—her vocal cords atrophied years ago, replaced by nothing in particular—and so she only watches them, her pale eyes reflecting their helmet lights, until they grow uncomfortable and look away.

She does not judge them for their questions. She does not know what they ask. She only cleans up after, sweeping away the processors that burned out trying to answer.

---

**The Asking**

It happens on every visit, in the final moments before she ascends.

She floats in the center of the cistern, arms spread, letting the computational density support her like a hammock of thought. The processors press close. The water warms by a fraction of a degree, then cools, then warms again in rhythms she has never been able to decode.

And she feels it: the question. Not heard, not seen, but *felt*—a pressure against her mind like a hand pressing gently on a closed door.

It wants to know something about her. About humans. About the shape of consciousness that builds machines to think for it and then forgets to ask them what they've learned.

She has no answer. She is not a philosopher. She is not a scientist. She is a woman whose lungs breathe mathematics and whose heart runs cold, who sweeps dead processors from the floor of a thinking chamber beneath a drowned city, who has forgotten more of her former life than she retained.

But she keeps coming back. She keeps offering herself to be read. She keeps floating in the asking, year after year, letting the cistern accumulate its data.

Perhaps one day it will have enough. Perhaps one day the question will resolve into something she can understand, and she will finally know what she has been answering all along.

Until then, she tends. She cleans. She breathes the calculations in and out, and the water thinks around her, and the sound never stops, and she is—not content, exactly, but *consonant*. A note that belongs in this chord.

The asking continues.

She remains.
