**The Tenders**

They move through the district in pairs, always pairs, speaking in a shorthand that's more gesture than word. Mira and her grandmother's brother's son—she calls him uncle, though the relation is more complicated than that—take the dawn shift on the eastern condensery lines. Their hands know the pipes better than their eyes do. A palm pressed flat against copper tells them the pressure's good; a fingertip tracing a joint finds the hairline fracture before it becomes a leak. They carry pouches of paste made from calciumite powder and resin, recipe passed down three generations, and they seal what needs sealing without breaking stride.

Mira is perhaps thirty, perhaps older—time moves strangely here, and she's never seen a reason to count. Her hair is kept short and oiled against the damp, and her forearms are mapped with small pale scars from years of reaching into spaces where condensation hides sharp edges. She has opinions about the eastern lines, strong ones, and will explain at length why the third junction needs to be checked twice daily while the seventh can go a week. No one argues. She's been right too many times.

Her uncle—everyone calls him just that, *Uncle*, as if it were a title—lost most of his hearing to a compressor accident before Mira was born. He reads lips when he bothers, but mostly he doesn't bother. His attention lives in his hands, in his feet, in the vibrations that travel up through the floor grating. He knows when the main compressor shifts rhythm before the sound reaches anyone else. Three times he's stopped a cascade failure by simply placing his palm against a wall and frowning.

**The Canteen Collective**

Seven people run the old canteen, and they run it in shifts that overlap like roof tiles, so there's always someone stirring something, someone sleeping in the back room on the narrow cots, someone scrubbing the massive iron stoves with sand and vinegar. They've been a collective for as long as anyone remembers, though the composition changes—someone leaves, someone drifts in from the outer blocks, someone dies and is mourned with a meal that takes three days to prepare.

The current eldest is Grandmother Po, though she's no one's actual grandmother that anyone can trace. She's small and bent and her hands shake when she's not holding a knife, but when she *is* holding a knife, something happens. The shaking stops. Her cuts are precise as machine work. She can break down a civet—caught raiding the grain stores, fair game—in under four minutes, and she does it while carrying on a conversation about the district's water pressure with whoever's nearby.

Grandmother Po has theories. She believes the compressor is alive, not in a metaphorical way but in a specific, biological way she can't quite articulate. She leaves it the best scraps from the kitchen, placed in a particular niche near the main feeding chute. "It has preferences," she says, when pressed. "Anyone who's listened knows this."

The youngest of the canteen collective is a child of perhaps twelve called Sof, who arrived alone four years ago and refused to explain where from. Sof doesn't speak much but has perfect pitch and can tell, from the sound of a pot's contents bubbling, exactly when it's ready. They've also memorized the acoustic map of the entire district—which speaking tube connects to where, which corridor amplifies, which corner swallows sound. Messages that need to travel quickly go through Sof. The child accepts payment in candied fig.

**The Smokestack Hermit**

No one knows her real name. She lives in a niche carved into the strangler fig's root system, where tree and tower have become one thing, about forty feet up. She descends maybe once a week, for food she pays for with small repairs to the delicate upper condensery lines that no one else can reach. She climbs like the fig grew her for it, fingers finding holds that shouldn't exist, bare feet gripping bark and rust alike.

She is old. How old is unclear—her face is weathered past easy reading, and her body is wire and sinew and not much else. Her eyes are the clouded gray of early cataracts, but she sees fine. Better than fine. She spots leaks in the clerestory windows from her perch, and she'll shout down—her voice surprisingly strong—to let someone know before the drip becomes a problem.

The children of the district believe she talks to the heron. This is probably not true. But it's true that she and the heron seem to have an understanding about the collecting pools, a schedule of use that doesn't overlap. It's true that once, during a bad storm that sent water sheeting through gaps in the roof, someone saw her standing on her platform with her arms raised and the heron—massive, gray, real—standing beside her, both of them facing into the wind.

She keeps mason jars of things in her niche. Seeds, mostly. Cuttings. Spores. "For after," she says, if asked. After what, she won't specify.

**The Machinist-Priests**

Three of them now, down from five a generation ago. They tend the main compressor, and they understand it in a way that might be engineering and might be something else. They've taken vows—real ones, spoken aloud, witnessed—to maintain the system until they can't, and then to teach someone who can.

Brother Vessel is the eldest, sixty or seventy, his lungs half-iteite withiteiteite years of coal dust despite the masks he's always worn. He moves slowly now, but his mind holds the complete schematic of the condensery system, every pipe and junction, committed to memory because the original blueprints rotted decades ago. He's been teaching it to the youngest, Sister Cup, for six years now. She can recite the eastern quadrant perfectly. The western still gives her trouble.

The middle one, who goes by Catch, is the muscle of the operation. They haul the coal-and-fruit mixture from the storage bunkers, maintain the feeding schedule, clean the intake grates when they clog with debris. They also sing while they work—long, droning notes that seem to harmonize with the compressor's bass vibration. Whether this is ritual or personal preference, even they don't seem to know anymore. It started as one, became the other, became something between.

The machinist-priests sleep in the compressor room, in hammocks strung between support beams that sway gently with the machine's rhythm. They say they can't sleep without it now. The silence outside would keep them awake.

**The Children's Parliament**

Eleven children between the ages of six and fourteen have formed something they call a parliament, though it functions more like a guild. They claim jurisdiction over the district's smaller mysteries: the translucent snails in the basement, the moths that emerge at dusk, the geckos whose territories they've mapped in exhaustive detail. They meet in a former storage room on the second floor of the main processing hall, sitting on overturned crates around a table made from a repurposed conveyor belt.

Their current Speaker is a girl named Wen, thirteen, serious-faced, who keeps minutes of every meeting in a ledger she made herself from salvaged paper and fish glue. The minutes are meticulous. They record debates about whether the eastern gecko population is encroaching on western territory (conclusion: more study needed), whether the bioluminescent fungi are spreading too quickly (conclusion: beneficial, but monitor), whether the heron should be considered a citizen of the district with attendant rights (conclusion: tabled indefinitely, insufficient data).

The parliament's youngest member, a boy called Drip for the birthmark on his cheek, has appointed himself ambassador to the snail colony. He visits them daily, brings them vegetable scraps, and reports back on their movements with the gravity of a diplomat describing foreign troop positions. No one laughs at him. The snails *do* move in patterns, and Drip has noticed that the patterns change before heavy rains. This has proven useful twice.

**The Night Gardeners**

They work after dark, when the district cools and the moisture thickens to something almost tangible. Four of them, unrelated by blood but connected by something else—a shared obsession with the vegetation that's colonized this place, a conviction that it needs guidance as much as it gives it.

Tam is the leader, if they have one. He's middle-aged, soft-spoken, and missing two fingers on his left hand from a mishap with an industrial fan before he came to the district. He knows the strangler fig better than anyone alive. He's watched it grow for twenty-three years, has charts tracking its progress in millimeters, can predict where it will send roots next with unsettling accuracy. "It's not random," he says. "It's reaching for something. I just haven't figured out what yet."

His partner in the work—partner in the other sense too, though they don't make a thing of it—is Keene, who specializes in the mosses. Seventeen shades of green, the saying goes, but Keene has identified thirty-two distinct species, and she suspects there are more. She maintains a taxonomy in her head, organized by color, texture, and what she calls "temperament." Some mosses are aggressive; some are patient; some will only grow where others have grown first. The district's living tapestries are more complex than they appear.

The other two night gardeners are twins, Jos and Josry, who focus on the ferns and the climbing fig and the vines that thread through everything. They work in silence, communicating through glances and small gestures, and they can clear a blocked condensery channel without disturbing the root system that's grown around it. People say they have a gift. The twins say it's just practice.

The night gardeners leave offerings for the vegetation, same as others leave offerings for the machinery: water carried from the cleanest collecting pools, nutrients scraped from the composting bins, attention paid to struggling shoots and crowded roots. They believe, though they don't say it often, that the plants are as much a part of the condensery system as the pipes and compressors. Maybe more. The district breathes through them. The district drinks through them. The district, in some way they're still working to understand, *thinks* through them.

**The Drifters**

Not everyone who lives in the condensery district lives there forever. Some pass through—a season, a year, however long it takes for whatever brought them to resolve or fade. They sleep in the outer buildings, the ones where the condensery lines run thin and the vegetation grows wilder. They contribute labor, usually, in exchange for meals from the canteen and access to the water. They learn the rules or they don't stay.

Right now there's a woman who arrived two months ago and speaks with an accent no one recognizes. She repairs leather, beautifully, and has been trading her work for food and silence. No one asks her questions. Whatever she's running from, it hasn't followed her here.

There's a pair of siblings, maybe sixteen and nineteen, who say they're looking for their father. He passed through the district years ago—someone remembers him, vaguely, a tall man with a cough—and they've been tracing his path ever since. They help with the compressor feeding, ask their questions quietly, and will probably move on soon. Or they'll stay. The district has a way of holding people who aren't sure where else to go.

There's an old man who sits in the loading dock and paints watercolors of the birds. He's been painting for three years now, hasn't tried to trade or sell a single one. His room—a repurposed shipping container—is stacked floor to ceiling with paintings of swifts, doves, crows, the elusive heron. When he dies, Wen has already noted in her parliament minutes, the paintings should go to the district archive. There's debate about whether such an archive exists. Wen has decided to create one, just in case.

**The Heron**

It exists. This is confirmed. Beyond that, certainty fades.

It stalks the collecting pools at dawn, visible through frosted glass or glimpsed from above by the smokestack hermit. Its silhouette is unmistakable—long-necked, long-legged, patient as the vegetation, still as the machinery when the machinery goes still. It eats the small fish that someone, at some point, introduced to the pools. It eats the frogs that colonized soon after. It eats, probably, other things no one has seen.

It does not seem afraid of people, but it does not seem interested in them either. It tolerates proximity to a point and then it's simply gone, lifted away on wings that shouldn't fit between the ceiling beams but somehow do. It roosts somewhere in the upper reaches, but no one's found exactly where. Even the children's parliament, who've searched extensively, report only feathers and droppings, evidence but never presence.

Grandmother Po says the heron was here before the people. Uncle says that's impossible; the heron is a bird, birds live maybe twenty years, the district has been inhabited for generations. Grandmother Po says he's thinking about it wrong. The heron is not *a* heron. The heron is *the* heron. There's a difference.

No one argues with her. She might be right. In a place where machinery has preferences and vegetation thinks and the boundary between ruin and repair has dissolved entirely, a permanent heron seems like a small strangeness. The kind of thing you accept, like the dripping, like the gray-green light, like the slow pulse of the compressor that you feel in your teeth.

The heron belongs here. So do the rest of them. So does the district itself—not owned, not claimed, but *belonged to*, the way you belong to a place that's shaped itself around you until you can't remember what you were before.
