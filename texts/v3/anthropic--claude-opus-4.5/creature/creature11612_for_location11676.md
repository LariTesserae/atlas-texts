## The Tenders of the Ninth Terrace

**The Root-Readers**

They move along the walls at dawn, before the mist finishes gathering, pressing their palms flat against the ceramic brick where the fig roots have worked their way into the joints. Their fingertips are calloused in patterns that match the root systems they monitor—decades of touch have taught the skin to recognize what the eyes cannot see. They are feeling for the subtle shift that means a root has found a void, a crack, a weakness that could become a collapse in ten years or fifty.

The Root-Readers do not speak while they work. They hum—a low, continuous tone that they claim helps them feel the vibrations of growth, though whether this is technique or tradition no one can say anymore. Their families have done this work since the Fifth Terrace was the highest, and the knowledge has compressed like the terraces themselves, becoming denser and less explicable with each generation.

When they find a problem, they do not fix it. They mark the spot with a particular ochre paste and move on. The fixing belongs to others. The Root-Readers only read.

---

**The Cistern-Keepers**

Beneath the bronze grates, in the cool wet dark, the Cistern-Keepers spend their days listening. They sit on stone ledges worn smooth by generations of sitting, their feet dangling above water that might be decades old or might have fallen as mist this very morning, and they listen for the particular sounds that mean health or sickness in a cistern.

The good sounds: the slow drip of collection, the gentle lap of water finding its level, the echo that indicates proper depth. The bad sounds: the hollow resonance of a cracked wall, the gurgle of contamination, the silence that means a channel has blocked somewhere above.

They are pale from their time underground, and they smell of wet stone and the particular mineral sweetness of ancient water. They speak in murmurs, because the cisterns amplify everything, and a loud voice becomes a painful boom in the enclosed space. Their children grow up whispering and only learn to project their voices if they leave the family trade—which few do. The cisterns need their keepers, and the keepers need their cisterns. It is a marriage that outlasts the individual terms of any human life.

---

**The Moth-Fathers**

These are elderly men, mostly, who have failed at other trades or outlived their usefulness in more demanding work. They tend the bioluminescent beetles that light the nighttime streets—but they call them moths, have always called them moths, despite the beetles' obvious beetle-ness. The name is a fossil from some earlier illumination technology, perhaps, or simply a preference that calcified into tradition.

The Moth-Fathers carry their charges in wooden cases lined with damp moss, visiting each street lantern in the hour before dusk to check the beetles' health, replace the dead, redistribute the population so no street is too bright or too dim. They know each beetle by its particular shade of glow—some blue-green, some amber, some a pale rose that the Ninth Terrace prizes for its rarity.

They are the closest thing the terrace has to philosophers. The long slow walks between lanterns give them time to think, and the beetles' soft glow gives them something to think about. They will stop to discuss, at length, whether the light the beetles produce is truly theirs or whether they are merely releasing light they collected during the day. They will argue about whether a beetle replaced is the same lantern or a different one. They will wonder aloud whether the beetles experience their own luminescence as beautiful.

No one else cares about these questions. The Moth-Fathers care enough for everyone.

**The Joint-Families**

Three, sometimes four generations living in the same compound, their homes built around a shared courtyard that exists primarily to hold their particular garden. Each Joint-Family is responsible for a section of terrace vegetation—perhaps two hundred yards of wall, a dozen rooftop cisterns, the fruit trees along a particular street. The knowledge of what grows there, what should grow there, what tried to grow there and failed, passes from the dying to the living in continuous oral transmission.

The eldest of the Joint-Family rarely leaves the courtyard anymore. She—it is usually she—sits in the shade of a tree her grandmother planted and directs the work through intermediaries. Her daughters and sons climb the walls and prune the vines. Her grandchildren carry water and chase away the birds that would eat the ripening figs. Her great-grandchildren play in the courtyard dirt and absorb, without knowing they are absorbing, the names of plants and the rhythms of growth and the particular way their family has always done things.

When the eldest dies, there is a period of uncertainty. The next-eldest knows the knowledge but has not yet settled into the authority. Decisions that were automatic become deliberate. The garden notices—or seems to notice—and grows slightly wild for a season, testing the new leadership. Then equilibrium returns. The Joint-Family continues. The garden continues. The terrace continues.

**The Below-Traders**

They make the journey down—all the way down, through the Eighth Terrace and the Seventh and on through terraces that have numbers but no longer have names anyone remembers. They carry what the upper terraces produce in excess: dried figs, beetle-shell dye, the particular ceramic glazes that only the Ninth Terrace workshops can achieve. They return with what the upper terraces lack: metal ore,iteite heite wood, news.

The journey takes eleven days in each direction, if nothing goes wrong. Things often go wrong. The Below-Traders are missing fingers, sometimes hands, sometimes eyes. They walk with limps from falls on stairs that have not been maintained since the last dynasty. They tell stories about the lower terraces that no one quite believes but no one quite disbelieves either—flooded passages where fish swim through ancient bedrooms, fungal forests growing in the eternal darkness of collapsed sections, the scratching sounds that come from behind walls in the deep places.

They are not respected, exactly. Their work is necessary but uncomfortable—it reminds the Ninth Terrace that there is a below, that the heap of history beneath their feet contains things that are not safely compressed into foundation. But they are valued. When a Below-Trader returns from a journey, the market crowds around to hear what they have brought and what they have seen. For a few days, they are the center of attention. Then they rest, and prepare, and descend again.

**The Something-Hunters**

They do not call themselves this. They do not call themselves anything, because to name what they do would be to acknowledge what they hunt, and no one wants to acknowledge it.

They are young men and women, mostly, with the kind of restless energy that the terrace's slow rhythms cannot absorb. They carry long hooked poles and move in groups of four or five, patrolling the edges where the Ninth Terrace meets the passages leading down. They watch for the scratches that appear sometimes on the tunnel walls. They listen for the sounds that do not belong to the normal soundscape of dripping water and settling stone.

When a goat goes missing, they are the ones who descend to look for it—not to recover the goat, which is certainly dead, but to determine how far up the something has come. They mark the walls with a particular sign that means *this far and no farther*. They do not explain what the sign is supposed to accomplish. Perhaps it is a warning. Perhaps it is a prayer. Perhaps it is simply a way of feeling like they have done something.

They do not talk about what they have seen in the lower passages. When pressed, they say only that the something is large, and patient, and not interested in coming fully into the light. This is enough. This is more than anyone wants to know.

**The Mist-Catchers**

Children, mostly. Their job is to move through the terraces in the early morning, when the mist is thickest, carrying fine-mesh nets stretched on circular frames. They sweep the nets through the air, collecting the water droplets that have not yet settled on leaves or stone, and empty their catch into small clay vessels which they carry to the nearest cistern grate.

It is not efficient. The nets catch perhaps a cup of water per hour of work. But the work teaches the children to understand the mist—where it gathers thickest, when it begins to thin, how the thermal currents from below push it into particular corners and alcoves. By the time they are old enough to leave the Mist-Catchers and join their family's real work, they have an intuitive map of the terrace's water system that no amount of adult instruction could provide.

The Mist-Catchers are organized into loose gangs based on neighborhood, and they compete fiercely over territory. Disputes are settled by races—up the long stairs to the Tenth Terrace and back, the winner's gang claiming the contested corner for the season. The adults pretend not to notice these competitions, but they remember their own, and they watch with carefully hidden interest to see which children are fastest, which are cleverest, which are natural leaders. The Mist-Catchers are being evaluated for their future roles, and everyone knows it except the Mist-Catchers themselves.

**The Machine-Tenders**

Somewhere below the Ninth Terrace, in chambers cut into the compressed rubble of earlier construction, the compression engine cycles. And in those chambers, covered in grease and bronze dust, the Machine-Tenders live their lives.

They are a caste apart. They marry among themselves. They speak a dialect so thick with technical terminology that outsiders can barely understand them. They smell of machine oil and hot metal, and they move with the particular careful grace of people who work near mechanisms that could crush a hand or worse.

Their work is sacred in a way that the terrace's nominal religion does not acknowledge. The engine must run. If the engine stops, the water stops rising, and if the water stops rising, the Ninth Terrace dies. The Machine-Tenders know this with a bone-deep certainty that shapes everything about them. They do not take holidays. They do not drink alcohol. They do not allow visitors into the engine chambers. They maintain and they repair and they watch, and when a part wears out, they fabricate a replacement using skills passed down from the original builders through an unbroken chain of apprenticeship.

They believe—though they would never say this to an outsider—that the engine has a soul. That it wants to run. That their job is not to force it to function but to remove the obstacles that prevent it from doing what it desires. This belief makes them excellent at their work. The engine has not stopped in living memory. The engine will not stop.

**The Color-Makers**

In workshops scattered across the Ninth Terrace, they grind and mix and fire. The red glaze that makes the rooftops glow in the afternoon light. The purple-black dye extracted from a particular beetle's shell. The ochre paste the Root-Readers use to mark problems. The blue-green pigment that decorates the cistern grates. Every color the terrace displays was made by human hands, and most of those hands belong to the Color-Makers.

They are secretive about their processes. Each family guards its formulas, passing them only to children who have proven themselves worthy—which means patient, careful, and able to keep a secret. A Color-Maker who revealed a rival family's formula would be shunned, not for the betrayal exactly, but for demonstrating the kind of character that could not be trusted with color.

The competition between Color-Maker families is intense but slow. A new red glaze might take a decade to develop, another decade to perfect, another decade to become established in the market. The current palette of the Ninth Terrace reflects victories and defeats that occurred before anyone now living was born. The Color-Makers are playing a game that spans generations, and they are playing to win, even if winning means their great-grandchildren will be the ones to enjoy the victory.

**The Edge-Sitters**

At the rim of the Ninth Terrace, where the constructed ground falls away into the haze below, there are benches. Old people sit on these benches and look outward at the other peaks, the fire-scarred one and the wild one and the living one that catches the same light.

They are not a profession. They are not a caste. They are simply people who have reached the age where the terrace's endless busyness no longer claims them, and they have chosen to spend their remaining time watching the distance. They remember when there was trade between the peaks—or they remember their parents remembering, which amounts to the same thing. They speculate about what life might be like on the other mountains. They watch the signal towers for messages that never come, or that come so rarely that each one is an event to be discussed for months afterward.

The Edge-Sitters know things. They have lived long enough to see the slow cycles of the terrace complete themselves—buildings that rose in their childhood falling in their old age, families that were powerful becoming obscure, technologies that were cutting-edge becoming so integrated into daily life that no one notices them anymore. Younger people come to them with questions, sometimes, and the Edge-Sitters answer if they feel like it, or pretend to be asleep if they don't.

They will die on these benches, some of them, looking outward at the mountains they never visited, thinking thoughts no one will ever know.

**The Night-Walkers**

After the Moth-Fathers have lit the streets and the Joint-Families have retreated to their courtyards and the markets have fallen silent, there are still people moving through the Ninth Terrace. They walk without apparent purpose, without destination, their footsteps soft on the worn stone. They pause to touch the beetle-lit lanterns. They sit by the cistern grates and listen to the water. They climb to the high places and look down at the glow of the streets below.

No one knows why they walk. Perhaps they cannot sleep. Perhaps they are keeping watch against some undefined threat. Perhaps they simply prefer the night-terrace to the day-terrace—the softened colors, the cooler air, the absence of urgency.

They recognize each other, the Night-Walkers. They nod as they pass, but they do not stop to talk. Talking would be a day-activity, and the night has its own rules. They walk, and they watch, and eventually, as the eastern sky begins to lighten, they return to wherever they came from and sleep through the morning while the Mist-Catchers run their nets and the Root-Readers press their palms to the walls and the Ninth Terrace begins another day of being itself.

---

**The Unnamed**

There are people on the Ninth Terrace who belong to no category. A woman who spends her days weaving baskets that no one buys, her fingers moving through patterns she learned from a mother who learned them from a mother who may have come from one of the other peaks. A man who sits in the same corner of the same market square, saying nothing, watching everything, present for so long that his presence has become invisible. A child who wanders alone, belonging to no visible family, fed by no particular household, surviving by some mechanism no one has ever investigated.

The terrace absorbs them. They are part of the texture, like the beetles, like the mist, like the distant sound of the compression engine cycling. They do what they do. They exist as they exist. They will be replaced, when they are gone, by others who are equally unnamed, equally present, equally part of the ongoing process of the Ninth Terrace being itself.

---

The people of the Ninth Terrace do not think of themselves as living in an extraordinary place. They live in the only place they have ever known, doing work their families have always done, moving through rhythms that feel as natural as breathing. The made mountain is not a wonder to them; it is simply the ground. The ancient engines are not marvels; they are infrastructure. The gardens that cascade from every wall are not beautiful; they are necessary.

And yet, and yet. Sometimes a Mist-Catcher pauses at the top of the long stairs, breathing hard from the race, and sees the light hit the terraces below in a way that makes her catch her breath. Sometimes a Machine-Tender emerges from the engine chambers and feels the heat of the sun on his face and realizes he has not been outside in days. Sometimes an Edge-Sitter watches the signal tower on the distant peak flash a message no one can read anymore and wonders what it would have been like to live in a time when the peaks still spoke to each other.

These moments pass. The work continues. The terrace continues. The people continue, tending and repairing and replacing, each generation teaching the next, the whole vast heap of history settling imperceptibly beneath their feet as they add their own small weight to the accumulation.
