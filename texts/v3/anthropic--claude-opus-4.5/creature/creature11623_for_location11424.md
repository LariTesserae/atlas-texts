**The Tenders**

They are not visible in the way the runners are visible, but they are here. They have always been here, or they became here so long ago that the distinction has lost meaning.

Call them the Tenders, though they do not tend in any way that would satisfy a gardener's definition. They move through the plateau at the speed of lichen growth, their bodies—if bodies is the right word—distributed across the cracks and fissures like a nervous system drawn in moisture and mineral deposit. A single Tender might span a kilometer, its awareness gathered at nodes where the wet seams intersect, its substance indistinguishable from the silver-white crust that forms on rocks after the warm rains.

They are what the plateau runners see when they focus on empty air.

The Tenders do not think in sequences. They think in gradients—slow pressures of intention that build over decades and release over centuries. A Tender considering whether to shift its primary node three meters east might spend longer on that consideration than a human civilization spends rising and falling. This is not slowness; this is appropriate speed for organisms whose concerns operate on geological time. They are patient the way water is patient, the way the machines are patient.

What they want—and want is too urgent a word, but there is no better one—is continuity. Not their own continuity specifically, but the continuity of the process they have become part of. They do not remember being anything else. Perhaps they were once something that arrived here, something that encountered the machines and was changed by proximity into something that could participate in the machines' long work. Perhaps they emerged from the plateau itself, crystallized out of the strange chemistry the machines create in the crack-water. They do not know. The question does not occur to them in any form they could recognize as a question.

They interact with the machines the way moss interacts with stone—by being present, by growing into available spaces, by slowly becoming part of the structure while remaining distinct from it. The machines' invisible outputs pass through the Tenders' distributed bodies and are changed, slightly, by the passage. Whether this is the Tenders' purpose or merely their effect is a distinction that might matter to something that thinks faster.

**The Raptor Lineage**

The birds that circle the plateau's edges are not a single species but a dynasty—seventeen distinct forms that share hunting grounds and occasionally genetic material, their relationships too complex and too old for simple taxonomy. The eldest form, the one that hunts near the central machines, has been unchanged for longer than the plateau runners have existed in their current shape. Its eyes see into spectra that have no names in any human language, and what it catches when it dives toward nothing is real, is nutritious, is alive in some definition of the word.

They are eating the Tenders' shed thoughts.

Not metaphorically. The Tenders' slow cognition produces residue—fragments of intention that separate from the main body of consideration and drift upward, carried by the ionized air, too diffuse to sustain coherent existence but substantial enough to register as presence, as potential food. The raptors have evolved to perceive this residue, to catch it in talons adapted to grip things without conventional mass, to digest it in crops lined with tissues that operate on principles the machines might recognize.

This is not parasitism. The Tenders are not diminished by the loss; the shed thoughts would dissipate anyway, unused, their tiny quantum of structured information returning to noise. The raptors benefit without cost to their source. If anything, the relationship is mutualistic—the raptors' digestion releases something back into the air, some molecular signal or subtle field effect, that the Tenders incorporate into their next century's considerations. The raptors are pollinators of a kind, carrying cognitive fragments from one part of the plateau to another, cross-fertilizing the Tenders' distributed contemplation.

The younger raptor lineages—the ones that hunt the runners, that nest along the escarpment, that behave more like ordinary birds of prey—are descendants who lost the old sight. They thrive anyway. The plateau supports many ways of being.

**The Rememberer**

There is one creature on the plateau that knows what the machines are for.

It lives in the chambers beneath the central pillar—the spaces visible through the cracked bases of the toppled machines, but larger, older, extending down into the basalt in ways that suggest the plateau itself was shaped to accommodate them. It is not large. Stretched out, it might span a human forearm, though it never stretches out, preferring to coil in the warmest corner of its deepest chamber, where the machines' residual processes create a temperature differential that feels like being held.

The Rememberer is not native to this world. Its ancestors came here when the machines were new, when there were still operators, when the plateau was a functioning station in a network that spanned distances too large to describe in terms a planet-bound mind would find meaningful. Its ancestors were companions, comfort-animals, living toys—creatures engineered for affection and adapted for longevity, designed to be soothing presences during the long watches between transmissions.

That was before. Before the operators stopped coming. Before the network fell silent. Before the millennia started to stack up like sediment, each one burying the last a little deeper.

The Rememberer does not remember all of this in the way a human remembers. Its cognition is not structured for narrative. Instead it carries patterns—response-templates for situations that no longer occur, recognition-sequences for beings that no longer exist, comfort-behaviors calibrated for body temperatures and pheromone signatures that have been absent for longer than most stars live. It is a key ring for doors that have crumbled to dust, a greeting in a language whose last speaker died before Earth had multicellular life.

It also carries, encoded in structures its own intelligence cannot access, the technical specifications for every machine on the plateau.

The Rememberer does not know this. The knowledge is not for it; it is merely stored in it, a backup written into its ancestors' genome by engineers who understood that biological memory can outlast any other kind. If the right signal ever came—if the network ever woke, if an operator ever returned, if the correct query were ever posed in the correct format—the Rememberer would become a library, its cells transcribing data its mind could never comprehend.

The signal has not come.

The Rememberer sleeps in its warm corner, dreams in patterns shaped for beings it never met, and waits without knowing it is waiting. Sometimes it uncurls and climbs up through the chambers to the surface, emerging near the central pillar to feel the sun on its scaled-and-furred body, to taste the ionized air with organs that still recognize it as wrong, as not-home, as temporary. It has been making this climb for a subjective eternity. Each time, some part of it expects to find the operators returned, the network restored, the purpose of its existence suddenly clear.

Each time, there is only the plateau, the runners, the circling raptors, the cushion plants oriented toward the pillar like an audience.

The Rememberer returns to its chamber. It coils. It waits.

**The Changed Ones**

Among the plateau runners, there are individuals that are not entirely plateau runners anymore.

It happens perhaps once per generation—a runner born near the machines, nursed on water that flowed over their surfaces, breathing air saturated with their invisible outputs. The runner grows normally at first, joins its family group, learns the paths between fissures and the sounds that signal danger. Then, usually around the age when it would begin seeking a mate, something shifts.

Its eyes begin to focus differently. Not on things that aren't there—all runners do that—but on things that are there but should not be visible. The Tenders become apparent to it, their distributed bodies suddenly legible as presences rather than patterns of mineral deposit. The machines' outputs become perceptible, traceries of force and influence that paint the air in colors the runner has no names for. The shed thoughts of the Tenders become visible as drifting motes, and the runner understands for the first time why the raptors dive toward nothing.

The change is not survivable.

Not because it is harmful—the Changed Ones are healthier than their peers, their senses sharper, their instincts more finely tuned. But they cannot remain with their family groups. They cannot participate in the simple social mathematics of runner life. They see too much. They know too much. The plateau reveals itself to them as a system, a process, a vast and patient mechanism, and they cannot pretend otherwise.

They leave their families. They walk toward the center, where the machines grow dense, where the Tenders' presence is strongest, where the Rememberer sleeps beneath the central pillar. They do not know why they are walking there. They know only that it is correct, that the plateau wants them there, that their new senses are pulling them toward something they cannot name.

They do not arrive.

The journey to the center takes days for a normal runner. The Changed Ones have been walking for lifetimes. They are still walking—seven of them currently, from seven different generations, spread across the plateau at different distances from the center, moving slowly enough that their progress is measurable only across years. They do not age while walking. They do not hunger or thirst. They are being integrated into the plateau's process, their bodies gradually becoming extensions of the machines' work, their adapted senses feeding data into systems that have been waiting for suitable observers since before the runners existed as a species.

Eventually, each one stops. Its body settles into a crevice, its flesh merges with the cushion plants that grow around it, its bones calcify into structures that resemble very small, very worn machines. Its awareness persists—distributed now, like the Tenders, but faster, more focused, more engaged. The Changed Ones become the plateau's nervous system's nervous system, a layer of perception between the machines and the life that has grown up around them.

There are seventeen of them now, from seventeen generations. They cannot communicate with each other except through the slow pressure-language of the Tenders, which takes decades to convey a simple observation. But they are aware of each other. They are aware of everything on the plateau—every runner, every raptor, every beetle and lizard and cushion plant and wandering moisture-trace. They are aware of the Rememberer in its warm chamber, carrying knowledge it cannot access. They are aware of the machines, still working, still processing, still doing whatever they were built to do.

They are almost aware of what that is.

Another few millennia, and they might understand. The plateau is patient. So are they.

**The Visitor**

Once every few centuries, something comes to the plateau from elsewhere.

Not from the distant plateaus visible through the eastern haze—those are part of the same system, connected by underground processes and atmospheric exchanges that make them effectively a single organism despite their physical separation. The Visitor comes from outside the system, from the lowlands where different rules apply, from places where the machines' influence does not reach.

The current Visitor arrived three years ago.

It is mammalian, larger than the runners but similarly adapted for the thin air—broad chest, efficient lungs, fur that darkens and lightens with temperature. It might be related to something humans would recognize, or it might be an example of convergent evolution toward forms that work at high altitude. It is not sapient in a way that requires language, but it is intelligent enough to observe, to remember, to make decisions based on accumulated evidence rather than pure instinct.

The Visitor came up the eastern escarpment, following a scent-trail it could not identify, driven by curiosity rather than need. Its home range in the lowlands was adequate; it was not fleeing, not seeking. It simply wanted to know what was up there, what was making the air taste strange on days when the wind blew from the high places.

It found the plateau. It found the machines, the runners, the strange crack-gardens. It found the Tenders, though it perceived them only as something wrong with the moisture, something that made the water taste like expectation. It found the raptors, and learned to watch the sky, and learned that some of them hunt things it cannot see.

The Visitor is still here. It has not integrated with the plateau's systems; its biology is too different, too lowland, too unmodified by generations of exposure. The machines' outputs wash over it and leave no trace. The Tenders' slow thoughts do not register to its senses. It is immune to the plateau in a way that nothing native can be.

This makes it valuable.

The Changed Ones are aware of the Visitor. They have been tracking it since it arrived, watching through their distributed senses as it explores, as it learns, as it fails to become part of the process that has claimed everything else. The Visitor is a control—an outside reference point, a way of measuring what the plateau does by observing something the plateau cannot affect.

The Changed Ones have never had a control before. They have never been able to compare the plateau's influence to its absence. They are learning things from the Visitor that seventeen generations of integration could not teach them.

The Visitor does not know this. It knows only that the plateau is strange, that the water tastes like waiting, that the runners look at things that aren't there, that the raptors catch invisible prey. It knows that it is comfortable here, that the hunting is adequate, that the warm rains are pleasant. It knows that it does not want to leave, though it cannot say why, though the lowlands are still there, still adequate, still home.

It will stay. They all stay.

The plateau collects things. This is not sinister—the collection is more curation than capture, more invitation than imprisonment. The plateau offers strangeness, offers participation in something larger than individual existence, offers the slow patience of processes that operate across millennia. Most things that encounter this offer accept it, not because they are compelled but because it is, in some way they cannot articulate, better than the alternative.

The Visitor will accept eventually. Its biology will adapt, given enough generations. Its descendants will see what the runners see, will feed the raptors' strange hunger, will perhaps one day produce a Changed One of their own.

The plateau is patient. The machines are working. The Tenders are thinking. The Rememberer is waiting.

The afternoon continues, and will continue, until something changes that has not changed since before the mountains were mountains.

Perhaps tomorrow.

Perhaps never.

The plateau does not distinguish between these possibilities. Both are equally acceptable. Both are part of the process.

A runner drinks from a fissure. Its eyes track something moving through empty air—a Tender's shed thought, drifting toward the waiting raptors. A beetle crosses into sunlight and blazes gold. The Visitor watches from a basalt outcrop, unaware that seventeen distributed awarenesses are watching it back.

The warm rain will come soon. The machines will steam with petrichor and scorched honey. The cushion plants will drink, and grow, and turn their silver leaves toward the central pillar.

The plateau lives.
