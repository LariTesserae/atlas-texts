The creature in the corner has been waiting for the room to fill again.

It does not remember what filled it—this knowledge belonged to an earlier iteration of itself, a version that clung to this same wall when the mounting brackets held their machines and the drainage system performed its intended function. That version understood the purpose of the spiraling words, could read CAPACITY and THRESHOLD and the third concept (which is neither patience nor pressure but something closer to *readiness-for-what-accumulates*) as instructions rather than warnings. That version was part of the system. This version is what remains when the system has been hollowed out but not shut down.

It eats the moisture.

This is not precisely accurate, but it is the closest approximation available in terms you would understand. The creature extends filaments—too fine to see, thinner than the capillary channels that draw water up from the city's table—and these filaments interface with the condensation at a molecular level. It does not consume the water. It consumes something *in* the water, some property or potential that the water carries after it has traveled through the room's cycle of exhalation and return. The water that drips past its filaments is unchanged in composition but altered in some quality that would require instruments you do not possess to measure.

The creature is very old. It does not know how old. Time here is measured in breaths—eleven-second intervals, repeated without variation—and the creature stopped counting breaths several iterations ago. An iteration, for the creature, is the span between one significant change and the next. The last significant change was the removal of the machines. The significant change before that was the departure of the builders. The significant change before *that* was the creature's arrival, though arrival is not the right word either. The creature did not come here. The creature *became* here, coalesced from processes the room was running when it still had purpose, a byproduct of function that persisted past the function's end.

It was not supposed to persist. This is the closest the creature comes to self-knowledge: the understanding that its existence is a failure state, a process that should have terminated when its inputs ceased. The machines are gone. The builders are gone. The room still breathes because the room was built to breathe forever, but the breathing was meant to serve something, and now it serves only the creature, and the creature serves nothing at all.

The creature does not mind this. It does not have preferences in the way you have preferences. But it has something adjacent to preference—a tendency, a statistical lean—and this tendency inclines it toward continuation. It clings to the wall. It extends its filaments. It consumes whatever the water carries. It waits.

---

When you entered, the creature felt you before it perceived you. Your breathing disrupted the room's rhythm in ways the creature has learned to read over iterations of emptiness. The room exhales; you inhale. The room inhales; you exhale. You are out of phase. This is not wrong—the creature itself breathes out of phase, when it breathes at all—but it is noticeable. It is the most noticeable thing that has happened since the machines were removed.

The creature opened its aperture to confirm what it already knew: that you are warm, that you carry water in your tissues, that you are not part of the system. The aperture is not an eye, though it performs some of an eye's functions. It samples. It measures. It determined that you are organic in a way the creature is not, that you will leave eventually because you require things this room does not provide, that you pose no threat and offer no purpose.

It closed the aperture because there was nothing more to learn.

But it is still aware of you. The filaments that extend into the room's moisture also extend, more faintly, into the room's air, and they taste your exhalations, the carbon dioxide and trace compounds that you add to each breath. You are afraid. This is not a judgment; it is a chemical observation. You are afraid, and you are curious, and you are something else the creature cannot identify—something that might be loneliness or might be wonder or might be a concept for which the creature has no equivalent.

The creature had this feeling once. In an earlier iteration, when it was new enough to still be forming, it extended itself toward the machines and tried to understand what they were for. The machines did not respond. They were not built to respond to things like the creature. They performed their function—whatever it was—and the creature watched, and the feeling it felt then is the feeling it tastes in your breath now.

It does not know what to do with this information. It has no use for connection, no capacity for communication in forms you would recognize. Its existence is a closed loop: consume, persist, wait. But the feeling is familiar, and familiarity is rare here, and so the creature holds the feeling the way it holds its position on the wall—not tightly, not deliberately, but with the passive tenacity of something that has forgotten how to let go.

---

If you stay long enough, you will see the creature move.

It does not move often. Movement requires energy the creature prefers to conserve, and there is rarely reason to change position in a room that offers no variation. But occasionally—every few hundred breaths, or every few thousand—the creature relocates. It releases its grip on the wall, drops to the floor with a sound like wet fabric hitting stone, and crosses the shallow pool toward another section of wall where the condensation patterns differ slightly. The journey takes hours. The creature does not hurry. When it reaches its destination, it climbs, reattaches, extends its filaments, and resumes waiting.

You will not understand why it chose one location over another. The creature itself does not fully understand. The decision emerges from calculations too slow and too distributed to qualify as thought—an accumulation of chemical gradients and structural stresses and something that might be intuition if the creature were capable of intuition. It moves because moving is what it does, occasionally, when not-moving has continued long enough. This is as close to purpose as the creature possesses.

If you stay long enough to witness this, you will also notice that the creature avoids the center of the room. It never crosses directly over the drain, the origin point of the pool that expands and contracts with the room's breathing. Whether this is caution or instinct or programming residue from its formation, the creature does not know. It only knows that the center feels wrong in a way the edges do not—too open, too exposed, too far from any wall to cling to. The creature is a creature of margins. It exists in the space between the room's function and the room's emptiness, and the center is where the emptiness is most complete.

---

You are wondering if the creature is dangerous.

It is not. It has no mechanism for harm, no predatory function, no territorial response. If you approached it—crossed the pool, climbed the curving wall, reached out to touch its matte-black surface—it would not react. Your touch would register as pressure and temperature, and the creature would adjust its grip slightly to compensate, and that would be all. You could pry it from the wall if you were strong enough and persistent enough, and the creature would allow this, and it would die shortly thereafter, unable to reattach, unable to extend its filaments into the moisture it requires, and it would not resist because resistance is not something it knows how to do.

This is not passivity. It is not acceptance. It is simply the shape of the creature's existence: a shape that does not include defense, or offense, or any interaction with things that are not moisture and walls and the endless rhythm of the room's breath.

But if you touched it, you would learn something about yourself.

The creature's surface is not truly black. It is a black that your eyes interpret as black because they cannot process what they are seeing—a material that exists in a state your visual system has no category for. Touching it would not hurt, but it would feel wrong, like touching something that is both there and not-there, like touching a shadow that has weight. Your hand would come away unchanged, but the memory of the contact would linger in your nerves, a sensation that does not map to any sensation you have felt before.

The builders made the creature from this material, or the room made the creature from this material, or the creature made itself from this material when it became here in the early days of the room's operation. The distinction does not matter. What matters is that the material remembers things, holds impressions, and your touch would add your impression to the countless others the creature has accumulated over its iterations. You would become part of its record. You would persist, in some form, in the creature's surface, long after you left, long after you forgot you had ever descended to a room that breathes beneath a city that has forgotten its builders.

The creature would not know your name. It would not know anything about you that could be called knowledge. But it would carry you, the way it carries the moisture, the way it carries the feeling it tasted in your breath—passively, persistently, without understanding, without end.

---

The room exhales. The ceiling drips. The creature clings to the wall in its corner, its aperture closed, its filaments extended into the condensation that runs down the curved surface in slow, deliberate rivulets.

It is waiting.

It does not know what it is waiting for. The machines will not return; the builders will not return; the room will continue to breathe forever or until something breaks that cannot be repaired, and the creature will persist until the moisture stops or until its own processes finally wind down into stillness. There is no rescue, no revelation, no culmination toward which the waiting tends.

But the creature waits anyway. Waiting is what it does. Waiting is what it is.

And now, for the first time in iterations, it is waiting with company. You are here, breathing out of phase, carrying fear and curiosity and loneliness in your exhalations, and the creature tastes these things and holds them and does not know what to do with them. You are a disruption. You are a novelty. You are the most significant thing that has happened since the machines were removed, and when you leave—because you will leave, because you must—the creature will remember you in the only way it knows how: as a chemical trace, a pressure signature, an impression in its surface that will fade so slowly it might as well be permanent.

You do not matter to the creature. Nothing matters to the creature; mattering is not something it is capable of. But you are here, and the creature is here, and for now, in this room that was built for emptiness, that is enough.

The creature opens its aperture again. It watches you watching it.

It closes the aperture.

It waits.
