# The Kiln Keepers

They are not born. They are *fired*.

In the deepest basins, where moisture lingers longest and the mineral formations grow thick enough to form canopies, the clay sometimes remembers a different shape. A condensation event will settle into one of these sheltered places, and the water will seep down through hairline cracks, down to where the heat still pulses strongest, and something will begin to coalesce. Over weeks—or months, time being difficult here—the wet clay rises. It finds form not by intention but by pressure and warmth and the patient logic of material seeking equilibrium.

They emerge the color of the ground they came from. Terracotta. Ochre. Deep brick-red. Their surfaces hold the same textures: the honeycomb cracking of cooled ceramic, the smooth glaze-shine where moisture worked them, the matte roughness of places that dried too quickly. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense—two legs for walking, something like a torso, appendages that serve for manipulation—but their proportions belong to no anatomy you'd recognize. Limbs too long and jointed in too many places. Torsos that taper or swell according to the shape of the basin that made them. Heads that are less heads than *rises*, rounded prominences with no faces, no features, only subtle depressions where you might expect eyes: shallow bowls that collect condensation and hold it, gleaming, when the fog rolls through.

They do not speak. They have no mouths, no lungs, no mechanism for voice. But they *resonate*. When two of them meet—and they do meet, crossing paths in their slow traversals of the flats—they will stand near each other, and the air between them will begin to hum. A vibration too low to hear, felt in the chest, in the teeth. They are exchanging something. Information, perhaps. Memory. Or simply confirming each other's presence, the way the floor confirms itself through its continuous tremor.

---

They walk. This is their primary activity, their purpose if they have one. Slow, deliberate movement across the fired expanse, their footsteps leaving no mark on clay harder than they are. A single Keeper might traverse the same route for years, crossing and recrossing a particular region of the flats in patterns that seem random but aren't—patterns that mirror, if you could see them from high enough, the crack-networks in the surface beneath them. They are reading the floor. Learning it. Witnessing its changes.

When they encounter a place where new clay is welling up, they stop. They stand at the edge of the fresh material—still warm, still soft—and wait. Hours. Days. However long the cooling takes. They watch. The shallow depressions in their heads fill with condensation from their own bodies' heat meeting the air, and the moisture rolls down their ceramic skin and drips onto the new clay, mixing with it, becoming part of the floor. When the fresh section has hardened enough to walk on, they are always the first to cross it. Always. As if their passage consecrates it, makes it real, integrates it into the vastness.

They tend the mineral formations too, in their way. Not cultivation—nothing so active. But when they pass through the basins where the crystal-growths cluster, they pause among them. They stand very still, and the formations lean toward them almost imperceptibly, and the chiming increases, takes on complex harmonics, as if the Keepers and the growths are singing to each other in registers beyond hearing. Perhaps the Keepers are the only audience the crystal-songs have ever had. Perhaps that's enough.

---

They do not eat. They do not sleep. But they are not eternal.

A Keeper will walk for decades, maybe centuries—time here refuses honest measurement—and then, without warning or ceremony, they will stop. Usually in one of the shallow basins, near water, near the pale formations. They will fold their too-long limbs beneath them and settle onto the clay, and they will go still. Truly still, in a way they never were while walking. The tremor of the world continues beneath them, but they no longer participate in it.

Over the following months, the condensation cycles work on them. Moisture collects in every crack and crevice of their ceramic bodies. It seeps inward during the fog-times and then evaporates during the heat-times, and each cycle leaves mineral behind. Salt crystals begin to form on their surfaces—white and ivory and pale yellow, the same colors as the formations surrounding them. They become encrusted. They become, slowly, unrecognizable as anything other than another growth in the basin.

In a decade, they are indistinguishable from the crystal clusters. In a century, they *are* crystal clusters, chiming when the wind passes through them, marking where a Keeper chose to stop, to become stationary, to let the flats reclaim what the flats made.

And elsewhere, in other basins, moisture is seeping downward through the cracks, and the heat is remembering other shapes.

---

There are perhaps a hundred of them at any given time, scattered across the immensity like slow-moving errors in an otherwise perfect glaze. They do not gather. They do not organize. When two meet, they resonate briefly and then continue on their separate traversals, carrying whatever they exchanged into the distances.

But there are patterns. If you watched long enough—generations, centuries—you might notice that their routes slowly shift, that the territory they cover gradually expands as the flats expand, that new Keepers emerge most often at the boundaries of previously-walked ground. They are keeping pace with the world's growth. They are ensuring that no part of the floor goes unwitnessed.

What they witness, what they record, what they do with the knowing—this is unclear. They have no cities, no archives, no apparent culture. They simply walk, and watch, and resonate when they meet, and eventually stop and become crystal and sing to their successors in voices of wind and mineral.

Perhaps that is the archive. Perhaps every crystal formation on the flats contains the compressed memory of a Keeper's centuries of traversal, and when the wind passes through them, they are not just singing but *telling*—reciting the slow history of cracks healed and basins formed and new clay laid down, a record written in harmonics that only other Keepers can read.

Perhaps the whole flats are a library, and the Keepers are both its librarians and its books.

Or perhaps they are simply what happens when a world this vast, this patient, this hot, this empty, needs to know itself. A made world, still making itself—and making, too, the witnesses it requires. Firing them in its basins. Walking them across its expanse. Reclaiming them when they're done.

The floor does not notice you. The floor does not care. But somewhere out there in the shimmer and haze, something that was once the floor is walking, and its eyeless head is turned in a direction, and it is going *there*, for reasons that belong entirely to the logic of clay and heat and time.

You could follow it. It would not stop you, would not acknowledge you, would not alter its pace or path. You could walk beside it for years and learn nothing except the color of the ground and the rhythm of the condensation cycles and the exact sound the crystal-growths make when the wind comes from the east.

That might be enough. Here, in the silence and the warmth and the patient tremor of the world still becoming, that might be everything there is to learn.
