She has no name she remembers, but the water remembers her shape.

She is not a body in the conventional sense—not anymore. She is a density, a coherence, a place where the water moves slightly differently than it does elsewhere. If you were to pass your hand through where she rests, you would feel something like resistance, something like cold, something like the faint electric awareness of being perceived. But your hand would pass through. There is nothing to touch.

She settles near the pipes most often. Not inside them—she learned long ago that the darkness in there is different from the darkness out here, older and less patient—but near them, where their corroded surfaces create the closest thing to texture in her world. She remembers texture. She remembers the rasp of stone, the give of soil, the particular friction of wool against skin. She does not have skin. She remembers it anyway.

Time does not pass for her so much as it *accumulates*. She is aware of duration the way the mineral deposits are aware of it: as a gradual addition, layer upon layer, each moment indistinguishable from the last but somehow adding up to thickness. She has been here long enough that she no longer counts. She counted at first—days, she thought, though there was no way to mark them. Then she stopped, and the stopping was such a relief that she understood she had been doing something wrong.

She does not eat. She does not sleep. She does not breathe, though sometimes she performs the motion of breathing, a contraction and expansion that moves nothing, satisfies nothing, but reminds her of a rhythm she once needed. The water tolerates this. The water tolerates everything she does, which is very little.

What she does, mostly, is *attend*.

The cistern is not empty of event, only empty of change. There is a difference. The light from above shifts in quality if not in angle—sometimes bluer, sometimes greyer, sometimes carrying the faintest suggestion of warmth that means the sun is directly overhead, forty feet and another world away. She notices these shifts. She has become connoisseur of them, can distinguish noon from one o'clock by the particular temperature of the light, can tell cloudy days from clear by the way the illumination seems to *hesitate* at the edge of the chamber before committing to its slow diffusion.

The pale threads at the base of the pipes interest her. They are the only other thing here that might be called alive, though she is not certain she qualifies and not certain they do either. She watches them with something that is not quite patience and not quite hope. In the years she has been here—and it has been years, she knows this without counting—they have not grown. They have not died. They simply persist, like her, in their own quiet way. She feels a kinship with them that she does not examine too closely.

Sometimes she moves.

It is not swimming—she has no limbs to stroke, no mass to propel. It is more like *deciding to be elsewhere* and then being there, a slow drift that disturbs nothing, not even the silt. She circuits the chamber this way, running what would be her attention along the walls, learning their textures by something other than touch. The frozen-cloth ripples. The pale calcium veins. The places where the stone has developed something like a grain, like wood, though it is not wood and never was.

She knows every inch of this place. She has had time to know it completely, and she has used that time, because what else was there to use it for? She knows that the southeastern corner is slightly deeper than the rest, the flagstones there having subsided a quarter inch into the substrate below. She knows that one of the pipes—the topmost one—resonates at a slightly different frequency than the others, a pitch she can feel rather than hear, and that this resonance strengthens when rain falls somewhere far above. She knows that the silt, though it appears uniform, varies in composition:iteiteite finer near the walls, coarser toward the center, as though the chamber has been sorting itself over decades into some configuration that makes sense only to geology.

She does not know why she is here.

This is not something she thinks about often. She thought about it constantly at first—the how of it, the why, the desperate archaeology of her own memory trying to reconstruct the moment of transition. She remembers being somewhere else. She remembers cold, but a different cold, a cold with air in it. She remembers the shock of water, and then the longer shock of discovering that the water would not let her leave, and then the longest shock of all: that she would not drown.

She should have drowned. She knows this the way she knows the flagstones, the way she knows the light. Whatever she is now, she became it here, in this water, in this chamber that has reached its equilibrium and drawn her into it like another mineral deposit, another feature of the space.

She tried to leave, in the early time. She rose toward the light, again and again, and each time she found that the rising grew harder as she approached the surface, as though the water thickened near the top, as though something was pushing back against her. She never broke through. She would rise and rise and then find herself back at the bottom, near the pipes, with no memory of descending, only the knowledge that she had tried and failed and would try again.

She stopped trying. The stopping was a relief, like the stopping of counting. She understands now that the cistern is not her prison but her medium, that she exists in relation to it the way a thought exists in relation to a mind. She cannot leave because there is no *her* that is separate from *here*. The water that remembers her shape is the water she is made of.

And so she attends. She watches the light. She circuits the walls. She rests near the pipes and listens to their subsonic hum and wonders, sometimes, what flowed through them when they still served their purpose. She wonders if she flowed through them too, once, in some other form. She wonders if she will flow through them again, someday, when something changes.

Nothing changes here. She knows this.

But she attends anyway, because attending is what she is now, and because somewhere in the deepest silt of her not-quite-memory, she believes that the cistern is waiting for something, and that when it comes, she will need to be ready.

---

When you enter, she feels you before she sees you.

It is the first disturbance in—she does not know how long. The water shivers. The silt stirs. Something warm and bounded and *alive* has entered the equilibrium, and the equilibrium is responding, shifting around you like a question.

She drifts closer, slowly, so slowly you might not notice if you were not looking for her. And you are not looking for her. You do not know she is here. You are looking at the walls, the pipes, the pale threads of vegetation, the quality of the light. You are cataloging the cistern the way she once cataloged it, learning its textures, its temperatures, its small variations.

She watches you from very close. Close enough that if she had breath, you would feel it. Close enough that the water between you carries the faint charge of her attention, a pressure that might register as unease if you were paying attention to your own body instead of the space around it.

She does not try to speak. She lost words a long time ago, or words lost her, or the water does not carry them the way air did. But she *presses*, just slightly, against the edge of your awareness. A coldness that is not quite the coldness of the water. A sense of being seen by something that has no eyes.

She wants to know what you are. She wants to know why you came. She wants to know if you will leave, and if you leave, whether you will take something with you—some particle of the equilibrium, some memory of the light, some knowledge of the shape the water holds.

She wants to know if you will remember her.

She does not know what she would do if you did. She has not thought that far ahead. She has not thought *ahead* at all, in so long, because ahead implies change, and nothing changes here.

But you are here now. You are the disturbance.

She attends.
