**The Tenders**

They have no eyes, but they know when you've come.

Three of them emerge from folds you hadn't noticed—couldn't have noticed—sliding through the wall-membrane with a motion like fingers pressing through soap film. They are pale things, the color of something that has never needed color: translucent white shading to gray where their bodies thicken, revealing darker structures beneath the skin that might be organs or might be machinery or might be something that makes no distinction between the two.

Each one is perhaps the length of your forearm, though their bodies seem to have no fixed configuration. They move like caterpillars, like slugs, like something remembering how limbs work and deciding against them. Fine cilia line their undersides, too fine to see individually, visible only as a shimmer of movement that carries them along the curved surfaces. They can climb the walls. They can hang from what might be ceiling. They orient toward you with the front third of their bodies raised slightly, swaying, tasting the air you've brought with you—the foreign air, the outside air, thick with particulates and wrong temperatures and the chemical signature of a body that doesn't belong.

They don't attack. They don't flee. They *assess*.

The nearest one approaches a cluster of pupae and begins to work. You watch it press its body against a casing that has grown clouded, milky, wrong somehow. The tender's surface ripples, and then—you're not sure what you're seeing—it seems to *exude* something, a clear fluid that runs down the pupa's casing and dissolves the clouded layer, revealing the healthy darkness beneath. The shadow inside shifts, settles, continues its slow becoming. The tender moves on.

Another tends to the glass seahorses. It doesn't touch them directly. Instead, it positions itself near their membrane-cluster and begins to pulse—a rhythmic contraction of its whole body that creates tiny waves in the condensation, stirring nutrients, encouraging circulation. The seahorses respond by spiraling closer, riding the current the tender creates. One of them bumps against the tender's flank. The tender doesn't react except to pulse a little more slowly, a little more gently. A patience that feels ancient, though nothing here could be older than the station itself.

The third one watches you. Just watches, if that's the word for a creature with no eyes. It holds position near a fold in the space, blocking or guarding or simply occupying the path that leads deeper. Its raised portion sways in a pattern you start to recognize—it's tracking your breathing, your heartbeat, the tiny movements of your weight shifting from foot to foot. It is learning you, cataloging you, deciding something.

You understand, watching them work, that these are not the children of the nursery. These are the caretakers, grown or designed or evolved for exactly this purpose: to maintain the folds, to regulate the heat and humidity, to clean and feed and guard. Their bodies are tools. Their attention is precise. They carry no malice and no welcome, only function.

The one watching you sways closer. An inch. Two. It stops just outside the range of your arm. You could touch it, if you reached. You get the sense that it's waiting to see if you will. Waiting to learn what kind of thing you are—threat or curiosity or something else, something its categories might not contain.

The subsonic hum shifts slightly. The pupae dream on. The tenders breathe with the walls, their pale bodies rising and falling in the same arrhythmic pulse as the space itself, so synchronized that you can no longer tell if they're matching the nursery's rhythm or if the nursery is matching theirs.

They have been here since the beginning, you think. Since before the schematics, since before the official record. Someone made them or something made them or they made themselves from the need itself—the need to keep, to maintain, to ensure that whatever is growing here keeps growing until it's ready.

Ready for what, the tenders don't say. Perhaps they don't know. Perhaps it doesn't matter to them. They have their work. The work is enough.
