## The Kerathin

They are born into dampness and silence, and these become the twin poles of their existence.

A kerathin child learns to walk softly before she learns to walk at all—carried first in a sling against her mother's chest, then set down on the cool stone with a hand pressed gently over her mouth, a reminder that lives in the body longer than any spoken rule. By the time she can toddle between the fern-grottoes, she has already absorbed the rhythm of the place: the way footsteps fall on the balls of the feet, the way hands move in slow arcs that do not startle, the way even a sneeze must be caught and smothered in the crook of an elbow, turned into a soft huff that the moths ignore.

Their bodies reflect generations of this careful living. The kerathin are slight, narrow-shouldered, with long fingers that seem made for delicate work. Their skin carries a pallor that speaks of filtered light—not sickly, but soft, like the inside of a shell. Many develop a faint freckling across the cheeks and forearms, pale brown against pale cream, as if the mist itself has left marks. Their hair grows in shades of dark brown and black, worn long by both men and women, often gathered in loose knots secured with pins of polished bone. Cut hair is never discarded; it is woven into the curtains that hang in doorways, adding human scent to the moss-fiber, a marker that says *this space is inhabited, this threshold is meaningful.*

### The Shape of Their Days

A kerathin wakes when the first hint of gray light seeps through the mist—not by any clock, but by the stirring of the moths on the walls of her home. They begin to move before dawn, a slow rippling of wings that creates a barely perceptible change in air pressure, in the quality of stillness. The kerathin have learned to feel this shift the way lowland farmers feel a change in wind. It is time.

Morning work centers on the basins. Each family maintains several of the carved stone catchments, checking them at first light for the night's accumulation. The water is not simply collected; it is *read*. A kerathin woman runs her fingers along the basin's rim, feeling for the slight residue of wing-scales that indicates heavy moth activity during the night. She notes which species have drunk here—identifiable by the pattern of scale-dust, the faint coloration left behind. This information matters. It tells her which breeding cycles are active, which populations are thriving, which may be struggling.

The water itself is transferred to ceramic vessels glazed with a slip made fromiteite ate volcanicite and moth-cocoon ash—a technique that keeps the water pure, that prevents the growth of the tiny organisms that would otherwise cloud it. These vessels are stored in the deepest parts of each home, where the temperature stays most constant. Water is not scarce here, exactly, but it is *variable*, and the kerathin have learned to smooth out the mountain's moods through careful storage.

Breakfast is eaten in near-silence, the family gathered on woven mats around a low stone platform. The food is simple: a porridge of ground tubers that grow in the moss-forests, flavored with the dried bodies of certain moth species whose taste is nutty and faintly sweet. Fresh greens when available—the cupped leaves of the epiphytes, the tender shoots of ferns. Occasionally, the pale flesh of a salamander, though this is considered a luxury, almost a celebration. The kerathin eat slowly, chewing thoroughly, making little noise.

After the meal, the family separates into their work. And work, for the kerathin, is inseparable from the moths.

### The Art of Moth-Tending

They do not herd the moths, exactly. The word *shepherd* in their name—given by lowland traders who visit perhaps twice a year—misunderstands the relationship. The kerathin do not drive or direct. They *attend*. They create conditions. They make offerings and observe responses. They have, over generations, entered into a negotiation with creatures that cannot negotiate, and the result is something that looks like partnership but feels, to the kerathin themselves, more like service.

The great moths—those whose wings span wider than outstretched arms—are the foundation. Their larvae spin silk of extraordinary quality: strong enough to hold the weight of a grown man when woven into rope, fine enough to filter mist into pure water when stretched across frames, lustrous enough to catch what little light penetrates the haze and hold it, glowing faintly, long after the source has faded. This silk is the kerathin's primary trade good, the reason lowland merchants make the difficult journey up the mountain's flanks.

But the great moths cannot be raised in captivity. The kerathin tried, in generations past, and found that confined larvae spin weaker silk, that adult moths kept in enclosures fail to breed, that something in the relationship between moth and mountain cannot be replicated in a cage. So instead, the kerathin have learned to *invite*.

The central plaza of the settlement—perhaps twenty paces across, the largest open space on the shelf—is given over entirely to the great moths' needs. Here, the kerathin have cultivated a garden of specific plants, each chosen over generations of observation for its attractiveness to specific life-stages. Low-growing mosses that the larvae favor for pupation. Flowering vines whose nectar draws breeding adults. Broad-leafed ferns whose undersides provide preferred resting spots. The plaza is not wild; it is gardened with an intensity that would exhaust a lowland farmer. But its design principle is not harvest. It is welcome.

Each dawn and dusk, a keeper walks the plaza in a slow circuit, observing. She notes which plants are being used, which are ignored. She watches for signs of disease—a subtle discoloration of wings, a stiffness in movement. She records, in a system of knotted cords that can be read by touch in the dim light, the approximate number of each species present, the stage of each visible breeding cycle, the general health of the population.

And then she makes adjustments. A plant that is failing to attract may be removed, replaced with a cutting from a more successful specimen. A resting spot that has become overcrowded may be expanded by careful placement of additional stones, angled to create the right shadows. If a disease appears, the keeper faces a terrible choice: remove the affected individuals—a process that requires killing—or allow the sickness to spread.

The kerathin do not enjoy killing moths. They do it with ceremony, with apology, with a small offering of water poured at the base of the nearest stone. But they do it, because the alternative is collapse, and collapse would mean the end of everything they have built.

### The Silk-Workers

Not all kerathin are keepers. Some are weavers, and their art is equally demanding.

The silk of the great moths is not collected from living creatures. When a larva has spun its cocoon and begun its transformation, the kerathin wait. They observe the cocoon daily, watching for the subtle signs that indicate the adult moth is about to emerge. At precisely the right moment—not too early, when the silk is still tacky and will tear; not too late, when the emerging moth will have damaged the fibers—a silk-worker makes a small incision in one end of the cocoon.

Through this opening, the moth emerges. The silk-worker guides it gently, ensuring it does not struggle and damage itself. The empty cocoon is then collected, its silk slowly unraveled over hours of patient work, a single continuous thread that can run the length of a hundred paces or more.

This thread is not immediately usable. It must be cleaned, treated with a solution of certain mosses that prevent decay, dried in the thin mountain air. Only then can it be woven.

The weaving itself happens in a single structure at the edge of the settlement, built partially into a natural overhang that keeps the light steady and diffuse. Here, several frames stand permanently, too large to move, strung with silk in various stages of completion. A weaver may work on a single piece for a season, adding threads one at a time, building up a fabric of extraordinary density and strength. The resulting cloth has a quality that defies easy description: it feels almost weightless, yet resists tearing; it seems to glow from within, yet casts normal shadows; it drapes and flows like water, yet holds its shape when still.

Lowland merchants pay extraordinary prices for this silk. A single length, no larger than a cloak, can purchase enough grain and salt and metal tools to sustain the entire settlement for half a year. The kerathin could, in theory, produce more—could expand their cultivation, could harvest more cocoons, could work faster. They do not. The mathematics of their existence require restraint: too much taking, and the moth populations decline; too much production, and the delicate balance of the plaza garden collapses; too much trade, and the merchants begin to see them as a resource rather than a people.

So they produce what they produce, and they live within those limits, and they have done so for longer than anyone can remember.

### The Old Tongue and the New

The kerathin speak two languages. The first is a lowland trade-tongue, learned from the merchants, used only when outsiders are present. The second is their own, and it is unlike any language spoken below.

It is built for silence. It has no voiced consonants—no B or D or G or Z, nothing that requires the vibration of the vocal cords. Its vowels are soft, breathed more than spoken, shaped by the position of the tongue and lips rather than by force of air. A kerathin conversation sounds, to an unaccustomed ear, like a series of whispered sighs and hisses, barely distinguishable from the rustle of moth-wings or the drip of condensation.

But it is fully expressive. It has terms for every species of moth that visits the shelf—over three hundred, classified by wing-pattern, by behavior, by the quality of their larvae's silk. It has a complex grammar of tense that distinguishes between events that happened during mist, during clarity, during heavy fog, during the rare bright days when the clouds part and direct sunlight touches the stone. It has seventeen different words for types of stillness.

Children learn this language first, absorbing it in the silence of their homes, in the gestures of their parents, in the slow patient instruction that takes place during the long dim hours when outdoor work is impossible. By the time a kerathin child is five or six, she speaks it fluently. The trade-tongue comes later, taught by those few adults who have made the journey to the lowlands and back, preserved as a practical necessity rather than a living thing.

### The Witness

Above the settlement, in the vertical fern-jungles that cling to the cliff walls, something lives.

The kerathin do not know what it is. In their language, they call it *keresheth*, which translates roughly as "the one who watches from the vertical." Over the generations, they have accumulated observations: it is large, at least the size of a grown man, possibly larger. It moves primarily at night, or during the thickest fogs when visibility drops to an arm's length. It is never seen clearly, only glimpsed—a shape crossing a gap between fern-clusters, a shadow moving against the expected flow of condensation-drip.

It has never attacked them. This is the most important fact, repeated in their oral histories, emphasized to every child. In all the time the kerathin have lived on this shelf, the witness has not harmed a single person. But neither has it revealed itself. It simply watches.

The offerings began, according to tradition, three generations ago, when a keeper noticed that moth-pupae left near the base of the cliff walls were disappearing. Not taken by other animals—the kerathin know the signs of salamander predation, of the small cliff-birds that occasionally venture down. Taken by something else. Something that came in the night and left no trace except absence.

So they formalized it. Now, at each new moon, a keeper climbs to the highest accessible point on the cliff wall—a narrow ledge perhaps fifty paces above the settlement floor—and leaves a portion of the season's harvest. Cocoons that failed, larvae that died before spinning, adult moths found dead on the stone. Nothing that would be used otherwise, but nothing insulting either. An acknowledgment.

The kerathin do not worship the witness. They do not pray to it or ask it for favors. But they recognize it as a power, as something that shares this place with them, as something whose tolerance cannot be assumed. The offerings are, in a sense, rent—payment for the privilege of living where something older and more mysterious has chosen to dwell.

Some kerathin believe the witness is a protector, that its presence keeps other predators away. Others believe it is simply indifferent, that the offerings are unnecessary superstition. A few, the oldest and most cautious, believe it is waiting—that something about the kerathin is not yet right, not yet acceptable, and that one day the waiting will end.

No one knows. The witness does not explain itself. It simply watches.

### The Old Structures

In the deepest moss-grottoes, where the bioluminescent glow is strongest, the kerathin do not build.

It is not forbidden, exactly. There is no law, no explicit prohibition. But the unease is universal, passed down through generations until it has become instinct. Those spaces belong to something else. Something that was here before.

The structures themselves are simple: arrangements of stone, fitted together without mortar, forming low platforms and partial walls and what might be altars or might be tables or might be something without a human analog. They are old—old enough that the stone has weathered smooth in places, old enough that moss has colonized every surface, old enough that the kerathin's oldest oral histories speak of them as already ancient.

And the moths favor them. This is the strangest part. The great moths, whose silk sustains the settlement, preferentially lay their eggs on these structures. Their larvae spin their cocoons in the crevices between fitted stones. The populations that breed here are healthier, their silk stronger, their numbers more stable than those that breed elsewhere on the shelf.

The kerathin harvest from these places, carefully, with more ceremony than they use elsewhere. They do not linger. They do not study the structures closely or attempt to understand their purpose. They take what is offered and they leave.

One keeper, four generations ago, attempted to build a replica of the old structures in the central plaza. The idea was pragmatic: if the moths favor these arrangements, perhaps creating more would increase their populations, would make the settlement more prosperous. She worked for a season, fitting stones together in careful imitation.

The moths ignored it. They continued to favor the originals, continued to pass over the replica as if it did not exist. Eventually, the keeper dismantled her creation and used the stones for other purposes. The story is still told, though, as a caution: *what was built before cannot be built again.*

### Growing Up Kerathin

A child born on the shelf enters a world of deliberate slowness.

She learns early that the adults around her move differently than she does, that her impulses—to run, to shout, to grab—are gently but firmly discouraged. She is not punished for these impulses; she is simply redirected, again and again, until the redirection becomes unnecessary. By the time she is four, she has internalized the rhythm of the place. By six, she is contributing to household work. By ten, she is ready to begin her training.

Every kerathin child chooses a path, though "choose" is perhaps too strong a word. The paths are limited: keeper, weaver, tender, maker. Keepers manage the plaza and observe the moth populations. Weavers work the silk. Tenders maintain the homes and gardens and prepare food. Makers craft the tools and vessels and structures that the settlement requires. A child gravitates toward one of these based on aptitude and interest, guided by adults who watch for signs of fit.

The training takes years. A young keeper spends her first season simply watching, memorizing the species, learning the knotted-cord notation, accompanying older keepers on their rounds without speaking or participating. Only in her second season does she begin to make observations of her own, still supervised, still corrected. By her third season, she may be trusted with minor decisions—which plant to relocate, which resting-spot to adjust. By her fifth, she may be ready to take responsibility for a section of the plaza.

This slowness is intentional. The kerathin believe that mastery cannot be rushed, that errors made in haste compound over generations, that the moth populations are too important to entrust to the merely competent. A keeper who makes a mistake—who misreads a breeding cycle, who fails to notice a disease, who adjusts too aggressively—may not see the consequences for seasons, even years. But the consequences will come. And they will be shared by everyone.

So the training is slow, and the standards are high, and those who cannot meet them are gently guided toward other paths. There is no shame in this. A kerathin who becomes a tender instead of a keeper has not failed; she has simply found her fit. All the paths are necessary. All are respected.

### Love and Bonding

The kerathin do not marry in the lowland sense. They *braid*.

When two kerathin decide to share their lives, they do so through a ceremony of hair. Each cuts a small lock from their own head; the locks are woven together by a third party, usually an elder with no close relationship to either; the resulting braid is sealed with resin and hung in the doorway of the home they will share. As long as the braid hangs, the bond holds.

This is not purely symbolic. The braids are taken seriously—more seriously, perhaps, than lowland marriages, which can be dissolved by paperwork. A kerathin who wishes to end a braiding must wait for a full cycle of seasons, must attempt mediation by elders, must demonstrate that the bond has truly failed. Even then, the dissolution is marked with mourning, with a small ceremony in which the braid is burned and the ashes scattered in the plaza.

Children are raised communally to a degree that would surprise lowlanders. A child belongs to her parents, yes, but also to their neighbors, to the settlement as a whole. Any adult may correct a child's behavior; any adult may comfort a child in distress; any adult may teach a child a skill. This is practical—in a community of thirty, specialized parenting is a luxury no one can afford—but it is also philosophical. The kerathin believe that children are shaped by their whole environment, that limiting their influences limits their growth.

Sexual relationships are treated with similar pragmatism. The kerathin do not expect or demand that braided partners be exclusive. Affairs are common and generally tolerated, as long as they do not disrupt household stability or produce more children than the settlement can support. This last concern is serious: the shelf can sustain perhaps forty people comfortably, and the kerathin have learned, through hard experience, that exceeding this number leads to cascade failures—not enough water, not enough food, not enough silk to trade for what cannot be produced locally.

So births are planned, discussed, sometimes delayed. A woman who wishes to become pregnant consults with the elders, with the keepers, with her own household. If the population is stable and resources are adequate, she proceeds. If not, she waits. Contraceptive knowledge—which plants prevent conception, which end pregnancies early—is passed from mother to daughter, from aunt to niece, considered as essential as any other survival skill.

### Death and Memory

When a kerathin dies, the body is given to the moths.

This is not metaphor. The dead are carried to a specific grotto at the edge of the settlement, a place where a particular species of moth—small, dark-winged, rarely seen elsewhere—gathers in unusual numbers. The body is laid on a stone platform there, and then the living withdraw.

Over the following days, the moths cover the corpse. They do not eat it—at least, not in the way a scavenger would. They lay their eggs in it. When the larvae hatch, they consume the flesh slowly, methodically, reducing the body to bone and silk. The silk is left behind: a cocoon that preserves the skeleton within, that holds the shape of the person who was.

These cocoons are collected, eventually, and stored in a cave at the highest point of the shelf. Generations of kerathin rest there, wrapped in silk, their bones preserved indefinitely by the constant cool and the antimicrobial properties of the moth-fiber. It is not a place the living visit often. But it is known, and remembered, and honored.

The kerathin do not believe in an afterlife, exactly. They believe in *continuation*—in the idea that the dead become part of the settlement in a literal, physical sense, that the silk produced by their bodies enters the weaving, the trade, the life of the community. When a weaver works with silk harvested from a death-grotto, she knows that some portion of it was once a person. She does not find this disturbing. She finds it comforting. The dead are not gone. They are woven into everything.

### The Traders

Twice a year, if the weather permits, merchants climb the mountain.

They come from the lowland cities, following paths that the kerathin themselves rarely travel. The journey takes four days under good conditions, longer if the mists are heavy or the mountain is restless. The merchants bring grain, salt, metal tools, medicines, small luxuries—pigments, spices, books in the lowland script that only a few kerathin can read. They take away silk.

The exchanges are formal, ritualized, conducted in a specific location at the edge of the settlement where a flat stone provides a natural trading floor. The kerathin do not invite merchants into their homes, do not share meals with them, do not speak to them more than necessary. This is not hostility. It is caution. The merchants are loud, by kerathin standards—even their whispers carry more than necessary, and their movements are quick and startling. The moths on the nearby walls shift uneasily when they are present. Better to conduct business quickly and let them leave.

The merchants, for their part, find the kerathin unsettling. They speak of them in lowland cities as ghostly, as uncanny, as not-quite-human. Stories circulate: that the kerathin drink the blood of moths, that they can see in total darkness, that they reproduce without touching, that they are the last remnants of an ancient race that once ruled the mountains. None of this is true. But the kerathin do not correct the rumors. Mystery has its uses. Better to be feared, a little, than to be seen as vulnerable.

### What They Hope For

The kerathin do not dream of expansion, of conquest, of legacy. They dream of *continuation*.

They know their place is precarious. The mountain is young and restless; a sufficiently violent tremor could shake the shelf loose, could send them tumbling into the void. The moth populations are healthy now, but diseases have swept through before, have reduced the great moths to a handful, have forced the kerathin to live on stored supplies and hope for recovery. The witness watches, and no one knows what it wants, or when its patience might end.

So they do not plan for centuries. They plan for seasons. They maintain what they have. They pass down what they know. They hope that their children will do the same, and their children's children, and on into a future that cannot be predicted or controlled.

This is enough. It has to be enough.

In the dim light of a misty morning, a keeper walks her circuit through the plaza. She notes the positions of the moths, the health of the plants, the small signs that only she can read. She adds a knot to her cord. She moves on.

Behind her, the moths settle back into stillness.

The mountain breathes.
