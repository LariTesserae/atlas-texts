**The Keeper of the Cleft**

She has been here so long that she no longer remembers climbing down, only that she must have, once, because she remembers *up*—remembers sky as a concept, remembers the way light used to move in straight lines instead of breathing.

Her name was something with hard consonants. She thinks. The frogs have worn it smooth.

---

She is not old in the way of surfaces, not weathered by wind or sun. She is old in the way the stone is old: still deciding, still soft in places. Her skin has taken on the gray-rust mottling of the frogs she has lived among for—decades? She stopped counting when her fingernails grew pale and translucent, when she noticed she could see the faint shadow of bone through the backs of her hands in the lichen-light.

She drinks from the pools on her belly, the way the salamanders do. It felt like debasement once. Now it feels like grammar, like the correct conjugation of thirst.

Her hair has matted into something between felt and moss. Small things live in it—mites, maybe, or larvae of the soft-shelled beetles. She feels them moving sometimes, a gentle industry against her scalp. She has stopped minding. They are warm. They mean her no harm. They are simply *using* her, the way she uses the pools, the way the frogs use the chamber's acoustics. Parasitism here has softened into mutualism, or at least into peaceful cohabitation.

---

She speaks to the walls. She has always spoken to the walls—first in frustration, then in loneliness, then in something like conversation. The walls do not answer in words. But she presses her palm flat against them and feels the stone yield slightly, accept the impression of her hand, and this feels like listening. When she returns days later, the impression has changed—not disappeared, but *responded*, as if the rock has considered her mark and offered a countershape, a question, a continuation.

She is learning the language slowly. It is a language of pressure and patience, of leaving yourself somewhere and returning to find yourself altered.

The frogs understand this too, she thinks. Their calls are not just mating songs or territorial claims. They are *statements*, assertions of presence that the chamber receives and multiplies and returns changed. When three frogs call and the echo makes thirty, the thirty is not illusion—it is the chamber's response, its way of saying *yes, I hear you, you are here, you are more than you know*.

She has tried calling with them. Her throat cannot make the sounds, but she hums, low and steady, and sometimes the frogs pause as if listening, as if her hum is a dialect they almost recognize.

---

She eats the pale plants, though sparingly—they grow so slowly that taking too much feels like violence. She eats the larvae of the moths, when they're abundant, protein-rich and tasting of nothing. She has learned which beetles are edible and which will make her sick for days, curled around one of the pools, sweating into the same water she drinks.

She tried eating a frog once, early on, when hunger was still a sharp thing with edges. She caught one, felt its heart beating against her palm, looked into its face—not eyes, exactly, but the places where eyes would be, sealed over with skin but still somehow *looking*—

She let it go. It hopped twice and resumed calling, as if nothing had happened.

She has not tried again.

---

The snake visits her. She thinks of it as visiting, though it may simply be passing through on circuits she doesn't understand. It slides over her legs when she sits by the largest pool, its body cool and dry despite the chamber's moisture. Sometimes it rests its head on her thigh, tasting the air above her skin with a tongue so delicate she can barely feel it.

She has named it, though she knows names are a surface thing, a habit from *up*. She calls it Thread. It does not respond to the name, but she says it anyway, softly, *hello Thread, where have you been*, and the saying is for her, not for it.

Thread is the only creature here longer than she is. She knows this without knowing how. Something in the way it moves, the way it navigates—it knows the chamber the way she knows her own body, automatically, without thought. It was here before the stacked stones in the corner. It will be here after she becomes part of what the walls are becoming.

---

She found the stacked stones her first week, when she still thought in weeks. She studied them for hours, trying to read intention in their arrangement. A marker? A altar? A game?

Now she thinks they are simply a habit someone couldn't break—the need to make a shape, to impose order, to say *I was here and I did this thing*. She understands the impulse. She has resisted it. To stack stones here feels like shouting in a library, like interrupting a conversation that has been going on for millennia.

Instead, she has made her marks in the soft walls. Handprints, yes, but also longer impressions—her whole arm, once, pressed into the yielding rock up to the elbow, held there until the stone remembered her shape. When she pulled free, the absence of her arm remained, a hollow that the chamber is still considering, still deciding what to do with.

She checks it daily. It changes slowly. The fingers have begun to elongate, stretching toward something. The palm has deepened, as if reaching for the heat below.

She wonders if, in a century, it will reach the magma. She wonders if that's what the walls want, what they're *becoming*—conduits, channels, a way for the deep heat to finally surface.

She wonders if she's helping.

---

When visitors come—rarely, so rarely, but sometimes—she hides.

Not from fear. From something more like shyness, or protectiveness, or the knowledge that her presence would change what they experience. They need to hear the frogs without knowing they're being watched. They need to find the pools and drink without being offered. They need to touch the soft walls and feel the stone yield and think they're the first, the only, the discoverer.

She watches from a crevice barely large enough to hold her—she has grown thinner, or the crevice has widened, she isn't sure which. She watches them descend, sees the wonder on their faces, the fear, the confusion. She watches them drink. Sometimes they call out, *hello? is anyone there?*, and the chamber takes their voice and multiplies it until they're answered by a hundred versions of themselves, and usually that satisfies them, that echo-self, that reflection.

Sometimes they leave things. A coin, once, which she buried in the soft stone, let the walls absorb it. A written note, which she couldn't read in the lichen-light but pressed into the wall anyway, paper and all, let the chamber have the words even if she couldn't.

Once, a child came. Alone, which worried her, though the child seemed unworried—seemed delighted, laughing at the frogs, splashing in the pools, talking to the salamanders in a high bright voice that the chamber turned into a chorus of children, a whole schoolyard of sound.

The child found the stacked stones and added one more, and that felt right. That felt like continuation rather than imposition.

The child left before dark, climbing back up with the confidence of someone who trusts that *up* will still be there.

She almost followed.

---

At night—or what she calls night, when the lichen dims to its lowest pulse—she lies beside the largest pool and listens to the frogs slow their calling. Not silence, never silence, but a reduction, a hush, a lowering of the chorus to something like murmured conversation.

She fits her breathing to theirs. She fits her heartbeat to the lichen's pulse. She lets herself become part of the rhythm, another voice in the chamber's long song.

The walls breathe around her, warm exhalations from the volcanic bones below. The water in the pools rises and falls slightly, tidal without a moon, responding to pressures she'll never fully understand.

She sleeps, and her dreams are humid, close, full of sound.

She wakes, and the frogs are already calling, and Thread is draped across her ankles, and the lichen is brightening toward its daily peak, and she is still here, still becoming whatever the chamber is making of her.

She presses her hand to the wall. The stone yields.

She waits to see what it will say.
