**The Keeper of the Becoming Place**

She has no name she would recognize as such, though the scratched mark on the root—that vertical crossed by horizontal—was her grandmother's way of saying *someone tends this*. Not "I was here" but "here is tended." The distinction matters to her, though she could not explain why.

She comes down through a different gap than the one you found, a passage that requires knowing which root to press against, which soil-shelf will hold weight and which is facade over hollow. She has been coming since she was small enough to be carried, and her mother before her, and back further than the soft clay vessel that is returning now to earth. The vessel was her great-great-aunt's, though the word "aunt" is wrong—the relationship was more complex, involving a child raised by a woman who found her, and the tending-duty that passed between them like a dowry of attention.

She is old now. Not ancient, not yet, but old enough that the climb down takes preparation, takes rest, takes a whole morning when it once took minutes. Her knees remember the route even when her breath fails her. Sixty-three years of descent have worn a knowledge into her body that exists below thought.

---

What she does here is not ritual, exactly. She does not pray. She does not offer. She *witnesses*.

She sits in the warm dark—her spot is near where the ceiling tangles lowest, where she can rest her back against a root-curve that has shaped itself, over her lifetime of leaning, into something almost like accommodation. She sits, and she listens to the dripping, and she feels the water condense on her skin, and she waits.

For what? She has never been certain. Her mother told her: *The place is becoming. Someone should know what it becomes.* This made sense to her as a child and makes more sense now. Things that become without witness become differently than things that are seen. She is not sure this is true, but she acts as though it is, and the acting has become indistinguishable from belief.

She tracks the changes. Here, where a new root-tip emerged last season, pale and sheathed—it has darkened now, begun to roughen, started its work of drinking and holding. There, where a pool was forming, uncertain of its edges—the edges have firmed slightly, the water finding its preferred level. The floor, still uneven, has shifted: a soil-hump she once used as a stepping-stone has compressed, sunk, begun its slow journey toward the chamber's bottom.

She does not write these observations. She holds them. Her memory of this place is layered like sediment, each visit deposited over the last, and she can feel her way back through the strata if she concentrates. The chamber as it was when she was forty. Thirty. Twelve. A child on her mother's hip, understanding nothing, already understanding everything.

---

She knows about the fish. The pale translucent thing you glimpsed—she has seen it many times, or seen its kind. She believes there is more than one, though she has never seen two together. They live in the gaps, in the water-passages too narrow for following, and they emerge sometimes into the pools as if checking on the progress of things.

She thinks of them as auditors. Fellow witnesses. They have been here longer than her line has, certainly. They were here when the first of her people found this place, when the vessel was new and the mark un-stretched. They will be here when her line ends, which it will—she has no children, no one she has chosen to teach the climbing and the sitting and the listening. This grieves her in a distant way, like mourning weather.

But perhaps it is right. Perhaps the place is far enough along in its becoming that it no longer needs human witness. Perhaps the fish are enough. Perhaps the roots themselves, in their slow vegetable sentience, have learned to watch their own work.

She does not know. She sits in the dark, and the water sounds its thousand small percussions, and she feels the place becoming around her—the ceiling lowering by fractions of fractions, the pools deepening by the width of a hair, the whole chamber inching toward some shape it has spent centuries imagining.

---

When she leaves, she presses her palm against the root that bears her grandmother's mark. The bark is slick, warm, alive in a way that is not animal but is not *not* animal either. She feels the water moving through it, the slow pull of drink and carry. The root does not acknowledge her, but it permits her, and permission is enough.

She climbs. The passage is harder each time. One day it will be impossible, and that day is not far.

Below her, in the dark she is leaving, the fish flickers through its pool, checking. The roots go on drinking. The dripping continues its chorus. The clay vessel softens further, remembering the earth it came from.

The place becomes.
