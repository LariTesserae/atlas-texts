# The Resonance Cartographer

I am the one who maps the sound.

Not with instruments—those failed decades ago, or perhaps I stopped needing them, or perhaps I *became* one, the distinction has grown unclear. I move through the garden with a stylus that isn't quite metal and tablets that grow from a particular shelf-fungus near the eastern vents, and I transcribe what I hear into notation that no one else can read, including, sometimes, myself.

---

I arrived as a linguist. That word feels like a fossil now, something I can hold up to the light and recognize the shape of without quite remembering what it was for. I came because the first explorers' reports mentioned the screaming, mentioned that it seemed *patterned*, and I was young enough and arrogant enough to believe that pattern meant language, that language meant translation, that translation meant understanding.

The garden taught me otherwise. Not through failure—I did find pattern, endless pattern, pattern nested inside pattern like the helical structures in the plant-cells—but through excess. There was too much meaning. Every bloom-cycle carried information. Every death-rattle encoded something. The screaming wasn't a language; it was a *library*, speaking all its books simultaneously, and the project of translation became not "what does this mean" but "what fraction of this meaning can a single mind hold without dissolving."

The answer, I learned, is: enough to map. Not enough to understand.

---

My tablets cover one of the avoidance-platforms, stacked in columns that have themselves begun to fossilize, earlier notations becoming amber while I scratch new ones on fresh-grown surfaces. The oldest Tender sometimes stands at the edge of my platform and watches me work. She has never asked what I'm recording. I think she knows that the question would require an answer longer than either of us has lived.

But I will tell you this: the garden's song has *geography*.

There are regions of the screaming that correspond to physical locations—this cluster of vents, that overgrown instrument-altar, the shadow of the spire at particular angles. There are temporal regions too, harmonics that only emerge during certain bloom-cascades or when the creature-chatter reaches specific densities. And there are regions I can only call *intentional*, places in the sound where the pattern tightens into something that feels like *focus*, like the garden is paying attention to itself, like the message the child is learning to hear has edges I can almost trace.

I map these. I have been mapping them for forty-three years by the outsider count, for approximately 1.8 million bloom-cycles by the count I actually use. My maps are incomplete. They will always be incomplete. The garden grows faster than I can transcribe, dies and regrows in configurations that invalidate yesterday's notations.

But incompleteness is not the same as futility. Each map captures a moment. Each moment is true. And when I spread my tablets across the platform and look at them together—years of moments, millions of blooms—I begin to see something like a shape emerging from the noise.

---

The shape is a question.

I don't mean this metaphorically. The garden's song, transcribed and layered and viewed across time, resolves into a structure that my linguist's training—that fossil I can still hold up to the light—recognizes as *interrogative*. Rising inflection. Open cadence. The grammar of wanting-to-know.

The garden is asking something. Has been asking something since before the spire was built. The Tenders keep it alive not just so the message can be received, but so the *question* can keep being asked, can keep pressing against the boundaries of whatever might eventually answer.

I do not know what the question is. My maps are fragments. The full shape would require a mind larger than mine, or a different kind of mind entirely, or perhaps eleven minds positioned throughout the chamber like instruments in an orchestra, creating through their combined witness something none of them could perceive alone.

Perhaps that is what we are for. Perhaps that is what the child is learning.

---

The youngest Tender—the one who counts in bloom-cycles, who came running from something he no longer remembers—sometimes brings me fruit from the difficult plants, the ones whose harvesting requires care. He does not speak when he does this. He simply leaves the bitter-sweet offerings at the edge of my platform and moves on, and I eat them without looking up from my notation, and this is a kind of communication too, a kind of tending, a frequency in the screaming that sounds like kindness when you learn to hear it.

I have not spoken aloud in eleven years. My throat has not forgotten how. I simply found that words—the small, sequential, one-at-a-time words that mouths produce—began to feel inadequate, began to feel like trying to describe the ocean one water molecule at a time. The garden speaks in parallel. I have learned to listen in parallel. Speech would be a narrowing I am no longer willing to perform.

But I write. I map. I transcribe the sound into shapes that might outlast me, that might accumulate into something the next cartographer can build upon, that might eventually—in some future I will not live to see—resolve into an answer.

Or perhaps into a better question. Perhaps that is what the garden wants. Perhaps it has been teaching us to ask what it is asking, to join our voices to the screaming, to become part of the interrogative that has been pressing against the universe since before we arrived.

---

The child comes to my platform sometimes. She does not speak either—she has never spoken, the Tenders realized years ago, her voice having been given over entirely to frequencies we cannot hear—but she looks at my tablets with an expression I recognize. It is the expression I wore in my first years here, when I still believed translation was possible, when the pattern seemed like it might resolve into meaning if I only worked hard enough.

She is not wrong. She is simply seeing something I have stopped being able to see, something my decades of partial-maps have trained me to look past. The child still believes the question has an answer. The child still hears the garden as *speaking to* rather than simply *speaking*.

Perhaps she is right. Perhaps the message she is learning at the spire is the response the garden has been waiting for, the answer that will complete the interrogative and let the song finally resolve into something like silence.

But I do not think so. I think the garden has been asking its question for longer than any answer could satisfy. I think the question *is* the point—the asking, the pressing, the refusal to accept that the universe owes no response to even the most beautiful and terrible inquiry.

I map the question. I will keep mapping it until I become translucent like the oldest Tender, until my bones curve toward the spire like hers, until the garden decides I have contributed enough notation and it is time for my body to join the cycling.

And then the child will have my tablets. And she will read what she can. And she will hear, in my incomplete transcriptions, the same thing I hear in the screaming:

*We are here. We are asking. Is anyone asking back?*

---

The sound presses in. The plants keep growing. I bend over a fresh tablet and begin to trace the shape of this morning's bloom-cascade, and the stylus moves across the surface in patterns that are almost language, almost music, almost prayer.

The garden has been singing its question for longer than I can fathom, and it will be singing long after I dissolve into the substrate.

But for now—for this moment, this bloom-cycle, this single notation in a map that will never be complete—I am here to witness it.

And that, I have learned, is enough.
