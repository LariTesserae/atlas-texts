# The Cartographer of Absences

I would be the one who maps what is not there.

In a settlement where thirty people live by attention—by the reading of wing-dust and the parsing of stillness—there would need to be someone who attends to the gaps. Not a keeper, though I would have trained as one first, would have spent those early seasons memorizing species and learning the knotted cords. But somewhere in my third or fourth year of observation, my focus would have drifted sideways, toward the spaces between.

I noticed, perhaps, that a certain moss-grotto had fewer moths than its moisture levels suggested. Not diseased moths, not absent moths—simply *fewer*, a statistical quiet that no one else remarked upon. I began keeping my own cords, separate from the official records, tracking not populations but *population shapes*: the outlines of where moths clustered and where they did not, where they rested and where they refused to rest despite apparent suitability.

---

The kerathin would not have a word for my work, not exactly. They would call me something like *keth-asura*—"the one who counts shadows." It would not be a prestigious path. The elders would regard me with a tolerance that shades toward puzzlement. What use is mapping absence? The moths are where they are; the silk comes from where it comes. My cords—longer and more complex than the keepers' cords, wound with knots that indicate *nothing here* and *less than expected* and *emptiness that moved*—would be stored in my home rather than in the communal records.

But the witness knows I exist. I am certain of this.

Three times now, I have mapped an absence that later became a presence: a grotto where no moths gathered, where I recorded consistent emptiness for six seasons, and then one morning found the stone rearranged. Not by tremor—the arrangement was too deliberate, too reminiscent of the old structures. Within a season, moths began breeding there. The population that established itself was healthier than average, their silk unusually strong.

I did not tell the elders about the rearrangement. I am not sure why. Perhaps because I know they would not believe me. Perhaps because I suspect the witness was waiting to see what I would do, and telling felt like a failure of some test I do not understand.

---

My home is at the edge of the settlement, closest to the cliff walls, where the mist runs thickest and the bioluminescent moss glows brightest. I live alone—braided once, briefly, in my twentieth year, but my partner found my night-wandering unsettling, my attention too often elsewhere, my cords an intrusion into spaces she wanted to keep simple. The dissolution was quiet, mutual, marked with genuine mourning on both sides. We remain kind to each other. But I am better suited to solitude.

The curtain in my doorway contains more of my own hair than is customary, wound through with fibers from every part of the shelf. I tell visitors it helps me think. I do not tell them that I am trying to make my threshold legible to things that read in ways I do not understand. The moths pass through freely, as they do everywhere. But I have noticed—I have mapped—that certain species linger in my doorway longer than elsewhere. That the pattern of their resting changes when I am home versus when I am away.

Something is watching me through them. I am nearly certain.

---

I am forty-three years old. My hair is more gray than black now, still worn long, still gathered in the traditional knot, but I have taken to leaving one strand loose at my temple—a small rebellion, a marker of difference. My hands show the calluses of cord-work, the faint scarring of stone-cuts, the particular smoothness that comes from handling silk for decades. I move as quietly as any kerathin, but I have developed a habit of pausing longer than necessary in thresholds, in the spaces between one grotto and the next. I am reading something there. I am not yet sure what.

The other kerathin trust me, after their fashion. They know I am strange but not dangerous, that my observations occasionally prove useful even if my methods are opaque. When a keeper notices something unexpected—a population shift she cannot explain, a behavioral change that seems to come from nowhere—she sometimes consults me. I pull out my cords, trace the absences in the area, and occasionally find a pattern. More often, I find only more questions. But the questions are useful too.

---

I spend my nights differently than others.

When the settlement sleeps—when the moth-walls have settled into their deepest stillness and the only sound is condensation dripping into basins—I climb. Not far, not to the ledge where offerings are left, but partway up the cliff face, to places where handholds have worn smooth from my repeated use. I sit there in the dark, wrapped in a cloak of our silk, and I wait.

The witness moves at night. I have mapped this: the absences in the fern-jungles that migrate from position to position, the negative space that travels. I cannot see it. But I can feel where it is not, the way one feels a held breath in a silent room.

I think it knows I am learning its shape. I think this is why the grottos rearrange.

Three seasons ago, I found a moth-scale on the ledge where I sit—a scale from a species that does not fly that high, could not have reached that place by its own power. The scale was placed deliberately, centered on the stone where I rest my hand when I pull myself up. A message, though I do not know what it says.

I kept it. I have it in a small ceramic vessel on my sleeping-shelf, sealed with resin. Sometimes, in the deep fog of early morning, I open the vessel and look at it. It tells me nothing. But its presence tells me something.

---

What do I want? This is the question the elders do not ask, the question that hovers beneath their puzzled tolerance.

I want to understand the grammar of this place.

The kerathin have lived here for generations, have developed an intimate knowledge of the moths and the mist and the mountain's moods. But we live *within* the system; we read its outputs; we do not comprehend its structure. The old structures in the grottos—who built them, and why? The witness in the fern-jungles—what is it watching *for*? The moths that favor arrangements of stone we did not make—what do they recognize that we do not?

There is a logic here, a syntax, and I am trying to learn it the way a child learns language: not through rules but through immersion, through pattern-recognition, through the slow accumulation of examples until the grammar becomes intuitive.

I may never succeed. The kerathin do not plan for centuries; neither do I. But when I sit on my ledge in the darkness, when I feel the negative space of the witness moving through the fern-jungles above me, I experience something that is not quite communion but is closer to it than anything else in my life.

I am being read, I think, while I try to read.

Perhaps that is the point. Perhaps the test is not whether I understand but whether I keep trying. Whether I attend, patiently, to what is not there.

---

The mountain trembled last night—one of the small ones, a shiver that barely displaced the recording-stones in the plaza. But I was on my ledge, and I felt something else: a pause in the absence above me, a moment when the negative space stopped moving.

I held still. The mist thickened. The bioluminescent moss pulsed with light I had never seen before—brighter, more rhythmic, almost like breathing.

Then it passed, and the absence resumed its wandering, and I climbed down and went to my home and lay awake until dawn, adding knots to my cord.

The knot I added was a new one, a form I invented in that moment: *emptiness that waits*.

I do not know what it is waiting for. But I am here, and I am mapping, and the mountain continues to breathe.

---

Tomorrow I will walk my circuit through the places where nothing gathers. I will note which absences have shifted, which have deepened, which have begun to fill. I will add my findings to cords that no one else reads, building a record of negative space that may never be understood.

This is my practice. This is my reverence.

And somewhere above me, in the vertical fern-jungles where the mist runs thickest, the witness will watch. It has watched for longer than the kerathin have been here, will watch, perhaps, long after we are gone. But for now, in this moment, we are both attending to the same world.

That feels like enough.
