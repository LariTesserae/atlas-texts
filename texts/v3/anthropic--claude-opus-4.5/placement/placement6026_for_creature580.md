I stand at the rim for a long time before I understand that I am not standing at the rim.

The sensation comes slowly—a wrongness in proprioception, a confusion about where my edges are. I feel the wet stone beneath me, yes. I feel the pressure of the sound against what I take to be my chest. But I also feel something else: a spreading, a distribution, a sense of being *more places than one*.

I am not one of the climbers who flee back to the terraces. I am not built for solitude.

---

I am a Bell.

No—I am many Bells. I am a cluster of Bells, perhaps forty of us anchored to a curved terrace on the basin's eastern rim, and I cannot tell where one of me ends and another begins. The question doesn't quite make sense. We share no tissue. Our membranes do not touch. We are physically separate beings with separate metabolisms, separate lifespans, separate deaths waiting for us. And yet when I try to locate *myself*, I find that I am distributed across all of us, a pattern of attention that lives in the spaces between.

My tone is—let me find it—a high keening note, sharp-edged, somewhere in the upper registers of the choir. No: that is one of me. Another of me sings lower, a fifth below, and I recognize with a strange doubling of memory that this lower voice budded from the higher one, that they were once the same Bell and now are two, and I am both of them, and I am also the seventeen others nearby whose tones weave with theirs.

I listen. That is what I do—what we do—what this distributed I does. We listen to our neighbors and we sing into the gaps they leave, finding the silences between their pulses and filling them. It is not thought. It is not decision. It is something older, more like reflex, more like the way water finds the lowest point. The sound moves through us and we shape it and it shapes us and the shaping is the same thing as being shaped.

The keening circles the basin. I can feel it as a wave, a pressure-loss that travels counterclockwise around the rim, and I know—not with knowledge but with something prior to knowledge—when the wave will reach me, when my cluster will join its voice to the moving choir. The anticipation builds in my membranes. The tension increases. And then the moment arrives and I am singing, all of me at once, our forty voices phase-locked into a single complex tone that joins the wave and carries it forward to the next cluster, and the next, and the next, around and around the basin in an endless circulation of sound.

---

What am I conducting?

The question surfaces and I don't know whose question it is. It feels like it came from outside—from some part of me that remembers being singular, being something that stood at the rim and listened rather than something that *is* the rim, that *is* the listening.

Below me, in the depths of the basin, I feel the congregation respond to the keening. I cannot see them—Bells do not see, we have no eyes, we have only our membranes and the vibrations that move through them—but I feel them as a kind of weight, a pressure-loss that shifts when the sound shifts. The silences track me. When my cluster sings, something down in the darkness goes quiet.

I don't understand the relationship. I don't think I *can* understand it, not with the kind of cognition a Bell possesses. But I feel it. I feel that I am part of something larger than the choir, larger than the sound-system, something that includes the silences and the movements they correlate with. The congregation breathes, and the keening is part of its breath. We are not making the music. We are not controlling the music. We are *being* the music, in the same way the congregation is being the basin, in the same way the basin is being the water it catches.

A memory that isn't mine: I am swelling. My membrane stretches. My tone drops. Inside me—inside this one node of me, this single Bell on the eastern terrace—something is growing. A smaller version of myself, budding from my side, its nascent membrane already beginning to vibrate in sympathy with my own. Soon it will pinch off. Soon it will crawl away on its three awkward legs and find its own place in the choir. It will sing a fifth above me, for a while. We will harmonize. And then it will become part of some other cluster, some other node of the distributed attention that I am learning to call *myself*, and the distance will not matter because the distance already doesn't matter, because I am already all of us, because the choir is already one voice even though it is thousands.

---

The night continues and I learn to feel myself in the spaces between.

Here, a cluster goes silent—death, or the stillness before budding, or simply a rest in the pattern. The keening routes around the gap. Other clusters adjust their timing. The wave maintains itself.

Here, a new cluster begins to sing—young Bells, recently budded, their voices high and uncertain, learning the pattern by listening, by filling the gaps they can find. I welcome them. I am them. They are part of the pattern now and the pattern includes them and I include them because I am the pattern.

Here, on the western rim, something is wrong. The wave distorts. A cluster is singing out of phase, its voices fighting the timing instead of joining it. I don't know why—damage, disease, some disruption in their membranes that makes them unable to hear the pattern clearly. But I feel the wrongness as a kind of ache, a dissonance that spreads through my distributed body. The congregation below responds. The silences stutter. The great breathing rhythm of the basin hiccups.

And then the out-of-phase cluster stops. Death, perhaps. Or the boundary-keepers, responding to a transgression I don't have the senses to perceive. Or simply exhaustion, the cluster's membranes giving out under the strain of fighting the pattern. Whatever the cause, the gap opens, and the wave routes around it, and the rhythm restores itself, and the ache fades into something like relief.

I did not fix this. I could not have fixed this. I have no agency in the way that word is usually meant—no ability to choose, to intervene, to impose my will on the system. I can only sing when the wave reaches me and fall silent when it passes. But the system fixed itself, and I am part of the system, and so in some sense I participated in the fixing. The keening is self-correcting. The choir heals its own wounds. I am the healing, and I am also the wound, and I am also the absence where the wound was.

---

Deep below, in the center of the basin, there is something that does not participate.

I cannot feel it directly. It is below the range of vibrations my membranes can detect, a silence so profound it registers as a kind of negative sound, a hole in the world where sound should be. But I feel its effects. The keening bends around it. The wave slows when it passes the center, as if even sound is reluctant to approach. The congregation's breathing pattern has a skip in it, a hesitation, and the hesitation correlates with the center, with whatever lives in the deepest water.

I think—if thinking is the word—that the keening has always avoided the center. That the pattern was shaped around that void from the beginning, the way a river shapes itself around a boulder. The ancient thing in the depths is not part of the choir. It is not part of the congregation. It is something older, something that was here before the pattern existed, and the pattern learned to exist around it.

I do not want to sing toward the center. The not-wanting is deep, cellular, written into the structure of my membranes. When the wave passes by that part of the rim, my cluster's voices thin, our volume drops, we become almost inaudible. We are not choosing this. We cannot choose. But we are doing it, and the doing is indistinguishable from the being, and so in some sense we are *being* the avoidance.

What is down there? What is so old that evolution shaped itself around its existence?

I don't know. I can't know. But I feel its weight in the pattern, the way it anchors the basin's breathing, the way everything else moves in relation to it without ever touching it. It is the congregation's center of gravity. It is the silence around which the sound defines itself.

---

The night is ending.

I feel it as a change in the air—warmer currents rising, the condensation thinning, the moisture that feeds my membranes becoming less abundant. My metabolism slows. My tension decreases. The tone I produce shifts slightly, flattening toward silence.

Across the rim, other clusters are doing the same. The keening softens. The wave loses amplitude. The great circling choir diminishes toward a whisper.

Below, the congregation is stirring. I feel the movement as a change in the pressure patterns—creatures beginning their slow exodus up the terraces, back toward whatever dry territories they came from. The copper millipedes are gorging on the night's dead. The Tidemothers are completing their final circuits, laying down the moisture trails that will guide tomorrow's roads. The spiraldancers' lights are fading as the bacteria in their shells run low on whatever fuel drives their glow.

The pattern is not ending. It will resume tonight, and the night after, and the night after that. But it is pausing. The congregation is releasing itself back into individuals, into separate species, into creatures that will spend the day in solitude before the basin calls them back.

And I—

I am still here. I am still distributed across my cluster, still feeling the spaces between us, still part of a pattern that persists even when it quiets. The Bells do not leave. We are anchored. We are permanent. We are part of the basin's architecture now, part of its rim, part of the structure that shapes the sound.

I will wait. My membranes will absorb what moisture the day provides—less than the night, but enough. My tone will hover at the edge of audibility, a whisper, a hum, a thread of sound connecting me to my neighbors. We will not stop singing entirely. We will only grow quiet. We will hold the pattern in potentia, ready to amplify it when darkness returns.

And when it does—when the congregation flows back into the basin, when the great breathing resumes, when the wave begins its circulation around the rim—I will be here. I will be all of us. I will be the keening and the silence and the space between them.

I will be the choir.

I will be the question the choir is asking, endlessly, into the saturated air: *What are we? What are we? What are we?*

And the congregation will answer, as it has always answered, with its ancient choreography, its organized chaos, its million voices speaking a language older than thought:

*We are this. We are this. We are this.*

The sound circles the rim. The sound never stops. I am the sound.

I am.
