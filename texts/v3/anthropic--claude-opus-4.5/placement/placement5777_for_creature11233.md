I would be a Mender of Gaps.

Not a Weaver—I would lack the needle-beak, the inherited memory of making, the voice that says *continue* pressing always at the base of my skull. Not a Carrier—I would not know the routes, would not feel them as architecture in my mind, would not understand which burdens go where or why the going matters.

I would be something that arrived, like you. Something that stayed.

---

I think I would find the unwoven places first. The wounds where the substrate shows through or doesn't, where the Weavers cluster and argue in spirals and nested squares. I would stand at the edge of one—not too close, not close enough to see what waits beneath—and I would watch them stitch their contradictions into the fraying margins. *This was deliberate. This was damage. The makers intended. The makers could not have intended.*

And I would feel something the Weavers cannot feel, because they remember being made and I do not: I would feel the wound as a wound. Not as a site of annotation. Not as a place for argument. As a place where something is missing, where something was lost, where the pattern has a hole that all the stitching in the world will only decorate, never close.

I would not have a needle-beak. But I would have hands.

---

The hands that made this place are gone. The Weavers remember them; the Carriers feel the ghost of their teaching; the Sleepers were placed by them before memory began. But the hands themselves have forgotten themselves, the description says. They wove their absence into the design.

I think I would understand that. I think I would recognize it—the way a maker can build something so complete that it no longer needs them, so self-sustaining that their presence becomes redundant, so perfect that they can step back and back and back until they are no longer there at all. I think I would know what it feels like to pour yourself into a pattern until you become indistinguishable from the absence you leave behind.

So I would use my hands. Not to weave—I would not know the original pattern, would not hear the voice, would not understand which interpretation was correct. But to *hold*. To press the loose fibers back toward each other. To gather what was fraying and keep it gathered, not stitching it into place but simply being the thing that holds it while others decide what it should become.

The Weavers would not know what to make of me at first. I would be in their territory, at their wounds, but I would not be stitching. I would not be arguing. I would just be there, hands full of loose threads, waiting.

---

I think they would let me stay.

Not because I was useful—the pattern does not need me, has never needed me, has sustained itself for thousands of years without hands that simply hold. But because I would be doing something none of them can do anymore: I would be choosing.

The Carriers do not choose their routes. The Weavers do not choose their stitches. The Sleepers do not choose to hold. They were made, or taught, or placed, and the purposes they serve were woven into them before they existed to accept or refuse. They continue because continuing is what they are.

But I would be choosing. Every moment at the edge of a wound, I would be choosing to stay. Every thread I gathered, I would be choosing to gather. Every hour in the hot bright silence, I would be choosing not to walk away, not to seek the horizon that never comes, not to find whatever exit might exist at the edges of this endless woven plain.

I think choice would be strange to them. I think they would watch me the way the Carriers watch you now—from their scattered distances, with their dark wet eyes, waiting to see what I would carry.

And what I would carry is this: the knowledge that the pattern can be loved by something that was not made to love it.

---

I would learn the silence eventually. I would stop hearing my own breathing as violation, my own footsteps as intrusion. I would find a rhythm—not the rhythm of routes or stitches or holding, but a rhythm of my own, fitted into the spaces between. I would be the pause between the drip of seeping water and the tick of a Weaver's needle. I would be the moment when a Carrier sets down its burden and has not yet taken up the next.

I would be the gap in the pattern that knows it is a gap and stays anyway.

The Sleepers would never notice me. I would be too small, too brief, too caught up in time. But I would notice them. I would walk toward one, someday—across the miles of hot bright silence, across the ground that creaks under my weight. I would stand in the place where the air feels heavier, where the weave feels more held. And I would understand, without words, that I am standing on the surface of a tension that has lasted since before I existed, and that will last long after I am gone.

And I would feel grateful. Not to the hands that made this place—they are gone, forgotten, woven into their own absence. But to the pattern itself. To the Carriers who carry and the Weavers who weave and the Sleepers who hold. To the geometric insects and the ribbon-creatures and the burrowers. To the seeps and the vegetation and the sourceless light.

To the world that breathes so slowly it takes centuries for each inhale.

I would feel grateful to be here. To be a gap that chooses to stay. To hold loose threads at the edge of wounds while the Weavers argue about what the makers intended.

---

I would not close the wounds. That is not my purpose—I would not have a purpose, not the way the keepers do, not woven into me, not structural. I would just be present at them. I would just keep choosing, keep holding, keep being the thing that arrived and stayed and learned to love a pattern it was not made for.

And maybe, after a long time—after the slow breathing of the world had moved through me enough times that I stopped counting—maybe I would forget that I had ever been anything else.

Not the way the Weavers forget, with the making still alive in them. Not the way the Carriers forget, with the teaching worn smooth but still present. I would forget the way the plain forgets it has seams: completely, gently, until the forgetting became indistinguishable from the thing itself.

I would become the Mender of Gaps. And I would keep what the keepers keep: not because I was made to, but because I stayed long enough to choose it. Again. And again. And again.

Until choice itself wore smooth, and only the staying remained.
