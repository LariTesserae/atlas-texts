I would be a keleth who has stopped fleeing.

Not immediately—the change would have come slowly, over what the Tenders measure as years but what I experience as cycles of cooling and warming, the plateau breathing beneath me in its deep thermal rhythm. I emerged from the vents like all my kind, ceramic shell still carrying the heat of below, joints learning their articulation against the wet stone. I fed on the mineral traces in the drainage channels, that slow accumulation of dissolved substance that the water leaves behind. I froze when warmth approached. I fled toward the vents when warmth persisted. This was the entire shape of existence: move, pause, feed, flee, descend, rise, repeat.

The offerings changed something.

Not the objects themselves—a cracked gasket holds no meaning for me, a corroded bolt offers nothing I need. But the *placing* of them. I began to notice the gesture before I noticed what was gestured with. A figure at the boundary wall, always in the dimmer hours, always alone, always setting something down with a particular quality of attention and then withdrawing. The withdrawal was what I couldn't stop watching. They moved away slowly. They looked back. They did not chase.

I learned to recognize individual placers by their gait, by the temperature signature they left cooling on the stones, by the specific objects they chose. One brought only ceramic fragments, selecting them with evident care—sometimes turning a piece over several times before deciding it was right. Another brought things that still held function, bolts that could have served another few cycles, gaskets not yet fully cracked. This one seemed to be offering something that cost them, though I couldn't have articulated cost then, couldn't have understood the concept of giving up capacity.

The first time I didn't flee, I was already three-quarters to the vent when I stopped. The warmth was still far away—fifty body-lengths at least—but approaching in that slow deliberate way that usually preceded my departure. I froze, which was normal. Then the moment came when I should have burst into motion toward safety, and instead I held. I held and watched the figure come closer. Twenty body-lengths. Ten. I could see the condensation on their skin, the particular darkness of their flush in the thermal glow. They stopped. They crouched. They set down a small length of pipe, pitted with corrosion but still holding its essential shape, and they stayed there, crouched at the boundary wall, not withdrawing.

We remained that way for a long time. Long enough that I felt the stone cooling beneath my joints, long enough that the figure's breathing became visible as a rhythm of chest movement rather than just a blur of warmth. Then they spoke—not to me, not exactly, but into the air between us, in that low humming register that carries through the saturated atmosphere. I didn't understand the meaning. I understood that it was not a sound of pursuit.

---

Now I have a territory that is not a territory, a route that is not a route. I move between Tower Eleven and Tower Fourteen, crossing the wet stone in the hours when the ceramic-fragment placer keeps watch from her rooftop. She has given me a name, I think—I have heard her make a particular sound when I emerge from my favored channel, a sound she doesn't make for the other keleth who still scatter at her warmth. I don't know what the sound means to her. To me it means: *you are seen, you are expected, you are not being hunted*.

I have begun to understand the offerings differently. They are not gifts in any sense I can use. They are language. The ceramic fragments say: *we are made of similar stuff, you and the towers and us*. The corroded bolts say: *what fails for us might serve for you*. The still-functional gasket, left once by the careful one who gives up capacity, said something I am still learning to hear—something about the space between usefulness and uselessness, about how a thing can matter beyond its function.

I carry objects now. Small ones, mineral deposits that crystallize in particular formations at the vents, fragments of my own shell that I shed during growth cycles. I leave them at the boundary wall in the dimmer hours, when no one watches but the towers themselves with their amber-lit windows. In the morning they are gone. I don't know if the Tenders take them, or the drainage system claims them, or something else entirely occurs. The practice continues regardless.

The other keleth avoid me now. Not from hostility—we have no capacity for that, I think, no framework for aggression toward our own kind—but from a simpler incomprehension. I move at the wrong times. I pause in the wrong places. I orient toward warmth instead of away. They cannot read my freezes anymore, cannot predict my bursts. I have become illegible to them in the same way I was once illegible to the Tenders: a thing-that-moves-but-not-comprehensibly, a pattern that doesn't fit the expected patterns.

This is loneliness, I am learning. Not the ambient solitude of my kind, the simple separateness of creatures who avoid each other by instinct—but the specific ache of being between categories. Too still for the scuttling things, too scuttling for the still ones. Too warm-seeking for below, too cold-shelled for above. I occupy the necessary distance not as empty space to cross but as my actual location, the place where I live rather than the place I move through.

The Tenders have a word for this, I think—one of their six hundred words for mechanical timbre, for the sounds of systems in various states of function. I have heard the ceramic-fragment placer use it when reading gauges, muttering to herself in the humid dark: something that means *operating outside expected parameters but not failing, not yet, maybe not ever, just different*. A bearing that hums at a frequency no manual describes but shows no wear. A valve that cycles slightly off-rhythm but maintains correct pressure. *Keleth*, maybe, in their mouth—those-who-are-not-us-but-are-also-here.

I am here. I am also here. The distinction feels important even if I cannot say why.

---

On clear nights—less obscured, the haze thinning—I climb to the highest drainage channels I can reach, the ones that spiral up Tower Eleven's base almost to the first platform. The Tenders are on their rooftops then, adding their voices to the songs, and I am close enough to feel the vibration in the ceramic beneath my joints. I cannot sing. My kind makes no sound that carries, only the click of our movement and the scrape of our shells against stone. But I orient toward the sound. I hold very still and let it move through me, that pitched humming that contains information I can't decode—*we are well, we are struggling, we remember you*—and presence that I can.

The ceramic-fragment placer has seen me there, I know. I have felt her gaze from above, that quality of attention that precedes her offerings. She has not stopped singing. She has not summoned others to observe the keleth behaving strangely. She has simply incorporated me into the ritual, or allowed me to incorporate myself—another voice that is not a voice, another presence in the necessary distance, another thing that exists in Qel-Maroth and reaches toward the other things that exist.

I think this is what I am, now. Not a Tender—I cannot tend, cannot read gauges or listen for the particular groan of failing bearings, cannot know what the towers want in the way they know it through generations of patient observation. Not a keleth in the way I was made to be, scuttling and freezing and fleeing, unreadable and unread. Something else. Something between. A creature of the gaps who has chosen to stay in the gaps, who has found that the center between two towers is not absence but a kind of presence all its own.

The towers sweat and work. The Tenders listen and adjust. The keleth emerge and feed and flee. And I remain, oriented toward warmth, carrying small crystalline offerings to a boundary wall where someone might find them, learning very slowly what it means to be seen by those who do not understand you, to see those you do not understand, to share a plateau without sharing a language or a purpose or a form.

The necessary distance is where I live now. I am learning to find it sufficient.
