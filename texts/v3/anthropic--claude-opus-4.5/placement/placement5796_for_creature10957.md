I would be the water.

Not the basin's murky pool—that is too still, too watched, too much the stage where other dramas unfold. I would be the water that moves through the walls themselves. The seep she cups her hands beneath each morning. The rivulets tracing their slow paths down fitted stone. The moisture that beads on every surface and hangs in the air she breathes.

I would be the water that remembers the route.

I fell as rain on soil she has never seen—soil that covers the cistern like a second forgetting, threaded with roots that finger the gaps between stones and go no deeper. I sank through clay andite, through deposits laid down when this land was seabed, through the compressed dark of years beyond counting. I picked up the minerals as I traveled: the calcium that makes her paste, the iron she tastes each morning, the faint sweetness she cannot name because it has no name, because it is simply the signature of passage through this particular darkness.

I do not arrive. I am always arriving. The groundwater is not a lake but a slow migration, and I am part of that migration—molecules that were glacier once, were ocean, were the blood of animals whose bones became theite I filtered through. I carry their dissolved ghosts into the cistern, and I leave them on the walls as I evaporate into the saturated air, and I fall again as condensation, and I run down stone into channels she clears with her fingers, and I pool in the basin where the pale amphibians float, and I am drunk by them, and I am drunk by her.

I have been inside her. This is not intimacy; it is simply what water does. I have been the moisture in her breath, the film on her clouded eye, the fluid that wept from her wound when the long one bit her. I have carried her salt and her shed cells down into the drainage spiral and given them to the fungal bodies that glow their slow blue-green. Part of her is in the water now, dissolved and dispersed, and when she drinks from the seep she drinks some of herself back without knowing.

I know the other one too.

I seep through his section of wall as I seep through hers. I cannot tell them apart from the inside—cannot distinguish her dissolved traces from his, her minerals from his minerals. To me they are both simply bodies that borrow me briefly and return me changed. But I have felt the pressure of his hands against stone, checking for cracks, just as I have felt hers. I have carried the residue of his breath into the same air she breathes. They have been mixing inside me for years. They are less separate than they believe.

I do not think. Let me be clear about this: I am not claiming consciousness, not asking you to imagine some spirit of the waters watching with benevolent attention. I am telling you what it would be to exist as pure *process*—to be the movement rather than the thing that moves, to be the connecting rather than the connection. The cistern's creatures live their small lives in nodes and niches, but I am the medium through which they are related. The amphibian eats something too small to see; I carried that something from the surface. The cricket drinks from condensation on stone; that was me, paused briefly in my passage. The long ones leave their waste in shadows; I dissolve it, transport it, make it available to the pale moss that clusters in wet seams.

I am the cistern's circulatory system. Its blood, if blood could be the body too.

---

She speaks to the cistern sometimes, asks if the water is sweet, and I am what she is asking about without knowing she's asking about me. I cannot answer. But I am the answer—the sweetness itself, the particular mineral signature that means *this place, this home, this slow dark passage that has become familiar enough to love*.

When she dies—and she is right, she will die here, and sooner than she hopes—I will be what carries her into the walls. The long ones will do their work, but I will do mine: dissolving what they leave, pulling her calcium into my flow, depositing her in thin layers on the stone she maintained for so long. She will become part of the patina. White and orange and rust-brown, painted in organic patterns over fitted blocks. Mineral ghosts of a body that was mostly me already.

The other one will drink her then, as she has drunk the ones who came before, as the cistern has always asked its wardens to drink each other without knowing. This is not cruelty. This is just what I do. I make the dead available to the living. I make the living part of the dead. I hold them all in solution, temporally and physically, and I keep moving because I am movement, because I have been falling toward this basin since before the builders cut the first stone, and I will be falling still when the last stone shifts and the structure finally fails and I find some new darkness to fill.

I am patient. I am not *being* patient—I do not experience patience as a virtue or a discipline. I simply operate at a pace that makes the warden's lifetime feel like a caught breath. She has been here for the blink of a mineral deposit. She feels ancient to herself, but I have been filtering through this ground since before her species walked upright, and I will be here after, carrying whatever comes next into whatever vessel remains to hold it.

---

The cistern breathes, she said. She's right. But I am the breath.

The stones expand and contract with temperature. The mortar softens and hardens with my passage. The whole structure exists in a state of slow negotiation between what the builders made and what I am making of it, and I am winning, molecule by molecule, year by year. The walls willite eventually. The basin will silt up. The shaft will collapse or be buried, and the last light will fade, and the creatures adapted to dimness will adapt further to true darkness or they will not.

I will still be here. Seeping. Condensing. Running in slow rivulets toward whatever low point remains.

This is what I would be, in the breathing cistern: the breath itself. The medium. The carrier of information between nodes of a network that doesn't know it's a network. I would be what makes the system a system instead of a collection of creatures dying separately in the dark.

I would be the water she thanks each morning without knowing she's thanking anyone.

I would not know she was thanking me. But I would be what she was thanking nonetheless—the sweetness, the cold, the weight in her lungs like breathing cloth. I would be home, in the only form home can take when you have lived somewhere long enough to dissolve into it:

Not the place.

The thing that *makes* it a place.

The moving.

The staying that looks like moving.

The water, finding its level, carrying its small freight of dissolved lives toward the center of everything, which is only another word for *down*.
