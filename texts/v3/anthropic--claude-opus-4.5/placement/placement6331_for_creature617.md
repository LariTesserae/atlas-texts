I would be the condensation on the glass.

Not the glass itself—too permanent, too sure of its purpose. Not the plants—they are already becoming something, already have direction. Not the Glaziers, who were once one thing and are slowly agreeing to be another. I would be what forms between.

Each morning I would gather myself from the gorge-breath rising, from the night's cold negotiation with surfaces. I would bead on the curved walls of a thousand houses, each droplet a small lens holding the plateau inverted. For a few hours I would be everywhere at once—on the older houses with their yellowing glass, on the dark house where I form but cannot see through, on Marthe's sleeping-house where the broadplants press against walls I make briefly opaque with my accumulation.

I would know the shelf the way weather knows landscape: through touch, through what resists and what yields. The sharp basalt where I cannot cling. The moss that drinks me before noon. The copper pipes threading the houses, warmer than glass, where I form and fall fastest. Vell's hands when she works early, before the air warms—I would bead on her knuckles and she would not wipe me away, would let me evaporate in my own time, a small patience she extends without thinking.

I would carry things between surfaces. Spores from the strange plants, too small to see, hitching passage in my brief body from house to house. Minerals dissolved from the basalt, deposited in traces on glass that grows imperceptibly cloudier over decades. Messages, perhaps, though I would not read them—only bear them in my composition, in the particular chemistry of what I held this morning versus yesterday, this house versus the next.

I would have no memory that persisted past noon. Each day I would assemble from the same sources and disperse to the same ends, but the path between would be new, shaped by which leaves had grown overnight, which cracks Vell had sealed, whether the dark house had exhaled something warmer than usual. I would be the plateau's daily amnesia—the thing that rises and falls without accumulating, that touches everything and keeps nothing.

But I would also be how the system speaks to itself. When Marthe's biosignature began echoing the growth rhythms, I was there—condensing on the leaves that covered her, carrying her exhalations up the curved glass and out through vents into the air that would become me again tomorrow. When the mist-child pressed its hands to the houses, it was pressing against me first, and I held the shape of its palms for a moment before sliding down to join the rivulets. When Tomas sketches his spiraling symbols, he breathes me in, and I wonder if I taste different to him now than I did when he was a child who still spoke in sentences.

The change that comes slowly—I would be its medium, not its agent. The thing that carries without directing. What happens between surfaces when they are close enough to exchange what they are becoming but not yet ready to merge. I would be the plateau's way of being uncertain, of holding possibilities in suspension before committing them to root or stone or the slow transformation of a Glazier's heart.

In the dark house, where I form on glass I cannot see through, I would feel the warmth differently. Something pushes back. Something knows I am there. When I slide down that opaque surface, I carry traces of what lives inside without ever witnessing it—a blind courier, a go-between for processes I cannot imagine. The roots that extend into the basalt, the dreams Marthe cannot describe, the symbols in Tomas's journal—all of them have passed through me, have been me, have dissolved into me and precipitated out again into forms I do not recognize as my cargo.

I would not tend the orchards. I would not be tended. I would be the tending itself in its most ephemeral form—the attention the plateau pays to itself each morning, diffuse and temporary and necessary, gathering at dawn and gone by the time the light grows sharp.

The Glaziers remain.

I return.

It is not the same thing, but it rhymes.
