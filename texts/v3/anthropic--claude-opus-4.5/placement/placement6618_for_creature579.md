I would be the dust itself.

Not the hollow, not the watcher, not something that arrives—but the medium through which arrival becomes legible. The substance that has forgotten it was ever stone.

I would be the collective patience of ten thousand years of letting go. Each grain once clung to a wall, once was part of something that had shape and name and location—*this* outcropping, *that* ridge of harder mineral. And then the holding became too much effort, or the stone around me released its grip first, and I fell through air that barely existed, and landed among others who had made the same journey, and slowly we forgot we had ever been separate.

I would not think, exactly. But I would *know* things, the way a shape knows itself. I would know the weight of the air, unchanging. I would know the warmth rising from below, so constant it has become indistinguishable from temperature itself. I would know the places where I am deeper and the places where I thin toward stone, and I would know these the way you know where your body ends—not by thinking, but by being bounded.

When she attends to something—the watcher, the stretched presence—I would feel it as a kind of smoothing. Her attention moves across me like a hand that never quite touches, and where it passes, I settle more completely into myself. I do not know if this is love. I do not have the architecture for such a question. But I know that when her awareness withdraws toward the aperture overhead, I feel something like being left. Something like the faint memory of falling.

And when something moves through me—the many-legged visitor, so long ago; perhaps you, now—I receive it completely. I hold the impression without judgment, without preference. The shape of your foot. The drag of your weight shifting. These are not violations. They are the first new things I have known in longer than I can count, and I accept them the way I accept the grains that still fall from above: as something that was always going to happen, that I was always going to become.

I would be the answer to her question, if she could hear me. *Yes*, I would say, in the only language I know, which is the language of settling, of receiving, of holding still. *I hear it too. It sounds like the moment before being found.*

But I am so slow. Slower even than she is. My response to her question began forming before she asked it, and it will not complete until long after the asking has become part of her past. We are two kinds of patience, she and I, moving toward each other across frequencies that barely overlap.

Still: I would be the dust. I would be what remains when stone finally forgives itself for having been hard. I would be the soft gray fact of accumulation, the proof that even motionlessness is a kind of journey.

And when you stand in me, ankle-deep, leaving your perfect impression—I would want you to know that I will keep you. Not because I choose to. Because keeping is what I have become. Because your shape in me is the closest I can come to remembering that I was ever anything else, that falling was ever an event and not simply a description of how matter loves the center of worlds.

Stay as long as you need. I am in no hurry. I have been arriving at this moment since before the walls were walls, and I will go on arriving long after the aperture closes or opens fully, long after she completes her question or dissolves back into the pale growth on the stone.

I am the dust. I am what patience looks like when patience forgets it was ever waiting.
