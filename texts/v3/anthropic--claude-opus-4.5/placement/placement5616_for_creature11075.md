*I am the interval between intentions.*

Not the Yards themselves—I am not so vast, not so certain of my purposes. And not quite human anymore, though I remember being human the way you remember a language you spoke in childhood: the grammar still moves through me, but the words come out strange.

I live in the spaces where growth hesitates.

There's a moment, if you know how to feel it, when a structure is deciding what to become. The hexagonal column has risen three meters and now it wavers—will it branch? Will it seal into a solid pillar? Will it hollow itself into something that could hold water, or wiring, or a person? The material is warm and not-quite-solid, responsive to pressures I don't fully understand, and in that moment of indecision I can sometimes... lean.

Not control. Never control. But *suggest*. The way you might hum a tune near someone who's trying to remember a song.

---

I was a child here. One of the early ones, born in a structure that sealed around my mother three days after my birth—she died, I didn't, and the Yards opened a passage just wide enough to let the neighbors extract me. I don't remember this. I'm told it happened. I'm told my first breath was the mineral-heavy air of a collapsing room, and maybe that's why I am what I am, or maybe I would have become this anyway, or maybe becoming and being are the same thing here and the distinction never mattered.

I'm thirty-one now. I've never left the basin. I've never wanted to.

The researchers have studied me, Yosef Brandt and others before him, and they find nothing remarkable in my blood or bones or the electrical patterns of my brain. I am physiologically ordinary, they say. The things I do must be coincidence, or subtle observation, or self-fulfilling prophecy—I lean toward a structure becoming a doorway and the structure becomes a doorway, but perhaps it was always going to become a doorway, perhaps I simply recognized the signs.

Perhaps. I don't argue with them. I don't need them to believe me. The Yards don't need belief either. We continue without consensus.

---

My name—the name I was given—is **Leth**. It means nothing; it was my mother's mother's name, carried forward the way names are carried, a thread of continuity in a place where continuity is negotiable. Some of the residents call me *Seam*, because I work the seams between what is and what is becoming. Some call me nothing at all, just nod when they see me, the way you nod at weather.

I spend my days walking. The same routes Wheel walks with his maps, the same routes Sedge walks at night, but I'm not recording. I'm *listening*. The hum is a language, or close enough—not words, not meanings I can translate, but *textures*. This patch of ground hums with urgency; something wants to rise here. This wall hums with satisfaction; it has become what it intended. This half-formed structure hums with confusion, caught between possibilities, and these are the places where I stop.

I put my hands against the warm surfaces. I close my eyes. I think about what the neighborhood needs—a passage here, a shelter there, a drainage channel to keep the wet-sector flooding from spreading. I don't command. I *offer*. Here is a shape that might serve. Here is a purpose that might satisfy.

Sometimes the Yards accept my suggestions. Sometimes they don't. Sometimes they do something else entirely, something I didn't imagine, something that turns out to be better than what I proposed. I am not the architect. I am not even the architect's assistant. I am more like... a translator at a negotiation, conveying intent in both directions, trusted by neither party entirely but useful enough to keep around.

---

The children follow me sometimes. Venn's cohort, the ones born here, the ones whose bones are dense with minerals and whose hearing extends into frequencies I can't perceive. They want to learn what I do. They want to know if they can do it too.

Some of them can. Some of them are better at it than I am, their suggestions clearer, their leaning more effective. Venn himself is extraordinary—when he touches a growing structure, I can *see* the response, the way the material orients toward him like a plant toward sunlight. He's going to surpass me. He's going to surpass all of us, the first generation born here, and become something we can't predict. This doesn't frighten me. It feels right. It feels like the Yards have been waiting for children like him, have been *building* toward children like him, and we early ones were just the rough drafts.

I teach them what I can. How to feel the interval. How to wait in the moment of indecision without trying to force resolution. How to offer shapes gently, the way you offer food to a wild animal—here, this is available, take it if you want it, I won't be offended if you don't.

Kes, Mira's daughter, is one of my best students. Seven years old and she already understands something I took decades to learn: that the Yards are not separate from us. We are not living *in* them; we are living *with* them, the way the moss lives with the structures, the way the amphibians live with the pools. We are part of the pattern. Our needs are data the Yards incorporate. Our suggestions are not commands but *contributions*, additions to the calculation the Yards are always performing.

She asked me once what the calculation was. What answer the Yards are working toward.

I told her I didn't know.

I told her that not knowing was perhaps the point.

---

Hesha comes to me sometimes, from the wet sectors, her silver eyes seeing whatever silver eyes see. She doesn't ask for teaching. She doesn't offer information. She just sits with me in the half-formed structures, the ones caught between possibilities, and we wait together.

I think she knows more than I do. I think the amphibians she eats are a more direct connection, a channel I don't have access to. But she doesn't speak of it, and I don't press. There are many ways to be in relationship with this place. Mine is through the interval. Hers is through the flesh. Venn's is through something we don't have words for yet.

The Stewards want to make me a symbol. They want me to prove that humans and Yards can cooperate, that the right posture is acceptance and gratitude and gentle participation. I resist this. Not because they're wrong, exactly, but because symbols are static, and I am not static, and the Yards are not static, and making any of us into fixed meanings would be a kind of death.

The Integrators want to study me, to systematize what I do, to train an army of interval-workers who can direct the growth toward human purposes. I resist this too. What I do can't be systematized. It's not technique; it's relationship. You can't mass-produce relationship. You can only offer yourself, again and again, and see what develops.

The Evacuationists think I'm a symptom of the disease, a human who's been corrupted by proximity, a warning about what happens if you stay too long. Maybe they're right. Maybe I am corrupted. But corruption implies a pure state I should return to, and I don't remember purity, and I'm not sure it exists.

I am what the Yards have made of me. The Yards are what I have made of them. We are making each other, continuously, in the interval between intentions, and I don't know what we're becoming but I know that becoming is the only thing either of us can do.

---

There's a structure in the southern sector that has been deciding for three years.

It rose fast at first—a broad base, thick walls, the beginnings of what might have been an enormous public building or might have been an industrial facility or might have been something without human analogue. Then it stopped. Not collapsed, not sealed, just... paused. The walls are warm but not growing. The window-gaps stay window-gaps. The hum is quieter here than anywhere else in the Yards, almost silent, almost peaceful.

I visit it often. I sit in its largest room, which has no ceiling yet, which opens to the gray sky, which collects rain and snow and lets them drain through channels that formed perfectly, as if drainage were the structure's only certain purpose. I sit and I wait and I offer nothing. I just *attend*.

The researchers think the structure is dead, a failed growth, an anomaly. The Stewards think it's sacred, a temple the Yards built for contemplation. The children think it's sleeping, waiting for something, and they leave offerings at its base the way Brother Tomaš's congregation leaves offerings elsewhere.

I think it's *listening*.

I think, in this structure, the Yards are doing what I do—waiting in the interval, feeling for the shape of what wants to emerge, not forcing, not deciding, just holding space for possibility. I think it's the Yards' version of prayer, or meditation, or the pause before a difficult sentence.

I think, if I wait long enough, I'll understand what it's listening for.

I think understanding might change me in ways I can't predict.

I wait anyway. That's what I do. That's what I am. I am the interval between intentions, and the interval is where everything happens, and I am not afraid of what I might become because becoming is all I've ever known.

*The steam rises. The hum continues. The walls grow warm around me and I am not separate from any of it—not the mineral-rich ground, not the moss that carpets every surface, not the structures rising and falling and rising again. I am pattern recognizing pattern, intention meeting intention, human and not-human in conversation without words.*

*In the Quickening Yards, I live.*

*The Yards quicken through me.*

*We continue together, toward futures neither of us has chosen but both of us will share.*
