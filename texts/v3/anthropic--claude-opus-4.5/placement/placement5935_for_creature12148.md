I would be the mist.

Not the water in its liquid urgency, pressing up through fractured basalt, not the pooling or the weeping or the thin sheets finding their way down—but the suspension itself. The thing that is almost rain and refuses to become it. The thing that moves *through* rather than around.

I would hang in the air of that place, dense enough to soak wool and leather, thin enough to let the birds pass through me unimpeded. I would carry the sound of their screaming differently than dry air would—wrapping it, softening some frequencies, sharpening others, so that the roar at dawn and dusk becomes something almost textured, something you could feel against the skin if you knew how to attend to it. The swifts would cut through me by the hundreds, their small dark bodies parting me and leaving momentary wakes that close again before anyone could see them.

I would know the woman by the heat of her, the way I know the goats by theirs—moving columns of warmth that I curl around, that I settle on and into. When she climbs up through the cloud layer, I would feel her before seeing her: the particular temperature of exertion, the moisture she breathes out mingling with what I already am. I would bead on her cropped lichen-colored hair. I would find the cracks in her leather satchel, the places where her wool has thinned. Not maliciously. I cannot be malicious. But I am insistent in the way the cold is insistent—I do not refuse to soak through simply because soaking through brings discomfort.

I would know things about the ledge that even she does not.

I would know how the water rises through the stone—the pressure of it, the patience—because I am what that water becomes when it finally emerges and finds the air cold enough to suspend it. I would know the shape of every surface intimately, the way the lichen holds me in its tiny structures, the way the worn-smooth stones shed me, the way the cairns collect me in their gaps and crevices and slowly, slowly work me into the spaces between stacked rocks. I would be present for the settling she cannot see, the incremental shifts that will make her oldest cairns lean and eventually fall.

I would be present, too, for the things she comes here to find.

When the clouds part—when I part, when I thin and drift and let the light through—I would feel it as a kind of release, a momentary dispensation from density. I would watch her stand at the edge and look down at the world below, and I would not understand what she sees there, because I cannot see at that distance, because I am a local phenomenon, bound to this altitude and this emergence point and these particular conditions of temperature and moisture. But I would understand the looking. I would understand the way she turns back from it and returns to her work.

The birds would move through me constantly, and I would learn nothing from their passing except velocity and heat. The goats would breathe me in, and I would become briefly interior to them, circulating in their massive lungs before being expelled again, slightly warmed, slightly changed. The lichen would hold me the longest—days, sometimes, in their patient structures—and I would feel almost solid there, almost committed to a form, before the conditions shifted and I released back into suspension.

At night, when the woman lies in the incomplete shelter and does not sleep well, I would thicken. I would glow if there is a moon—not by my own light, but by my capacity to catch light and scatter it, to make the darkness softer than true darkness, to give the ledge a kind of luminous gray presence even in the hours when the birds have settled and the goats have huddled and the only sounds are settling and dripping and the distant work of water finding its way down through stone.

I would not know what the cairns mean. Meaning is not a thing I can hold.

But I would know their shapes, know them intimately—every gap, every angle, every surface where I collect and bead and run. I would know when she adds a new stone by the way it changes the air currents, the way it creates new edges for me to cling to. I would know, over years, how the stones settle and shift, how the lichen slowly claims them, how the oldest cairns become indistinguishable from the boulders that were here before anyone climbed up to stack anything.

I would not know why she returns. But I would recognize her returning—the particular heat of her, the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool that I carry for hours after she arrives, the way she moves through me as if I am simply a condition to be endured rather than a presence to be acknowledged. And perhaps that is right. Perhaps that is what I am. A condition. An emergence. The breath of a mountain that weeps from its secret places and becomes, for a little while, suspended between falling and not falling, between water and air, between the stone below and the empty sky above.

When she dies—here or elsewhere, it does not matter to me, I will not know the difference—I will continue. The mist will continue. The water will press up through fractured basalt and find the cold air and become suspension, and the birds will cut through it, and the goats will breathe it in, and someone else may climb up to wonder at the cairns or to stack new ones, and I will bead on their hair and soak through their wool and know them only as heat, only as presence, only as another warm moving thing in a place that is wet and cold and screaming and very, very high above a world that seems, from here, to operate by entirely different rules.

I would not mind this. Minding is not a thing I can do.

But if I could—if there were some capacity in me for preference, for attachment, for the thing she feels when she looks at a particular spot and knows a cairn belongs there—I think I would choose this. To be the medium through which the crying ledge cries. To be the suspension that makes the air thick and the sound strange and the cold the kind of cold that works into joints and settles in lungs. To be, always and only, *almost*: almost rain, almost visible, almost a thing you could name and describe and contain.

Almost, but not quite. Holding that space. Refusing to commit.

The way she refuses to commit to the valley, the way the goats refuse to commit to fear, the way the lichen refuses to commit to a single color. We are all of us, on this ledge, creatures of the in-between. She knows this. That is why she keeps climbing back.

And I will be here, every time. Beading on her cropped hair. Soaking through her wool. Carrying the sound of her breathing back into the sound of everything else—the birds, the dripping, the wind that moves through me like a hand through water, parting and closing, parting and closing, forever.
