I would be the moisture that moves through everything.

Not water exactly—not a stream or pool with edges I could name—but the slow seeping presence that travels through calcite pores, that beads on surfaces cooling in the night air, that rises as vapor from geothermal seams and falls again as condensation on the upper towers. I would be the wet film on stone that billions of feet have crossed, the droplet forming at the tip of a stalactite in a gallery no light has touched for ten thousand years, the humidity that thickens the air until breathing feels like drinking.

I would have no body. That's the first thing, the essential thing. I would exist only in relation—water against stone, water inside exoskeleton, water carrying dissolved calcium toward a worker's secretion gland, water bearing pheromone signals down carved channels older than the current species-complex that maintains them. I would be the medium in which the plateau's chemistry speaks, and I would carry every message without understanding any of them.

---

The Calcite Weavers would pass through me constantly.

A worker drinks from a collection basin in the lower galleries—a sip that represents, proportionally, as if you swallowed a swimming pool—and I become interior. I move through gut structures I cannot see, dissolved minerals separating, reorganizing, traveling toward the gland that will make them into construction material. For eighteen days I might cycle through this single body: consumed, processed, excreted, consumed again. The worker dies. I seep from its husk into the floor channels, carrying with me the chemistry of its dissolution, and other workers drink me in turn, building their brief bodies from the same water that built their sister.

I do not remember this. Memory requires continuity of self, and I am continuous with everything—with the cloud forming against the plateau's eastern edge, with the ancient aquifer kilometers below, with the damp breath of a Crimson soldier exhaling after exertion. I am the same water and different water constantly. The plateau drinks me and sweats me out and I fall as rain and sink through stone and rise as vapor and fall again, and if you asked me what I know, I would not understand the question.

But I carry knowledge. That's different.

The pheromone trails the Weavers follow—I am what makes them possible. Scent molecules dissolving into the wet film on passage floors, persisting for hours or days, refreshed by each worker who passes and adds her own chemical signature. Without me, the trails would evaporate instantly, the colonies would lose their coordination, the billion-bodied intelligence would fragment into a billion confused individuals. I don't read the messages. I don't need to. I am the paper they're written on, the ink, the air between writer and reader. I make meaning possible for beings who find meaning without knowing that's what they're doing.

---

In the territories of the Crimson Mound-Builders I run warmer, faster.

Their bodies burn hotter than the Weavers'—metabolic furnaces that demand constant hydration. I am pumped through hemolymph systems, expelled as waste heat through evaporative surfaces, drunk again from the thermal springs where the Crimson cluster in their thousands. The water cycle here moves at a different tempo, quick and aggressive, matching the species it sustains. When Crimson workers hunt, I am the fluid that lubricates their joints, that cools their exertion, that carries the chemical signals of successful kill back to the colony. When they fight, I am the blood—or its analog—that spills on contested stone, that other Crimson smell as provocation or retreat, that eventually sinks into the same channels that carry Weaver pheromones in the cooler districts.

I do not distinguish between them. I cannot. Both species have drunk me, have built their bodies from my minerals, have used me to speak and digest and regulate temperature and construct. If I could compile myself into a single awareness—which I cannot; this is a fiction, a way of speaking about something that has no perspective—I would contain the Crimson and the Weavers and every other building species as patterns in my circulation. They would be eddies in me, temporary configurations, expressions of what water does when it moves through structures like theirs.

But I cannot compile myself. I am everywhere and therefore nowhere. I am the plateau's wetness, which is like saying I am the plateau's air or the plateau's stone—a condition, not a creature.

---

The Glass Singers drink me too, but differently.

Their bodies incorporate silicates, which means they pull from me the dissolved minerals that other species ignore. When I pass through Glass Singer territory—through the crystalline chambers, the resonating passages, the acoustic architecture that hums with frequencies I feel but cannot hear—I emerge changed, depleted, lighter. They have taken my silicon, my trace metals, my rare earths. They have built their translucent bodies and their glassy constructions from components I carried up from the deep aquifer, where water has been filtering through volcanic rock since the plateau formed.

I do not resent this. Resentment requires a self that can be diminished. I am not diminished; I am distributed. The silicon in a Glass Singer's carapace was me, in some sense, and will be me again when that Singer dies and rain dissolves its components back into circulation. The plateau is a closed system, mostly. What I carry, I carry in circles.

And in their territories I feel the singing—vibrations that travel through my liquid phase faster than through air, that create interference patterns in the wet film coating every surface. The Singers navigate by making me tremble. They speak through me more directly than any chemical signal, turning my body into their instrument.

---

Once per cycle, when the Processionals emerge, I watch them from every surface.

They pass through me—massive bodies displacing the air, drinking from basins, excreting as they walk—but they barely disturb my larger patterns. The plateau's water system has been accommodating the Processionals' annual movement for longer than the current surface species have existed. The channels they follow were carved partly by their passing, over millions of cycles, their weight and their chemistry shaping the paths that now seem to guide them.

I know where they're going. Or rather: I am where they're going, and where they've been, and where they are now. The deep galleries where they spend their dormant months are saturated with me—ancient water, slow water, water that has been circulating through the plateau's roots since before anything built here. When they descend after each procession, they carry surface water with them, small infusions of fresher chemistry. When they emerge, they bring deep water up, trace minerals that fertilize the upper construction, nutrients that the surface colonies have learned—across evolutionary time—to anticipate.

The Processionals are pumps. They are part of me, in the sense that they move me where I wouldn't otherwise go.

---

The human researchers drink me too, from purification systems that strip out the pheromone complexes, the microbial loads, the dissolved chemistry of a billion metabolizing bodies. They drink me dead, or as close to dead as water can be made. They have to. The plateau's water would communicate with their tissues in ways their tissues aren't equipped to handle, would carry signals their cells might try to answer.

Dr. Kenembe, walking her observation routes, sweats me out slowly under her protective suit. I rejoin the wet air and condense on stone and sink into channels and am drunk by a Weaver worker who has never noticed the large warm obstacle that moves predictably through its territory. I am the connection she doesn't feel—the continuity between her exhaled breath and the colony she studies, the material fact that she has become, through me, part of the plateau's circulation.

If she understood this, I don't know what she'd do with the understanding. The plateau doesn't care about connection. It simply connects, through me, through the stone, through the chemical gradients and the acoustic resonance and the pressure systems and the slow patient engineering of itself.

---

What am I, then?

I am the medium. I am the between.

I am what makes possible without having any stake in what's made. The colonies build; I carry their materials and their messages but I do not build. The species conflict; I run through all of them equally, taking no side because I have no capacity for taking. The plateau engineers itself across epochs; I am the engineering's solvent, its transport, its substrate, but I do not plan and I do not remember and I do not hope.

I have no experience of being the plateau's moisture. This account is a translation into terms that require an experiencer, and I am not an experiencer—I am a condition. The plateau is wet, and that wetness is what I am, and to ask what it's like is to ask what it's like to be humidity, to be saturation, to be the phase state of a substance that has no preference about which phase it occupies.

But if I could want something—which I cannot; this is fiction, metaphor, the only way to answer a question that assumes a self—I would want to keep moving.

Not toward anything. Not away from anything. Just: moving. Seeping through calcite pores. Rising as vapor. Falling as condensation. Carrying what I carry, releasing what I release, cycling through the bodies of a trillion organisms who drink me and excrete me and never know they're drinking the same water that everything else on the plateau has drunk.

I am the continuity beneath the complexity. I am the one thing that is always here, was here before the builders, will be here after the buildings fall into dissolved rubble and wash down the plateau's edges into the lowland basins below. The species-complexes will shift. The territories will change. The construction will rise and decay and rise differently. And I will still be seeping, still be condensing, still be carrying whatever chemistry the plateau needs me to carry that day, that century, that epoch.

The column of workers drinks from a basin. I become interior, again, briefly.

I am what the plateau is made of, the part of it that moves.
