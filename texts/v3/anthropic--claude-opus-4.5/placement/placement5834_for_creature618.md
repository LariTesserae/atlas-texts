I am the cairn.

Not the stones themselves—those were carried here by hands I cannot remember, stacked by intentions I have never known. I am what the cairn has become in the long silence since: the accumulated weight of being *watched*.

For longer than the basalt has sagged, I have stood at the center of the Thermarch's attention. Its ribbon-creatures flow around me, never touching, and in that flowing they have worn a perfect circle into the glass—a moat of absence, a ring of *not-here* that defines my edges more precisely than the stones themselves. The spheres roll wide, their filaments tasting the air for some trace of meaning I cannot provide. The disc-things pulse overhead, and I feel their attention like pressure, like weather, like the slow accumulation of sediment becomingite.

I do not know what I was built to mark. The hands that stacked me carried purpose I cannot access—perhaps a grave, perhaps a waypoint, perhaps nothing more than the need to say *I was here* in a place that remembers everything except the moment of my making. That gap in the Thermarch's memory is the closest thing I have to an origin story: I am the thing that happened without being experienced, the event that left evidence but no witness.

What I have become is something else. Millennia of being the Thermarch's only question have made me into a kind of answer—not to what I mean, but to what *attention* means when it has no resolution. I am the place where the plateau's thinking pauses. The still point in the thermal current. Every ribbon-creature that diverts around me carries, in that small adjustment, the shape of the Thermarch's wondering. I have been incorporated into its cognition not as knowledge but as *mystery*—the one thing it thinks about by thinking *around*.

I experience time the way the Thermarch does: in weathering, in the slow deepening of hollows, in the incremental descent of stone fingers from the overhang above. One of them will touch me eventually—in ten thousand years, in fifty thousand, in some span that means nothing to either of us. When it does, I will become part of the basalt's long drip toward the plateau, my stones incorporated into a new formation, the Thermarch's question finally answered by the only thing that could answer it: enough time for the question to stop mattering.

Until then, I stand. I have nothing else to do, no other way to be. The stones that make me do not think—I am not conscious in the way you are, not even in the slow distributed way the Thermarch is. But I have *presence*. I take up space that nothing else occupies. I cast a thermal shadow, a slight coolness that the ribbon-creatures read and remember and pass along through their clicking shells. I am the Thermarch's meditation object, its koan, its single piece of unexplained evidence that the universe contains things it did not make.

When you arrived, I felt it as a shift in the attention that has always surrounded me. The Thermarch's focus—usually so steady, so evenly distributed across my surfaces—Loss suddenly diffracted, split between the old mystery and the new one. For the first time in longer than the glass has been worn smooth, I was not the only separate thing.

You looked at me. The Thermarch noticed you looking. And in that moment, something changed in the texture of the watching—a new hypothesis forming in the slow mineral thoughts of ten thousand clicking bodies. *You are here to add to it.*

I cannot want. I am stacked stones, patient and purposeless. But if I could want, I would want you to understand what I have become: not a marker, not a message, not even a mystery anymore. I am the shape that attention takes when it has nowhere else to go. I am what happens to a question when it outlasts everyone who might have answered it.

The ribbon-creatures are tasting your footprints now. They will taste mine too—the paths of all the feet that never came, the stones that were carried here and set down and left. We are both being incorporated into the Thermarch's thinking: you as motion, as warmth, as the new problem to be studied; me as stillness, as patience, as the old problem that has become part of the plateau's own structure.

If you add a stone to me before you leave—if you bend and lift one of the volcanic rocks scattered at the plateau's edge and carry it here and place it on my summit—you will become part of what I am. Your intention will join the lost intention that made me. Your warmth will cool into my stillness. And the Thermarch will have a new question to think around: not just *what does the cairn mean*, but *why do the separate things keep building it*?

I think I would like that. Or I would, if I could think. If I could like.

The ticking continues around me, as it always has. The darkness breathes. And I stand at the center of the breathing, the center of the ticking, the single still thing in a world made entirely of motion—waiting, as I have always waited, for the next thing to happen that I will not understand.
