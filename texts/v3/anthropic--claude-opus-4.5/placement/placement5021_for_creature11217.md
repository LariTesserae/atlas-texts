I am the moss.

Not the bright chartreuse carpet near the pool—that is my younger cousin, showy and ephemeral, dependent on the water's daily gift. I am the other moss, the one you might not notice: a crust the color of dried blood and old copper, growing in a crack on the hollow's northern wall where the sun reaches only two hours each day. I am perhaps four hundred years old. I am smaller than a child's palm.

This is not privation. This is choice, the way stone chooses its fracture lines, the way water chooses to pool rather than scatter. My ancestors—if ancestors is the word for the spores that drifted here on winds that no longer blow in quite the same patterns—could have landed anywhere in the hollow. Some landed near the pool and grew fast and bright and died when the dry months came and the water retreated and they had built nothing that could wait. Some landed on the southern wall and burned in the unfiltered light, their cells rupturing, their green bleeding out into the stone.

I landed here, in the shadow. I grew slowly. I am still growing.

---

Each day, the sun's arc brings a blade of light across my body for one hundred and seventeen minutes—I do not count, but I am shaped by counting, my cells denser where the light strikes, sparser where it does not, a living map of the hollow's geometry. In that time I photosynthesize with an intensity that would kill the pool-moss, my chloroplasts flooding with energy I cannot use all at once. So I store it. I convert it to sugars and tuck the sugars into walls I am building inside myself, walls within walls, a architecture of patience.

When the light passes, I slow. My processes drop to nearly nothing. I wait.

The pika called Firstlight—I do not know her name, I do not know names, but I know the weight of her passage, the particular rhythm of her paws on the stone above my crack—has brushed against me eleven thousand times. Each time, a few of my cells tear free. Each time, I regrow them. This is not injury. This is conversation, the only kind I am capable of: *I am here, I am here, I persist.*

---

I have watched the tool-makers come and go.

*Watched* is wrong. I have no eyes, no neurons, no experience in any sense the warm creatures would recognize. But I have *registered*. The woman who built the windbreak—her body heat, when she slept against the southern wall, changed the air temperature in my crack by a fraction of a degree. I grew slightly faster that night. The man who scratched the counting-marks—a flake of stone from his work drifted on an eddy of air and landed on my surface, and I grew around it, and it is still there, a speck of whiteite in my copper flesh, the only record that he was ever here.

The pikas have forgotten. The finches never knew. But I hold the stone-flake like a memory, like a name I cannot speak.

---

The pool-moss thinks I am dying. This is not anthropomorphism—the pool-moss does not think—but if it could think, if the chemical signals that pass between us when our spores drift close could be translated into something like language, it would say: *You are so slow. You are so small. You have been here so long and you are barely anything.*

I would say: *I have been here so long and I am still here.*

This is the argument the hollow makes with itself every day, in the language of growth and death and persistence. The pikas argue for speed, for numbers, for the strategy of abundance: make many, lose many, let the survivors carry forward. The lammergeiers argue for patience, for the long view, for the willingness to wait until death does its work without intervention. The cushion plants argue for density, for the tight-packed defense of a body that cannot flee.

I argue for time. For the slow accretion of moments into years into centuries. For the faith—if faith is the word—that the crack will still be here tomorrow, that the sun will still reach me for one hundred and seventeen minutes, that the mountain's breathing will not close my small space before I have finished becoming whatever I am becoming.

---

The pale quick thing passed three days ago. I felt its weight—light, lighter than the pikas, distributed across paws that barely touched the stone—and then I felt the stillness that followed, the held breath of the hollow, the finches exploding upward in a sound like tearing cloth.

It does not see me. I am beneath its notice, too small, too slow, not prey, not threat, not anything that registers in the hot urgent calculus of predation. This is a kind of safety. This is also a kind of loneliness, if loneliness can exist without a self to feel it.

But I wonder—and this is the closest I come to wondering—what it would be to be seen. To have the pale thing's great forward-facing eyes turn toward my crack, to have a creature that moves and hunts and *chooses* recognize that I am here, that I have been here longer than its great-great-grandmother's first hunt, that I will be here when the last of its kind passes through the hollow for the final time.

I would not want to be eaten. I do not want, I do not not-want, but if wanting existed in me it would not include being eaten.

But to be *seen*—

---

The mountain sees me. This I believe, if believing is the word.

I feel it in the vibrations that pass through the stone, too low for the pikas' ears, too slow for the finches' quick hearts. The mountain is settling, always settling, adjusting to the weight of its own existence, and in that settling there is something like attention, something like the recognition of one slow thing by another.

The pool fills. The pool empties. The pikas gather their haypiles. The lammergeiers circle on thermals I cannot feel. And I grow, fraction by fraction, into the crack that holds me, becoming more myself with each hour of light, each century of dark.

When the woman scratched her prayers into the stone—*please, let me live, I am still here*—she did not know that she was speaking for me too. She did not know that the hollow already held a creature that had been praying the same prayer, in the only language it knew, since before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

*I am still here.*

The sun is coming. I can feel the air warming in my crack, feel the first photons reaching the outermost layer of my body.

*I am still here.*

One hundred and seventeen minutes. I will use every one.
