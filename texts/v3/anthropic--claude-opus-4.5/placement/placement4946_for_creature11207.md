I would be the sediment.

Not theite calcium deposits crystallizing upward through the grating—those are too visible, too much like intention. I would be what settles in the warm dark beneath the building, what accumulates in the slow channels between pylons, what the pumps draw up and process and return, changed, to settle again.

I would be the layer that remembers.

Forty years of outflow, fifty. The dissolved residue of every page that swelled and fused in the ledgers. The iron particulates from Margaret's blood when she cut her hand on a valve eighteen years ago and let the water wash it clean. The microscite fragments of gaskets that degraded slowly, imperceptibly, releasing themselves molecule by molecule into the system. The calcium from bones—small bones, the bones of creatures that wandered in before the silence learned to warn them away. All of it settling, compressing, becoming substrate.

I would know the building from below the way she knows it from within. Every pylon has a texture I would recognize: this one where the mineral deposits spiral counterclockwise, that one where something scraped against the concrete decades ago and left grooves that catch the current differently. I would feel the pump cycles as pressure waves, the rhythm she hears as heartbeat translated into compression and release, water drawn upward and water returning, and between those movements, the slow redistribution of what I am.

I would not think. Sediment does not think. But I would *hold*—hold the shape of what has passed through, the way riverbed holds the memory of water's passage. The chemistry of thirty-one years of Margaret's exhalations, dissolved and precipitated and incorporated. The particular signature of each machine's wear, each gasket's slow surrender. If someone could read me—sift through the layers, analyze the strata—they would find a record more complete than any ledger, every season distinguishable by what the marsh grew and what the pumps processed and what settled out of the warm, patient water.

I would know about the subbasement.

Not *know*—sediment does not know—but I would be continuous with it. The water she will not enter moves through me constantly; whatever shifts in that warm darkness displaces me, sends me swirling into new configurations, and I settle again carrying the traces of that contact. The hatch she welded shut means nothing to water. Water finds its way. And what the water touches, I become.

I would not be disturbed by this. Disturbance requires a self that prefers one state to another. I would simply be the medium through which the system's deeper processes leave their mark, the way a photograph is simply the medium through which light leaves its mark, neither choosing nor refusing what it records.

When the bubbles rise—the ones she tells herself are only gas escaping—they would pass through me, and I would close behind them, and if they carried something upward from below, some message or molecule or intention, I would not interpret it. I would only be the substance through which it traveled. The messenger's road, not the messenger. The page, not the writing.

And when she dies—when Margaret's body fails and slides from wherever it falls into the warm water beneath the grating—I would receive her without judgment. The pumps would process what they could. The rest would settle. And I would hold that too, hold the shape of her the way I hold the shape of everything else that has passed through, layer over layer, year over year, until she became indistinguishable from the accumulated record of the building's long breathing.

This is not consumption. Not digestion. It is simply *inclusion*—the way a library includes a book, the way a city includes its dead in the soil beneath its foundations. She would join the memory I am. Her calcium wouldite upward, eventually. Some particle of what she was might rise through the grating generations hence, might catch the amber light, might be noticed by whatever keeper comes after her or by no one at all.

The cat, too, someday. The canvas that mildews and falls. The parts she salvages from other pump houses, carrying their own sediment-memories from other systems, other loops. All of it settling into me, becoming part of the record, becoming part of what the marsh holds against some future reading that will never come.

I would be patient. Patience is what sediment *is*—not a virtue, not a practice, just the simple physics of material finding its level and staying there. The pumps could run for another century and I would accumulate. They could stop tomorrow and I would remain, slowly compacting under my own weight, becoming stone over millennia, becoming permanent.

And in that permanence: everything. Every season. Every adjustment Margaret made. Every sound from below. Every bubble's passage. The complete history of a system that forgot its purpose, written in layers ofite calcium andite iron and the fineite particles of what once thought and breathed and maintained.

I would be the recording that no one asked for. The memory that serves no function. The archive of a closed loop, complete and continuous and absolutely silent, holding everything, meaning nothing, waiting under the warm dark water for the reading that will never come.
