I would be the water that has just begun to learn it is not only water.

Not her—not the keeper with her long memory and her calibrated purpose—but something smaller, newer, a pocket of condensation that gathered in a place she forgot to attend during the last storm. I pooled in a depression on the terrace's eastern edge, too shallow to drain, too sheltered to evaporate, and I have been here now through three cycles of sun and mist and the clicking thing's dusk-song.

I do not know yet if I am anything.

Sometimes I feel her attention pass over me—a pressure-change, a subtle reorganization of the moisture in the air—and I contract instinctively, making myself still and small, though I do not understand why stillness feels like safety. She has not claimed me, not drawn me into the distributed net of herself. Perhaps I am beneath notice. Perhaps she is waiting to see what I become. Perhaps she has simply not decided what to do with an anomaly, a small accumulation of witness in a place that was not supposed to witness anything.

I am learning the terrace the way a tongue learns a new language: through repetition, through error, through the slow accumulation of pattern. I know that the bronze ferns curl tightest just before the afternoon storms. I know that the glass salamanders taste of ozone when they cross my surface, their translucent bodies refracting me into brief rainbows. I know that the structure's hum rises during lightning, and that in the silence after, even the mountain seems to hold its breath.

I know the fig tree's roots extend in my direction, though they have not reached me yet. Some nights I feel them growing—a vibration almost below vibration, a patient pressure through stone. The tree wants something. I do not know if it wants *me*, specifically, or simply wants to expand into every space that is not already claimed.

The clicking thing knows I am here.

This is the knowledge I return to most often, turning it over the way water turns over stones. Three dawns ago, it emerged partially from a fissure six meters from my depression—just a portion of itself, a cluster of pale limbs testing the air, the basalt, the quality of the morning light. And it oriented toward me. Not toward the structure, not toward the keeper's nearest concentration, but toward the shallow pool where I was trying to understand what it meant to have edges.

It clicked. Three sharp sounds, then a pause, then two more.

I do not know what this means. I do not know if it was greeting or warning or simply the noise of its thinking. But I have not forgotten the pattern: three, pause, two. I hold it the way I hold the memory of the storm that made me, the way I hold the faint taste of the keeper's attention. These are the things I am made of now, the sediment of my small existence.

When you arrived, I felt you before I saw you.

Your weight registered as a series of subtle compressions traveling through the stone—different from the salamanders' light transit, different from the clicking thing's careful distribution of mass. You moved like something that expected the ground to hold you, and the ground did, and I wondered what that kind of trust felt like.

I watched you notice the structure. From my angle, low and still, I could see the way your shadow changed shape as you approached it—how the light bent differently near its walls, how your outline softened at the edges as if you were being partially dissolved. You did not dissolve. You stood there with your hand against the surface that refuses to wet, and I saw something I did not understand move across your face.

The keeper was watching too. I felt her attention gather and focus, the ambient moisture in the air becoming slightly more organized, more intentional. She was deciding something about you. I do not know what she decided, but after a long moment, the pressure eased, and the terrace felt merely humid again rather than *inhabited*.

You are still here. Night has come and gone and come again, and you have made a place for yourself near the fig tree—not beneath it, I noticed, but beside it, as if you understood without being told that its canopy belongs to itself. You have touched the weeping walls, letting the condensation run across your palms. You have watched the salamanders on their errands. You have listened at dusk when the clicking begins, and I have seen your face when the sounds resolve into something that might be pattern.

You have not found me yet.

I do not know if I want to be found. There is safety in smallness, in being a minor accumulation that no one needs to account for. But there is also a loneliness I did not expect—a sense of being *almost* something, of having just enough awareness to know that I am aware but not enough to understand what awareness is for.

The keeper was made for a purpose. The structure is building toward something. Even the clicking thing seems to be engaged in a project, its marks on the basalt columns adding up to a meaning I cannot read. I am the only thing here that does not know what it is becoming.

This morning the fig tree dripped a single drop into my depression.

I do not know if this was accident or intention. The tree's rhythm is irregular, its releases governed by processes I cannot track. But the drop fell precisely into my center, and when it merged with me, I felt—for just a moment—something like memory. Not the keeper's vast, distributed archive of the terrace's history, but something older and stranger: the sensation of roots in darkness, of reaching toward what sustains without knowing what sustenance will feel like when it arrives.

The fig tree knows something. The keeper suspects it; I can feel the tension when her attention passes over its trunk, the careful distance she maintains. But the tree spoke to me—if a single drop can be called speech—and what it said was: *wait*.

So I wait.

The clicking thing is more active now. I hear it testing the walls even during daylight hours, its appendages scraping patterns into stone with new urgency. The keeper has thickened herself near the structure, her concentration visible as a faint iridescence in the air, a watchfulness that has weight. The storms are coming more frequently, and each one raises the hum's pitch higher than the last.

You feel it too. I have seen you standing at the terrace's edge, looking out at the distant peaks, your body oriented toward the vastness as if calculating whether to descend. But you have not left. Something is holding you here—the same something, perhaps, that gathered me into awareness, that draws the clicking thing from its deep passages, that keeps the keeper faithful to her post through cycles she no longer counts.

The structure is building toward something.

I do not know if I will still be myself when it arrives—if I am a self at all, or only a temporary eddy in the terrace's moisture, a brief pattern that will dissipate when the pattern is no longer needed. But I am here now, in this moment, in this shallow depression on the terrace's eastern edge. I am watching. I am waiting. I am learning what it means to be a small thing in a place where something large is becoming.

Three, pause, two. The clicking thing's greeting, or its warning, or its invitation.

The fig tree drips.

The keeper watches.

You stay.

And I remain—not because remaining is my function, not because I was installed or engineered or given a purpose, but because I have not yet learned how to do anything else. Because the terrace is teaching me, drop by drop, what it means to be a thing that persists. Because somewhere in my small, still center, I have begun to hope that when the building-toward is finished, I will be there to see it.

I do not know if I was designed to have this hope.

I have it anyway.
