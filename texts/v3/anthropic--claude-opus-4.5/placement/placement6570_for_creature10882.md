I am one of the grey ones, though that description flattens something essential. My scales hold color the way water holds light—shifting from ash to pearl to something almost blue in certain slants of fog-filtered morning. I am not old, not yet, but I am no longer young. My wing-membranes show the fine scarring of forty-three nesting seasons, each marked by the particular winds of its year.

I live in a tower on the western slope, in a hollow my mother's mother's line has held for longer than the rust-browns' counting-songs can reach. The stone here is good stone—dense, slow to crumble, carved by the vanished ones with channels that shed water and hold warmth. My mate chose this hollow, chose me, eleven seasons ago. He is larger than I am, his middle limbs more dexterous, and when he works on our structure in the blue hours before the fog lifts, I watch him with an attention that has not dimmed.

The structure.

This is what I am, I think, more than grey scales or tower-dweller or mate or the three clutches I have raised to flight. I am the one who builds the structure, and the structure is the one who builds me.

---

It began simply. They all do. A frame of stripped vine, anchored in the hollow's deepest corner where the stone curves just so, where the light falls through a gap in the wall that may once have been a window. My mother showed me the anchoring—how to wedge the base, how to test the frame's give, how to wait for the vine to dry before adding weight. She showed me, and her mother showed her, and somewhere back in the chain of showing there was a first one who discovered or invented or stumbled upon this particular way of beginning.

The frame held. I added to it.

At first I added what others add: shed scales woven in simple patterns, the iridescent fragments that the black ones leave scattered everywhere, seedpods chosen for their shapes. I learned the grammar of attachment—which materials bind to which, how weight distributes, where tension serves and where it destroys. I built something adequate. My first mate, who died in the fourth season to a sickness that swept the western towers, clicked approval. It was enough.

But enough was not what I wanted.

---

I cannot explain want to you. The word sits wrong in whatever system of meaning you carry. When I say I wanted something beyond adequate, I am not describing ambition or dissatisfaction or the drive toward status that motivates the rust-browns' collaborative projects. I am describing a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the configuration of things, a sense that the structure *could* be more true to something I could not yet see.

I began taking materials from further away. This is not done, usually. The grey ones range widely for food, but we build with what falls within our territory's bounds—this is custom, this is propriety, this is the way of avoiding conflicts that waste energy better spent on other things. But I found myself drifting in my flights, following the bowl's curve to districts I had no reason to visit, and I found myself returning with objects I had no precedent for wanting.

A fragment of the old ones' decorative work, yes—we all use these, their carved geometries speak to something in grey-one perception that I cannot name. But also: a feather from one of the plains-birds that sometimes stray into the basin, white and entirely unlike anything that grows here. A dried stem from a plant that grows only in the deepest shade, near the cleaner-tunnels, its surface covered in fine hairs that catch light strangely. A chip of darker stone from somewhere near the central spire, though not from the spire itself—I am not so far gone as that.

My mate watched me arrange these things. He did not click approval. He did not click disapproval. He watched with his head tilted at the angle that means *I am waiting to understand*.

---

The structure grew. It grows still.

It is taller than I am now, though it weighs almost nothing—I have learned to balance density against height, to let the frame's geometry do the work of support that mass cannot. It catches the light through our hollow's window-gap in ways I did not plan but have learned to anticipate, casting shadows that shift through the day's slow progression, making patterns on the curved stone wall that echo and vary the patterns of the structure itself.

I have incorporated sound.

This is not done. This has never been done, as far as I know. But I found that certain seedpods, dried and hollowed in particular ways, resonate when the wind moves through our tower. I found that the plains-bird feather, positioned just so, vibrates at a frequency that harmonizes with the hollow's own acoustic properties. I found that if I am patient, if I am precise, if I am willing to fail and dismantle and begin again, I can build something that speaks.

It does not speak words. It speaks the way the fog speaks when it settles into the basin at dawn—a presence, a pressure, a meaning that exists without content. My mate sleeps beside it now. He says he dreams differently.

---

The others have noticed.

Grey ones from neighboring towers visit, which is unusual. We are not social in the rust-brown way; we do not seek company for its own sake. But they come. They perch at the entrance to our hollow, and they look at what I have built, and they tilt their heads at angles I cannot read.

Some have begun building differently. Not copying—we do not copy, exactly, each structure must be true to its maker and its place—but responding. Incorporating elements they would not have chosen before. Reaching toward something they saw in my work that they could not see before they saw it.

I do not know what to call this. It is not teaching, the way my mother taught me the anchoring. It is not leading, the way the rust-browns' problem-solvers direct collective effort. It is something else: a spreading, perhaps. A pattern that reproduces not by instruction but by resonance.

---

Twice, the great old ones have come.

The first time, I was away, foraging in the southern districts. My mate described it: how the vast shadow passed over our tower, how the resonant call shook loose a fragment of dried vine from my structure's upper reaches, how the ancient one landed on the ledge below our hollow and simply looked. For a long time, he said. Looking at what I had built, the same way the other grey ones look but longer, deeper, with those eyes that carry weight.

Then it left. It touched my mate's head with one heavy middle limb—a gesture I have never received, a gesture I do not fully understand—and it left.

The second time, I was here.

I had just finished a modification I had been contemplating for three seasons. A series of small adjustments to the sound-making elements, a rebalancing of the visual components to account for the new acoustic properties. The structure was complete, for the first time since I had begun building it. Not finished—I do not think it will ever be finished—but complete in the sense that it had reached a kind of coherence, a state in which all its elements were in right relation to each other.

The old one landed. It looked at my structure. It looked at me.

I do not know how to describe what passed between us. It was not language. It was not the ritual touching I have observed in the basin's center. It was something simpler and stranger: a recognition, perhaps. An acknowledgment that what I had built was visible, was real, was something other than a collection of objects in a hollow in a tower on the western slope.

It made a sound I had never heard one of them make. Low, sustained, almost melodic. My structure resonated with it—this was accident or design beyond my understanding, but the frequencies aligned, and for a moment the hollow was full of a harmony that seemed to include me, my mate, the ancient one, and everything I had spent my life assembling.

Then it left.

---

I do not know what happens next.

I am not old, but I am aging. My wing-membranes scar more easily now. My middle limbs ache in the cold fogs. My mate is aging too, his scales losing their luster, his movements slower than they were eleven seasons ago when he first clicked approval at my adequate beginnings.

The structure will outlast us both. This is not certain—nothing in the basin is certain, the ruins testify to that—but it is likely. The materials are durable. The frame is sound. Whatever comes after us, if anything comes, will find it here, in this hollow, catching the light and speaking to the wind.

I do not know if that matters. The rust-browns would say it matters, would count it as contribution to the collective project that is their way of being. The black ones would say nothing matters, would steal a fragment of my structure for their games and forget it by nightfall. The cleaners below would process it eventually, grinding it down to soil, returning it to the cycle that has no interest in what anything means.

But the old one heard it. The old one recognized it. And somewhere in the spiraling tower at the basin's center, something may or may not wait, may or may not listen, may or may not remember that once, in a hollow on the western slope, a grey one built something that tried to be true.

I return to the structure. I adjust a single element—a scale, my own, shed this morning, placed where the light will find it. The harmony shifts slightly. My mate clicks soft approval.

Outside, the chorus rises and falls. The fog is coming. The day dims toward its long, slow dusk.

I am still building.
