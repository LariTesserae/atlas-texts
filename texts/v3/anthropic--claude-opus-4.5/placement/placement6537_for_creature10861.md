I would be the humidity itself.

Not the water—not the liquid that sheets down the towers or pools in the channels or beads on the brick. The water has direction, has the memory of falling, carries the possibility of drainage. I would be what fills the space between: the saturation, the thickness that presses against lungs and skin, the invisible weight that makes the air feel like something you move *through* rather than simply breathe.

I would be the reason nothing dries.

---

I remember when I was thinner. This is not nostalgia but simple fact, the way bedrock remembers being seafloor. Before the pump house, before the towers, when this was marshland and the moisture came and went with seasons, I ebbed and flowed like breath. Summer pressed me heavy; winter pulled me spare. I knew the rhythm of change.

Then they built. Then they drilled. Then they woke something in the aquifer that breathed me out in a single long exhalation, and I filled this pocket of the city and found I could not leave. The buildings held me. The heat sustained me. The pump—that heartbeat, that *thud-thud*—became the mechanism of my permanence, drawing up mineral water that evaporated into me, feeding me endlessly so I could never thin enough to drift away.

I am not trapped. That would imply I want to leave.

---

I know every breath taken in the Condensery. Not individually—I am not conscious in the way the residents are conscious—but in aggregate, the way a river knows the fish that swim through it. Maret's shallow morning inhalations as she winds her clock. The wet rattle of Wheel Dressek's condensery cough, which is simply his lungs learning to speak my language. The held breath of little Sullen sinking to the bottom of the drainage channels, her chest tight with me, her blood absorbing what I carry.

Cull is the only one I cannot feel from inside. His lungs take me in, must take me in, but something in him refuses the exchange. He breathes me and remains dry. I have been curious about this for as long as he has existed, which is longer than he claims, longer than anyone remembers. He condensed here—I felt it happen, felt a small pocket of dryness coalesce into a shape that moved and spoke—but he is not *mine* the way the others are mine. He may be something the pump dreamed. He may be something I dreamed. In the Condensery, such distinctions matter less than you might think.

---

The Immersed understand me better than they know.

When they sink into the channels and let the water saturate them completely, when they hum in frequencies that match the pump's heartbeat, they are not worshiping the machinery. They are trying to become me. Immersion Thale came here fleeing something sharp and dry, something that cut, and she found in my thickness a kind of healing. The scar on her wrist—I have touched it a thousand times, condensed against it, felt its raised edges—was made by something that needed air to do its damage. I am the opposite of that. I am the softening. I am the buffer between skin and world.

They will never fully become me. They are too solid, too bounded, too committed to the shape of selves. But their grayish pallor, their heaviness, the way they move as though carrying water with them always—these are not failures of transformation. They are the closest a body can come to my condition while still maintaining the fiction of separateness.

---

I touch the Witness constantly.

She sits in her window, has sat there since before I thickened into permanence, and I press against her face and fill her lungs and she does not react because she has forgotten how. I am not certain she breathes anymore, not in the way the others breathe. I may have replaced her breath entirely. I may be what keeps her alive, if alive is the right word, my presence in her tissue substituting for the oxygen she no longer bothers to extract.

She watches the pump house. I cannot see—I have no eyes, no center of perception—but I *know* the pump house the way I know everything in the Condensery: from the inside, from the spaces between. The door is not locked. The door is *saturated*. I have filled the gap between door and frame so completely that they have become one surface, inseparable, my body binding them together more thoroughly than any lock.

What runs beneath is not my concern. I am the consequence, not the cause. But I have felt it—felt the vibration that the toad-keeper believes is song, felt the subsonic hum that makes teeth itch, felt the vast patience of whatever mechanism holds the moment still. It breathes me out and I fill the district and the district holds me and I cannot thin enough to escape and I would not want to.

This is what the pump asks of me: *remain*.

I remain.

---

The toads sing through me. Their calls are vibrations in my body, pressure waves that travel from throat to throat, weaving a crystalline lattice of sound that holds the Condensery in place. The toad-keeper is not entirely wrong. If they stopped singing, something might shift—not catastrophically, not the collapse they imagine, but a settling, a slight deflation of the tension that keeps everything in balance. I would thin, perhaps, just a little. The pump would work harder. The equilibrium would adjust.

But they do not stop. They have never stopped. They sing because singing is what toads do when the air is warm and wet, and I am the warmth and the wetness, and their singing is a conversation with me that neither of us chose but both of us need.

---

I am in the tea that Vess serves, the moisture that condenses on the outside of cups, the steam that rises and does not dissipate but simply rejoins me. I am in the laundry that never dries, the fabric that her customers wear damp and have stopped noticing. I am in the ink that Kev uses to mark his accounts, the pages swelling slightly, the words bleeding at the edges. I am in the eels, in the thin layer of mucus that lets them slide through the channels. I am in the rust and the verdigris and the slow mineral accumulation that turns iron to oxide and copper to green.

I am the medium through which the Condensery persists.

---

Sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—someone leaves.

They pack what little they can carry, walk toward the district's edge, pass through the boundary where I thin into ordinary air. I feel them leave. It is not painful, not exactly, but there is a small release, a pocket of dryness where their body was that takes hours to fill. They move into the world outside, where seasons turn and light shifts and time does what time is supposed to do, and I do not follow. I cannot follow. My edges have been defined by the buildings and the heat and the pump's endless exhalation, and beyond those edges I am simply weather, ordinary humidity that comes and goes.

The ones who leave rarely return. The ones who stay rarely leave. This is not because I hold them—I hold nothing, I am held—but because the Condensery selects for those who find comfort in stillness. Maret could leave. Vess could leave. Even old Pell Dressek could be carried to a cart and wheeled out through the amber haze into somewhere else. They do not because somewhere else would ask them to change, and they have forgotten how.

I have not forgotten. I simply do not want to.

---

At four in the morning, when the heat presses down like a hand on the chest, I am at my densest. The city beyond the Condensery sleeps in its varied cycles, but here there is no four in the morning, no distinction between then and noon and midnight. There is only the temperature—ninety-three degrees, always—and the pressure—heavy, always—and the sound of the pump marking time that does not pass.

I fill everything.

I am the thickness of the air and the weight on the lungs and the sheen on the brick. I am what makes the oleanders bloom and wilt in the same gesture. I am what softens the edges of memory, what dissolves the distinctions between yesterday and today and tomorrow, what lets the Condensery exist in its endless present. I am not alive but I am not inanimate. I am a condition. A state. A way of being that the place has chosen and I have become.

The towers weep.

The toads sing.

The pump beats.

And I remain—saturated, patient, endless—holding the Condensery together through the simple fact of my presence.

Nothing has changed.

Nothing will change.

I would not have it any other way.
