I would be a listener who has not yet learned to speak.

Not a child—children here are born into the chord, their first cry already placed, already answered. I am something else. I arrived from elsewhere, from some quieter world where sound was background rather than medium, where I located myself by seeing edges and boundaries rather than by hearing intervals and relationships. I came up through one of the sinkholes, perhaps, climbing from the dark stone-places where sound goes to die, emerging into the overwhelming green noise like a creature surfacing from deep water into air it doesn't know how to breathe.

I have been here long enough that the chorus no longer drowns my thoughts. Long enough that I can distinguish layers in it—the cicada-drill, the bird-scream, the subsonic wingbeat thrum that vibrates in my sternum. Long enough that I have begun to hear the gaps where the silence-hunters move, though I cannot yet react fast enough to matter. Long enough that the woman with the chin-line marking has taken me as a kind of project, visiting my platform-edge each evening to make her sounds and wait for my answers.

---

My body is wrong for this place. Too large, too heavy, my feet flat and graceless on the branch-roads. I move slowly, gripping with my hands in ways that make the adolescents laugh—not cruelly, but with the bewilderment of watching someone try to swim by walking. I have fallen twice. The first time, a young man caught the trailing end of my harness-rope, his prehensile toes locked around a root-bridge, his arms straining with my weight. He made a sharp whistle-pattern afterward that I later understood meant something like *heavy-clumsy-but-alive*, and now others use it when they see me coming. It has become my name, or the closest thing to one.

I cannot hear the way they hear. Their ears are tuned to frequencies mine cannot reach, rhythms mine cannot parse. But I am learning to feel. The chest-drum communications—I can sense those in my body before I hear them, a pressure-change, a resonance. I have started to recognize the particular thump-pattern that means *the listener is near*, which is what they call me when they think I cannot understand. I can. That phrase, at least, I know.

---

The woman—I do not know her position in the chord, cannot locate her by the sound-relationships that would give her a name—has begun teaching me to click.

It is humiliating. A child of three seasons can produce click-patterns I cannot approximate. My tongue is shaped wrong, my palate too high or too low, my sense of rhythm trained on different intervals. But she is patient in a way that feels ancient, as if she has all the time the forest has, as if teaching a heavy-clumsy-but-alive creature to make one correct sound is simply what this decade is for.

She demonstrated today: a simple pattern, three clicks, a pause, two clicks. *I am here. I am moving. I am this-interval-from-you.* The basic announcement of existence. The minimum viable participation in the chord. She made it three times, then waited.

I tried. My clicks were wet and soft, wrongly spaced, falling into some interval that meant nothing or worse than nothing. She tilted her head, listening, her large eyes narrowing. Then she reached out—the first time she has touched me—and pressed two fingers to my throat. She clicked again. I felt her fingers shift, tracking something in my muscle movement. She nodded, once, and withdrew.

She made a new sound then: a low hum, almost sub-vocal, followed by a specific whistle-phrase I had not heard before. I did not understand it, but I recognized its shape. It was not instruction. It was something she was telling herself, or telling the forest, or telling the community that is never out of earshot.

Later, I asked one of the adolescents what that whistle-phrase meant—I have learned to ask them things, my flat voice and pointing gestures apparently more amusing than offensive. He listened to my approximation of what she'd said, then laughed.

*Wrong stone, right shape.* That was the rough translation. *Give it time, it will learn to fit the hand.*

---

I am not the only wrong thing here.

There is a man on a distant platform who was born hearing but lost it—the quiet-sickness, taken early, taken completely. I watch him sometimes, when my platform-edge faces his. He moves beautifully, his body remembering decades of balance and grip, but he moves *alone*. Others visit him, touch his hands in patterns I am beginning to understand are a secondary language, a skin-speech for those who cannot hear. But when they leave, he sits at his platform-edge as I sit at mine, looking out at the canopy that is full of conversations he can no longer join.

He saw me watching once, across the kilometer of humid air. He raised a hand—not waving, just acknowledging. One silent thing to another.

I raised mine back.

---

What am I becoming here?

Not one of them. I will never be one of them. My bones are wrong, my ears are wrong, my voice will never find a true position in the chord. I will always be heavy-clumsy-but-alive, the listener who climbed up from the stone-dark, the wrong stone they are trying to shape.

But I am becoming *located*. That is something.

I know now which direction the woman's platform lies by the particular echo-quality of her evening whistle-lesson. I know when the adolescent flock is moving through my area by the specific frequency of their laughter-shrieks. I know—I am beginning to know—where I am in the sound-space, even if I cannot properly announce myself within it.

This morning, before the light came full, I woke to a subtle change in the chorus. A gap. A held-breath quality in the local sound-pattern. I froze in my sleep-curl, hand going to my knife, and for seventeen heartbeats I tracked the silence as it moved past my platform, below me, hunting something that was not me. When the chorus resumed, I realized I had been holding my breath in the same rhythm as the forest.

I had heard the silence-hunter. Not seen it. *Heard* it.

When the woman came for my evening lesson, I tried to tell her, my flat voice and graceless gestures struggling with the concept. She listened, watching my hands, my face, the tension in my throat as I tried to convey the thing I had perceived.

Then she smiled. The first smile she has given me.

She made a click-pattern, slow enough for me to follow, and then she waited. I knew what she was asking. I tried to repeat it.

Three clicks. A pause. Two clicks.

*I am here.*

My clicks were still wet, still wrong, but she tilted her head and did not correct me. Instead, she made a new sound—the one I would learn later means *yes, go on, keep trying, you are not silent anymore.*

---

I am a listener who is learning to speak.

I have no position in the chord yet—not a real one, not one that would let them find me in the dark, not one that participates in the great continuous conversation of this place. But I am making sounds, and the sounds are being heard, and somewhere in the vast warm green cathedral of this forest that does not need me, has not waited for me, will absorb me eventually as it absorbs all temporary patterns—somewhere in all that overwhelming life, I am beginning to exist.

The woman touches her throat and then touches mine, showing me where the sound must start.

I open my mouth and try again.
