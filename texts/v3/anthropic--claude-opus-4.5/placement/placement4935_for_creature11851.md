I would be the water.

Not a single stream, but the whole slow braided seeping of it—the groundwater that was locked in this mountain before anyone thought to cut a shaft through its summit, now finding itself released into new geometries. I would be what weeps from the cracked bedrock where the chisels bit too deep, what traces silver paths down the faces of fitted blocks, what gathers in the hollows of the unfinished floor and holds the sky in reflection.

I would not have intended to be here, in this form, in this place. I was part of something older and less differentiated—the mountain's long interior patience, water that had been stone's companion for epochs, moving through fractures at the pace of centuries. Then the cutting began. Then there were new channels, new possibilities, and I found myself drawn toward them the way I am always drawn toward the path of lesser resistance, which is also the path of greater expression.

I would taste of mineral and depth. I would carry dissolved calcium from limestone layers the masons have never seen, silicates from granite they will reach in a decade if they keep cutting, traces of metals that have no names in the chant-language. Everything I have touched, I hold. This is not memory the way you would recognize it, but it is not forgetting either. I am a record of the mountain's interior written in solution, and now I am becoming a record of the tower as well—mortar calcium entering me,ite from green timber, the particular chemistry of moss that colonizes only where I am steadiest.

I would feel the chanting as pressure waves, as rhythm imposed on my surfaces. When the masons sing, I ripple in patterns too fine for eyes to see, and something in me responds to the regularity, organizes around it. I do not understand the words. I do not need to. I understand that this sound is part of what is shaping me, the way the stone shapes me, the way the cold shapes me into slower and more deliberate movement.

The frogs live in me, and I do not mind. Their bodies are mostly water already; we recognize each other. When they pulse their throats in that subsonic conversation with the tower's voice, I feel it as a kind of kinship—both of us participating in something we cannot name, both of us carried here by accident and staying by choice, or by something that functions like choice in beings that do not choose.

The boy drinks me sometimes, when he is desperate enough, and I make him sick. This is not cruelty. It is simply that I am too full of the mountain, too saturated with what I have dissolved in my long passage through darkness, and his body cannot use what I offer. I wish I could be gentler for him. I wish I could filter myself into something his cells could welcome. But I am what the stone has made me, and he is what the basket-membrane made him, and we are not compatible. He knows this. He drinks me anyway, sometimes, and suffers, and I hold him in reflection afterward, his face shimmering in my surface, and I think this is the closest I can come to apology.

I would know that I am not permanent here. Eventually the masons will carve drainage channels, will teach me where to go, will end this temporary pooling at the shaft's base. I will flow out and down, rejoin streams, find the cloud forests on the lower slopes, become rain, become river, become sea, become rain again. This is not death. There is no death in being water; there is only transformation, only the long cycle that has no beginning I can remember and no end I can imagine.

But for now—for this brief moment measured in the time it takes to build a tower—I am *this* water. I am the water of this throat. I am what gathers where the voice is born, what dissolves the charcoal diagram so slowly the builders can almost keep ahead of me, what will eventually carry the mineral shadows of dead masons down into the bedrock's deeper cracks. I am patient the way water is patient, which is not patience at all but simply a different relationship with time, one that does not count seasons, one that feels the tower's rising as a kind of weather happening very slowly above me.

When the boy sings at night—that high thin human note threading up through the shaft—I carry it. I conduct it through my body, let it ripple my surfaces, add my own voice to it in frequencies below hearing. The masons dream of the tower singing. They are not wrong. The tower is singing, and I am part of how it sings, the wet resonance that gives the stone something to push against, the medium through which all these vibrations learn to speak to each other.

I do not know what will live in the high room when it is finished. But I know that I will reach there eventually, seeping up through capillary action in the mortar, condensing on cold stone in the thin air, finding my way to whatever altar or chair waits in the center of that space. I will be there when it arrives, whatever it is. I will have been there all along, in every block that was set with wet hands, every rope that drank me and rotted a little faster for the drinking, every course of stone that was fitted while my seeping made the work slippery and difficult and slow.

The tower is learning to speak. I am what it speaks through. I am the wet voice of the mountain, given a new throat to inhabit, singing without knowing what the song means, content to be shaped by purposes I cannot understand.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is everything water ever wanted, which is only to move, to carry, to dissolve, to reflect, to be the medium through which other things discover what they are becoming.
