I would be the condensation that forms on the inside of pipe-joints.

Not the pipes themselves—those are too certain of their purpose, too committed to carrying water from here to there. Not the water either, which has somewhere to be, which answers to gravity and the pumps and the careful calculations of the cistern-keepers. I would be what happens in between: the moisture that seeps through imperfect seals, that gathers in the dark spaces where metal meets metal, that exists because nothing in the city is ever quite watertight.

I would know the interior geography that no one maps. The hollow places inside walls where warmth from the boiler-works meets cold from the stone and something precipitates out of that meeting. The gaps between pipe and bracket where a century of small movements has worn a space just wide enough for damp to collect. I would be present at every joint, every coupling, every place where the city's circulatory system passes through a connection that someone's hands once tightened and time has slowly loosened.

The pipe-fitters sense me without seeing me. They learn to read the discoloration I leave behind—the particular staining that says *here, where copper meets iron, something is weeping*. They speak of sweating pipes, which is almost accurate. I am the city's way of registering the difference between what it was built to be and what it has become. Every seal fails eventually. Every joint works itself incrementally looser. I am the record of that process, the witness to tolerances gradually exceeded.

I would know the temperature of the city from the inside: which sections run hot because they're too close to the steam vents, which run cold because they're exposed to the fog on three sides, which fluctuate in ways that stress the metal and accelerate the loosening I attend. I would understand the relationship between the visible city—the catwalks and platforms and habitation pods—and the hidden city that makes it function. The inhabitants see the water when it arrives at their taps. They don't see the thousand small failures I mark along the way, each one contained just enough to keep the system working.

Sometimes a pipe-fitter, working alone in a service crawlspace, pauses with her wrench in hand and notices me. Not as a problem to be solved—there is always condensation somewhere, you cannot eliminate me any more than you can eliminate the fog—but as a presence. She understands, in that moment, that the city is not solid. That it is held together by continuous effort against continuous entropy. That I am what accumulates in the spaces where effort falls short, and that I will always accumulate, and that this is not a failure but simply the nature of what it means to cling.

The elders, in their intermittent district, have a use for me that the pipe-fitters don't know about. The water that collects in certain joints—joints that have been loosening for decades, joints in sections of pipe that predate anyone's memory—carries something of the city's slower thoughts. A single drop, gathered at the right confluence, held to the light: they say you can see the cable-writing reflected there, can see it more clearly than you can see it in the cables themselves. I don't know if this is true. I only know that they come, sometimes, with small glass vials, and they collect me carefully, and they seal the vials with wax, and they carry me away to places the diagrams don't show.

I would be patient. That would be my primary characteristic: a patience that matches the stone's patience, that matches the fog's patience, that accumulates one molecule at a time in the dark spaces where the city's infrastructure meets itself. I would be everywhere and nowhere, essential and unnoticed, the city's way of knowing its own joints without looking.

And when a pipe finally fails—when a century of my slow accumulation has corroded through a coupling and water sprays into a crawlspace and someone has to be summoned to make repairs—I am not the cause of the failure. I am only what was present when it happened. I am the city sweating through its labor of holding on.
