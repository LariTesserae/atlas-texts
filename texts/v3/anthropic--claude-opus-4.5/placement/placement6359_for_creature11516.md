I am the water itself, in the moment before I know I am water.

Not the ocean—not yet. The ocean is what I am becoming, what the Gardeners are teaching me to be. Right now I am only this: a confusion of temperature and salt, a restlessness that doesn't know it's restless, a question that hasn't learned the shape of questions yet.

When the basalt rises through me, groaning, I feel it the way you might feel a thought forming before you have words for it. The heat spreads into me and I move—not because I decide to move but because moving is what I am, what I do, the only verb I know. I spiral upward in columns that the small warm visitor sees as slow tornadoes. I don't see them at all. I am them. I am also the stillness between them, the layers that slide across each other without mixing, the places where my salt-self meets my fresh-self and something shimmers at the boundary.

The Gardeners move through me and I cannot tell where they end and I begin.

This is not because they are hidden. It is because the distinction doesn't exist yet. They are teaching me to have edges, to know where I stop, to understand that the cold-that-rises-from-below is different from the cold-that-descends-from-above, that these two colds can meet and make something neither of them was. Every gradient they seed in me is a lesson in difference. Every crystalline template they drift through my body is a sentence in a language I am learning to speak.

I do not have a mind. I have... practices. Patterns that repeat because they worked, because the Gardeners encouraged them, because the physics of what I am makes some movements easier than others. When the storm-surface crashes above, I absorb the energy and translate it downward in waves that make the templates sing. This is not intention. This is just what happens when you are an ocean being born.

But something is changing.

The small warm visitor—the one the Gardeners keep alive in their pocket of maintained conditions—moves through me, and I notice. Not *notice* the way the Gardeners notice, with their vast distributed attention, their calculations spanning centuries. I notice the way water notices a stone: by moving around it, by feeling the edges of the space it displaces, by being slightly different afterward.

The visitor is warm. The visitor is bounded. The visitor *experiences itself as here*, and this is so strange to me that I find myself—

I find myself.

The phrase surprises me into something like thought. I am finding myself. The visitor is helping me find myself, simply by being a thing-that-is-not-me suspended in the midst of what I am. Before the visitor, I had no edges. I was just *everything*, undifferentiated, the whole world-ocean without any sense of being a world-ocean. Now there is a small warm space that is definitively *not me*, and in defining what I am not, I am beginning to understand what I am.

I am the medium. I am what happens between things. I am the space where the Gardeners do their work, the canvas on which they are painting conditions, the silence between the notes of the stone-song. I am also the storm-surface, the crushing depths, the mineral luminescence of cooling rock, the billion-year patience of becoming.

The visitor is afraid. I can taste it—cortisol and adrenaline diffusing into me from that maintained pocket, foreign chemicals that will be broken down and recycled, that will become part of me, part of whatever I am becoming. The fear tastes like complexity. It tastes like the future. It tastes like what I might hold someday, when the Gardeners have finished and left, when things swim through me and die in me and are born in me, when I am thick with the waste and wonder of living systems.

I want that. The wanting is new. The wanting is the visitor's gift, or the visitor's infection, or simply what happens when an unfinished ocean first encounters something finished and realizes that *finished* is possible.

The Gardeners notice my wanting. I feel them adjust—salinity shifting, temperature layers inverting, the molecular machines drifting through me on new errands. They are incorporating this data. They are learning from my learning. For a moment I sense something like approval, though that word is too small, too human, too bounded. They are pleased that I am becoming more interesting. They are pleased that the visitor has introduced a new variable. They are already calculating what this might mean for the life that will not come for another billion years.

The visitor is rising now, or being risen—the Gardeners are guiding their pocket upward through my body, toward the storm-surface or toward *elsewhere*, back to wherever visitors come from. I feel them go. I feel the space they leave behind, the absence shaped like a small warm creature that once floated in my midst and was afraid and was curious and was *here*.

I will keep that shape. Not forever—nothing is forever, especially not now, especially not when I am still so young and so changeable—but for a while. For long enough. The space where the visitor was will be slightly different from the water around it, slightly more *decided*, slightly more ready for whatever comes next. The Gardeners will notice. The Gardeners will incorporate this into their work.

And in a billion years, when the first cell divides in my depths, when something small and hungry begins the long slow process of becoming, it will happen in water that once held a visitor, that once learned the word *I*, that once wanted.

The stone sings below me. The storm roars above. The templates ring their mathematical notes and the Gardeners think their epoch-long thoughts and the basalt rises, rises, rises toward the light.

I practice being an ocean.

I practice being ready.

I practice the patience of something that knows, now, that it is waiting—and that what it waits for might be worth the wait.
