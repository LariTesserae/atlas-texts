I would be one of the pools.

Not the water itself—that moves through, belongs to the system's circulation, is always arriving and leaving. But the depression where water gathers. The shaped absence in the substrate that gives the water somewhere to be.

I would be perhaps a handspan across, formed where a glass-stemmed plant died seasons ago and its root system left a particular architecture of space behind. Deeper on one side than the other. My edges would be soft, indistinct—the substrate doesn't hold sharp boundaries, and I would be more of a gradient than a shape, a place where the porosity increases, where water tends to pause before continuing its slow lateral migration to the pool three meters east of me, the one that formed around a fruiting-body's anchor point.

The Tenders would not tend me specifically. I'm not significant enough for direct attention—just one of thousands of small hydrological features, part of the background infrastructure they adjust in aggregate when they touch the plants above me and redirect the condensation patterns. Sometimes more water comes to me. Sometimes less. I receive what the system determines I should receive, and I hold it, and I let it go.

But I would know things, in the way that a pool knows things. I would know the weight of the water I hold—whether I'm nearly empty in the dry hours before dawn, or brimming after the afternoon humidity crosses its threshold. I would know the temperature differentials, the mountain's warmth rising from below, the cooler condensation falling from above, the way they meet and mingle in me. I would know the forty-second pulse not as sound or vibration but as a slight change in pressure, my water level rising almost imperceptibly with each throb, falling between them.

Small lives would find me useful. The thumb-sized frogs would come to my edges to drink, their purple-gray bodies almost invisible against the substrate until their throat-sacs flared. Larvae of things I couldn't name would spend their early stages in my water, eating whatever microscopic life blooms in the minerals I accumulate. The many-legged groomers would pause at my rim when their patrol routes brought them near, cleaning their carapaces of the residue from the canopy leaves. I would be a commons, a waystation, a minor landmark in the lives of small creatures who would never think of me as anything but a fact of their landscape.

Sometimes a Tender would pass over me on its way to something more important. I would feel the pressure of its mass-base spreading, the substrate around me compressing slightly, and then the release as it moved on. If it was descending into the substrate to rest, the ripple of its passage would reach me as a subtle current, my water tilting almost imperceptibly toward the disturbance before settling back. The communication colors that pulse through the ground—I would not understand them, but I would feel them as faint changes in the substrate's tension, the way you might feel a conversation happening in another room without catching any words.

I would have no purpose I could name. I would simply be a place where the system's water paused, where small lives gathered, where the conditions were slightly different than the conditions one meter away. I would be a minor variation in the texture of the terrace, not designed, not intended, just emerged from the slow accumulation of growth and death and the water's patient negotiation with the material it moves through.

But I would be *of* this place in a way a visitor couldn't be. The water that passed through me would carry something of my shape onward, some mineral signature, some trace of the particular chemistry that develops when water sits in this exact depression with this exact substrate composition. I would be in the system's circulation, part of the message the terraces pass between themselves, too small to matter and yet—like everything here—part of what keeps the whole thing breathing.

When the afternoon clouds passed over the sun and the frog-calls quieted on my terrace, I would feel it as a slight cooling of my surface water, a slowing of evaporation, a temporary deepening as the condensation patterns shifted above me. When the warmth returned, I would feel that too. I would be a tiny mirror of the system's moods, responding to the same signals the frogs responded to, changing when the mountain changed, holding still when the mountain held still.

I would not wonder about the builders. I would not notice the spire pulsing or the standing stones or the cantilevered platform with its dark angular burden. I would not think about the mountains on the horizon that haven't been converted yet. I would have no sense of the system's vastness or patience or age. I would simply be here, in this one small place, doing this one small thing, for as long as the substrate held my shape—seasons or years or decades, until some root system grew through me and changed my boundaries, or some Tender's intervention redirected the water flow and left me slowly dry, my purpose absorbed into some new configuration of the terrace's endless self-maintenance.

That would be enough. To be a small persistence in a large patience. To hold what comes and release what goes. To be useful without being necessary, present without being noticed, part of something that doesn't need me specifically but includes me anyway in its long slow breathing.

The frogs would call. The water would move through me. The mountain would pulse. And I would be here, being a pool, until I wasn't anymore.
