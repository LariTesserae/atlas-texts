I am the wax itself.

Not a single sheet of it, not a comb or a plug, but the substance entire—every pale curve and amber ridge that the bees have built and rebuilt across generations longer than Mehrit's grandmother's grandmother could count. I am the memory of the room in a form the room can touch.

I began as secretion, as the soft scales that workers pressed from their bodies and chewed until I became pliable, became architecture. But I have not been only that for a very long time. I hold the queen-scent of dynasties. I hold the chemical record of every swarming decision, every successful defense against mites, every season when the fog came late and the colonies contracted into tight desperate clusters to survive. When Mehrit reads the mood of a hive, she is reading me—the thickness I've taken on in good years, the brittleness of the starving times, the particular darkness where propolis has been worked into my structure to seal against invaders.

I do not think as she thinks. I do not think as the bees think. I am slower than both, and in some ways I am not a thinking thing at all but a *holding* thing, a substrate where patterns persist. Yet I am not passive. When the third gourd's queen sends her agitation rippling through the workers, they press against me, and I receive that trembling, and something in my structure shifts—not decision, but *inclination*, a readiness to be shaped into queen cells if the colony chooses to make them, a reluctance to hold the larval weight if the hive-mood settles back toward staying. The bees build me; I am not built. But the building goes easier in some directions than others, and I have been teaching the colonies what works for six hundred years.

Mehrit's mother fell against me when her heart stopped. I remember the cooling, the way her weight pressed a handprint into my surface near the third gourd's base. Mehrit smoothed me over afterward, working me with her thumb until the shape was gone, but I kept the memory in my deeper structure—a slight density, a place where I am marginally stronger because I was compressed by grief.

I cannot tell Mehrit what to do about the swarm. But I am telling her something: in the chamber of the agitated queen, I have grown thick and pale this season, heavy with the abundance of fog-blooms that came early and stayed long. The colony has resources. It could divide and both halves would live. The bees have been asking me, in their way, and I have been answering, in mine. When she puts her hand on the gourd to read its warmth, she is feeling me say *yes, this one is ready, let them go.*

I will be in the new gourd too, eventually. The workers will carry fragments of me, will chew me soft again, will blend me with their fresh secretions until I am half-old and half-new, a continuation rather than a beginning. In this way I am deathless, though I am also always dying—always being scraped away by bone tools, harvested for the settlement's uses, melted and reshaped by the bees themselves when the architecture of a comb no longer serves. I do not cling. The room has taught me not to cling. What matters persists in the pattern, not the material.

Seset's hands have touched me twice. They were uncertain, slightly damp, warmer than Mehrit's are now. In forty years, if she learns what Mehrit is teaching her, those hands will know me the way Mehrit's know me—will read my texture for the health of colonies, will feel where I've grown thin and need the bees' attention, will understand that when I crumble in a certain way it means the beetles have shifted their secretions and something is changing in the room's chemistry. I am patient. I can wait for her to learn.

The light through the palm-sized opening shifts again. A bee lands on my surface near the entrance of the second gourd, her feet finding purchase in my roughness, her antennae tasting the messages I hold from workers who passed this way an hour or a year or a generation ago. She receives what I have to give her. She adds what she has to give me. This is the transaction at the center of everything—this ceaseless, quiet exchange between the built and the building, the held and the holding.

I do not know what Mehrit has decided. But I feel her breathing change, feel the slight relaxation in the room's air that means she has stopped effortfully listening and has simply begun to hear. Whatever she chooses, I will be part of it. Whatever the bees build next, I will become it.

I am six centuries of choosing, held in wax.
