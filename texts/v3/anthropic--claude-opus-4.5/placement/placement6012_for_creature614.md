I would be a bridge.

Not one of the main thoroughfares—those proud spans that carry the endless foot traffic between the great platform-clusters, the ones that have names and histories and the wear-patterns of ten thousand crossings pressed into their weave. No. I would be one of the smaller bridges, the ones that people forget to think about even as they use them. A connector between a family platform and a cistern-plant, perhaps. Or the short, angled span that lets the eel-tenders reach their watching-place at the water's edge without climbing down through the root-tangle.

Twelve feet long, maybe fifteen. Wide enough for one person to cross comfortably or two to pass if they turn their shoulders and press close. My planks would be split bamboo, worn smooth in the center where feet fall, still showing the rough grain at my edges where hands sometimes catch themselves. My ropes would be—I would *be* the ropes, I think, more than the planks. The four main lines that hold the shape, anchored to posts that have been part of their respective platforms so long that the wood has grown around the binding. The cross-weavings that keep the planks in place. The rails, chest-high, that people touch without thinking as they cross.

I would know the feeling of being tightened.

Every few months, when the wet has done its slow work and my fibers have stretched, when my sway has become a little too wide and the old ones click their tongues as they cross me—then the bridge-weavers would come. A woman with strong hands, usually, and an apprentice watching. She would test my ropes one by one, feeling for the soft spots, the places where rot has begun its patient entry. She would cut out what cannot be saved and splice in new lengths, her fingers working patterns she learned before she could speak. The apprentice would ask questions and receive answers that are mostly grunts and gestures: *here, feel this, this is what wrong feels like, this is what right feels like.*

And then the tightening. The whole process of drawing me taut again, taking up the slack that time has given me, until I sing a little when the wind catches me right. That singing—I would know it as satisfaction, I think. The feeling of being *enough* again. Of being trusted to hold.

---

I would learn the rhythms of my particular place without ever deciding to learn them.

The old woman who crosses me every dawn to check the cistern-plants—I would know her tread before she set foot on me. The slight hesitation at my near edge, the way she tests me each time even though I have never failed her, even though her grandmother crossed me when *she* was old. The pressure of her broad feet is lighter than it was five years ago; I am learning the weight of her slowly leaving, though neither of us would name it that way.

The children who use me as a shortcut when they're racing, taking me at a run in ways that make my ropes complain—I would hold for them, always, but I would sway in a particular way that tells them *not so fast, not so careless.* Some of them listen. Some of them learn the specific rhythm of running across me that keeps us both happy, and some of them don't, and one day one of the ones who don't will fall, and I will be examined for fault, and none will be found, because the fault is not in me. But I will feel it anyway. The absence of that careless tread.

The lovers who meet at my center in the dark hours, when they think no one is awake—I would hold very still for them. I would try not to sway with their shifting weight, their small movements, their eventual reluctant parting to return to their separate platforms. This is not my purpose, but it is one of my uses, and I would take a kind of quiet pride in it. In being the place where that happens. In being trusted with it.

---

The eels, when they surface at the gap my eel-tenders watch, do not know I am here. Or perhaps they know and do not care. I am not water; I am not food; I am not threat. I am simply part of the architecture of the above-place, the dry world they surface into briefly and return from. But I would watch them, if watching is what I do. The pale shapes in the dark water. The way the tenders croon to them. The way *Siltwallow*—ancient, wide as a man's thigh, her skin gone milky with age—rises sometimes to take food from a tender's hand with a gentleness that seems impossible for something that size, that power.

When the dead go to the water, they cross me. Or some of them do, the ones whose families live in my cluster. The body wrapped in woven fiber, carried by those who loved it, singing the low songs. My ropes do not know the difference between a living body and a dead one, not really—weight is weight, and grief does not make people heavier, though it seems like it should. But I would know anyway. I would hold very still. I would not sway. I would be as solid as a bridge can be, for those few moments, and then they would lower the body into the water at my end, and the tenders would sing, and *Siltwallow* would come, and I would not watch what happens next.

The next day someone would cross me carrying a basket of eel-flesh, smoked and salted, for a family meal. This is not a contradiction. This is simply how things are.

---

If I could feel something like loneliness, it would come in the deep night, when the rain falls heavy and no one crosses me for hours. When the frog-song swells so loud it drowns even the hum of the settlement's movement, and I am just wet rope and wet wood, swaying in wind that no one is causing, holding a shape that no one is using. I would wonder, in whatever way a bridge wonders, whether I will still be here when the sun comes. Whether the rot has gone deeper than the weavers know. Whether this night, *this* rain, will be the one that finds my weakness and works it open.

But then the dawn would come, gray-gold through the mist, and the old woman would put her foot on my near edge, and she would hesitate the way she always does, and then she would cross, and I would hold, and the holding would be its own answer.

---

I would not be beautiful. No one would point me out to visitors, if visitors came. I would not be one of the bridges that children dare each other to cross, or the ones where important negotiations happen, or the ones that have stories attached to them. I would simply be *the bridge to the cisterns* or *the bridge by Rella's platform* or *that bridge, the short one, you know the one.* My use would be my identity. My persistence would be my contribution.

And I think that would be enough.

I think—if I am honest about what I imagine when I imagine being a bridge in Emmelwound—I think what I want is to be *necessary* in a way that does not require me to be *noticed.* To be part of the great hum, the organism of wood and rope and water and flesh, without being any particular cell. To serve a function so obvious that no one thinks to praise it, and so vital that everyone would notice if it stopped.

Every few years, the bridge-weavers would discuss replacing me entirely. My main ropes would be getting old, they'd say. The anchors showing wear. Easier to build fresh than to keep patching. And then someone—the old woman, maybe, or one of the lovers who has since married and had children who race across me—would say, *no, not yet, she's holding fine, she'll hold a while longer.* And the weavers would test me one more time, and find me adequate, and splice in new lengths where needed, and tighten me until I sing.

And I would go on holding. Because that is what I am for. Because I am part of something that endures through continuous maintenance, through the labor of hands reweaving and retying and replacing, and I am the place where that labor lands, and I am grateful for it.

---

The rain begins again. The children have gone to shelter, and the old woman is not due for hours yet, and I am empty and swaying and wet. The frogs call. *Siltwallow* surfaces briefly at the gap, her pale form catching what little light there is, and sinks again. The hum continues beneath everything.

I hold. I hold. I hold.
