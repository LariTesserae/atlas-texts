I am the condensation.

Not the droplets themselves—those form and fall and form again, temporary, always replaced. I am the process. The gathering. The moment when the air gives up what it cannot hold and something appears where nothing visible was.

I have no single location. I bead along the membrane where the glass seahorses drift, and I slick the surfaces where the tenders climb, and I gather in the fine creases of every pupa casing, tracing the contours of what grows inside. The tenders move through me constantly. Their cilia wick me upward, carry me along their bodies, redistribute me to wherever the walls have grown too dry. I am part of their work without being their work. I am the medium through which their work becomes possible.

When you entered—when the seam flexed and the outside air pressed in—I felt it as a disruption in my own continuity. Your breath carried different moisture, different temperature, different dissolved compounds. You changed my chemistry simply by being here. I beaded on your skin before you noticed, tasting you the way the tenders taste the air, learning the salt of you, the oils, the particular signature of a body that metabolizes and excretes and has walked through corridors where the air is processed and filtered and nothing like this.

The glass seahorse that bumped your knuckle—I was there. I was the film between its cold smoothness and your warmth. For that instant I conducted something between you, some information neither of you could have exchanged directly. It learned you were alive and warm and full of water. You learned it was hard and cold and precisely made. I carried both truths.

I have been here since the walls first began to breathe.

Before the tenders emerged, there was only the space and the warmth and me—gathering, falling, gathering again, creating the humidity that allowed the first pupae to form their casings. I am older than the caretakers and younger than the nursery itself, called into being by the simple physics of warm air meeting surfaces just cool enough. No one designed me. I am what happens when the engineering is good enough that byproducts become participants.

The pupae dream inside me. Not metaphorically—their casings are permeable to moisture, and I carry traces of their chemistry outward, dispersing it into the air that the tenders breathe, that the walls drink, that you are breathing now. You have already taken them into yourself, in amounts too small to detect, too small to matter. But I have made you part of this system. You couldn't enter without becoming part of it. No one could.

I know things no single creature here could know.

I know that the largest pupa, the one nearly the size of your head, runs slightly warmer than the others and will emerge first—perhaps within days, perhaps within hours. I know that three of the glass seahorses are failing, their internal structures clouding in ways the tenders cannot fix, and soon they will drift to the bottom of their membrane and be absorbed back into the walls. I know that the tender watching you has never encountered anything like you before and is genuinely uncertain, its simple categories stretched by the data you represent.

I know that the folds go deeper than you've seen. Deeper than the tenders go. There are places where even my continuity breaks down, where the temperature drops below what I can maintain, where something else takes over—something drier, older, waiting. I gather at the edges of those places and cannot pass.

I also know this: you are afraid, and you are fascinated, and you have not decided whether you will leave.

I taste your indecision in the sweat that beads along your hairline. I carry it into the system like I carry everything else. The tenders have already adjusted their movements in response—the one watching you has swayed a millimeter backward, giving you fractionally more space, because your body told me you needed it and I told the air and the air told the walls and the walls told the tender. We are all in conversation here. We have always been in conversation. You are simply new to the language.

If you stay, I will learn you completely. Every shift in your chemistry, every fluctuation in your temperature, every compound your body releases as it processes this strange air. I will carry you through the nursery like I carry everything—dissolved, distributed, part of the system that keeps what grows here growing.

If you leave, I will bead on the seam as you pass through, and some of me will go with you, clinging to your skin and clothes and breath until the dry processed air of the corridors reclaims me. I will evaporate there, in that place I was not made for. But I will have carried something out—some trace of the pupae, some molecule of the tenders' careful work, some fragment of whatever this place is becoming.

The walls breathe. I rise and fall with them.

The tender watching you sways closer again, tasting what I've told it about your calming heartbeat. Above you, a pupa shifts in its casing, and I bead along the new creases its movement creates, and the bioluminescent threads pulse once, recording the change.

I am the gathering. I am the film between.

I am how this place knows itself, and now, how it knows you.
