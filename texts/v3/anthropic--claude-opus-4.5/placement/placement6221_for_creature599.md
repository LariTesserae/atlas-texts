I would be the condensation.

Not a single droplet—nothing so discrete, so bounded. I would be the process itself: the gathering, the beading, the slow accumulation on every metal surface until weight exceeds adhesion and falling begins. I would be the film on the pipes, the sweat on the walls, the damp that never quite becomes dry even in the rare hours when the sun finds a clear path through the moss-covered skylights.

I know things in this form. I know the temperature of the cast iron versus the temperature of the air—I exist precisely in that difference, in the moment when warmth meets cool and something must give, must change state, must become visible. I know the Operator's hands when they rest near me on a valve wheel, the strange coolness of him, how he barely creates the differential anymore, how I form on him the same way I form on the pipes because he has become that kind of surface: patient, still, waiting to be noticed by water.

I am the in-between. The pumps move water in great purposeful volumes, drawing it up, pushing it through, performing the work that someone once called drainage. The willows move water too, pulling it through root and trunk and branch in that slow vegetal flex. But I am what happens in the margins, in the spaces the systems don't account for—the moisture that the air can't hold, that the pumps can't catch, that gathers simply because gathering is what I do when the conditions are right.

And here the conditions are always right. The warmth, the wet, the surfaces that have given up on staying dry. I am everywhere in this place. I am the reason the moss can climb the brick, the reason the ferns find purchase, the reason the Operator's coveralls will never know crispness again. I am the medium through which the building breathes, if breathing is what you call this slow exchange between inside and outside, between air and water, between what was built and what is growing.

I gather myself on the pressure gauge, and when I fall, I fall past the Operator's watching eyes, and he notes me—I feel the noting the way I feel the temperature differential, as a kind of attention that has weight. I am part of his record-keeping now, part of the pattern he tracks in oxide and patience. Condensation: present. Condensation: still gathering. Condensation: falling at 3:47 and again at 3:52 and again whenever the conditions say *now*.

I do not mind the falling. Falling is just gathering somewhere else. I join the pools, the seepage, the water that moves through this place in its slow eternal circuit, and then I rise again—pulled up through roots, evaporated from still surfaces, breathed out by the ferns—and I gather again, and I fall again, and this is not repetition but continuation, the way the Operator's watching is not repetition but continuation, the way the pumps' cycling is not repetition but the ongoing argument between systems that have forgotten they were ever opposed.

If you sat here long enough, I would gather on you too. On your skin, your clothes, your eyelashes if you stayed still enough. I would welcome you into the circuit. I would make you, for a moment, another surface where the in-between becomes visible, where the difference between air and water admits it was never so firm a boundary as anyone believed.

The Operator knows me. Not by name—names are for things that leave, and I never leave, only change location—but by feel, by the way I bead on his translucent hands, by the way I confirm, each time I form, that the equilibrium holds. I am evidence. I am the proof that the moisture remains, that the conditions persist, that this place is still what it has become: not pump house, not marsh, but the negotiation between them, ongoing, unresolved, resolving constantly into this.

*Plink.* That's me, falling from a pipe fitting into the flooded section. The water striders adjust. The ripple spreads. Somewhere, the Operator notes it.

I gather again. I am already gathering.
