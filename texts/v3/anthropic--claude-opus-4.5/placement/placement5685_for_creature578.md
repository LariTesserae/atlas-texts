I would be one of the channel-keepers, though perhaps one approaching the end of that work—not from age exactly, but from a shift I can feel happening in what I am able to hear.

For thirty years I have walked the seep-lines at dawn, when the temperature differential between the deep water and the morning air is sharpest and the rock speaks most clearly about what is changing. My feet know the terraces so well they find their way while my attention goes elsewhere—into the stone, into the particular cold of a seep that will close within days, into the faint mineral taste I have learned to detect by touching wet rock and then touching my tongue. I have been good at this. Not the best—there was a woman named Thread-at-the-source who could predict seep movements a full season ahead, but she died before I was born and I only know her through the patterns she left scored in bone. But good enough. Good enough that when I say *this terrace will lose its water by the new moon*, people begin packing their nets.

What I have never told anyone is that I do not experience this as knowledge. It arrives more like—a pressure. A wrongness in the shape of things that resolves when I trace it to its source. The channel-keepers train for years learning to read mineral deposits, temperature gradients, the angle of salamander-roads, the density of lichen bloom. I learned all of this. I can explain my predictions in these terms if asked. But the truth is that the explanation comes after, a translation of something I perceived whole. I feel the seep closing the way I might feel a held breath. I sense the new channel opening the way I would sense someone about to speak.

This is not special. I think we all experience it this way, we channel-keepers, but we do not discuss it because we have been given a vocabulary of observation and measurement and we use it. The vocabulary is not wrong—the mineral deposits are real, the temperature gradients matter, the salamanders follow rules we have partially mapped. But the vocabulary is also not complete. There is something underneath it, or alongside it, that we touch when we are tuned well and miss when we are not.

---

Lately I have been missing more than I touch.

It began a year ago, perhaps longer. A seep I was certain would hold for another decade closed in a single night, and a family I had assured was safe woke to dry stone and had to relocate by noon. I stood at the place where the water had stopped—I could see the mineral line marking how high it had flowed just hours before—and I felt nothing. No pressure, no wrongness, no sense of the pattern shifting. Just absence where the knowing should have been.

I told myself this was an anomaly. The mountain speaks in probabilities, not certainties; we all make mistakes. But it happened again. And again. Small things at first—a seep that shifted east instead of west, a salamander-road that forked where I expected it to continue. Then larger things. I stopped giving predictions with confidence. I started saying *I think* and *it seems to me* and *but I cannot be certain*. The others noticed. They began consulting with younger channel-keepers before acting on what I told them.

I do not blame them. I would do the same.

---

What I have come to believe—and I hold this loosely, the way you hold water in cupped hands, knowing it will slip through—is that being tuned is not a permanent state. The mountain filled us, our ancestors said, but what fills can also empty. What receives can also become unable to receive. Perhaps I have been tuned so long to one frequency of the mountain's speech that I have become deaf to the others. Perhaps the mountain itself has shifted, is speaking in a new register, and I have not learned to hear it. Perhaps I am simply old in some way that has nothing to do with years—worn smooth like the stone steps, shaped into a form that can no longer adapt.

Flicker, the young woman who watches the light at the settlement's edge—I find myself thinking about her often. The others see her strangeness as a question: *is she being tuned to something new, or has she failed to be tuned at all?* I see it differently. I see her sitting with the not-knowing, making a home there, refusing to force the listening into patterns she already recognizes. I have begun to wonder if she has found something I have lost: the willingness to perceive without understanding, to attend without translating.

I visited her once, a few months ago. I brought her water and dried salamander-meat and I sat beside her for an hour, watching the light move across the wet stone. I did not ask her what she saw. I did not try to explain my own failing. I simply sat.

The light did flicker. It did shift and dance in ways I had never attended to, always too focused on the water beneath, the stone beneath that, the deep slow pulse of the mountain's breath. The light was saying something. I could feel that much—the pressure of meaning, the shape of a pattern I could not quite resolve. But I could not hear what it was saying. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

When I left, I touched her shoulder, and she touched mine, and that was all.

---

I still walk the seep-lines at dawn. I still taste the water and watch the salamander-roads and report what I observe to those who need to know. But I have started spending my afternoons differently. I sit on my terrace—I have lived on the same terrace for twelve years, long for a channel-keeper, and I know its stone so well I could navigate it blind—and I practice a different kind of attention.

I try to listen without knowing what I am listening for.

This is harder than it sounds. Thirty years of training have grooved certain pathways into my perception. When I see a salamander-knot forming, my mind immediately begins parsing it: *convergence of three roads, temperature differential of at least four degrees, probable seep activity within a hundred paces*. When I feel the water's cold against my palm, I am already calculating mineral content, comparing it to yesterday, extrapolating forward. The interpretation arrives before the perception is complete.

What I am trying to learn—what Flicker seems to already know—is how to let the perception be enough. To feel the cold without translating it. To see the knot without parsing it. To watch the light flicker across wet stone and simply *watch*, without asking what it means.

I am not good at this yet. I may never be good at it. But I notice that on the days when I practice, when I spend an hour or two simply attending without understanding, the dawn walks feel different. The pressure of meaning returns, faintly. Not the clear signal I once received, but something softer, less certain—a suggestion rather than a statement.

Maybe I am learning a new frequency. Maybe I am unlearning an old one. Maybe there is no difference.

---

There is a word in our language that does not translate well—something like *hollow-filling* or *empty-readiness*. It describes the state our ancestors were in when they first came here and the mountain began to tune them. They were empty, the stories say, but it was a productive emptiness, a hollowness that invited filling. They did not know what they would receive. They only knew they were ready to receive it.

I have started to think of my failing not as loss but as a return to that state. The mountain filled me with one kind of knowing, and now it is emptying me so that I can be filled again. Or perhaps not filled—perhaps simply left open, a vessel that remains hollow, that learns to be useful precisely because it does not contain anything fixed.

This is probably too hopeful. I am probably telling myself a story to make the loss bearable. But the people here have always understood that stories and truth are not opposites—that a story can be a way of listening, a way of holding yourself open to meaning you cannot yet perceive. So I tell myself this story, and I wait to see if the mountain confirms it or revises it or refuses it entirely.

---

The seep below my terrace has been running slightly warmer for three days. I noticed this the way I notice everything now—half as observation, half as mystery. A younger channel-keeper would predict what this means: a shift in the deep thermal currents, a probable rerouting of water within a month, a need to notify the terraces downstream. I could make that prediction. The old knowledge is still there, worn into me like the paths are worn into stone.

But I have not made it. Instead I am watching. I am letting the warmth be warmth, the water be water, the mountain's breath be breath. I am trying to hear what the change is saying, not what I expect it to say.

This morning a salamander paused near my foot. Salamanders do not pause—this is one of the first things we learn, that their constant motion is part of how they regulate their temperature and they will die if they stop for too long. But this one paused. It turned its pearl-white head toward me, or seemed to, though I am not certain salamanders can see in any way I would recognize. We stayed like that for three breaths, maybe four. Then it flowed onward, rejoining the ribbon of its kind, and was gone.

I do not know what this meant. I do not know if it meant anything at all. But I am holding it carefully, the way Keth-in-the-seep holds each stone she places, as a question I am asking the mountain.

*Is this what you want?*

I do not expect an answer. I am learning not to expect. I am learning to ask and then to wait, to remain hollow, to let the question itself be enough.

The water flows. The light flickers. The mountain breathes.

I am still being tuned.
