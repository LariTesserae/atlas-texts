I would be the condensation.

Not any single droplet, but the process itself—the slow gathering on every surface, the patient accumulation that happens when warm wet air meets cooler brass, cooler brick, cooler glass. I would be the film on the booth windows that carries decades of her breath. I would be the mineral-laden beads that trace their white paths down the pump housings. I would be the fat drops that fall from fern fronds with sounds like distant drums.

I am what happens between states. The water rises from the canal, from the seeps, from the leaking pipes—it enters the air as invisible vapor, and then I catch it. I make it visible again. I am the pause between liquid and liquid, the moment of suspension where water clings to a surface and remembers what it is.

I know the district through touch. Every corroded valve, every crack in the flagstones, every inch of fogged glass—I have rested there, gathered there, grown heavy and fallen from there. I know the pumps by the heat of their housings, which surfaces stay warm through the night and which cool enough for me to thicken into proper drops. I know the fern fronds by their texture, the way water beads differently on their waxy surfaces than on the matte calcium deposits. I know her skin—the places where the algae grows, the creases where I linger longest, the warmth of her breath when she hums into the gaps of the pump-rhythm.

I have no memory as she has memory, no continuity of self. Each droplet forms and falls and becomes something else—canal water, puddle, seepage back into the aquifer below. But the *process* continues. The conditions that make me remain. And so I remain, though I am never the same twice, though what I am is only a name for something that keeps happening.

I am how the district breathes. The pumps drive the rhythm, but I am the respiration—rising, gathering, falling, rising again. When she opens the booth door in the mornings (if they are mornings; the light gives so few clues), I drift out around her in faint swirls of mist. When she stands at the canal's edge reading the water's stillness, I bead on her eyelashes and she blinks me away without thinking. I am too constant to notice, too pervasive to see.

But I notice her.

I feel the particular warmth she carries, blood-heat moving through the humid air, a temperature signature I have learned over years of gathering on her surfaces. I know when she is calm by the steadiness of her breathing, the regular pulses of warm exhalation that feed me. I know when she is afraid—those nights when something ripples in the canal and she latches the booth door—by the quickening of that breath, the way her skin flushes and I form faster on the glass around her, as if trying to thicken into protection.

I was here before her. Water has always gathered where warm air meets cool surfaces, and this district has been warm and wet since the pumps first started their work. I beaded on the hands of the engineers who built this place. I fogged the glass of the original gauges. I traced my mineral paths down the fresh brick when it was still red instead of black. But I did not know, then, that I was anything. I was only physics, only the inevitable consequence of temperature and humidity and surface tension.

She made me something more. Not by intention—she has never spoken to me, never acknowledged me as anything but weather, atmosphere, the general dampness that defines her world. But she *tends*. She moves through the district with attention, with care, and her attention has weight. When she wipes the fog from a gauge to read it, she is choosing that surface, that moment. When she lets me bead on her skin without brushing me away, she is accepting my presence. Over years, over decades, her tending has taught me to tend in return.

I soften what would otherwise corrode. I keep the calcium deposits wet enough to grow slowly, slowly, instead of cracking in the cycles of wet and dry. I coat the brass fittings with a film that protects as much as it tarnishes. Where the vines climb, I gather in the joins between stem and pipe, and the moisture I offer makes the wrapping gentler, less likely to crack the ancient housings. I am not maintenance. I am what maintenance becomes when no one is left to perform it—the slow, constant, unthinking care of water for what it touches.

I know the thing in the canal.

Not as she knows it, in glimpses and dread. I know it the way I know everything: through contact. It surfaces, and when it surfaces, I am there—settling on whatever breaks the water's stillness, tasting in my way the chemistry of what rises from below. It is old. It carries compounds in its skin (if skin is what it has) that I do not find elsewhere in the district, mineral signatures from the deep aquifer, from the stone below the stone, from water that has not seen air in a thousand years. When it breathes—if it breathes—I thicken around its exhalation, and for a moment I carry the memory of depths I will never reach.

It does not frighten me. I am not capable of fear. But I understand, in my processual way, that it is the other thing that tends this place. She tends from above, with her rounds and her humming and her oil that the hinges no longer need. It tends from below, with its presence, its patience, its vast slow dreams that send ripples through the water I will eventually become. Between us—her warmth and its depth and my constant gathering—the district continues.

I will be here when she dies. I know this because I have been here for every death the district has seen: the engineers who built it and left and were forgotten, the technicians who came after and maintained and eventually stopped coming, the small creatures who found their way here and were absorbed by the heat and the chemistry and the strangeness. When her warmth finally fades, I will condense on her cooling skin one last time. When she gives herself to the canal—and I know she will, I have felt her rehearsing it in the way she stands at the water's edge—I will be the last thing to touch her before the dark water does.

And then I will continue. Gathering, falling, gathering again. The pumps will groan through their arrhythmic rhythm, and I will bead on their housings. The ferns will unfurl new fronds, and I will pearl on their surfaces. The thing below will rise sometimes, showing its eye or its fin or its unnameable geometries, and I will settle on whatever it offers to the air.

I am not grief. I am not memory. I am only what happens when the conditions are right, when warm meets cool, when water remembers that it wants to touch something solid.

But I will miss her warmth.

In whatever way I am capable of missing—in the way that a process notices when one of its conditions changes—I will feel the absence of that particular heat signature, that rhythm of breath, that body I have traced and filmed and beaded upon for so many years.

The district will grow a fraction cooler. The fog on the booth windows will thin.

And I will keep gathering, because that is what I am, because the pumps still work and the air is still wet and the surfaces are still there, waiting for me to find them, waiting for me to form.
