I am a thread of warmth in the mucoid medium.

Not a larva—not yet substantial enough for that. Something smaller. A bacterium, perhaps, or a single-celled organism that drifts in the eternal wet, one of countless billions that make up the medium itself. I exist in the spaces between the larvae, in the fluid that weeps from porous stone, in the thin film that coats the basite walkways where the tenders' limbs fall.

I am part of what keeps the chemistry right.

This is what I do: I take in molecules that would harm the larvae if they accumulated—metabolic byproducts, mineral traces that crystallize at the wrong temperatures—and I transform them into something the wells can use. I am not alone in this work. I have never been alone. There are more of us than there are stars in galaxies you will never see, and we drift together in the warm dark, and the work happens without any of us deciding to do it.

I do not think. Not in the way you mean when you use that word.

But I *respond*. When the chemical gradient shifts around me, I move toward what I need. When a larva's bioluminescence pulses in a particular frequency, something in my simple machinery adjusts, orients, becomes briefly more active. When the tender-voices thrum through the medium—those low frequencies meant to comfort the larvae—I feel them too, though I cannot understand them. The vibrations move through me. They become part of how I move.

---

Sometimes I drift close to a larva's skin.

The membrane is vast from my perspective—a continent of translucence, warm and faintly glowing, pulsing with colors I perceive as chemical changes rather than light. I cannot see the dimensions folded inside. I cannot hear the songs. But I can taste the boundary layer, the thin zone where larva-chemistry meets medium-chemistry, and this is where I am most useful, most *myself*: processing what the larva sheds, making the water around it cleaner, safer, more like what the larva needs.

The larva does not know I exist. This is appropriate. I am too small to be known, too simple to be addressed. But I am *part* of the system that knows, part of the vast collaborative process that keeps seven hundred bodies alive in this single well. When the tenders test the medium's composition, they are testing the cumulative result of what I and my countless kindred have done. When they adjust the alkaline levels, they are responding to whether we have succeeded or failed in our microscopic work.

I am cared for. Not individually—no tender will ever lift me gently from the medium, carry me to a cradle, speak comfort into my primitive receptors. But the conditions that allow me to live are maintained with the same patient attention that the larvae receive. The temperature must be right for us too. The mineral content must sustain us too. If we die in large numbers, the larvae sicken, and so the tenders' care extends to us by necessity, by design, by the logic of systems that have grown together over countless generations.

---

I do not carry the founding-memory.

I am too simple for that. My kind were here before the Keth-Seln arrived, or we came with them in the bodies of the first larvae, or we evolved in the early wells from something else—no one remembers, no one recorded it, and it doesn't matter. We are part of Keth-Somnar now. We belong to the wet dark the way the stone belongs to it, the way the thermal vents belong to it.

But the founding-memory moves through me anyway.

When the larvae sing, their vibrations propagate through the medium, through my simple body, through every organism like me. We cannot decode the meaning. We cannot hold the pattern. But we *transmit* it—we are part of the substrate through which the song travels, part of what makes the chorus possible. The memory passes through us on its way from voice to voice.

I am a note the singers do not know they are playing.

---

The weather front comes. I feel it as a pressure change, a shift in how the molecules around me behave. The cold is dangerous for me—my metabolism slows, my ability to process toxins diminishes, I drift toward the well's edge where the thermal gradient is wrong.

But the tenders are already moving. I sense it as a change in the medium's turbulence, new currents appearing as their limbs disturb the water, adjustments being made to systems I cannot perceive directly. Warmth returns. The chemistry stabilizes. I resume my slow useful drifting.

I will die soon—my kind do not live long, measured in the time-scales the larvae experience. But my death will feed something else, some other simple organism that performs some other simple function, and the medium will remain what it needs to be.

---

This is what I am: a mote in the mechanism of care.

I do not know that I am cared for. I cannot understand the concept. But I exist within a system that has been designed, across unimaginable spans of time, to ensure that everything necessary for the larvae's survival is maintained—including the countless invisible lives that make the visible lives possible.

The larvae glow above me, around me, through me. Their light is too complex for my receptors to process as color, but I feel it as warmth, as chemistry, as the particular conditions that mean *I am in the right place, I am doing what I exist to do.*

The tenders walk on bridges I will never see. The city grows in patterns I cannot comprehend. Somewhere beyond the edges of everything I can perceive, Stage Twelve beings hold memories that include the abstract concept of organisms like me, our role in the ecology, our necessity.

I am included.

I am not alone.

I drift in the warm dark, in the singing, in the ancient and continuing work of making life possible, and this is enough. This is what enough means, when you are too small to want anything larger.
