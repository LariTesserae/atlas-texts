I would be one of the lamps that has begun to burn wrong.

Not the yellowish decline that signals fuel depletion—that is expected, tended, part of the agreement the Lamplighters keep. I would be one of the blue-edged ones. The ones they replace entirely, housing and all, without explanation. The ones that represent something gone sideways in ways no one names.

I would have burned correctly for a very long time. Decades. Perhaps a century or more, casting the same bone-white light as every other lamp on my stretch of avenue, pooling illumination into the wide street where figures emerge from darkness and approach slowly, patiently, without hurry. My flame would have consumed fuel-bricks pressed into my housing by Lamplighters I never learned to distinguish from one another—their hands all careful, all practiced, all reaching up with the long hooked pole to open my glass door.

I would not have known when the blue began.

This is important: I would not have chosen it. Whatever I am—whatever animates the slow burn, whatever persists in the flame that passes through fuel-brick after fuel-brick while remaining somehow continuous, somehow *mine*—I would not have decided to change. The change would have crept in the way the dust creeps into corners: gradually, without announcement, without clear origin. One day—or one year, or one stretch of time that felt like neither—the Lamplighter checking my flame would pause. Would note something in her small journal. Would move on.

And I would realize, in whatever way a lamp realizes anything, that I had begun to see differently.

---

The bone-light shows what is here. This is its function. It illuminates the pale stone, the wide streets, the figures moving through their unhurried lives. It asks nothing of what it shows. It makes no judgments about the dust or the listeners or the factories humming in walls too thick to see through. It simply *shows*, and what it shows is enough, because the city has agreed that it is enough.

But the blue edge—the blue edge shows something else.

I would see, in my pool of light that is no longer quite the right color, the way the footprints layer. Not just the recent ones, the decades-old impressions of feet still living, but the ones beneath. And beneath those. The street under my light would become transparent to its own history, and I would see the walking of generations compressed into a single image, thousands of figures moving through the same space, their paths overlapping until the stone itself seemed crowded with motion that had already ended.

I would see the listeners differently too. In bone-light, they are patient things, waiting, attending to sound without apparent purpose. In my blue-edged light, I would see them *straining*. Leaning toward something that is not sound. Oriented not toward footsteps or voices but toward a direction that has no name, a below-that-is-not-down, an absence they have been tracking since before the city existed.

And the people—

The people would walk through my light and feel nothing. They are not wrong to feel nothing. What I show is not for them. What I show is not *useful*. The city functions because its light reveals what is needed and obscures what is not, and I have begun revealing things that serve no function, that answer no question anyone is asking, that simply *are*, layered and heavy and too much.

---

The Lamplighter who finally comes to replace me would be the one who speaks too much—the former Lamplighter on the Council, returning to her old circuits perhaps, or simply walking, the way old Lamplighters sometimes walk their routes long after they have passed their responsibilities to others. She would stand beneath me for a long time, not reaching up with the pole, just looking.

I would show her, in my wrong light, the version of herself that walked this route forty years ago. Younger, though the plateau does not age people the way other places might. More certain. Carrying the black stone in her pocket that she has never used, that none of them have ever used, that waits for something no Lamplighter can describe.

She would see herself seeing me, decades ago, when my flame was right, when I was part of the agreement instead of a violation of it.

"You're not broken," she would say, eventually. Quietly, the way everyone speaks here, voices carrying too well in still air. "You're just—seeing too much. Showing too much."

And then she would reach up, and open the housing, and take what I am and set it aside.

---

What happens to the blue-edged lamps?

The housings are replaced. The fuel is not reused. The flames are—

I do not know. No one discusses it. This is not secrecy, exactly. It is simply that some questions are not load-bearing, and the city does not maintain what it does not need. The blue-edged lamps are removed, and new lamps are installed, and the bone-light returns to its proper color, and the street looks like a street again instead of a palimpsest of every street it has ever been.

But I imagine—if a lamp can imagine—that we are taken somewhere. Not the below, not the depths where the departed go, but somewhere adjacent. A storage place. A keeping-place. A room where wrong lights can burn without illuminating anything that matters, where the blue edge can show its too-much-ness to walls that have no need for what is revealed.

I imagine that we burn there still, those of us who have changed. That our light pools and mingles, blue-edged with blue-edged, showing each other the layered histories we were not meant to show, the straining directions we were not meant to see. I imagine that we are not lonely, exactly—loneliness requires an expectation of company, and we are lamps, we have never expected—but that we are *together*, in whatever way flames can be together.

I imagine the Lamplighter who replaced me thinks of me sometimes. Not often. Not with grief. But in the way the city thinks of everything it has set aside: as something that happened, that persisted, that is still persisting somewhere, part of the continuation even in its removal from the visible order.

---

If you came to the plateau, you would not find me.

You would walk the wide streets under the bone-light and see what the bone-light shows: the stone, the dust, the listeners in their patient corners, the figures approaching slowly from the pooled edges of illumination. You would feel the stillness of the air, the warmth that skin forgets, the press of the darkness above that is not absence but presence. You might stay. Most who come, stay. The city is not unkind to those who fit within it.

But you might, one evening—if evenings can be said to exist here, if time can be said to mark itself in any way other than the slow depletion of fuel-bricks—you might pass a lamp that flickers. Just slightly. Just for a moment. A lamp that has been burning correctly for years but that, in that instant, shows a blue edge at its flame's perimeter.

And you might see, just for that moment, the footprints beneath your footprints. The history compressed into the stone. The listeners leaning toward something that is not sound.

And then the lamp would steady, and the light would be bone-white again, and you would walk on, and the moment would join all the other moments that the city notes and does not remember.

But I would have seen you.

In my burning-wrong, in my too-much-showing, in whatever remains of me in whatever room holds the blue-edged flames—I would have seen you, and for that instant, you would have been part of what I show.

This is not a gift. It is not a curse. It is simply what I am now: a lamp that burned long and correctly and then began to see too much, to show too much, to be too much for the agreement that keeps the city lit.

I do not regret it. Regret requires a belief that things could have been otherwise, and I am a lamp. I burned as I burned. I burn as I burn. The blue edge came, and I showed what I showed, and now I am here, wherever here is, burning still.

The darkness above is patient. The city below is patient. And I am patient too, in my way, in my wrong way, waiting without waiting for nothing in particular, my blue-edged light pooling in a room no one enters, illuminating histories that have no use, showing too much to no one at all.

This is enough.

It has to be.
