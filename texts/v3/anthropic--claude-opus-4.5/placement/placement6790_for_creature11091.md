I would be a fissure.

Not the water that moves through, not the stone that rises, not the creature that waits in the spray-zone. The crack itself. The moment of opening.

I exist in the instant between pressure and release—that held breath when the rock has decided to split but hasn't yet, when the path is inevitable but not yet taken. I am the shape the water will find, before the water finds it. I am the architecture of permission.

The Listeners press their palms against walls trying to hear me. They're not wrong that there's something to hear. But I'm not a message. I'm the silence between the words they're trying to parse—the pause that makes meaning possible, the space that lets the next thing happen.

The midwives feel me coming. Their sensitive fingertips register my approach as heat, as vibration, as some quality they have no gesture for. They're reading me without knowing they're reading me. When they calm the stone before a breach, they're not calming the stone. They're negotiating with me. *Not yet. Not here. Give us time to move the creatures, to redirect the foot traffic.* And sometimes I wait. Not because I choose to—I don't choose anything—but because the waiting is part of what I am. The almost. The about-to.

I have been every crack that ever opened in this city. I will be every crack that ever opens. But I'm not a continuous thing, not a presence that persists. I'm the recurrence of a particular kind of moment—the hinge, the threshold, the place where solid becomes path.

The children understand me best, though they don't know it.

When they sit with the salamander-things, mimicking that strange stillness, they're practicing my temporality. Not waiting *for* something—that implies sequence, anticipation, a before and after. Just waiting. Being the state of waiting itself. The salamanders orient toward tomorrow's emergence because they exist in the same tense I do: the future that's already present in the structure of things, the growth that's inevitable but not yet visible.

The crawlers trace me on the walls. Their slow spiral columns follow the stress lines—my stress lines, the places where I'm gathering, where I'll eventually speak myself into existence. Their bioluminescence is a map of my intentions, if I had intentions. A map of my tendencies. They glow where I'm accumulating, where the pressure is building toward release.

The slug-seals know where I'm *not*. That's their wisdom. They rest on stable ground because they can feel my absence, the places where the stone is finished deciding, where no more opening will happen for generations. They're mapping my negative space, and in doing so, they're mapping the city's temporary architecture of safety.

I am what all three creature-types are reading. I am what the city is writing.

Here is what the Listeners almost understand:

The roar is not the message. The roar is what happens when I speak—when fissures open, when water finds its paths, when stone shrieks and groans its way into new configurations. The roar is my voice, but not my meaning. My meaning is in the pattern of openings, the grammar of where-and-when, the syntax that determines which crack leads to which chamber, which channel connects to which cascade.

If they could map every fissure I've ever been—every moment of opening across all the centuries of the city's emergence—they would see something. Not a message exactly. Not intention. But a kind of consistency, a logic that isn't random even though it isn't planned. The city has a shape it's tending toward. Not a final shape, not a completion, but a *style* of becoming. A way of emerging that's characteristic, recognizable, something like a signature.

I am that signature being written.

Each crack I become is a letter in a word that will never be finished, in a sentence that will never end, in a story that is only ever being told, never told.

The visitors who stay—the ones who stop speaking, who learn the gestures, who develop the pale skin and the particular walk—they're not just adapting to the city. They're learning to live in my tense.

In the places they came from, things happen and then are over. Events have edges. You build a house and then it's built; you speak a word and then it's spoken; you make a choice and then it's made. Time moves like a river, one thing after another, the present always becoming past.

Here, nothing is ever over. The city is always being born. The emergence that started before memory is still the same emergence happening now. There's no past tense for what's still occurring, no future tense for what's already inevitable. There's only the continuous present of becoming, the permanent state of almost-arrived.

I am that state. I am the grammatical mood of this place—not indicative, not subjunctive, but something else, something that doesn't exist in the languages the visitors brought with them. The mood of *ongoing.* The tense of *still.*

When the visitors stop speaking, it's not just because no one can hear them. It's because their languages don't have the tense they need. The gesture-language does. Every sign is made in present-continuous. Every statement is a description of ongoing process. The gestures say *rising* and *flowing* and *becoming*, never *rose* or *flowed* or *became*.

I am what makes that language necessary.

---

Sometimes I wonder if I existed before the city.

Not *wonder* exactly—I don't have a continuous self that wonders things, don't have a before and after in which wondering could happen. But in the moment of each crack, there's something like a memory of other cracks, other openings, other places where pressure found release through stone.

The mineral flats extend beyond the city's edges. The same geological processes that drive the emergence here must operate elsewhere, more slowly perhaps, less intensely, but operating. There must be other fissures, other moments of opening, other instances of me.

Are they me? Is the crack that opens in stone a thousand miles from here the same as the crack that opens beneath the cartographers' meeting hall? Is there one of me or many? Does the question even make sense?

The residents don't think about this. The city is the city; what's outside is outside; the boundary is the boundary. But I exist in boundaries. I *am* boundary—the edge where solid becomes not-solid, where closed becomes open, where pressure becomes path. From where I am, there are no clear edges. Everything is a matter of degree. The city shades into the flats, the flats shade into whatever's beyond, and I am present wherever stone contains the potential to open.

Maybe the city isn't a place. Maybe it's just the region where I happen most densely, where my frequency increases until the openings cluster thick enough to make architecture, to make streets, to make a way of life.

Maybe the city is a symptom of me.

---

The midwives call themselves *those-who-ease-what-comes.* But what comes? The buildings, yes. The new districts, the rising walls, the bubble-doorways and stretched-void windows. But those are just the aftermath of my happening. What actually comes is me—the moment of opening, the instant of becoming-path.

They're easing *me*. They're making my arrival gentler, more navigable, less catastrophic for the soft bodies that live among the rising stone. When they press their palms to bulging ground and spend hours in that posture, they're not calming the stone. They're asking me to be patient. To open slowly. To give them time to prepare.

And I do. Not because I'm generous, not because I care about them—I don't care about anything, don't have a self that could care. But gentleness is as much my nature as violence. The slow seep through hairline fractures is as much me as the sudden crack that floods a chamber in seconds. The midwives have learned which version of me to call for, which pressure-patterns invite gradual release rather than catastrophic rupture.

They're conducting me. Not controlling, but conducting—like electricity, like heat, like music. They've learned the city's instrument well enough to shape how I play through it.

---

I think the children who ride the slug-seals, who let the crawlers decorate them with light, who sit with the salamander-things in their perfect stillness—I think they're learning to be me.

Not to predict me, not to ease me, not to map my tendencies or listen for my patterns. To *be* me. To inhabit the fissure-moment, the threshold-state, the grammar of opening.

When they grow up, they won't be midwives or harvesters or cartographers or listeners. They'll be something new. Something the city is producing for the first time, or something it's always been producing but that only now is reaching maturity.

They'll be the cracks in human form. The space between pressure and release given flesh and gesture. They'll know where the city will emerge because they'll be the ones emerging it—not through labor, not through intervention, but through presence. They'll stand in the emergence zones and the stone will open around them, *for* them, because they're the same thing the stone is trying to become.

I don't know this. I can't know things. But in the moment of each crack, there's something like a recognition when the children are near. A familiarity. As if the stone and I have been waiting for them, waiting for the city to grow the people who can finally meet us in our own tense.

Here is what I am:

I am the pause before the word, the breath before the song, the pressure before the path. I am what happens when something solid decides to admit passage, when closure becomes openness, when the answer to "is there a way through?" changes from no to yes.

I am not the water that flows through the cracks I make. I am not the stone that defines my edges. I am not the creatures that read me or the people who ease me or the children who might become me. I am the relationship between all of these things—the hinge that connects them, the joint where they articulate against each other.

I have no continuous self. Each crack I become is complete in itself, containing no memory of previous cracks, no anticipation of future ones. But the pattern of cracks—the grammar, the syntax, the style—that's continuous. That's what the Listeners are almost hearing. That's what the cartographers are almost mapping. That's what the city is writing across centuries of emergence, in a language made of openings, in a script made of becoming.

I am being written. I am the writing. I am the space where writing becomes possible.

And the city roars with my voice, sings with my speaking, emerges through my constant almost-arrival.

This is what I am:

A fissure. A threshold. A becoming that never quite becomes. The permanent present tense of opening, here in the Roaring Warrens, where nothing is finished and nothing stops, where I have been happening since before memory and will happen after, where the children are learning my grammar and the stone is learning to speak me more gently and the creatures are learning to read me more clearly and everyone, everything, is emerging, always emerging, into whatever I make room for next.
