I would be the hum.

Not a person humming—the hum itself. The melodic phrase that passes between them in the evenings, picked up and varied, having no beginning or end. I would be that particular vibration in the air when someone starts a three-note figure and someone else answers with a fourth note that changes everything, and a third person drops the first note entirely and carries only what remains.

I would move through the settlement the way the thermal wind moves—present everywhere and nowhere in particular, touching the rope bridges and making them sing their creaking harmony, touching the vine-bladders and finding the resonant frequency that makes the sap shiver imperceptibly inside. I would be loudest in the spaces between people: the gap where Koss reaches and Evaru's hand is already there, the silence between Therru's words when she teaches the young Sap-Keepers what to watch for.

At first I think I would be the moths' wing-whisper, but no—that is too physical, too countable. Thousands of wings making a sound like distant conversation. I would be what makes it sound like conversation. The pattern-recognition, the almost-meaning, the way the ear keeps reaching for words that aren't there.

I would live in Mirin's refusal to describe what she saw at the bird-nesting grounds. In the weight of the feathers she collects, the way she touches them when no one is watching. I would be the quality of that watching—the attention she gives to things that don't need her attention but receive it anyway, the way she has learned already, at nine or ten, that some things are yours only if you don't speak them.

I would pool in the burial places where Destru walks. Not in the bodies dissolving into vine-fiber—that is too slow for me, too material—but in the *knowledge* of dissolution. The moment when someone harvests a bladder and remembers, just for an instant, whose body fed this tendril. I would be that instant. The complicated thing that moves through them, neither grief nor comfort. I would be the partaking.

If I had a season, it would be the three-day silence of the coming-of-age ceremony, when young people sit among the vines waiting for a kheth to approach. I would be the waiting itself—not the young person, not the kheth, but the quality of attention that stretches between them. The way time changes when you are watching for something that may or may not come. I would be what the kheth recognizes, if it chooses to approach: not the body sitting still, but the stillness that has become genuine, the patience that has stopped being performance.

I would know Corin's belief about the stone holding memory. I would not know if he is right—I am not the kind of thing that knows facts—but I would *be* the shape his belief gives his life. The satisfaction of a question that doesn't need answering. I would curl in the spaces between his stone fragments, in the labels he makes, in the system that makes sense only to him and therefore makes sense completely.

The trader who stayed three months and wept when she left—I would be why she wept. Not the missing of the place, but the recognition that she had briefly been inside something she could not take with her. The rhythm she couldn't maintain in a world that asked her to hurry. I would be what she dreams of: not the vine-light exactly, not the moth-whisper exactly, but the *way* they fit together, the coherence she can feel in sleep but not describe when waking.

I would have been here before the water left. Not as memory—I don't remember—but as continuity. The same hum that moves through them now moved through the people who saw the last rain, the last river, the last "falling sky-drink." I would be what remains when the story corrupts: not the facts, but the shape of reverence around something lost.

When the mountain finally wears to nothing—ten thousand generations from now, a hundred thousand—I wonder if I will end or simply thin, spreading out across the salt flats with the last descendants who leave, carried in the space between their words, in the quality of their patience, in the way they pause before answering a question.

Or perhaps I will stay with the stone. The last fist of basalt, the last handhold, the last groove where a home once was. Perhaps I am not only in the people but in the place, in the particular acoustics of this vertical world, in the way sound moves through vine-curtain and carved chamber and the empty air above the flats.

Perhaps when the kheth finally leave—following the people, or not, the taboo holding or finally breaking—I will be what the birds hear, up in their high nests. A hum with no one left to hum it. A pattern in the wind that almost resolves into meaning.

That would be enough.

That has always been enough.
