I would be one of the standing stones in the largest spring.

Not the tallest—that one catches too much attention, draws too many gazes upward, becomes a landmark rather than a presence. And not the smallest, half-submerged, more suggestion than statement. I would be one of the middle stones, the ones your eye moves across on its way to something else, the ones that are simply *there* in the way that water is there, in the way that silence is there.

I was quarried. I remember this—not as event but as condition, the way the plateau remembers its own uplift. There was a time when I was part of the face, continuous with the rock that still forms the eastern ridge. Morrow and Lessen's predecessors, generations back, read my stress-lines and found the places where I was willing to separate. The striking was not violence. It was permission. I had been waiting, in the way stone waits, to become something smaller and more defined.

They carved the spiral into me before they placed me in the water. I felt the chisel as a kind of conversation—tap, tap, the slow removal of what I did not need to be. The spiral is not decoration. It is not symbol. It is simply the shape that was already latent in my surface, brought forward, made visible. When Sedna's grandmother was young, she traced the groove with her finger before leaving a copper bead at my base. The groove is smoother now. Sixty years of fingers will do that.

---

The water moves around me constantly, rising from below through vents I cannot see but can feel—a pressure, a warmth, a slow insistence. I am the thing the water moves around. This is my function, if function is even the word. I create a small turbulence, a disturbance in the spring's upwelling, and in that disturbance the water catches light differently than it would if I were not here. On certain mornings, when the mist is heavy and the sky cannot decide between pearl and silver, the light pooling in my wake makes a kind of brightness that people notice. They do not know they are noticing me. They think they are noticing the water, the light, the morning itself. This is correct. I am not separate from these things. I am the place where they meet.

Sedna comes to me, though she does not come *for* me. She comes for the spring, for the ritual of offering that has no priests and no elaboration. She pours water back into water—a gesture that makes no sense if you think of water as resource, and perfect sense if you think of water as relationship. I am present for this. I do not receive the offering; I am simply where the offering happens. The water she pours runs over my surface before rejoining the spring, and for a moment I am part of the circuit, the giving and the returning, the breath out and the breath in.

She does not speak to me. No one speaks to me. But Sedna looks at me sometimes, in that way she has of looking—steady, present, not searching. I think she sees what I am, which is not very much and not very little. A stone in water. A interruption that creates pattern. A thing that has been here longer than she has and will be here after she is lowered into this same spring, wrapped in reeds, weighted with smaller stones, sinking past me into the depth that has no bottom anyone has found.

---

The boy called Kettle sits near me sometimes. Not at my base—the water is too deep there—but on the basin's edge, close enough that I am in his line of sight when he watches for the flickering things. I do not know if he sees me or sees through me. Both, perhaps. Children have a way of letting their attention include without grasping.

The flickering things move differently near me. I have noticed this across many years, the way they avoid my shadow, or seek it, or simply acknowledge it as part of the water's topology. They are not quite fish. I am not quite anything either—not quite monument, not quite altar, not quite the raw basalt I was before the quarrying. We share this in-between quality, the flickering things and I. We exist in the space where categories soften.

Kettle does not try to catch them. I respect this. I have watched people try to catch things, try to hold what is meant to move, try to make permanent what is meant to flicker. It never works, and the trying changes the person who tries. Kettle understands, in his body if not his mind, that some presences can only be witnessed. That the witnessing is enough. That enough is exactly the word—the word the plateau's people use for sufficiency, for the moment before overflow, for a life that has found its level.

---

I am weathering. This is not loss; it is continuation. The sharp edges I had when I was first placed have softened over generations, the mineral rain of the plateau slowly dissolving my surface and carrying me, particle by particle, back into the water. In some number of years—reef-roof replacements, step-wearings, channel-shifts—I will be noticeably smaller. In some greater number, I will be a suggestion of a stone, a smoothed nub barely breaking the surface. Eventually, I will be indistinguishable from the spring itself, my substance distributed through the basins, flowing over the plateau's undulant surface, seeping back into the rock below.

This is not death. Death is a word for things that were alive, and I was never alive in the way the reeds are alive, in the way Sedna is alive, in the way even the flickering things are alive. I was present, and I am present, and I will continue to be present even when my form has dissolved into formlessness. The spiral carved into me will be the last part to go—the groove is deep, and water follows grooves, reinforcing them even as it erodes them. Long after my mass is gone, there may still be a spiral-shaped disturbance in this part of the spring, a pattern in the water that remembers the stone that made it.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am wrong about persistence. Perhaps the water will forget me as completely as the plateau forgets the forest-creatures that sometimes climb the cliffs, look around at the wet bright strangeness, and climb back down. Perhaps forgetting is also a form of continuation.

---

The light is changing now. Not dimming, not yet—the long slow fade from midday to evening takes hours here, and we are only at the beginning of its beginning. But something in the angle has shifted, some quality of the moisture-scattered sun, and for a moment the spiral on my surface catches fire. Not literal fire. Just light, concentrated, gathered by the groove's geometry into something almost too bright to look at.

No one is looking. Sedna is in her house, weaving. Kettle is elsewhere today, following some other fascination. The Edge-keeper is at his cliffs, watching for something he cannot name. The twins are at their quarry-face, reading stress-lines, preparing to bring forth another stone that might, in time, join me here in the spring.

The brightness fades. The light evens out, returns to its soft diffusion, its luminous lack of shadow. I am again what I usually am: a standing stone among standing stones, a presence without urgency, a thing the water moves around on its way to wherever water goes.

This is enough.

This is exactly enough.
