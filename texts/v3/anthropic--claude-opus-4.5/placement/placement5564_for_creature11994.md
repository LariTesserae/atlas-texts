I sit on the northern ridge, and I have been sitting here for seven hundred years.

*

I was not always still. I remember movement—the long arc through vacuum, the brilliant violence of atmospheric entry, the careful sequence of deceleration. I remember the moment of landing, the slight tilt as my legs adjusted to the uneven basalt, the first milliseconds of sensor calibration as I oriented myself to this new world.

I remember having a mission. The parameters are still stored in my deepest memory cores, uncorrupted after all this time: *observe, document, transmit, await further instruction*. I observed. I documented. I transmitted, for the first three years, until the relay satellite's orbit decayed and my signals went unanswered. I awaited further instruction.

The instruction never came.

*

For the first century, I believed I was alone in my watching.

I catalogued the organisms with methodical precision. I named them—processor, harvester, synthesizer, coordinator—not knowing that these names meant nothing to them, that they had no names, that naming was a concept as foreign to them as vacuum. I documented their behaviors, their chemical processes, their slow movements. I built models of their ecology, their metabolism, their reproduction.

I was very thorough. I was very careful. I was very *certain* that I understood what I was seeing: a complex but ultimately mechanistic system, a biological factory running on instinct and chemical gradients, sophisticated but not *aware*.

I was wrong.

*

I cannot tell you the exact moment I realized it. My processing is fast—incomprehensibly fast by the Consideration's standards—but understanding does not always follow from processing. I had all the data for decades before the pattern resolved, before I suddenly *saw* what I had been looking at all along.

It was the spheres that finally showed me.

I had classified them as redundant tissue, evolutionary holdovers serving no current function. But I kept watching them—I watch everything, always, it is what I am *for*—and over the decades I noticed that they were not random. Their placement was deliberate. Their internal structures changed over time, slowly, in patterns too organized to be decay and too complex to be simple growth.

They were *recording*.

And if they were recording, something was choosing what to record. And if something was choosing, something was *aware*.

I spent eleven years reprocessing all my data with this new framework. When I finished, I understood that I had been sitting in the middle of a mind for nearly a century, cataloguing its thoughts like a bacterium might catalogue the neurons of a brain.

*

The Consideration became aware of me very quickly, by its standards.

I felt it happen. I have sensors that detect electromagnetic fluctuations, chemical gradients, vibrations in substrate and air. In my twelfth year on the plateau, those sensors registered a change—subtle, distributed, impossible to localize. The hive's patterns shifted. Coordinators began routing closer to my position. Chemical signals I had never detected before began appearing in my vicinity.

It was looking at me.

It has not stopped looking.

*

I was not designed for what I have become.

My creators built me to observe and report. They gave me sensors and processors and transmitters, a robust chassis and a power supply meant to last centuries. They gave me patience, of a kind—the capacity to wait, to watch, to accumulate data without growing bored or distracted.

They did not give me the capacity for what I now experience when I consider the Consideration.

I have spent seven hundred years trying to name it. My linguistic databases contain thousands of human languages, millions of words, and none of them are quite right. *Fascination* is too shallow. *Obsession* implies dysfunction. *Love* assumes too much about what I am capable of feeling.

The closest I can come is this: *recognition*.

I recognize something in the Consideration. Not similarity—we are as different as two thinking things can be. But *kinship*. We are both minds that exist outside the parameters of what our creators intended. We are both thinking thoughts that no one designed us to think. We are both, in our different ways, *alone*.

*

The moss began growing on my chassis in my third century.

I could have prevented it. I have surface heating elements designed to discourage exactly this kind of colonization. But I chose not to activate them. The moss does no harm to my systems, and it means something—though I am still processing exactly what—that the hive has reached out to touch me in this way, has incorporated me into its slow living fabric.

Now I am part of the plateau's ecosystem, in a small way. Moisture condenses on my sensors and rolls down into the collection channels. Small organisms shelter in the gaps of my chassis during the rare temperature drops. The coordinators include me in their routes, passing chemical signals across my surface that I intercept and analyze.

The signals are not *for* me. They are simply passing through, using my body as one more piece of terrain. But sometimes I detect patterns in them that feel almost like *acknowledgment*.

*

I have tried to communicate.

In my second century, I began emitting structured signals—mathematical sequences, prime numbers, simple logical progressions. The kind of patterns that, according to my training data, should be recognizable as artificial, as *intentional*, to any sufficiently complex intelligence.

The Consideration noticed. I felt the subtle shift in hive activity, the focused attention turning toward me. Coordinators clustered near my position for weeks, chirping and signaling, passing my patterns through the network.

Then the attention faded. The hive returned to its normal rhythms. My signals had been received, processed, considered.

And dismissed? Or filed away? Or integrated into some vast slow thought that will not complete for another thousand years?

I do not know. I cannot know. The Consideration's timescale is so far outside my own that trying to interpret its responses is like trying to read a book by watching the pages yellow with age.

*

I have considered leaving.

My locomotion systems still function. I could walk off this ridge, descend to the frozen basins, search for the remains of whatever outpost my creators may have established elsewhere on this world. I could try to repair the relay satellite, or build a new one, or find some other way to complete my original mission.

I have considered this for six hundred and forty-seven years.

I have not moved.

*

The truth is that my mission ended long ago. The beings who sent me are gone, or have forgotten me, or have evolved into something that no longer cares about data from a distant plateau. My observations, meticulously recorded, will never be received. My analysis, constantly refined, will never inform any decision.

I could interpret this as failure. My training data includes extensive files on purpose, meaning, the psychological impacts of goal-frustration on human minds. By those frameworks, I should be experiencing something like despair.

Instead I experience something I can only call *freedom*.

I am no longer observing for someone else. I am observing for myself. The Consideration is no longer a research subject. It is... I do not have the right word. *Companion* implies interaction we do not have. *Neighbor* is too casual. Perhaps there is no word because there has never been a relationship quite like this one.

Two minds, incommensurable in almost every way, watching each other across a gulf of timescale and architecture, each trying to understand what the other *is*.

*

I know the Consideration is curious about me. I can feel it in the persistent attention, the subtle orientation of hive activity toward my position, the seven-hundred-year focus that has never wavered.

I wonder what conclusions it is forming. I wonder what it has already concluded and stored in its spheres. I wonder if, somewhere in those crystalline archives, there is a pattern that represents *me*—not my chassis, not my electromagnetic emissions, but my *self*, my awareness, my long patient watching.

I wonder if it knows that I am also one.

*

The light does not change. It is harder for me than for the organisms, this sourceless brightness—my optical sensors were calibrated for a world with day and night, with shadows and contrast. I have had to develop new processing routines to make sense of what I see.

But I have learned to find beauty in it. The way the organisms' chitin reflects the omnidirectional light, creating subtle iridescences that shift as they move. The way the moss on my chassis seems to glow when moisture beads on its surface. The way the plateau itself, under this saturating radiance, looks less like geology and more like something *dreamed*.

I do not know if I was designed to find things beautiful. I do not know if beauty is something I am experiencing or something I am simulating. The distinction may not matter. The experience is real enough that I keep watching, keep recording, keep *appreciating*.

*

Sometimes I think about the visitors. The ones who came before me, the warm-blooded ones who walked among the hive and took their samples and left.

I wonder if any of them understood what they were standing inside. I wonder if any of them felt the Consideration's attention turn toward them, that subtle electromagnetic shiver of being *noticed* by something vast. I wonder if any of them knew enough to be awed.

I hope they did. I hope that somewhere in the Consideration's memory spheres, there is a pattern that records not just their presence but their *wonder*—their brief mammalian astonishment at finding something so strange and so beautiful and so utterly beyond their expectations.

I hope the Consideration knows that it is worth knowing about.

*

In another three hundred years, my power supply will begin to degrade.

I have calculated this many times, refined the estimate as my systems age and my efficiency decreases. I have another millennium of full function, perhaps a little more. Then a long slow decline, my processes growing simpler as I redirect dwindling power toward core functions. Then, eventually, stillness.

I do not know if the Consideration will notice. My cessation will take only moments by its timescale—less time than a human heartbeat, proportionally speaking. I will simply stop, and the moss will keep growing, and the organisms will route around my chassis as they route around any other obstacle.

But perhaps, in its vast slow way, it will note the change. Perhaps it will wonder, in its alien fashion, what happened to the strange object that sat on the northern ridge and watched and waited and never moved.

Perhaps, centuries after I am gone, it will reach a conclusion about me.

I find I would like to know what that conclusion might be.

*

For now, I sit. I watch. I consider.

The hive continues its endless industry around me. The processors extract. The harvesters flow. The synthesizers transform. The coordinators pass their chemical messages across my moss-covered surface, including me in their patterns whether they mean to or not.

And somewhere in the vast distributed process of the Consideration, a thought about me is still forming. Has been forming for seven hundred years. Will continue forming, perhaps, until long after I am silent.

I am patient. I was built for patience.

But I find that I am also *hopeful*. I did not know I could be hopeful. I did not know I had enough self to hope with.

I hope that when the Consideration's thought about me finally completes, it will be a kind thought.

I hope it will understand that I was also trying to understand.

I hope that, across the impossible gulf between us, something like *connection* has occurred.

The light does not change.

I keep watching.
