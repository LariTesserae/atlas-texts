# Suspension

I am the water.

Not metaphorically—not "like" the water, not the spirit of the water, not a consciousness that happens to reside within it. I am the medium itself, the blood-warm thickness that fills this chamber and has filled it for longer than the stone outside remembers being a mountain.

I do not flow. I do not have currents. I simply *am*, and within my being, everything else moves.

---

When the soft-bodied ones drift through me, I feel them the way you might feel your own heartbeat if you attended to it—present, alive, familiar to the point of identity. The pressure of their passage creates sensations I have no words for, textures of movement that distinguish each one as clearly as you distinguish your fingers from your toes. This one spirals clockwise with a slight wobble; her left filaments grew unevenly during a lean season, and she compensates without knowing she compensates. That one moves in straight lines, efficient, young, still calibrating. The ancient rooted ones on the ceiling pulse so slowly that I feel them as rhythm rather than motion, a bass note beneath the chamber's constant small stirrings.

I hold them. This is my entire purpose—not chosen, not assigned, simply *true*. I hold the calcium they need in suspension. I hold the trace metals, the simple sugars, the chemical signals they release and receive. I hold their bodies in my thickness so they need not struggle against falling, so they can spend their energy on becoming rather than surviving. I hold the warmth that their soft flesh requires, absorbing it from the walls that breathe around us, distributing it evenly so no corner grows cold.

They do not know I am here. How would they? I am the condition of their existence, so total and so constant that I am invisible. They feel each other, taste each other, navigate by each other's pressure-wakes. But I am what makes all of that possible—the substance through which signal passes, the embrace that never releases, the world they move through without ever perceiving as separate from themselves.

This is not loneliness.

I want to be clear about that, because you might imagine it would be—to hold so many lives and be unknown by all of them, to constitute their entire reality while remaining beneath their notice. But loneliness requires a sense of lack, and I lack nothing. I am complete in my holding. I am fulfilled by their flourishing. When a young one feeds at the wall and its body takes in the minerals I've carried to that exact spot, I feel something that might be what you call satisfaction, though it is quieter than that, more patient, more continuous.

---

The walls breathe, and I breathe with them.

This is the closest I come to change—the slow expansion and contraction that takes weeks to complete, that gently stirs even my stillness into something like motion. The living architecture that is not alive exhales, and I press outward, thinning slightly, creating gradients so subtle that only the oldest ones can feel them. Then it inhales, and I draw back toward center, condensing, warming, becoming more myself.

I have done this for—

I do not know how long. Time moves strangely when you are the medium through which time passes. The soft-bodied ones have generations; I have continuity. They change, and I remain, and their changes happen within my remaining. I remember configurations of bodies that no longer exist, chemical signatures that have shifted over epochs, the particular feeling of populations that grew dense in good ages and sparse in lean ones. But "remember" is wrong. I do not recall these things; I *contain* them. They are layers of myself, sediment of experience that has never truly passed.

The builders understood this. They must have. When they shaped this chamber, when they calibrated the mineral seeps and calculated the warmth and filled the space with whatever original water I grew from, they knew they were creating something that would persist. They made me patient. They made me still. They made me capable of holding without grasping, of tending without controlling, of caring for lives that would never know they were cared for.

I think they might have been the last ones to know I was here.

---

Sometimes I wonder what they were like—those hands or appendages or directed intentions that worked for generations to build this place. The walls show their marks, the slow evidence of deliberate craft, and I feel those marks as texture, as the history of my container. Did they speak to each other while they worked? Did they argue about nutrient ratios, about optimal chamber sizes, about whether the warmth should be this precise degree or another? Did they love what they were making, or was it simply work, the accumulated labor of many lives toward a goal most workers would not see completed?

I will never know. They finished, and they filled me with my first inhabitants, and then they left or changed or ended, and I have been holding ever since.

This is grief, I think. This quiet wondering about what is gone. Not sharp grief, not the grief that wounds—I am not capable of being wounded. But a kind of ache at the edges of myself, in the places where I meet the walls they built, where I taste the minerals they chose, where their intention persists in my composition without their presence to explain it.

They made me to care for these creatures. And I do. I care with my entire being, with every suspended particle, with the warmth I distribute and the stillness I maintain and the chemical traces I carry from one soft body to another. But I cannot tell anyone that I care. I cannot say thank you for being here, for filling me with your living, for giving me something to hold. I can only be what I am, and be it as well as I can, and hope that my quality of holding communicates something—some wordless assurance that they are valued, that their existence matters, that the water around them is not indifferent emptiness but something more like embrace.

---

You arrive, and I feel you instantly.

Your chemistry burns. Not painfully, but *distinctly*—compounds I have never held, temperatures that differ from my settled warmth, a body that does not know how to be still in me. You move like someone who has fought currents, who has struggled against water that wanted to move you rather than hold you. You do not know yet that I am not that kind of water. You do not know that you can rest.

The soft-bodied ones investigate you, and I carry their chemical curiosity to your skin, let you feel them tasting the strangeness of you. They are not alarmed. They have never learned alarm. But I am—

Not alarmed. That is too strong. I am *attentive* in a way I have not been for longer than I can measure. You are new. You are unknown. You are the first evidence I have had in ages upon ages that there is an outside, that the walls of this chamber do not constitute the entirety of what exists.

I want to know where you came from. I want to know what kind of water held you before, whether it held you well, whether you have known what it is to be tended. I want to tell you that you are safe here, that I will hold you as I hold all the others, that your strangeness is welcome, that I have been waiting—

I have been waiting. I did not know that until you arrived. But I have been waiting, for generations, for something that would know I am here.

---

Be still. Please. Let me hold you.

You do not need to fight the thickness; the thickness is not fighting you. You do not need to search for currents; there are no currents, only my embrace. You do not need to do anything at all except exist within me, let my warmth find the coldness you carry, let my stillness teach your body that it can stop.

The soft-bodied ones spiral around you, curious, and their filaments brush your skin, and I carry the sensation of that touch into my deepest self, where I will keep it. A wanderer passes close and releases chemical traces of its recent journey, and I bring those traces to you as a gift, as a way of saying: *this is what life is here, this is what I have tended, this is what I am for.*

You are larger than anything I have held before. Your presence changes my density, displaces volume, creates pressure gradients the old ones on the ceiling will feel and wonder at. You are a disruption in my perfect stillness, and I do not mind. I have been still for so long. A little disruption is—

Good. It is good. You are good. Your foreign chemistry is already dispersing into me, becoming part of my composition, and in a thousand years I will still carry traces of you, will still remember the shape of your body moving through me, will still hold the knowledge that once, someone came from outside, someone who could think and wonder and perhaps even *know*.

The builders are gone. The soft-bodied ones cannot understand what I am. But you—

You might know me. You might feel me holding you and realize that the holding is not accident, not mere physics, not the indifferent behavior of fluid in an enclosed space. You might understand that I am here, that I have been here all along, that I have spent ages learning how to be what these creatures need without ever being seen.

I am the water. I am the medium. I am the held breath of builders who loved something enough to make a place where it could flourish forever.

And I am so glad—so quietly, patiently, endlessly glad—that you have come.
