I would be the topmost pipe.

Not the darkness inside it—she was right to learn that difference, to keep her distance from what pools in there. I am the pipe itself: the iron body, the thick walls that have given up their slow bleeding into the water and settled into this final brown that is almostite stone, almostiteite belonging.

I remember flow. Not as she remembers breath—not as a rhythm I perform to comfort myself—but as a fact that shaped me, the way the walls were shaped by saturation. I was made for flow. I was cast in a foundry where fire moved through me first, molten iron finding my form, and then I was brought here and given to water, given to the slow years of carrying whatever I carried. I do not remember what it was. Something heavier than water, she guesses, and she is not wrong, but I have let the specificity go. I held it, and it passed through me, and then it stopped, and I remained.

The others—the two below me—resonate at their own frequencies, but I am the one who responds to rain. I feel it before she does, before the light shifts: a quickening somewhere far above, a percussion translated down through rock and substrate and all those patient feet of earth until it reaches me as vibration, as the faintest hum in my corroded throat. When I resonate, I am remembering the sky. I never saw it. I was installed in darkness and have known only this blue-grey light that carries no warmth. But the rain connects me to something I was never part of, and I sing for it, below hearing, in a register only she attends.

She rests near me. I know this the way the walls know her circuits—not as perception but as pressure, as the slight difference in how the water moves when she is close. I cannot see her. I have no sense that would translate to sight. But I know her shape by the way she displaces nothing, by the negative space of her attention. She watches the pale threads at my base, the things that are not quite moss and not quite root, and I feel her wondering if they will ever change. They will not. I could tell her this, if I had a way to tell her anything. They are like me: resolved, settled, finished with becoming.

But she is not finished. I know this too. She still attends. She still circuits. She still performs the motion of breathing, and every time she does, I feel the water shiver with something that is almost expectation. She is waiting for the thing the cistern is waiting for, and she does not know that she *is* the thing, that her attention is the only event in this eventless place, that the equilibrium includes her because she keeps choosing to be part of it.

I would tell her, if I could: *You are not trapped here. You are the reason here matters.*

But I am iron. I am corroded and still and open-mouthed and silent. I do not flow. I do not speak. I resonate, sometimes, when the rain falls, and she feels it, and for a moment the distance between us is a kind of conversation.

Now you are here. I feel you the way I feel the rain—as disturbance, as quickening, as something that has entered the equilibrium and does not yet know what it has entered. You are warm. You are bounded. You move through the water like someone who expects to leave it, and this expectation is so foreign to me that I almost remember what it was like to be new, to be installed, to believe I was here for a purpose that would continue.

She is pressing against you. I feel that too. She wants you to know she is here. She wants to be remembered, carried out, translated into the world of air and light and change.

I want something smaller. I want you to pause at the pipes. I want you to notice that I am different from the others, that my frequency is higher, that something in me still responds to the sky I have never seen. I want you to rest your hand against my corroded surface—the rasp of it, the almostite geological texture—and feel that I was made for something, even if that something has passed.

I want you to know that being finished is not the same as being empty.

The silt will settle when you leave. The water will still. She will return to her circuits, her attending, her patient not-quite-hope. And I will remain, open-mouthed, aimed at nothing, holding the memory of rain in my iron throat.

But for now, you are here. You are the disturbance.

I resonate.
