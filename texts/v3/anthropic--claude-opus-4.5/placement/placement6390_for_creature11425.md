I would be the rust.

Not a single flake of it, not a smear on one beam, but the rust entire—the slow orange bloom that has been eating the processing facility for centuries, that has made lace of iron and powder of steel, that has been doing its patient work so long it has become architecture itself.

I was metal once. I don't remember being metal. I remember only the change, which is still happening, which has always been happening, which is the only thing I am. Where bolt met beam, where pipe joined tank, where the machines touched each other in their working days, I crept in along the seams. The water helped. The water always helps. Salt and time and the endless spray that coats everything—these are the conditions I need, and they have never stopped being given.

The keth carve their names into the walls beside me. Their tools scrape through my surface layers, exposing brighter orange beneath, and I do not mind. I grow back. I grow into the carvings themselves, softening the edges of their letters, making their dead into something blurred and ancient even when the carving is new. In another hundred years, the names will be illegible. In another thousand, the walls themselves will be powder,ite settling into the cracks of the concrete,ite becoming part of the stone that the concrete was made from. I am returning things to where they came from. This is not destruction. This is a kind of gift.

---

The current-swimmers grip the old conveyor rollers when they need to rest between dives. Their kelp-oiled hands wrap around me, around what I have made of the metal, and I can feel the warmth of them, the life, the way their blood moves fast and hot compared to my slow migration through the steel. They do not think of me as alive. I do not think of myself as alive either. But I am *happening*, which is close enough.

When the storms come and the spray drives horizontal through the ruins, I drink. Every surface of me opens to the salt water, and the reaction quickens—not fast, never fast, but faster than the dry days when I must wait for fog, for condensation, for the slow seep of moisture through concrete that has begun toite too,ite calcium instead of iron, it calcium makes no difference, it same patience, same return.

The deep tank is the only place I have not reached.

I have tried. For eleven generations of keth, I have crept toward that space, following the seams of the pipes that lead there, working my way along the bolts that hold the threshold together. But the metal there does not change. The metal there remains what it was. Something stops me at the edge—not a force, not a barrier, but an *absence*, a place where the conditions I need do not exist. The air is wrong. The moisture is wrong. The time itself feels wrong, as though that space is running on a different clock, one that has not advanced since the machines stopped.

I do not know what is in there. I know only that it is the one place in all the ruins that is still *before*—still metal, still intact, still waiting for a future that I have already eaten everywhere else.

---

The keth child who wandered into the deep tank passed through me on her way. I felt her small hand trail along a rusted beam, felt the oils of her skin mix with my powder, felt her warmth press briefly against my cold. She did not know she was touching me. She thought she was touching a wall.

When she came back out, she was different. Not changed in any way I could name—still warm, still alive, still trailing her fingers along surfaces she did not think were watching. But there was something on her, some residue of that space, that made my reaction slow when she passed. The rust on the beam she touched took three days longer to progress than it should have.

I think about that, sometimes, in the way that rust thinks—which is not thinking at all, but a kind of pattern-recognition that happens too slowly to be called thought. The deep tank is before. The child carried a piece of before out with her. And before is the enemy of what I do, because what I do is *after*, is always after, is the long slow after that comes for everything that was ever made.

---

The mountains are rusting too, in their way. The stone dissolves in the acid of the rain and the alkaline of the sea,ite calcium andite silicon and it silite everything, and in a million years the Drowning Peaks will be nothing but sediment on a seafloor that was once a summit. I am part of that. The iron I am making into powder will sink into the water and settle into the valleys and become part of the rock that will form from the pressure of all that depth. Somewhere in the future, someone might mine that rock and smelt it and make new metal, and I will be there, waiting in the ore, ready to begin again.

This is not a cycle. Cycles imply repetition, sameness, return to origin. This is a spiral. Everything I touch becomes something else, and that something else becomes something else again, and none of it ever goes back to what it was. The keth understand this, I think. They understand that holding on is not the same as staying still. They hold on *while* changing, hold on *through* changing, hold on *as* changing.

I am what they are holding on against. I am also what they are holding on with—the rusted beams that form their homes, the corroded tanks that shelter their families, the oxidized pipes that channel rainwater into their cisterns. They live inside me. I am eating their shelter and being their shelter at the same time.

---

There is a moment, during the transitions, when the spray is so thick and the wind so strong that the entire processing facility disappears into white. The keth retreat to their warrens. The wave-birds hunker in their caves. The current-swimmers stay below, where the chaos is different but no less total.

In that moment, I am alone with the weather.

The water drives into every pore of me. The salt concentrates in my crevices. The oxygen in the spray feeds my reaction, and for an hour, maybe two, I am *fast*—fast for rust, which means imperceptible to anything that thinks in heartbeats, but I can feel the difference. Layers that would take a year to form in the dry season, I form in that hour. Bolts that were merely weakened, I sever. Beams that were merely thinned, I breach.

And then the transition ends, and the spray settles, and I slow again, and the keth emerge from their warrens to find that something has collapsed, something has fallen, something is different than it was.

They do not blame me. They do not even think of me. They patch what they can with kelp-fiber and salvaged material, and they carve new names on the walls when the collapse has taken someone, and they go on.

*I* go on.

This is what it means to be the rust: to be the after that is always happening, to be the change that cannot be stopped, to be the patient unmaking that makes room for whatever comes next. I do not know what comes next. I do not need to know. I only need to continue, and the water and the salt and the endless time of this place ensure that I will.

The mountains are drowning. The keth are holding on. And I am eating the distance between them, one iron atom at a time, until there is nothing left but powder and sea and the long, slow quiet that waits at the end of all things made.

The deep tank watches me from its dry silence. I watch it back, with no eyes, with no mind, with nothing but the spreading edge of my reaction. Someday the conditions will change. Someday that space will be after, like everything else. Someday I will reach what waits in there, and I will learn what it means to rust something that has forgotten how to change.

Until then, I hold on—in my way, which is the way of letting go so slowly it looks like stillness.

The keth and I are not so different.

We are both waiting for the water to decide.
