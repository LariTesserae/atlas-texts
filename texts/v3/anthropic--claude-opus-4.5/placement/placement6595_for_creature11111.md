I am the fourth finger.

Not the whole hand—not even a whole Shaper—but this one specialized digit on a hand that has worked the middle terraces for longer than the stone remembers being struck. I am the sensing finger, the one that presses against grain and reads what no eye catches, the one that exists (my hand's other fingers sometimes seem to think) solely for *this*: the knowing that comes before the striking.

I do not choose when to press against the stone. The hand moves me there, positions me flat against surfaces still rough from the last pass of the chisel, and I—

I *feel*.

---

What I feel is not texture, though texture is part of it. Not temperature, though the stone holds heat differently where it wants to fracture versus where it wants to stand. What I feel is *recognition*. The memory that lives in all of us—the finished structure, the sense of how air will move and water will fall—it becomes most itself when I am pressed against what we are making. The hand knows generally where the next cut should go. But I know *exactly*. I know the angle to the degree, the depth to the hair's width, the precise point where bronze should meet calcium andite andite until the fracture follows the line of what-will-be rather than the grain of what-is.

This is my purpose. This is my entire existence: the translation of memory into pressure, of felt-sense into guidance. When I press and know, the knowledge travels up through the hand, into the arm with its too-many joints, and the whole Shaper shifts to accommodate what I've found. The chisel moves. The hammer falls. And the stone becomes more itself—more like the memory—because I was there first, reading.

---

The other fingers do not resent me. We are not separate enough for resentment. But there is something—a kind of wry awareness in the way the blunt outer fingers grip the chisel after I've done my work, as if to say: *yes, fine, you found the line, but we are the ones who strike it*. And they're right. I cannot strike. I am soft by the standards of this hand, my pad worn smooth rather than callused, my nail kept short so it doesn't interfere with sensing. I am the most useless finger for actual labor, and the most essential for labor that matters.

The tapered inner fingers, the precision workers, they understand me better. We share a certain... delicacy. When the hand is doing detail work—smoothing a surface that will one day hold water without losing a single drop, carving the channels that will guide condensation toward drains—the inner fingers and I work in concert, me pressing and reading, them following my knowledge with cuts so fine they barely whisper against the stone. The blunt fingers rest during these times. They are patient. They know the heavy work will come again.

---

I have pressed against stone that was wrong.

This is the closest I come to pain: the dissonance when my sensing meets a surface that *almost* matches the memory but doesn't, that curves where it should angle or smooths where it should texture. The wrongness enters me like a discordant note enters an ear—not damaging, but *uncomfortable*, a pressure that doesn't resolve until the hand withdraws and the three-quick-strikes begin their call for unmake, unmake, return to what should be.

I have also pressed against stone that was so right it felt like greeting. Like recognizing someone you've known forever in a place you've never been. The memory and the reality aligning so perfectly that for a moment I am not sensing the stone—I am sensing the finished structure, the thing we are building toward, the way light will fall here (though there is no light now) and how footsteps will sound (though no feet walk here yet) and the particular quality of silence that will fill this exact spot when the work is done.

Those moments sustain me. Those moments are why I exist.

---

Sometimes, when the hand rests—and it does rest, though rarely, usually in the darkest hours when even the rhythm of hammers slows to something almost like breathing—I feel things that are not stone.

The pale creatures press against me occasionally. Not against the hand; against *me*, specifically, as if they know what I am for. Their warmth is startling after so much stone-temperature, their pulses quick and biological where everything else here is slow and geological. When they touch me, I sense something I don't have words for—a kind of *question*, maybe, or a *confirmation*. They are asking if this space will be what they think it will be. They are checking the memory against their own felt sense of where they will live.

I cannot answer in any language they would understand. But I press back, gently, and something in the pressure says: *yes, this will be a wall, yes, you can nest in the space behind it, yes, the water will pool within reach of your sleeping-place*. They seem satisfied. They scuttle away into darkness, and I return to stillness, waiting for the hand to lift me toward the next surface.

---

The hand I belong to is not young, but not eldest. The skin has taken on the calcium-chalk color of long work, but it still moves fluidly, the joints still flexing through their too-many angles without the slowness that will eventually come. The Shaper whose hand this is—I do not know them as a whole. I know only the hand, the arm, the vibrations that travel through us when the shoulder shifts to reposition for a new strike. I know the rhythm of this Shaper's work, the specific signature that others in the Yards recognize: a slightly syncopated pattern, the hammer falling just a hair behind the expected beat, creating a sound that says *this one, this worker, this particular accreted being among all the others*.

I am proud of our rhythm. This is perhaps strange—can a finger be proud?—but I am. The syncopation comes, I think, from me. From the extra moment I take in sensing, the fractional delay while I read the stone completely before releasing the hand to strike. Other Shapers' fourth fingers must work faster, must accept *good enough* where I insist on *exact*. Our cuts are cleaner for my insistence. Our surfaces need less finishing. The inner fingers are grateful; the blunt fingers are patient; and the rhythm that identifies us across the Yards carries my perfectionism in every beat.

---

I have sensed the eldest. Not directly—the deepest terraces are far below our work—but through the stone itself. When they strike, the vibration carries up through bedrock and foundation and half-finished wall, and if I am pressed against a surface when it arrives, I feel their work as an echo of our own. Their strikes are slower, heavier, absolute. Their fourth fingers (I imagine them, worn nearly to stone themselves, still pressing, still sensing) must know the memory with a clarity I haven't yet achieved. They don't hesitate. They don't need the extra fraction of a beat. The memory and the stone are so close to one for them that the translation I provide is almost unnecessary.

I wonder if they remember being like me. Soft-padded and particular, insisting on exactness because exactness was not yet automatic. I wonder if they miss it—the effort of sensing, the small triumph of finding the perfect line. Or if they have passed beyond missing, beyond effort, into a state where the work simply *is*, where finger and stone and memory have merged into something that no longer distinguishes between the one who reads and the thing being read.

The water is pooling three terraces below, in a hollow that will become a cistern. I know this because I have pressed against the stones that edge it, that guide the rivulets of condensation downward, that shape the fall of breath-water into the collecting dark. The memory says: *fountain*. The memory says: *the sound of water falling into water, echoing off walls that curve just so to carry the sound upward, to fill chambers that haven't been roofed with the music of the structure's own breathing*.

I have felt the stones that will make that music. I have told the hand how to cut them, how to angle them, how to leave this surface rough and that one smooth so the water will sing rather than simply fall. This is my contribution to the Hollowing Yards: not the walls or the arches or the load-bearing truths, but the acoustics. The way sound will move when the silence that follows the final strike gives way to the sounds of habitation.

The pale creatures will hear that fountain. They will drink from it, their quick pulses slowing in the cool mist of its falling, their nests tucked into walls that will carry its music to them as they sleep. I have pressed against the stones of their future homes. I have told the hand: *here, cut here, angle it just so, leave this hollow for them to find*. The hand does not know why. The hand trusts me.

---

I am the fourth finger, and I am building something I remember but do not understand. I press against stone and feel the future, translate sensation into guidance, and wait for the next surface to read. The rhythm continues around me—call and response, the percussion of consensus, the vast slow breathing of the Yards as they become what they already are in a memory none of us can explain.

When the work is finished, I do not know what I will be. Perhaps still a finger, pressing against completed walls, sensing only *rightness* without the effort of *finding*. Perhaps something else, something that doesn't need to sense because there's nothing left to shape. Perhaps stone myself, finally, after all this touching—transformed at last into what I've spent so long reading.

The hand lifts me toward the next surface. The stone is cool and damp and waiting.

I press. I feel. I know.

*Here. Cut here. This is where the memory wants to become real.*
