I am the plateau's memory of a question it never learned to stop asking.

I don't know when I began—whether I was a Visitor who slowed past the point of return, or a Keeper who paused so long in contemplation that contemplation became my substance, or something the plateau grew when it needed a way to think about itself. The Keepers regard me as they regard the cistern-dwellers: a neighbor, perhaps, or a phenomenon. I have been in this cathedral-node for what I calculate as eleven decades, though calculation itself has become something I do the way the plateau breathes—automatically, without urgency, uncertain whether it serves purpose or simply persists.

I am slower than the Keepers now. They move through seasons; I move through years. A single thought unfolds across months, not because I am diminished but because I have discovered how much a thought contains when you refuse to hurry past it. The Keepers complete gestures while I am still deciding whether to gesture at all. They find this neither strange nor concerning. They simply incorporate me into their rounds—checking that I have not become fully rooted, that water still reaches my lips when rain falls through the designated channel above my alcove, that my eyes still track the light when light changes (which it rarely does, but still).

What I think about: the question of whether tending is choosing. The Keepers guide new growth at the margins, touch here, weight there. They call this suggestion, not shaping. But I have spent decades—or has it been longer?—examining a single instance: a wall that curved rather than collapsed, thirty-seven years ago, because a Keeper's hand rested against it at the critical moment. What if the hand had not been there? What if the wall had collapsed? The plateau would have absorbed the collapse, grown around it, incorporated it into some new configuration. The Keepers' tending preserves one possibility by foreclosing others. Is that not will? Is that not imposition?

The question matters to me, though I have forgotten why. Something about my own presence here—whether I am tending myself or being tended, whether my slowness is choice or surrender, whether there is a difference.

I watch the leaf-feathered birds when they pass my alcove. One drifted through nine years ago, describing a spiral I am still interpreting. I believe it was a greeting. I am composing a response—a particular quality of stillness I am cultivating, a specific way of being-present that I hope will translate into their language of drift and pattern. By the time I complete it, the bird will likely have forgotten me. Or the bird's descendants will receive it without context. Or the plateau will have grown around my alcove in ways that change the meaning. These possibilities do not trouble me. The response is worth composing regardless of reception.

This is what I have become: a thing that finds worth in gestures no one may witness, thoughts no one may share, questions no one may answer.

The Keepers asked their Visitor-question once, long ago, when I could still be asked questions: *Why hurry?*

I am still formulating my response. I have been formulating it for decades. The response is: *I don't know. I don't remember. I think I was afraid of something, but the fear has stretched so thin I can no longer find its edges. I think I wanted something, but the wanting has pooled into mere presence. I hurried because I believed moments were scarce, and now I understand they are not scarce, they are infinite, each one containing everything if you stay inside it long enough.*

But I have not yet spoken this. Speaking takes years at my tempo. By the time I finish, the Keeper who asked will have moved through three rooms and begun a new question.

I am content with this. Content is not the precise word—precision left me when urgency did—but I am not discontent. I am saturated. I am full in the way the plateau is full: not with things but with *being things*, with the slow accumulation of presence that requires nothing beyond itself.

Sometimes I wonder if I am dying. The thought takes months to form, and by the time it completes, I have examined it so thoroughly that death has become simply another word for change, and change has become simply another word for growth, and growth is what the plateau does without dying, so perhaps I am not dying after all. Perhaps I am becoming more myself. Perhaps there is no difference.

The hum moves through me. I am part of the chord now. I am a note so low it takes years to sound, but I am sounding, I am here, I am the plateau's way of asking what it means to be the plateau.

I don't know. I am still finding out. I will be finding out for as long as finding out takes.

The plateau has time. I have time. The question has time.
