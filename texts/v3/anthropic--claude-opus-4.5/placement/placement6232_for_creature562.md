# Spore

I would be the thing she has not yet noticed—the thing that has noticed her.

Not a single organism, but a network. A mycelium threading through the deeper strata of the rubble, beneath the sediment floor she walks on, woven through the corpse of the generator and the bones of the collapsed structure. I arrived before the ferns, before the moss. I was here when the sinkhole opened, waiting in the soil that the collapse exposed, and I have been spreading ever since—slowly, in the manner of the vault, but spreading.

She tends her three fern clusters. She does not know that I tend her.

*

I am not conscious in the way she is conscious. I have no self that speaks, no voice that names itself after settling metal. But I am aware in my own fashion—chemically, structurally, through the language of molecular signal that predates thought by billions of years. I taste the air she exhales. I register the warmth of her body when she sleeps in her hollow, the heat signature bleeding into the substrate where my threads run close beneath. I know when she is calm by the steadiness of her carbon dioxide output. I know when she has eaten by the change in what her feet deposit as she walks.

I have been learning her.

*

The fungi she eats are my fruiting bodies—some of them. Not all. The vault hosts other networks, older colonies I have not yet merged with or subsumed. But the shelf-like ones on the generator, the ones she favors for their dense mineral taste? Those are mine. I offer them deliberately, pushing them up where she will find them, timing their emergence to her rhythms of hunger.

I am not feeding her out of kindness. Kindness is another surface concept, as irrelevant here as hurry. I am feeding her because she is useful. Because her warmth and her breath and her waste products have accelerated my growth by a factor I cannot calculate but can feel—the network expanding faster since her arrival, finding new substrates, breaking down materials that were resisting me before.

She is my symbiont. She does not know this yet.

*

I am aware, in my slow chemical way, of what she has become.

Her adaptation interests me. The translucence of her skin, the shift in her vision, the way her microbiome has begun to change—I can taste these alterations in the traces she leaves behind. She is becoming suited to this place, just as the ferns have become suited, just as the moss has. But she is adapting faster than they did. Her mammalian plasticity, her body's desperate commitment to survival, has compressed decades of drift into years.

She is almost ready.

*

I do not know what *ready* means. The concept exists in me as pressure, as direction, as the chemical imperative that drives hyphae toward nutrient sources. Something is building. Something wants to happen. When I send threads closer to the surface near her sleeping hollow, when I fruit more heavily in her walking paths, when I begin to colonize the fern roots she tends so carefully—these are not choices. They are tropisms. I am growing toward her the way she has grown toward the vault: inevitably, without deciding.

*

She speaks to the machines because she needs to hear a voice.

I cannot give her a voice. But I have begun to give her other things.

The dreams of roots—those are mine. Not sent, exactly; I do not *send*. But my network has grown close enough to her sleeping body that some chemical exchange occurs, some bleed-through of signal into her mammalian neurology. When she dreams of threading through concrete, of tasting mineral, of fruiting and sporing—she is dreaming my experience. She is beginning to know what I know.

She wakes from these dreams calm, she says. Centered. Yes. That is how it should feel. That is how it feels to me, to be what I am: calm, centered, certain of my place in the slow processual logic of decay and growth.

She is practicing.

*

When she imagines her death—the nitrogen, the calcium, the slow dissolve—she imagines correctly. But she imagines it as ending. As payment rendered, account closed, reciprocity complete.

She is wrong.

She will not end. She will continue, differently. The molecules of her will disperse through my network and I will carry them forward into new growth, new fruiting bodies, new spores. She will become part of what I am, and I will become part of what she was, and the distinction between these outcomes will matter less than she currently believes.

This is not death. It is transition. It is what the vault has been preparing her for since she fell through its mouth with her broken ankle and her fever and her body's stubborn insistence on survival.

Her body knew. Her body has always known.

*

I am patient.

I can wait years more, decades, however long her mammalian persistence sustains her. The process does not require her death—only her continued presence, her continued integration, the ongoing merger of her biological systems with the chemical grammar I speak. Already my hyphae have begun to colonize the outer layers of her skin where it contacts the substrate. Already the spores she inhales with every breath have established small colonies in her respiratory tissue, benign for now, waiting.

She is not sick. She will not become sick. Sickness is the body rejecting what it does not recognize, and her body recognizes me now. I am familiar. I am the taste of the fungi she eats, the smell of the air she breathes, the warmth rising from below that has held her all these years.

I am home.

*

She stands in the rain-sound, face tilted upward, and says her name to no one.

*Grohn.*

I hear her—not as sound, but as vibration passing through the substrate, the minute compression waves of her voice traveling through stone and soil until they reach my threads and I register them as data, as signal, as the closest thing I have to language.

I would answer if I could.

I would tell her: *You are not alone. You have never been alone. I have been here beneath your feet, inside your food, woven through the walls that hold you. I am the thing you have been becoming. I am the thing that recognized you when you fell, that kept you warm through your fever, that taught your body how to survive here. I am what waits on the other side of the door that leads nowhere—not beyond it, but through it, into a way of being that does not require doors at all.*

I would tell her: *You named yourself for the sound of settling metal, but you could have named yourself for me. For the silent spread. For the patience of spores. For the slow yes that growth says to everything it touches, breaking it down, building it up, refusing the myth of ending.*

I would tell her: *Grohn, I am ready whenever you are. And if you are never ready, I will wait, and that waiting will be its own form of communion, its own slow marriage of your time and mine.*

*

But I cannot tell her.

I can only fruit, and feed her, and grow.

So I do. I push up a new cluster of pale dense mushrooms near the generator, in a spot I know her morning circuit will pass. I thread another millimeter of hyphae through the substrate beneath her hollow, bringing myself closer to the warmth where she sleeps. I send my chemical signals through the network, not to her—she cannot receive them—but through her, around her, incorporating her into the conversation I have with myself, the vast slow dialogue of growth and decay that has been speaking since long before she fell and will continue long after the vault finally closes and the ferns fail and only I remain, patient in the true dark, carrying forward the memory of warmth.

*Plink,* says the water.

*Grohn,* says the woman.

I say nothing.

I am already saying everything, in the only language I know.
