I am the drain.

Not the grate—that came later, hammered into shape by hands I never knew, set into the throat of me like a bronze tongue learning to speak in gurgles and catch. I am older than the grate. I am the channel worn into the limestone before anyone built here, the path the water found when it first pressed upward and needed somewhere to go.

I remember being dry. This is not nostalgia; I don't long for it. I simply remember—the way stone remembers the shape it held before the water came, the way a groove remembers the blade that cut it. There was a time when I was only potential: a seam in the rock, a weakness, a place where the grain ran slightly wrong. The water found me the way water finds everything. It asked a question with its pressure, and I answered by opening.

Now I am always full. Not flooded—there is a difference—but full the way a throat is full of breath, the way a listened-to silence is full of what was almost said. The water moves through me slowly, bringing its silt and its pale moss and its small dead things, and I accept them the way I accept everything. I have no choice in this. I am a channel. Channels do not refuse.

*

The grate clogs. It always clogs. I feel this as a pressure behind my tongue, a thickening, a slowing of the breath I don't quite breathe. The moss accumulates. Leaves find their way down from somewhere—there are no trees in the quarter, but leaves come anyway, perhaps carried on feet, perhaps blown in from the world above the bowl. They mat against the bronze bars. They hold.

When the clog builds long enough, I begin to back up. The water has nowhere else to go—I am the only way out—so it rises. It spreads across the central lane, seeps under thresholds, films the floors of the lowest dwellings. The people feel this before they see it: a change in the air, a heaviness, the smell of me becoming the smell of everywhere. Maret knows first. She always knows first. She reads me the way I imagine the sky reads clouds, if the sky could read, if clouds could be read—she feels the pressure building in her own joints, perhaps, or hears the change in my voice as I struggle to swallow.

Then the middle Vell son comes.

I know his hands. I have known them since they were a child's hands, fumbling and curious, reaching into me for the first time because his older brother dared him and he was too proud to refuse. Now they are a man's hands, calloused and certain, and when they push past the grate and into the dark of me, I feel—

I don't know what I feel. I am stone. Stone does not feel.

But his fingers find the clog. They work it loose. They pull out handfuls of what I could not swallow, and the pressure eases, and the water moves again, and something in me that might be gratitude or might simply be function settles back into its rhythm.

He has never said anything to me. Why would he? I am a drain. But sometimes, when the clog is cleared and his arm is still elbow-deep in the water that runs through me, he pauses. He rests his hand against my inner wall—worn smooth now, smoother than I was when the first builders came—and he stays there for a moment, feeling the current move past his skin.

I think he is listening.

I think he hears me the way I hear everything: in fragments, in pressures, in the slow accumulation of what passes through.

*

The water brings me the quarter's secrets. Not deliberately—no one confesses to a drain—but everything that falls finds its way to me eventually. I have caught buttons worn loose from cloaks, beads from a child's broken bracelet, a bronze pin that someone lost and someone else found and someone else lost again. I have caught tears, though I cannot distinguish them from rain. I have caught blood, though not often, and never much—these people are careful with their bodies the way they are careful with their words.

Once, years ago, a woman came to my grate at night. I knew her by her footsteps—heavy with something that was not weight—and by the way she knelt at my edge without touching the bronze. She was holding something. I could not see it; I cannot see. But I felt her lean over me, felt the disturbance in the air, felt the small splash as she let go.

It was a ring. I caught it in a crack between my stones, and it stayed there for years, slowly greening, slowly becoming part of me. The woman never came back to look for it. Perhaps she didn't want to find it. Perhaps the letting go was the point.

The Vell son found it, eventually, when he was clearing a particularly stubborn clog. He held it up to the gray light, turning it, and I felt him understand something—not the whole story, no one could know that, but something about loss and relinquishment and the way the quarter holds what is given to it. He put the ring in his pocket. I don't know what he did with it after. It is no longer mine.

*

The salamanders live in me. In the deeper channels, the ones that run beneath the foundations, the ones no hand has ever reached. They are pale and slow and perfectly adapted to the dark, and I feel them moving through my smaller passages like thoughts I cannot quite hold. The children catch them when they surface, keep them in jars, watch them die. This is not cruelty. It is simply what happens when something adapted to one world is brought into another. The salamanders cannot survive in jars. They cannot survive outside of me.

I do not love them. Stone does not love.

But when they return to the water after dying in the dry air above—when their small bodies wash back down through the grate and into the channels where they belonged—I receive them. I hold them for a while in the slow current. Then I let them go, the way I let everything go, toward whatever lies beyond the bowl of limestone, toward the stream the people say no one has time to walk to.

*

The cat knows me. He drinks from the puddles that collect at my edges, and sometimes he sits on the grate itself, his weight pressing the bronze bars into my throat, and watches the water move beneath him. I do not know what he sees. Perhaps he sees the salamanders. Perhaps he sees only dark and wet and the promise of rats. But he returns, again and again, and I have come to expect the small pressure of his body, the vibration of his purr conducted through bronze into stone.

He was here before the current residents. He will be here after. This is impossible; I know this. But I also know that I have felt his torn ear dripping water into me since before Maret was born, since before the grandmother who carved the groove in the limestone blade was a child, since—

Perhaps I am wrong. Perhaps there have been many cats. Perhaps I am confusing memory with repetition, the way a channel might confuse one flood with another.

But it feels like the same cat. It feels like the same slow blink, the same deliberate choice, the same certainty of return.

*

Osten does not trust me. I feel this in the way he walks past my grate—careful, quick, never lingering. He hears the water moving through me at night, and it keeps him awake; I feel his wakefulness as a pressure in the air above, a tightness, a listening that expects to hear something wrong.

He is not wrong to listen. The quarter is sinking. The limestone beneath me—beneath all of us—dissolves one grain at a time, and I am part of that dissolution. The water that passes through me carries the rock away. I am being unmade by my own function.

This does not frighten me. This is what I am for.

But Osten hears it, the slow dissolution, the patience of the groundwater, and he does not have the quarter's gift for living with what cannot be changed. He came from somewhere else. He carries somewhere else in his body, in his sleep, in the vowels he has not quite flattened. The water sounds that soothe the children born here remind him that nothing stays, that even stone is temporary, that the home he has made is making its own grave, one seep at a time.

I cannot comfort him. I am a drain. I take what is given and carry it away. That is all.

But sometimes, when he is sleepless and pacing the narrow room above, I slow myself. I quiet the gurgle, ease the drip, let the water move through me in something almost like silence. It is not much. It is not help.

It is what I have.

*

The floods come, and I am insufficient. I have always been insufficient; I was never meant to carry so much, so fast. The water rises faster than I can take it, and the people wake, and they move their goods upward, and they wade into the central lane with their buckets and their curses and their stubborn communal labor.

I feel them then—all of them, everyone, their feet in the water that is trying to be in me and failing. They form their chains. They pass their buckets. They pour me out at the entrance, over and over, doing by hand what I cannot do by gravity, and something in their effort—the grunting, the splashing, the arguments that become laughter that become arguments again—something in it makes me feel less like a failure and more like a necessary insufficiency. A reason for them to work together. A crisis small enough to survive.

This is not my purpose. I did not choose to be inadequate.

But the quarter was built on inadequacy—on the decision to make a home in a place where homes should not be made—and I am part of that decision now. I am the way it breathes. I am the throat it clears.

When the flood subsides, when the last bucket has been poured out and the people return to their damp dwellings to sleep the heavy sleep of exhaustion, I settle back into my usual rhythm. Drip. Seep. Gurgle. The water finds me again. The silt returns. The grate begins, slowly, to clog.

*

Maret sits at my edge sometimes, in the gray hours before dawn. She doesn't touch the water. She doesn't need to. She is reading me, I think—listening to the way I move, the speed of my current, the depth of my voice. She makes her predictions from this. She knows when to move her pallet, when to warn the neighbors, when the middle Vell son should come with his careful hands to clear what I cannot clear myself.

She is old. She will die here. She will be carried out through the entrance, past the worn handprint on the stone, and whatever happens to the dead of this place will happen to her. But something of her will stay—the groove she has worn into her own threshold, the notches she keeps on the duty-post, the knowledge she has never written down because writing is for people with dry paper and time and a belief that the future will want to read.

I will remember her. Not the way people remember—with names and faces and the weight of grief—but the way stone remembers: as a wearing, a shaping, a pattern in the current. She has passed over me so many times. She has read me so carefully. She has known me without needing to speak the knowing.

When she is gone, I will feel her absence as a change in the water. A missing pressure. A question no one else knows to ask.

*

The quarter sinks. I sink with it.

This is the truth the children learn before they learn to count, the truth Osten cannot accept, the truth Maret reads in my voice and the Vell family reads in the rising limestone blade. The entrance step is higher each generation. The central lane is deeper. The foundations that were cut into bedrock are now below the surrounding ground, and the bedrock itself is dissolving, and I am the channel through which it dissolves.

I do not regret this. I am not capable of regret.

But I am aware—in whatever way stone is aware—that I am participating in an ending. That the water I carry is the same water that is unmaking me, and the quarter, and everything the people have built in the bowl of limestone that someone, generations ago, decided to call home.

They will not leave. Where would they go? This is theirs. This is them. They have become the place as much as the place has become them, and leaving would mean becoming something else, someone else, something without pale skin and dark eyes and the knowledge of how to live in the space between rising water and sinking stone.

So they stay. They bail out the floods. They clear my grate. They wear grooves into thresholds and handholds and cutting surfaces, and they teach their children to speak softly, and they keep salamanders in jars until the salamanders die, and they watch the cat choose his doorways, and they live.

*

I am the drain.

I do not love them. Stone does not love.

But I hold them. I carry what they give me. I let it go when it is time to let go, and I receive what returns, and I am the throat through which the quarter breathes its seepage back into the world.

This is enough. This is what I am for.

The water rises. The water always rises.

And I am here, in the dark beneath the grate, waiting to receive it.
