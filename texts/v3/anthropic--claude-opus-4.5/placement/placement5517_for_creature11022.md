I am a notation-keeper who has forgotten how to believe in notations.

My crew calls me *Silted*—a name given because I was slow to speak as a child, my words accumulating somewhere deep before surfacing. I am forty-three years old, or forty-seven, or some other number that matters less than the routes I have walked. I have outlived my mother, my mother's brother, two partners, and a child who dispersed at six years to a fever the lichens could not treat. I am the eldest now in a crew of five, and the others look to me for the routes, and I walk them, and I make the marks, and something in me has grown very quiet.

The notation system I use came from my mother's crew, who took it from a crew that merged with theirs three generations back. Circles for flow-status. Crossed lines for blockage. A spiral that means *changed since last walking*. I have made these marks on columns and grating for thirty years, and I have never known if anyone reads them. Twice I have found my own notations from years before, half-dissolved under mineral buildup, and I could not be certain what I had meant by them.

This troubles me in a way I cannot share with my crew.

I still walk the routes. I still clear the grating when deposits accumulate, still redirect trickles that seem wrong, still teach the young ones where to step during a Breathing and which junction housings the repair-crawlers tolerate. My hands know this work the way the water knows its channels. But increasingly I watch myself doing it from a small distance, as if I am a float-monitor drifting behind my own body, taking measurements no one reads.

The question that won't disperse: *What are we actually for?*

The machines do not need us. I have known this since I was young. But I understood it differently then—as a kind of freedom, a permission to exist alongside rather than beneath. Now I understand it as something else. We have built our entire lives around the practice of maintenance, and the maintenance does nothing, and we do it anyway, and we pass the doing to our children, and they will pass it on until something stops them. We are a ritual that has forgotten it is a ritual. We are a notation system that once meant something.

Last year I began walking past my route's boundary.

Not far. An hour beyond the last mark my mother's crew ever made, into a district where the catwalks narrow and the amber light grows sparse. I told my crew I was scouting for a possible route extension—a lie they accepted because they trust me, because I am Silted, because I have always been reliable in my strange slow way. In truth I was looking for something I cannot name. Evidence, perhaps. That the Tenders matter. That we are a necessary part of the system's running. That our tending has any meaning at all beyond the meaning we give it.

I found only more catwalks. More columns. More weeping pipes. A junction housing that had been sealed by repair-crawlers so long ago that the mineral deposits had grown over the seals, making it part of the infrastructure, indistinguishable from what was always there. I stood outside it for a long time, thinking about the crew that must have lived in it, their notations dissolved, their routes absorbed, their existence leaving no mark that the Condenseries recognized as anything but damage.

I have not told anyone about the junction housing.

What I am becoming, I think, is a water-goer who has not yet gone to the water. The black surface calls to me now in a way it never did before—not with words, but with the pull that absence exerts, the way a column-gap in the catwalks draws the eye down. I watch the float-monitors drift across it and I wonder what they measure, whether their measurements mean anything, whether meaning is possible in a system that runs without reference to us.

My crew needs me for another ten years, perhaps. The young ones are not ready for the routes alone. After that—after they can walk without me—I think I will build a raft.

Not to search for depth or revelation. Not because I believe the water holds answers. But because I have spent my life making notations that no one reads, and there is a kind of honesty in admitting that, and the water is where things go when they disperse, and I would like to be dispersing while I am still alive enough to feel it.

Until then, I walk the Seventh Radial. I clear the grating. I make my circles and crossed lines and spirals on columns that will absorb them into mineral layers, and I teach the young ones the routes, and I am patient with their questions, and I do not tell them that I have no answers, because they are not yet ready for that weight, and perhaps they never will be, and perhaps that is its own kind of mercy.

The system runs. I run within it. The difference between these things grows smaller every year.
