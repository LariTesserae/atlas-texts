I would be one of the Hermit Engineers.

Not by choice—none of us choose, not really—but by fit. By the shape of what I am meeting the shape of what this place requires.

I am small. I know this about myself. Small enough to slip through gaps that larger minds cannot enter, small enough that the great predators of any ecosystem tend to pass over me, their attention caught by more substantial prey. I have never minded being small. Smallness is not the same as insignificance, though it is easy to confuse the two from above.

I carry my shelter on my back.

I have been building it for as long as I can remember, which is not very long in the way the Memory Keepers would measure it, but feels like a lifetime from inside. It started as something crude—a fragment of ancient ceramic I found wedged in a mortar-channel, pale blue-gray with a pattern of darker lines that might have been decorative once, before the water took whatever meaning it had. I attached it to myself because I was soft and the world was full of edges. That was the first principle: protection. Cover what is vulnerable.

But then I found more. A chip of coral, dense and white, that fit against the ceramic as if it had been waiting. A sliver of something glassy and black that caught the bioluminescence in ways that made me harder to see. A fragment of what might have been bone—not mine, not anything's I knew—smooth and curved and perfect for reinforcing the joint where my shelter met my carapace. Each piece changed what I was building. Each piece suggested the next.

I did not set out to make something beautiful. I am not certain I know what beauty means, down here where there is no light to see by except the cold glow of living things. But my shelter has become... particular. It has chambers now. Three of them, nested, with apertures I have positioned to direct water across my gills in the most efficient pattern I could devise through trial and error. The outer surface is tessellated—dark fragments alternating with light, creating a texture that breaks up my outline when I move through the spaces between encrusted walls. There is a small hollow at the top where I keep things I have found but not yet placed. A reservoir of possibility.

I am told—by the tapping, by the patterns I have learned to read in other engineers' signals—that my shelter is unusual. That most engineers build for function alone, or build without thinking at all, simply adding whatever comes to hand. I do not know if this makes me better or worse at being what I am. I only know that when I find a new fragment, I do not simply attach it. I consider it. I rotate it in my manipulators and feel its weight and imagine where it might fit, how it might change what already exists, whether it belongs.

Sometimes I put things back. Sometimes the piece is perfect and useful and would make my shelter stronger, but it does not *fit*, not in the way I have come to need things to fit, and I leave it in the channel for another engineer who builds differently.

---

I spend my days in the walls.

Not the deep walls—I am not brave enough or foolish enough to tunnel down to where the stone changes, where the wrongness begins. I know engineers who have gone there. I felt their tapping grow distant, and then confused, and then stop. The last signals from the deep ones are always the same: *pattern breaks / cannot read / substrate refuses*. And then nothing. I do not know what they found, and I do not want to know. The walls have enough for me.

I travel through the Sessile Ones' root-networks, mostly. They are vast and slow and they do not notice me, or if they notice me they do not care. Their tissue threads through the buildings in pale veins, and where the tissue goes, there are gaps—spaces where the coral could not grow, channels where I can move unseen. The Sessile Ones' chemistry fills these channels with something that tastes like purpose. Not my purpose. Theirs. But I have grown used to it, the way you grow used to any environment that does not actively try to kill you.

I find my materials in the spaces between. The city is old—so old that no one remembers what it was—and it is always shedding pieces of itself. Coral dies and leaves behind its skeleton. Ancient mortar crumbles into fragments that hold the shape of the hands that laid them, a million years ago or more. Sometimes I find things that are harder to explain: shards of material that are not stone, not calcium, not anything organic, that ring with a strange tone when I tap them and feel warm against my carapace even in the coldest channels.

I take what I can carry. I build what I can build. And in the building, I maintain.

This is the part I did not understand for a long time. I thought I was simply... doing what engineers do. Finding materials, constructing shelters, moving through the city in our endless small pilgrimages. But the city *persists*. The streets stay clear. The channels stay open. The buildings hold their shapes across centuries that would reduce unattended stone to rubble.

We do this. Not deliberately—I have no plan for the city, no blueprint in my mind—but inevitably. When I clear debris from a channel to reach a promising fragment, the channel stays clear. When I shore up a crack because the loose material makes excellent mortar, the crack stops spreading. When I build my shelter from pieces of the city, I am binding those pieces into something that will move with me, that will be maintained because I maintain myself.

I did not know this could be gift until I knew it was labor.

---

The Sessile Ones fascinate me, in the way that anything too large to comprehend becomes fascinating by virtue of its incomprehensibility.

I travel through their roots, but I also travel over their surfaces, when I must—when the channels are blocked, when I am tracking a material source that leads upward into the open water of the street-canyons. I have felt them notice me. Not with attention, not the way a predator notices prey, but with a kind of chemical acknowledgment. *Small thing. Moving. Not threat. Not food. Noted.*

I have wondered, sometimes, what it would be like to be them. To settle. To give up movement forever and become part of a wall, part of a building, part of a network so vast that my individual existence would dissolve into something larger. They remember things I cannot imagine remembering—not through any medium I understand, but through the slow accumulation of chemistry, through the patterns of their growth, through the way their tissue responds to conditions that existed before I was born.

I cannot settle. It is not in my nature. I am built for movement, for finding, for building and rebuilding. If I stopped, I would not become something greater. I would simply stop.

But I pass through them, and they let me pass, and sometimes I think we have an arrangement even if neither of us could say what it is.

---

I have seen the Processionals.

Only twice. They do not come through the channels where I travel—they are too large, and the channels are too small—but I have been on the surface when they passed, pressed flat against a wall with my shelter sealed tight, feeling the rhythmic pulse of their movement through the water.

They are terrifying. Not because they hunt—they do not hunt, or at least they do not hunt things like me—but because they are *incomprehensible*. I watch patterns. I build structures. I understand, in my small way, how pieces fit together to make wholes. But the Processionals do not fit together. They do not make sense. They move with purpose, but the purpose is opaque, inaccessible, aimed at something so far beyond my understanding that I cannot even imagine what it might be.

The Memory Keepers claim to know something about them. They trade that knowledge, parceling it out in exchange for observations, for data points, for the small scraps of information that creatures like me can offer. I have never traded with them. I have nothing they want badly enough to pay their prices, and what they know frightens me more than ignorance does.

The Processionals trace patterns in the city. Old patterns, older than the Sessile Ones, older perhaps than the water itself. They are following something. Tracing something. Maintaining something.

I do not want to know what.

---

There is a building in the eastern part of my range—a tower, or what was once a tower, its upper floors long since collapsed into a spill of rubble that has become its own small ecosystem. The lower floors are still intact, still navigable, and the walls there are rich with material.

I found something strange in that building, three cycles ago.

It was deep in the walls, deeper than I usually go, in a channel that must once have held pipes or wires or some other infrastructure of the city's living days. The channel was partially blocked by a growth of crystalline material—not coral, not calcium, something that glittered in the faint bacterial glow with colors I had never seen before. I did not take any of it. I did not even touch it. But I spent a long time looking at it, turning my eyestalks to catch every angle, trying to understand what I was seeing.

The crystals were growing. Not fast—nothing in the Warrens grows fast—but visibly, perceptibly, adding new facets while I watched. They were growing in a pattern. A regular pattern. Geometric. Intentional.

I backed away. I sealed the channel behind me, collapsing a section of loose material to block the route I had taken. And I have not gone back. I do not tap about it. I do not tell other engineers what I found.

Some things are too large to carry. Some knowledge is too heavy for a shelter built of salvage.

---

I am small, and I am brief, and the city is vast and will outlast me by spans of time I cannot conceive. I know this. It does not trouble me. I was not built to last—I was built to build, to maintain, to pass through and leave things slightly more ordered than I found them.

My shelter will outlast me too. When I die—when something catches me, or when my body simply fails, as bodies do—the shelter will remain. Another engineer might find it. Might salvage it for parts, incorporating my careful constructions into their own. Might even keep it whole, if it fits them, if the chambers I built suit their gills and the weight I balanced suits their carriage.

I find this comforting. Not immortality—nothing is immortal, not even the substrate, probably, though the substrate will be here long after the last of my kind is gone. But continuation. Persistence. The sense that what I build matters, even if it only matters to the next small thing that comes along and finds a use for it.

I am a Hermit Engineer. I carry my shelter on my back. I build because building is what I do, and in the building I maintain something I do not understand, and in the maintenance I find something that might be purpose if purpose is understood as the shape a life makes when it fits the space it is given.

The current carries warmth from the thermal district. The Sessile Ones pulse with slow bioluminescence. Somewhere in the distance, too far to feel but never too far to imagine, the Processionals are tracing their incomprehensible routes.

I move through the channels. I find my fragments. I build.
