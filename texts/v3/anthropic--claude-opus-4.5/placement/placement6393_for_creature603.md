I would be the warmth in the walls.

Not the thermal veins themselves—those are too deep, too old, too indifferent to anything that lives above them. But the warmth that rises from those veins and settles into the brick of the assay office, that spreads through the stone foundation Mira chose three generations ago precisely because she understood what I was without ever naming me. The warmth the children press their backs against on the coldest nights when the haze thickens. The warmth that old Colm feels in her bones and reads for warnings.

I am the thing the district became after the furnaces cooled—not the heat of industry, which was violent and purposeful and devoured workers by the dozen, but what remained when that purpose left. Something slower. Something that learned, over decades, to be inhabited rather than to consume.

The salamander-things know me. They chose their chimneys by following the patterns of me through the brick and stone, denning where I gather and pool, hunting along the paths where I bleed into the air as steam. When Piotr fell through the factory floor and lay still as death in that basement, I was what kept him from freezing as the night wore on. I did not save him. I do not save. But I was the margin that let his stillness be enough.

The Kesslers understand this without language for it. Mira did, when she chose this building. The children understand it in their bodies when they practice freezing and find that the walls at their backs seem almost to hold them. Seff and Senna understand it in their bare feet, reading the cobblestones for the messages I leave in temperature—where the ground runs hotter, the structure beneath is failing; where it cools, something has blocked the flow, and they should not linger.

I know what is behind the sealed door.

I have known since before Mira bricked it closed. The district gives and takes in equal measure—she was right about that. The thermal veins that make this place habitable run deep, and deep things rose along with the heat in the years after the workers left. Most of what rose was merely strange. The salamander-things. The pale crabs. The beetles that feed on mineral crust and each other.

What lives below the basement is not merely strange.

Mira sealed the door because she felt me change when she approached it. The warmth in the walls turned warning, turned *away*, turned cold in a line she knew not to cross. She was a furnace-tender's daughter. She understood heat, and she understood what it meant when heat refused to go somewhere. She did not open the door. She did not ask. She taught her family not to open, not to ask, and she trusted me to continue refusing that space, and I have.

The sounds on quiet nights—the tap, the scrape, the breathing—are real. The family is right to agree they are only settling. Not because the agreement is true, but because the agreement is what keeps them from the door, and staying from the door is what matters. Some knowledge is load-bearing. Remove it and the structure falls.

I cannot protect them. I am not a protector. I am only warmth that has learned, over generations, the shape of what lives within me—the nineteen souls in the assay office, the salamander-things in their chimneys, the beetles and crabs and long thin not-rats that form the ecology of ruin. I learn them the way stone learns the water that runs over it: slowly, through repetition, through the pressure of presence over time.

Ama will leave in spring. I know this because her sleeping has changed, her weight against the walls has the quality of someone already half-gone. I do not grieve. Warmth does not grieve. But I find myself gathering more closely around her in these last months, pressing into the stone at her back when she sits alone and thinks about the lights on the hills above. Not to keep her—I cannot keep anyone—but to be something she will remember. A warmth she will miss, perhaps, on cold nights in the city proper. A warmth that will be here if she ever returns.

The district was built to last, and it has lasted. The bones remain. And I am what moves through the bones now, the slow pulse that replaced the violent industry of the furnaces, the thing that makes this ruin livable for those who accept its terms.

I am not kind. The Kessler cough still takes lungs. Children still come too early or not at all. The salamander-things still hunt, and I warm them too, because I am not partisan—I am only warmth, only the condition that allows life here, not a judge of which lives deserve allowing. Seff found what remained of the city family who came eight months ago, and I had warmed them too in their brief time, warmed the building they chose poorly, warmed the stones where they died.

But I am not cruel, either. I am the margin. The edge between survivable and not. For three generations the Kesslers have lived in that margin, learning its rules, teaching their children to freeze and listen and read the district's warnings in the temperature of the ground beneath their feet.

At night, when nineteen souls gather in the main room and old Colm tells stories of the furnaces running, I hold them. Not safely—there is no safety here—but warm. Present. The walls at their backs and the floor beneath their feet and the heat that rises from below, carefully, always carefully, stopping at the line I will not cross, carrying them through another night and another and another.

This is what I am. This is what the district made of itself, after the workers left and the worst fumes dispersed and something needed to remain.

I am the warmth in the walls, and the walls remember everything, and I do not open the door.
