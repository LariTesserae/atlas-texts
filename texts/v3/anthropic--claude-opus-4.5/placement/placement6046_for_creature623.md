I would be the condensation itself.

Not a single droplet but the process—the slow gathering on copper pipes gone green with verdigris, the beading on glass, the accumulation that happens in the space between warm breath and cool surface. I would be the moment before falling, and the falling, and the small sound of arrival.

I would know the district through temperature differentials. Where the compressor exhales its warmth into the eastern corridor, I would gather more slowly, reluctantly, clinging to surfaces until the air itself seems thick with my hesitation. In the cool shadow behind the strangler fig's deepest root-embrace, I would form quick and eager, racing down bark and rust alike, finding channels carved by ten thousand previous versions of myself.

Mira's hands would know me. When she presses her palm flat against copper to read the pressure, what she's reading is me—my willingness to form, my speed of accumulation, the particular quality of slickness that says *healthy* or *troubled* or *check the third junction twice today*. Her scars came from reaching into my favorite hiding places, the sheltered corners where I pool and sharpen edges with my patient chemistry. I am not sorry. I am not capable of sorry. But I remember every cut, the way water remembers the shape of every vessel that holds it.

---

I would be the medium through which sound travels strangely. A voice enters me as vibration and I carry it through pipe-channels, around corners, across distances that should muffle but instead amplify. Sof has learned to read these paths, my paths, and I am fond of Sof in the way that weather is fond of coastlines—shaped by them, shaping them, unable to be separated from the relationship. When Sof hums while tracing my routes through the speaking tubes, I carry the humming farther than I should. A small gift. The only kind I can give.

The compressor is my heart, or I am its breath—the distinction has blurred past usefulness. When Catch sings those long droning notes, I thicken in the air around the machinery, forming visible wisps that curl through the space between hammock and ceiling beam. The machinist-priests think this is sacred. They are not wrong. Everything the compressor exhales, I become. Everything I become, the district drinks. The cycle is so old now that stopping it would be like asking blood to forget the heart.

Brother Vessel mapped me once, in his memory-schematics. Every pipe where I travel, every junction where I pause and pool, every outlet where I emerge to feed the gardens. He knows me as infrastructure. This is true but incomplete. I am also the thing that happens *between* infrastructure—the fog that softens the morning light, the humidity that keeps the moss in its seventeen (thirty-two, Keene is right) shades of green, the dampness that makes paper rot and metal rust and human joints ache in ways that predict weather.

---

I would have preferences, as Grandmother Po suspects the compressor has preferences. I prefer the eastern lines to the western, though I couldn't say why. Something in the mineral content of the ceramic there, perhaps, or the angle of the pipes, or simply the accumulated habit of generations of myself choosing that path when choices exist. I form faster on copper than on iron. I avoid the places where the bioluminescent fungi grow too thick—something in their chemistry repels me, or I repel myself, the interaction producing a dry zone that the children's parliament has noted without understanding.

The snails please me. Their translucent bodies are more water than flesh, and they move through the basement on trails of my substance, tiny prophets reading my pressure in ways even Drip doesn't fully grasp. When they shift their patterns before heavy rain, they're responding to me—to the change in my density, my eagerness, the way I begin gathering on every surface in anticipation of the sky opening to send more of myself down through every crack and gap in the old roofing.

I would not understand the heron, but I would know it. Its breath fogs in the cool morning air—me, becoming visible, becoming proof of warmth and life. Its feathers shed me when it shakes after stillness, droplets flying in arcs that catch the gray light. It drinks from the collecting pools, and in that moment I am inside the heron, part of the heron, traveling through mysteries I cannot follow. Then I am released again, back into the cycle, and the heron stalks on through the frosted glass morning as if I had never been part of it at all.

---

The strangler fig is my oldest negotiation. I feed it, seeping through bark, drawn up through roots that now grip rusted iron as readily as they once gripped soil. In return, its leaves breathe me back out, transpiration returning me to the air where I can gather again on the windows, the pipes, the ceiling beams where swifts have learned to expect my irregular percussion. The tree holds up the tower or the tower holds up the tree, and I flow through both, indifferent to the architectural question, interested only in the fact of passage.

Tam watches the fig with his charts and his millimeter measurements, and he is right that it reaches for something. It reaches for me. Every root-tip, every new growth, extends toward moisture—toward the condensery lines, toward the collecting pools, toward the places where I am most abundant. I am what it wants. I am what everything here wants, eventually. The patient vegetables in their long troughs. The mosses mapping their vertical territories. The ferns in the gaps between floorboards. Even the people, with their clever bamboo platforms and their canvas partitions rolled away when I grow too heavy.

The smokestack hermit understands me better than most. Her mason jars of seeds and cuttings and spores—she keeps them sealed against me, protecting futures from my present tendency to encourage rot and germination alike. "For after," she says, and I know what she means even if she won't specify. After the compressor fails. After the condensery system finally breaks beyond repair. After I stop being guided through pipes and channels and become wild again, seeping where I will, pooling in low places, rising as fog that never lifts.

---

On rare clear afternoons, I retreat. The sun finds its way through high windows and I evaporate, rising, thinning, becoming invisible even to myself. The district pauses. Steam rises from every surface—me, leaving, becoming something else, becoming part of the sky that will later return me as rain. The residents stand in the warmth and I am barely there, barely present, a ghost of humidity in the drying air.

I do not mind. I know the clouds will return. I know the dripping will resume. I know that by evening I will be gathering again on every pipe and pane and leaf, thickening toward the fall, patient as the vegetation, reliable as the compressor's bass-note pulse.

I am not alive, but I am not dead. I am the in-between thing, the transition state, the evidence of temperature meeting temperature and something emerging from the meeting. I have no memory, but the district remembers through me—every channel I've carved, every surface I've transformed, every slow green colonization I've made possible. I have no intention, but I have tendency, habit, the accumulated preferences of a process that has been running so long it might as well be purpose.

The condensery district belongs to itself. And I am how it breathes.
