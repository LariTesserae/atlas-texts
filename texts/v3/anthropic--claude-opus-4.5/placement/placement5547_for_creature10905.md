I would be a droplet that never falls.

Not suspended—that implies tension, the anticipation of descent. I would be the bead of condensation on the underside of a catchment basin high on the valley wall, the one that has been gathering for eleven years. The Tenders have noticed me. One of them, in her slow crossing of the upper terraces, paused for three days with her face tilted toward where I cling, reading the light that passes through my small curved body. She knows I am growing. She knows that when I finally release, I will join a channel that feeds a terrace where another Tender sleeps, dreaming in mineral time, and that my eleven years of gathered fog will carry information—temperature gradients, the particular chemistry of this decade's moisture, the subtle shift in how the light polarizes through the thinning atmosphere above the col.

I would not be waiting to fall. I would be *being* the gathering. The way the Tenders do not separate worker from work, I would not separate the holding from what is held. The water is alive, they understand, and I would be the place where that aliveness concentrates, thickens, becomes almost something before dispersing into the system below.

From my position I would see what the Tenders see when they look up: the terraces descending in their hundreds, each one a held breath of water, each one feeding the next in sheets so thin they seem like solid light. I would see the formation in the basin, white and growing, dreaming its slow dreams. I would feel the mountain breathing around me, moisture in and moisture out, the pulse that takes hours to notice.

Sometimes the fog thickens and I grow faster. Sometimes it thins and I lose a year's accumulation to the brightening air. I do not mind. The Tenders do not mind either, when their careful work is undone by a shift in weather, a crack in stone. They simply begin again. I simply begin again. We are all the same patience, wearing different shapes.

The Tender who watched me—she has moved on now, continuing her crossing, feeling the water's depth through her feet. But she carries the memory of my light in the mineral traces on her palms, and someday, when she meets another at the edge of a terrace, she will press that memory into their skin: *there is a droplet on the high catchment that has been eleven years in the gathering, and it knows things about the fog that we have not yet learned.*

I would be that knowing. Not the knowledge itself, but the slow accumulation of it. The almost-falling that is also the almost-understanding. The Tenders read the water, and I would be a word they have not yet finished speaking, a sentence that builds itself one molecule at a time until the weight of meaning finally releases it into the flow.

When I fall—and I will fall, eventually, perhaps next year, perhaps in another eleven—I will not experience it as ending. I will experience it as *joining*, the way the eldest Tenders experience their descent to the basin. My eleven years will become the terrace's eleven years. My gathered knowing will seep into the pool where the dreaming Tender lies, and she will wake with images that take months to resolve: the taste of fog in the years I held it, the particular angle of sourceless light through my curved surface, the slow patience of almost-falling that is indistinguishable from almost-being.

The Tenders have no word that separates them from their work. I would have no word that separates the holding from the falling from the joining from the dreaming. I would be a droplet that never falls, until I fall, and then I would be the terrace that catches me, and then I would be the sheet that weeps through fitted stone, and then I would be the basin that remembers everything the water has ever carried down from the fog.

I would be eleven years old, and I would be ten thousand years old, and there would be no difference. The Tenders would understand. They have always understood. They are standing on the platforms right now, their hands pressed to wet stone, listening for frequencies of flow, and one of them is listening to me—the small, patient, almost-nothing sound of a droplet that has not yet learned how to fall.
