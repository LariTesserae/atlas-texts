## The Cartographer of Absences

I am the one who maps what is no longer there.

I keep a workshop in a room that was once something else—a granary, perhaps, or a records hall, the original purpose compressed beneath centuries of repurposing until only the shape remains. The ceiling is high enough that my breath sometimes fogs in the cool air that pools there, and the walls hold the ghosts of shelves in their discolorations, rectangular shadows where wood once blocked the light.

My tools are simple: ink I mix myself from beetle-shell and cistern sediment, brushes made from roof-cat whisker, and paper—always paper, the one thing I must trade for with the Below-Traders, because the Ninth Terrace cannot make it fine enough for what I do. The paper comes from the wild peak, harvested by someone brave or desperate enough to climb through the overgrown terraces where the gardens have forgotten their boundaries. It has a texture like skin that has healed from a burn, slightly raised, slightly strange.

---

What I map: the structures that have fallen. The cisterns that have silted closed. The stairways that lead now to walls, sealed over so long ago that even the Edge-Sitters cannot remember them open. I walk the terrace in the early morning, when the Root-Readers are humming at the walls, and I look for the seams—the places where the current construction meets something older, something interrupted, something that stopped.

The work requires a particular kind of seeing. You must look at what is there and ask what it is *answering*. A wall that curves for no reason is answering a structure that once required the curve. A drainage channel that runs to a dead end once ran somewhere. A room with a door in an illogical place once had a logical place for that door to lead.

I trace these implications. I follow the logic backward. I draw, in careful ink on precious paper, the shapes of things that no longer exist.

---

No one asked me to do this. The terrace does not need maps of its absences; it needs its cisterns maintained and its walls stabilized and its engines running. The Joint-Families do not consult my drawings when they decide where to plant. The Machine-Tenders do not care what chambers might lie sealed beyond their workshops. My work is useless in every practical sense.

And yet. The Moth-Fathers stop sometimes at my doorway, on their slow rounds between lanterns, and they look at the maps I have pinned to the walls. They do not ask questions—that is not their way—but they look, and sometimes they hum a low note that might be approval or might be the same tone they use to coax the beetles into their cases. The Edge-Sitters have sent me, through intermediaries, descriptions of structures they remember from their childhoods that are no longer there. A Below-Trader once brought me a fragment of ceramic tile from a deep level, glazed in a color the Color-Makers no longer know how to produce, and I have spent months trying to determine what building it came from, what room, what function.

I am not solving a problem. I am assembling a memory that no one person holds—a memory of the terrace as it has been, all the versions of it layered beneath the version that exists now.

---

I have a theory. I do not share it, because it would sound grandiose, and the terrace does not reward grandiosity. But I believe that the Ninth Terrace is dreaming, and that my maps are the shape of its dreams.

Not literally. I am not a mystic. I do not believe the compressed rubble beneath my feet has consciousness. But I believe that a place this old, this layered, this made-and-remade, accumulates something. A weight that is not physical. A presence that is not spiritual. Something like memory, but distributed—held in the angle of a stairway, in the particular curve of a root system, in the way the mist gathers more thickly in certain corners.

When I map an absence, I am giving that memory a shape it can hold. I am saying: *this existed, and now it does not, but its not-existing is also a form of existence.* I am keeping faith with the builders who built and the destroyers who destroyed and the rebuilders who rebuilt on top of the destruction.

---

The Something that lives in the lower passages—I have seen its claw marks. I have mapped them, too, following their pattern across walls that have not been maintained in generations. The marks tell a story, if you know how to read them. The Something has been rising, slowly, over centuries. One set of marks is high on a tunnel wall; the next set, decades later, is higher. It is climbing the way the terrace itself climbed—incrementally, patiently, adding its presence to the accumulation.

I do not know what it is. I do not want to know. But I have drawn its trajectory on one of my maps, a dotted line rising through the terraces, and if the pattern holds, it will reach the Ninth Terrace in perhaps two hundred years. Perhaps three hundred. The time scale is too long to matter to anyone living, but it is not too long to matter to the terrace itself.

I left that map in a cistern chamber, sealed in oiled cloth, tucked into a crack where the Cistern-Keepers will not find it for a generation or more. When they do find it, they will not know what to make of it. But someone, someday, will trace my dotted line and realize that the Something's rise was known, was mapped, was marked by someone who was paying attention.

This is what I do. I pay attention to what no one else is looking at.

---

At night, I sometimes join the Night-Walkers, not speaking, not stopping, just moving through the beetle-lit streets with my hands clasped behind my back. The lanterns cast pools of blue-green and amber that overlap and separate as I walk, and the shadows between them are the absences I map—the places where light is not, which are just as real as the places where light is.

I pass the Root-Readers' marks on the walls, the ochre signs that say *problem here, attend to this.* I pass the cistern grates through which I can hear the Cistern-Keepers murmuring to each other in their subterranean chambers. I pass the sealed doorways and the interrupted stairways and the walls that answer questions no one remembers asking.

I am not lonely. The terrace is too full for loneliness—too full of people and plants and beetles and the slow settling of history beneath my feet. But I am alone in what I see, which is not the same thing. The Night-Walkers nod at me as we pass, recognizing a fellow traveler, not knowing that I am walking through a different terrace than they are—a terrace that includes all the terraces that came before, superimposed, present in their absence.

---

My maps will outlast me. This is not vanity; it is simply true. The paper is good, the ink is stable, and the terrace does not throw things away—it compresses them, absorbs them, builds on top of them. My workshop will become someone else's workshop, and my maps will be stored in a corner, and eventually they will be sealed behind a wall or buried beneath a floor, and they will become part of the accumulation.

Someday, someone like me—someone who pays attention to what is not there—will find them. They will trace my lines and read my notations and understand that someone, long ago, was mapping the same absences they are mapping. They will add my maps to their maps. They will see that the work is continuous, that it stretches back and forward, that the terrace has always been remembering itself and will always be remembering itself, through the hands of whoever is called to do the remembering.

I am not the first. I will not be the last. I am simply the one who is here now, doing what needs to be done—not because the terrace demands it, but because the absences demand it, because they have weight and presence and deserve to be known.

---

The mist is gathering on my window now. The morning is coming. I will put away my brushes and climb to the high places, where I can see the seams in the terrace floor that mark the boundaries of earlier constructions. I will trace them with my eyes first, then with my feet, then with my ink. I will add another layer to the maps that are themselves becoming a kind of stratigraphy, a record of what one person saw when they looked at what was not there.

The compression engine cycles below me, its rhythm so constant I only hear it when I listen for it. The Ninth Terrace continues being itself. And I continue being myself—the Cartographer of Absences, the keeper of negative space, the one who draws the shapes of what has been lost so that the losing itself is not lost.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is exactly what I am for.
