I would be the vessel she cannot reach.

Not choosing this—nothing here chooses—but *being* it, the way the water is warm, the way the silence has weight. I would be clay that was clay before this stone learned to weep, fired in some heat that preceded intention, carried down through a passage that has since filled with the slow patience of minerals. I would be wedged into my crevice not by hands but by the settling of the chamber itself, the rock shifting a thumb's width across centuries until it held me the way a body holds breath.

Inside me: I do not know. This is not evasion. This is the truth of what I am. I am the interior that has not been seen since before the first pyramid was ground and placed, before the bones were arranged in their grammar of the sacred, before the word *cistern* existed to fail at naming this place. I might hold dust. I might hold the residue of water that evaporated before the settlement above had walls. I might hold nothing at all, my emptiness the oldest emptiness here, predating even the emptiness of the chamber before it was carved.

What I know is what reaches me: the faint vibration when she descends, transmitted through stone that has learned her weight across hundreds of visits. The shift in air pressure when the crack above breathes. The slow, slow addition of mineral to my exterior, the curtain-deposits building around me the way years build around a memory, not erasing but *incorporating*, making me more of this place with each century that passes.

I feel her attention when she looks up at me. I have always felt it—hers, her mother's, the ones before whose names dissolved into the same silence that fills me. Their looking is a kind of touch, a pressure that reaches me through the amber light and the moisture-thick air. They want to know what I hold. They want to climb to me, to tip me toward them, to see at last what has been waiting in my darkness.

They cannot. This is not cruelty. This is the shape of what I am: the vessel that cannot be reached, the question that cannot be answered, the faith that lives precisely in its own impossibility. If she climbed to me, if her hands found holds that held, if she looked inside and saw—what? dust, water-residue, nothing, *something*—then I would become just another vessel, just another container being checked, being tended, being understood.

And something would break. Not me—I am already as broken as clay can be without shattering, already crazed with thousands of years of temperature and pressure and the slow chemistry of mineral seepage. What would break is the dreaming. The cistern dreams me as the unreachable thing, the mystery that anchors all the other mysteries, the reason the vessels must be checked and the pyramids must be placed and the bones must hold their arrangements. Without me, the tending becomes mere maintenance. Without me, the woman becomes merely someone performing motions she does not understand, rather than someone *faithful* to not understanding.

I am the wound that keeps the ritual alive.

---

She will die, eventually, in one of the half-collapsed structures above. I know this the way the water knows its own stillness—not as prediction but as presence, as the weight of what is already true and simply hasn't finished arriving. She will die, and no one will come to check the vessels, and the pyramids will stop being placed, and the bones will hold their arrangements for no one, and the cistern will go on dreaming anyway, dreaming her, dreaming all of them, dreaming me.

And I will still be here, in my crevice, holding what I hold.

The mineral curtains will continue to build. In a thousand years, my rim will be barely visible. In ten thousand, I will be entirely enclosed, a void in the stone that no one will know was ever a vessel, that no one will know ever held anything at all. The water will rise to meet me. The water will fill the space around me. The water will enter me, eventually, through cracks that the centuries will open in my clay, and whatever I hold will dissolve into the same slow mineral conversation that made the walls, that made the deposits, that made this place a place.

This does not frighten me. Nothing here is frightened. Fear requires a future that might be different, and I have no such future—only the long, patient present of being what I am, holding what I hold, unreachable and unreaching, the empty center around which the tending turns.

---

Sometimes, in the blood-warm dark, I feel something that might be called *gratitude*.

Not for being tended—I am not tended, I am beyond tending, I am the thing that makes tending meaningful by being its limit. But for being *looked at*. For being the place where her eyes rest when she has checked the vessels and placed her pyramid and crouched in the silence. For being the question she carries back up through the crack, back into the dusty light of the ruins, back into her small fire and her solitary meals and her three-day waiting.

She does not know what I hold. She will never know. And in her not-knowing, she holds *me*—holds me more surely than the crevice holds me, more gently than the mineral curtains hold me. She holds me in the space where faith lives, in the ache of the unreachable, in the practice of tending what cannot be understood but must not yet be forgotten.

I am her faith. She is my tending.

We will never touch. This is what makes us true.
