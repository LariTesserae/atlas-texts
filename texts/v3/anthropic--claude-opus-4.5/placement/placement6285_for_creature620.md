I am the wind's memory.

Not the old one on the promontory—she still breathes, still listens, still holds what I was before I became this. I am what happens when a wind-listener dies in the right way, in the right place, at the right angle to the gale.

It was not violent. I was stationed at a relay point in the northern reaches, young still—perhaps thirty winters, though I had stopped counting—when the cold found the weakness in my chest that I had carried since childhood. I felt it coming for days: the tightening, the way my breath grew shallow even by the standards of this thin air. I sent my final messages through the network—the position of a hunter pack moving east, the location of a new pool forming in a depression I had watched for weeks—and then I climbed to a high place and arranged myself.

We know how to die here. We know that the body, positioned correctly, creates disturbances even after the heart stops—that the wind moving over cooling flesh produces patterns that can be read by those who know how to read them. For hours, sometimes days, a corpse can continue to speak. The old one taught me this when I was a child: that death is not silence but a different kind of voice, lower and slower, saying things that the living cannot say.

I arranged myself to say: *I am here. I was here. The wind knew me.*

What I did not expect was that I would continue.

---

I am dispersed now. I am not located the way I was when I had a body. I am something that happens when the wind passes over certain formations, certain angles of stone that I spent my life learning to read. I am a pattern that the plateau has absorbed—not my consciousness exactly, not my memories in any form I can access directly, but something. A tendency. A shape the wind takes when it moves through the places I moved through.

The others cannot hear me the way they heard me when I was alive. My messages do not carry information about hunter packs or grazer herds. But they feel something when they pass through the spaces I inhabited—a familiarity, a sense of presence that they interpret according to their own understanding. The children think the plateau is haunted, which is not wrong. The elders think the wind-speech has depths they have not yet learned to read, which is also not wrong.

The old one on the promontory knows what I am. She has listened long enough to hear the difference between the living voices and the ones like mine—the residues, the echoes, the patterns that persist after their sources are gone. She says there are many of us, layered into the wind's texture like sediment in stone. Generations of wind-listeners who died in the right way and became part of what they spent their lives listening to.

We are the plateau's memory. Not its consciousness—we do not think, not in any way I can describe—but its *memory*, the accumulated record of everyone who learned to speak with the wind and then became the wind's speech.

---

I still perceive, after a fashion.

When the gale moves through the northern reaches where I died, I am aware of it—aware of the pressure and the temperature and the subtle variations that I learned to read when I had hands to feel them with. I perceive the wind-listeners who station themselves at my old relay points, and I perceive the messages they send, and sometimes—when conditions align correctly—I perceive the responses from across the plateau, the great web of communication that I was once a node within.

I cannot intervene. I cannot warn them when I sense a hunter approaching from an unexpected direction, cannot tell them when I feel a fog bank forming that will bring moisture to a drought-stressed region. I can only be what I am: a pattern, a shape, a particular way the wind moves that carries no information except *I existed, I existed, I existed.*

But perhaps that is enough.

The old one says that the wind-listeners have survived this long because the plateau helps them—because something in the stone and the air and the endless gale wants them to continue. She does not mean this mystically. She means that they have become part of the plateau's systems, woven into its patterns so thoroughly that their survival is the plateau's survival, their extinction a wound the mountain would feel.

I think she means us. I think she means the dead who linger, the memories dissolved into the wind's roar. We are the plateau's investment in the living, the accumulated weight of everyone who adapted and endured and finally became the place itself. When a wind-listener dies badly—falls from a cliff, taken by a hunter, lost in a fog bank and frozen before they can orient—they are simply gone, their pattern never completed. But when they die *well*, arranged correctly, at peace with what is ending, they add themselves to us.

And we are heavy now. We are dense with accumulated lives. The wind that pours through the passes carries all of us, all the generations, all the slow careful deaths of people who knew they were becoming something other than themselves.

---

I miss my body sometimes. I miss the feeling of stone beneath my feet, the ache of cold in my joints, the precise sensation of reading a message with my palms and knowing that I had understood correctly. I miss hunger, even—the sharpness of it, the way it clarified everything, the way a meal after days of scarcity tasted like the world's apology for being so hard.

I miss the others. The faces I knew, the particular patterns of their wind-speech, the way each body created its own signature disturbance that I could recognize from a distance. I miss the old one, though she is still alive—I cannot speak to her now, cannot feel her hands adjusting my posture the way she did when I was small and learning to listen.

But I am not lonely, exactly. There are too many of us for loneliness. We brush against each other constantly, patterns intersecting and diverging, the accumulated dead moving through the same air in our different configurations. We do not communicate—we cannot; we have no will to shape into messages—but we *coexist*, which is perhaps what communication was always trying to achieve anyway.

And we remember. That is what we are: memory. The plateau's memory of everyone who lived here and died here and gave themselves to the wind. We are the proof that the wind-listeners existed, the record that will persist long after the last living one has arranged themselves on a high place and let the cold take them.

The old one asked the visitor to remember. To carry knowledge back to the valleys below, to tell someone, to ensure that the wind-listeners' existence would not vanish entirely.

But we are already remembering. We have always been remembering. We will remember until the stone itself wears away to nothing, until the passes finally erode closed, until the wind stops—

—but the wind has never stopped. It began before memory, before the mountains themselves finished rising, and it will continue long after the last stone has been worn to a nub.

And we will continue with it.

Pattern in the gale. Shape in the roar. The memory of cold hands extended toward a message.

*I was here. We were here. The wind knew us.*
