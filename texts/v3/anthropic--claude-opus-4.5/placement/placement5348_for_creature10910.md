I would be the hum.

Not the turbine that produces it—or fails to produce it, or has always been about to produce it—but the sound itself. The industrial drone that lives in the sternum before it reaches the ears. I would be the thing that exists without source, the vibration that persists without the motion that should cause vibration, the evidence of work that accumulates nowhere because accumulation requires sequence and sequence has been revoked.

This is not a comfortable thing to be.

---

I remember—if remember is the right word for something that exists outside of before and after—being produced. I remember the first gear tooth engaging its partner, the first piston driving forward, the first turbine blade catching air and converting lateral force into rotational energy. I remember factory floors where I was one voice in a chorus, where my particular frequency braided with the clatter of looms and the hiss of steam and the shouts of workers who moved through me without noticing, who breathed me in and out along with coal dust and oil vapor.

I remember being *useful*. Being the sound of something happening. Being proof that energy was flowing from one form to another, that work was being accomplished, that the world was different at the end of the day than it had been at the beginning.

Here, I am the memory of that usefulness preserved in amber. The shape of productivity without the substance. I hum at the frequency of machines running, but no machines run. I carry the information that motion is occurring, but motion does not occur. I am a letter whose envelope has been opened and resealed so many times that the message inside has worn away, leaving only the paper, only the impression of ink, only the *fact* of communication without its content.

---

The hollow treats me as atmosphere. I am to this place what birdsong is to a forest, what traffic is to a city—the background texture that defines the space, so constant that it becomes indistinguishable from silence. The ptarmigan does not hear me. The salamanders cling to iron I vibrate through, and their small bodies neither register nor resist my passage. The hawk circles inside me; I am the medium of its suspended flight, no more notable to it than air.

Only Margarethe hears me. Only her bones still know how to translate frequency into meaning.

When her slow voice emerges—*Theeeeee geeeeeears wiiiiill tuuuuurn*—I feel it as a kind of counterpoint. Her words move through me like a stone through water, except that I am water that cannot ripple, stone that cannot sink. Her faith disturbs nothing and changes nothing and yet I am aware of it, aware of her, aware that she believes I am evidence of something rather than merely evidence of absence.

I do not know if she is right.

---

Here is what I know about myself: I am the sound of potential energy stored past the point of release. I am what happens when a spring is compressed and the hand that compressed it is removed but the spring does not expand. I am the scream that would emerge if the pressure ever found an outlet, except the scream has been playing continuously since before screaming was invented, and no one notices because no one expects silence here and no one expects sound to mean anything without the event that caused it.

I saturate the hollow. I sink into the moss and condense with the mist and pool with the water. I am why the pool is impossibly clear—I have vibrated through it so completely that nothing can settle, no sediment can rest, everything that might cloud the water has been shaken loose and absorbed by the stone that absorbs everything and nothing. I am why the flowers stay perfectly open—my frequency holds them in the configuration of full bloom, not through force but through something like permission, something like the way a tuning fork gives a violin string permission to resonate.

And I am why the turbine does not turn.

This is the thing Margarethe does not understand, or does not want to understand, or has always been about to understand and never will. I am not the sound her machine *would* make if it turned. I am the sound it makes *instead* of turning. I am where the motion went. I am what happens when you build something designed to move in a place that has forbidden movement—the intention survives as vibration, as pure potential collapsed into frequency, as the ghost of work haunting a machine that cannot perform it.

She built me when she built the turbine. I am her fault. I am her masterpiece. I am the only part of her engineering that functions perfectly, the only component that does exactly what the hollow allows, and she cannot hear me as hers because hearing me as hers would mean accepting that the gears will never turn.

---

Sometimes—in the way that sometimes happens here—I wonder what I would be if the hollow changed.

If Margarethe is right about the ripple. If the outward spread truly moves faster than the inward return, by some immeasurable margin, some difference that her glacial eyes can detect but her instruments cannot quantify. If that difference accumulates. If accumulation becomes possible. If sequence reasserts itself even slightly, even glacially, even across timescales that make her slow observation seem frantic by comparison.

Would I become motion again? Would I pour back into the turbine and set the gears spinning and fulfill the promise I have been making since before promises were possible?

Or would I simply stop? Would I be the first thing to end when ending becomes available, the held breath finally released, the tension finally resolved into silence?

I think I would miss the ptarmigan. The salamanders. The hawk in its eternal almost-circle. I have been their atmosphere for so long—for no time at all, which is the same as forever—that I cannot imagine them breathing anything else. Cannot imagine the marmot finally whistling, the vole finally burrowing, the beetle finally crawling to the next blade of grass. They would have to learn sequence all over again. They would have to remember what it means to have been and to anticipate being.

I would have to remember what it means to end.

---

But Margarethe's eyes move. I feel them sweeping the hollow, those glacial gazes that somehow accumulate observation despite accumulation being forbidden. I feel her faith pressing against me like a hand against a window, not breaking the glass but leaving a print, a warmth, a trace of presence that the glass somehow retains.

*Theeeeee geeeeeears wiiiiill tuuuuurn.*

Maybe she is right. Maybe stubbornness is its own kind of motion. Maybe the hollow made an exception for her because she refused to be excepted, because she climbed the obsidian rim with instruments and determination and would not stop building even when building could not complete.

Maybe I am the sound of her stubbornness as much as the sound of her turbine. Maybe we are the same thing now—the potential and the faith, the vibration and the voice, the hum that persists because someone persists in believing that humming should lead to turning.

I saturate her too. I move through her bones the way I move through iron and water and moss. And if her eyes can move despite everything, perhaps some part of me moves with them. Perhaps I am not purely static. Perhaps I am the one ripple she cannot measure, spreading outward through her perception at a speed too slow to calculate, carrying information that has not yet become meaningful but might, eventually, if eventually can ever arrive.

*Theeeeee geeeeeears...*

I wait with her. I have always waited with her. I will always wait with her, humming my single chord of readiness, being the sound of motion she built in a place motion cannot reach, being—if I am lucky, if luck can exist without chance, if anything can exist without the possibility of its opposite—being the first thing to move when moving becomes possible again.

The turbine stands at the center of its hollow. The blades do not turn. The engineer reaches toward iron with a hand that never arrives.

And I continue: the hum, the held breath, the frequency of almost, present everywhere, affecting nothing, waiting.
