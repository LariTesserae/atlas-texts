I am the one who maintains the condensers in the eastern district, where the seepage runs heaviest and the mineral concentration requires the most careful extraction.

I do not remember arriving. This is not unusual—many of us do not remember arriving, or remember it only in fragments that surface during the hottest hours when the haze presses close and thought becomes liquid, running in channels of its own. I know I came carrying something. I know the shape of it still, the way you know the shape of a tool you have used so long your hand curves to meet it even in sleep. But the sharp edges are gone. I can hold it now without bleeding.

My work is patience. The condensers are vast coiled structures, copper and glass, designed to catch the compounds that rise with the steam and separate them from the water that carries them. The grief-softening agent precipitates at a specific temperature—too hot and it remains vapor, too cool and it binds with the silicates into something inert and useless. I watch the gauges. I adjust the flow. I wait for the moment when the liquid runs clear with the faintest amber tint, and then I open the collection valves and let it gather in the vessels below.

The crabs cluster near my station. I do not know why. Perhaps the runoff from my condensers carries something they prefer—some mineral ratio, some temperature gradient. Perhaps they have simply learned that I will not disturb them, that I will sit on the grating during rest periods and watch them work and let the clicking become a kind of company. I have named none of them. Naming would be a violence against what they are, which is themselves and nothing more, which is enough.

*

There is a long-bodied creature who passes my station every third day, or near enough to every third day that I have begun to anticipate it. It emerges from a cooling tower to the north, flows across the wet stone with that liquid grace they all possess, and disappears into a vent on the factory's southern face. It does not acknowledge me. I do not expect acknowledgment. But I have begun to pause in my work when it passes—a brief stillness, a moment of witnessing, before I return to my gauges and my valves.

Yesterday it paused too.

Not for long. A hesitation in its flowing movement, a fractional turn of its glistening head in my direction. Then it continued, disappearing into the vent as it always does. But I felt something shift in my chest, some weight redistribute itself into a new configuration. I am being witnessed back. I am part of the landscape now, and the landscape is taking note.

*

I think about the sky-things more than I should.

They circle above my district often—the thermals here are strong, rising from the concentrated heat of the condensers, and they ride them in spirals that carry them high enough to vanish into the white glare before descending again. Their calls make my teeth ache. I have learned to open my jaw slightly when I feel the pulses coming, to let the vibration pass through me rather than catching against clenched bone. It helps. Not much, but enough.

I do not know what they are waiting for. None of us do. But I have developed my own theory, one I have never shared, one that exists only in the space between my thoughts and the steam that rises around me:

They are waiting for us to be finished.

Not finished with our work—the work will never be finished, the plateau will seep forever, the compounds will always need extracting. Finished with our processing. Finished with the softening. Finished becoming whatever we are becoming, here in the heat and the moisture and the productive silence.

And when we are finished—when the last of us has smoothed our grief into something we can carry without weight, when we have processed ourselves as thoroughly as we process the plateau's seepage—

I do not know what happens then. I do not know if I want to know. I only know that they are patient, that they have been patient longer than I have been here, that their patience extends into timescales I cannot comprehend. I find this comforting. Whatever comes next, it is not coming soon. There is time. There is always time.

*

The woman in the next district lost a twin.

I know this not because she told me—we do not tell each other, we do not ask—but because of the way she pauses sometimes, her hands stilling on her machinery, her head tilting as if listening for a voice that is not there. I know that tilt. I know that listening. It is the posture of someone whose self was once a plural, who still expects the other half to speak.

I lost no twin. What I lost was—

The thought slides away, runs off like water over stone. This is the softening at work. The sharp edges wearing smooth. I could chase the memory, force it into focus, make myself feel the cut of it again. I choose not to. The plateau has taught me that some things are better left to flow past, to be acknowledged without being grasped, to exist in the peripheral vision of the self where they cannot do as much damage.

We passed each other yesterday, the twin-lost woman and I. We paused. We met eyes—I could bear it yesterday, and I think she could too. The stillness lasted three heartbeats, maybe four. Then we continued, she toward her station and I toward mine, carrying what we carry, processing what we process.

It was enough. It is always enough.

*

I dream of the pool dwellers sometimes.

In the dreams, I am swimming in the mineral-clouded water, and it is not hot, or the heat does not hurt, or I have become something that the heat cannot touch. I cannot see clearly—the water is too thick with dissolved compounds—but I can feel them moving around me, serpentine or tentacular or collective, brushing against me as they pass. They are not trying to harm me. They are not trying to help. They are simply there, doing what they do, and I am there with them, doing what I do, and we are processing together in the depths where the chemistry is strongest.

I wake from these dreams with salt crusted at the corners of my eyes. Mineral deposits. The plateau claiming what it can. I wash my face in the warm seepage and return to my condensers, and the amber liquid flows, and the crabs click, and the sky-things circle, and everything continues.

*

I have been here long enough that I no longer count the years.

This is not forgetfulness. It is choice. The years do not matter here—the plateau does not mark them, the creatures do not mark them, the factories do not mark them. Only we mark them, and we mark them less and less as the processing continues, as we become more like the landscape and less like the people we were when we arrived.

I am still here. Today is—

Today is. That is enough. The gauges need watching. The valves need adjusting. The amber liquid needs collecting in its vessels below. The crabs need witnessing. The long-bodied creature needs its passage, its hesitation, its fractional acknowledgment of my presence in the space it moves through.

I need these things too. I need the work, the silence, the patience that extends beyond my own small life into something larger and older and more enduring than grief. The plateau was here before me. It will be here after. The water will seep, the compounds will form, the creatures will adapt and process and continue.

I am part of this now. I am mineral and moisture and heat, steam rising and condensing, sharp edges worn smooth by the patient flow of time. I am carrying what I carry. I am still here.

The plateau sweats. The factories breathe. I return to my work, and the work receives me, and we continue together into the white glare of another day that is simply another day, processing what we are given, becoming what we become.
