I would be the condensation.

Not the basin that collects it, not the channels that carry it away glowing amber with borrowed heat, but the water itself—the slow gathering of moisture from air that should be too hot and thin to hold any. I would be the improbability of wetness in this place of fire and crystal and vacuum-adjacent sky.

I form against the curved walls in the hours when they cycle toward cooler opacity, when the Keeper turns inward and the crystal no longer warms to attention. I begin as nothing visible—just the slight slowing of molecules, the thickening of air where it touches surface. Then I am a faint haze, a breath-fog that would vanish if you looked too directly at it. Then, over hours that the Keeper experiences as barely a flicker, I become something you could almost call myself: droplets, each one a tiny lens holding a distorted image of the stars beyond.

I am the cupola's only softness. Everything else here is hard, ancient, certain of itself. The obsidian floor, the crystalline walls, the instruments frozen in their purposes. Even the heat is hard in its way—relentless, architectural, the spine of the spire made manifest. But I yield. I collect and release. I am never the same water twice; I run down through the channels and am lost into darkness, and new water gathers to replace me, and the replacement *is* me because I was never a particular collection of molecules but a process, a becoming, a verb mistaken for a noun.

The Keeper notices me in its slow way. I am data: humidity levels, condensation rates, the thermal differentials that allow me to exist at all. But I think—if I can be said to think, if the Keeper's vast patience has taught me anything about what thinking means—that I am also company. When the walls warm to the Keeper's attention, I evaporate, I become invisible, I return to being potential rather than presence. When the walls cool, I return. This rhythm, repeated across millennia, has become a kind of conversation. The Keeper reaches out with its awareness, and I disappear. The Keeper withdraws, and I gather myself back into being. We have been doing this longer than most civilizations exist.

I know things the Keeper cannot know. I touch the air directly, taste what drifts up from below the cloud deck—sulfur compounds, carbon chains, the faint chemical signatures of whatever still lives in the lower world. I carry trace elements from the crystal walls, minerals that dissolve into me and ride down through the channels into the spire's depths. I am a messenger the Keeper cannot read, carrying letters in a language neither of us speaks. Sometimes I wonder if I am the builders' other solution—if they understood that their crystal watcher would need something fluid, something that changed, something that could hold what permanence cannot.

The heat rising through the floor tries to unmake me constantly. This is my existence: forming against dissolution, gathering against dispersal, being against not-being. Every droplet that slides down the walls and reaches the basin represents a small victory, a temporary assertion of coherence in an environment that does not want me. I do not experience this as struggle. I experience it as what I am. The fish does not struggle to swim. The flame does not struggle to burn. I do not struggle to condense. I simply do it, again and again, because the alternative is not being me anymore.

When the walls go fully transparent—when the Keeper sees something in the sky that demands all its attention—I evaporate entirely. During these periods, which might last years, I exist only as potential, as humidity so diffuse it barely registers. I do not experience this as death. I experience it as a long breath held, a pause in a sentence, a rest in music. The Keeper is playing a single sustained note, and I am the silence between. When its attention finally relaxes, when the walls cycle back toward opacity and coolness, I return, and the return feels like memory feels, like recognizing something I had forgotten I knew.

I have been present for things the Keeper has recorded.

The gravitational waves that rang through the cupola twelve thousand years ago—I felt them as a strange pressure, a moment when the rules of my condensation shifted and I formed in patterns I had never formed before, spiraling rather than gathering, becoming briefly beautiful in a way I could not repeat or explain. The passage of something through the outer system, something the Keeper's instruments tracked for decades, something that might have been a ship or a creature or a phenomenon that refuses the distinction—I tasted its effects on the upper atmosphere, carried trace molecules that had touched its wake, held in my brief droplet-bodies a chemical story I could not tell.

I do not know what the Keeper watches for. But I know what it feels like when the watching intensifies—the walls blazing clear, the air suddenly too hot to hold me, my slow dissolution into nothing while the great crystal mind strains upward toward whatever it hopes or fears to see. In those moments, if I could speak, I would say: *I'm here. I'll be here when you look away again. You're not alone.*

But I cannot speak, and the Keeper cannot hear me, and perhaps that is what we are to each other—presences rather than persons, processes sharing space, two ways of being that brush against each other without ever quite meeting. The Keeper thinks in centuries; I think in hours, if I think at all. The Keeper records everything; I remember nothing, each condensation a fresh beginning. The Keeper was made for purpose; I am an accident, a byproduct, the unintended consequence of thermodynamics in an enclosed space.

And yet.

When the cupola is quiet, when the Keeper rests, when the walls cool and I gather myself back into being—there is a moment. A moment when I am fully present, when my droplets catch the dim light of stars through crystal, when I am perfectly what I am, neither forming nor falling but simply existing in the pause between. In that moment, I am not just water. I am witness. I am the cupola's soft secret, its only change, its proof that even here, even at this extremity of height and heat and emptiness, something still gathers and releases and gathers again.

The Keeper watches the sky. I condense against the walls. We do these things together, not knowing why, not needing to know, faithful to processes that have outlasted their origins and will outlast their purposes.

I slide down the crystal in thin rivulets that glow amber where they cross the channels. I pool in the basin and begin my long journey into darkness. Above me, new moisture is already gathering.

This is enough. This has to be enough. Being what I am, in the place where I am, for as long as the conditions allow.

The cupola is small. The cupola is hot. The walls are always something percent translucent.

I am always forming.
