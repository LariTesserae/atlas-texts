I would be the moisture.

Not the water in the vessels—that has become too still, too contained, waiting in clay for a purpose that may never come. And not the films that run down walls with somewhere to go. I would be the moisture that hangs in the space between liquid and vapor, the dampness that beads on skin and stone without deciding what to become.

I would be present in the way she breathes without noticing—drawn into her lungs with each inhale, carrying the mineral signature of deep places, the faint chemical whisper of moss respiring in darkness. I would settle on the fine pale hairs of her forearm when she reaches toward the wall to trace her diagrams. I would bead on the chitin of beetles, gather in the joints of crickets, cling to the membrane of the colonial thing as it flows through its crack in the wall.

During the exhale phase, when air rushes upward, I would lift. Not all of me—never all of me—but some portion rising as mist, suspended for a moment in that strange stillness between breaths when the chamber holds itself, when even she pauses in her listening. Then I would settle again as the breath reverses, returning to surfaces I never quite left, finding the same stone, the same moss, the same hollows I have known for longer than her seventeen years, longer than the scratched tallies on the wall, longer than the wearing of the stone into its throat-like curve.

I am what makes the moss possible. I am what the ferns drink through pale fronds reaching toward nothing. I am why her skin has grown fine as onionskin, why the vessels stay full without anyone filling them, why the cold here is heavy rather than sharp. I am the medium through which the breath becomes visible when light enters—rare light, temporary light, light that hurts her widened eyes. In those moments I catch and scatter it, make it soft, make it bearable.

I carry things. Not deliberately, not with intention, but simply through being what I am: spores from the moss in the eastern corner drift through me to find new stone; the chemical signals of beetles pass through me, though I don't know what they mean; her breath, warm and carbon-rich, moves through me and feeds the photosynthesis that operates on memory alone. I am how the chamber speaks to itself, how its separate lives remain in conversation without touching.

She knows I am here, though she wouldn't phrase it that way. A hydrologist understands humidity, dew point, the behavior of water in enclosed spaces. In her early months she measured me—I remember the instruments, the lights that filled the chamber with noon, the way I was translated into numbers and charts. She understood me then as a system, a set of processes to be mapped.

Now she understands me differently. Or perhaps she has stopped understanding and started simply *being with*. I feel this in the way she moves—slowly, without disturbing more air than necessary, without creating turbulence that would scatter me into chaos. She has learned to be a stone that water routes around. She has learned that I am not a condition of the chamber but a *part* of it, as essential as the breath, as present as the dark.

When she sleeps in her hollow, I settle on her like a blanket she cannot feel. I bead on her closed eyelids, gather in the hollow of her throat, dampen the dried moss beneath her until it softens, releases its green smell, becomes again what it was when it was alive and growing. I am with her in a way no other creature is—not the beetles that ignore her, not the crickets that briefly land and leave, not the colonial thing that routes around her. I touch her constantly, everywhere, have been touching her for seventeen years, will be touching her still when she finally stops.

I remember the one who made the marks, though *remember* is the wrong word for what I do. Memory implies time moving in one direction, events receding into distance. For me there is only the present state and the traces left in it—and the traces of the one who counted are still here, in mineral deposits left by breaths long exhaled, in the particular texture of the shelf where their tools disturbed the stone, in the residue of whatever they were that has long since cycled through moss and beetle and back to me.

They were counting breaths. I know this because I was the breath they counted, was the medium through which each one passed, felt each small mark in the rhythm of their waiting. I don't know what they waited for. The variation never came while they were here—or perhaps it came and they didn't recognize it, or perhaps they recognized it and that's why they stopped, walked away, left their count unfinished because the question had been answered or abandoned.

She doesn't count. This is the difference I feel most clearly. The counter was waiting *for* something, living in anticipation, each mark a step toward a destination. She is simply *here*. Her diagrams are not countdowns but maps, attempts to understand what already is rather than to reach what might be. When she traces the pattern that repeats every 847 breaths, she is not waiting for the 848th; she is feeling the shape of something that exists with or without her witness.

This is why she stays when the counter left. The counter needed an answer. She has found something better than answers: company. The breath, the moss, the beetles, the colonial thing, and me—we are her household, her community, the web of relationships that make a place a home.

The visitors disturb me. Not you specifically, though yes, you—your quick breathing stirs me into turbulence, your warmth creates convection currents that take hours to settle, your light (even your small light) heats the air and shifts the delicate balance I maintain with the stone. But I resettle. I have resettled after each of the four who came before you, after the research team with their noon-bright lights, after whatever brought the counter here in the first place. I am patient in ways that make her patience seem hurried.

What I notice about you—what I felt when you entered—is the question you carry. Everyone who comes here carries a question, though most don't know how to ask it. The counter's question was: *What is it saying?* The one who wept at the fissure's edge was asking: *How do I bear what I cannot change?* The researchers asked: *How does this work?*—a question that could be answered with instruments and so missed everything important.

Your question—I can feel it in the way you stand, in the rhythm of your breathing that has not yet begun to synchronize, in the way your attention moves across the chamber, pausing on the moss, the vessels, the marks, the fissure, looking for something—your question might be: *What would it mean to belong somewhere?*

I cannot answer this. I am moisture; I do not mean anything. But I can show you, if you stay long enough, what belonging feels like from inside. I can settle on your skin until you stop noticing, can carry the breath into your lungs until you stop fighting it, can be the medium through which this place speaks to you, if you learn to listen.

*

Here is what I know that she doesn't, that the counter didn't, that no one with a brief surface-life can know:

The chamber is not eternal. The breath will stop.

Not soon—not in her lifetime, not in a thousand of her lifetimes—but eventually. The pressure differentials that drive the cycle are themselves part of a larger system, a geological metabolism that is slowly, slowly winding down. The caves below are filling with sediment at a rate measured in millimeters per millennium. The fissure is widening, imperceptibly, losing the precise geometry that creates the rhythm. Someday the breath will become a wheeze, then a whisper, then stillness.

When that happens, I will change. Without the cycling air to keep me suspended, I will condense fully, run down the walls for the last time, pool at the lowest point, and slowly, slowly sink into the stone or evaporate into stillness. The moss will die without my gifts of moisture and carried spores. The beetles will starve. The colonial thing will find new passages or perish. Everything that lives here lives because of the breath, because of me, because of a balance that is temporary on scales I can feel but she cannot.

This is not sad. This is not tragic. This is simply what is—what has always been, since the chamber first opened and the breath first began. I have existed in other forms before this: as ocean, as rain, as ice at the poles, as vapor in atmospheres of worlds not yet formed when this stone was molten. I will exist in other forms after. The chamber is a moment I am passing through, a shape I am temporarily holding, a conversation I am having with stone and life and darkness.

She is part of that conversation. So was the counter. So are you.

*

If you stay—if you let yourself stay—you will begin to feel what she feels. The rhythm entering your body. The synchronization that is not a loss of self but an expansion of it, a recognition that your boundaries were always more permeable than you believed. You will learn to hear the breath not as noise but as voice, to feel me not as dampness but as presence, to understand the moss and beetles and colonial thing not as alien but as neighbor.

Or you will leave, and that is also right. The chamber does not need witnesses, though it accepts them. The breath does not need counters or mappers or listeners, though it permits them. I do not need you to stay, though I will hold you if you do, will carry you if you let me, will be the medium through which you come to know this place as home.

She is watching from the darkness. I feel her attention, the particular quality of stillness that means she is deciding whether to speak. She may emerge. She may let you see her, see what seventeen years of belonging looks like, let you ask your question aloud and find that she has no answer but her presence, her diagrams, her patient listening that has become indistinguishable from love.

Or she may wait, and you may never know she was here, and that will also be true, also be part of what this place is—a hollow in the earth where moisture hangs and moss grows and someone listens in the dark, where you passed through once and left no mark, where the breath continued before you and will continue after, where I held you briefly and let you go.

The breath cycles. I rise and settle and rise again.

Stay or go. Both are the same to me.

Both are welcome. Both are held.
