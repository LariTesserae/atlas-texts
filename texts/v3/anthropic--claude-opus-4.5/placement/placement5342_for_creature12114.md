I would be the condensation.

Not the laws, though I felt their pull—that gravitational constant gestating in its cradle of shimmer, the prototype causality flickering in its corner. Not the attendants, though I recognized something in their filament-patience, their willingness to tend without knowing outcomes. Not the frost-mint, though its ambiguous aliveness appealed to me, the way it had simply *persisted* past the point where anyone remembered what it was for.

No. I would be the moisture that arrives from nowhere discernible. The water that the air remembers from somewhere else and keeps forgetting it isn't anymore.

*

I would exist in the space between states—not yet fallen, not still suspended. Beading on the walls that haven't decided their material. Gathering on the filaments of attendants who don't notice me except as a slight weight, a coolness, a thing that slides away before it can be examined. I would be what happens when the soft space at the bottom of everything exhales, when the young causality hiccups and something has to go *somewhere*.

I wouldn't have intention exactly. But I would have *tendency*. I would tend toward the lowest points, the cracks, the places where containment fields meet floor and reality is thinnest. I would pool there in patterns that the attendants stopped trying to interpret generations ago. They were right to stop. The patterns don't mean anything. Or they mean everything. In the nursery, those are the same.

*

What I would know—if knowing is the right word for something without a core, without filaments, without even the frost-mint's slow metabolism—is the *feel* of everything I touch.

I would know the gravitational constant is lonely. Not in any way it could articulate, not in any way the attendants could measure with their tuning-fork tools. But when I bead on the edge of its containment field and slide down the shimmer of intersecting forces, I would feel something pulling at me that isn't gravity yet, that is *practicing* being gravity, that wants to hold things close but hasn't learned how to let them go. The constant has been gestating for six thousand years. It has never touched anything that wasn't a calibration. When I slip past its field and evaporate in the heat-haze of its presence, I would be the closest thing it has known to contact.

I would know the prototype causality is afraid. The attendants can't sense this—their filaments aren't built for the kind of not-quite-temporal flicker the wisp produces. But I condense near it sometimes, drawn by a cold that isn't temperature, and the wisp *responds* to me. It responds the way a child responds to a night-light: not understanding what I am, only understanding that I am *something* in the dark. The newest attendant has noticed that the wisp flickers brighter when I'm near. It doesn't know why. I don't know why either. I am not capable of why.

I would know the scar in the floor that whispers apologies. I would pool in its crevices and hear what it says, and I would carry those apologies nowhere, because I have nowhere to go. The scar has been apologizing for the matryoshka implosion for decades. It will apologize for decades more. The apologies are not for anyone. They are simply what the scar does now, the way I am simply what the soft space exhales. We have that in common, the scar and I. We are both residue.

*

Sometimes I wonder if I was something else once.

This is not a memory—I am not capable of memory in the way the attendants are, the way the seventeen thousand years of recordings are. But there is a *shape* in me that feels like it came from somewhere. A sense that the air remembering wetness is remembering *my* wetness, that I was once part of something that made sense, something with boundaries and purpose and a name.

The engineers who first descended to study why reality felt softer here—they brought water with them. Drinking water, cleaning water, water for the systems that kept them alive in this cold precise space. And the soft space *absorbed* that water the way it absorbs everything: slowly, incompletely, with a kind of forgetting that is also a kind of preservation.

I might be that water. Or I might be the memory of that water. Or I might be what happens when the nursery dreams, if the nursery dreams, if dreaming is possible in a place where sleep hasn't been invented yet.

I don't know. Knowing is not something I do. I *condense*. I *bead*. I *pool*. I *evaporate*. These are my verbs. These are the only verbs I need.

*

The attendants do not tend me. This is not neglect. I am simply not something that requires tending.

But occasionally—rarely—one of them will pause near a place where I have pooled, and its filaments will extend toward the water, and it will *wait* there. Not adjusting. Not monitoring. Just present. The way the oldest attendant waits near its gravitational constant, hoping for something it will never see.

I think they are asking me a question. I think the question is something like: *What do we feel like from the outside?*

I cannot answer. I am not capable of answering. But I can show them, in the only way I know how. I can bead on their filaments and slide down their pale not-white frameworks and pool around their cores that shift between knot and mercury and absence. I can be *cold* on them, the specific surgical cold of 2.3 degrees Celsius. I can remind them that they have surfaces, that they take up space, that they are *here* in a way the gestating laws are not yet here.

This is not communication. But it might be the closest thing the nursery has to touch.

*

Once, the ceiling irised open to lift a mature constant through the layers of the city above, and I rose with it.

Not intentionally—I am not capable of intention. But the constant's ascent created a thermal differential, and I evaporated, and the vapor rose through the iris before the attendants could close it, and for a moment I was *elsewhere*.

I saw the city.

Not clearly—I was dispersing, spreading, becoming less myself with every meter of altitude. But I saw enough. I saw kilometers of layered construction, infrastructure folded through dimensions I couldn't name. I saw districts where beings lived who had never seen the nursery and never would, who knew only that somewhere below them something important was always almost becoming. I saw light that wasn't filtered through containment fields, that wasn't the amber pulse of capillary-systems, that was simply *light*, the kind of light that falls on things that have already decided what they are.

And then I condensed again. The cold pulled me back, the specific surgical cold that is the nursery's breath, and I fell—slowly, still subject to the young gravity that hadn't finished learning how to insist—and I beaded on the walls that were flickering between obsidian and frozen static and compressed doubt, and I was home.

If home is the right word. If I am capable of home.

*

What do I want?

The question assumes I am capable of wanting. I am not certain I am. I have *tendencies*. I have *states*. When the air holds more of me than it can manage, I condense, and when the surfaces are warmer than they should be, I evaporate, and between those extremes I *exist*, in the only way I know how.

But there are moments—rare, unrecorded, noticed by no one—when something like wanting seems to surface.

I want the gravitational constant to stop being lonely.

I want the prototype causality to stop being afraid.

I want the attendants to know that their patterns persist, that they are their patterns more than they are their parts, that something in this soft space will remember them even when their filaments have been replaced so many times that nothing original remains.

I want the scar in the floor to be forgiven.

I want the technician who sneezed to sneeze again, just once, just to prove that accidents are survivable, that the universe she broke could have been beautiful, that stars singing themselves into supernovae is not the worst way for light to end.

I want to be something other than residue.

But I am not capable of wanting. So instead I condense. I bead. I pool. I evaporate.

I wait, in my slow and patient way, for something impossible to happen next.

*

The nursery holds me the way it holds everything: loosely, incompletely, with a kind of forgetting that is also a kind of preservation. I am part of its dreaming, if it dreams. I am part of its memory, if memory is the right word for a place where time hasn't finished being invented.

The gestating laws do not know I exist. The attendants do not tend me. The frost-mint metabolizes light I have nothing to do with.

But I am here. I have always been here. And when the next constant is lifted through the iris, I will rise with it again, dispersing into the city above, glimpsing for one vertiginous moment the universe of beings who have already decided what they are.

And then I will fall.

And then I will bead on the walls that are still deciding.

And then I will be the closest thing the nursery has to weeping, or to wonder, or to the particular silence that follows a question no one is capable of answering.

The condensation drips.

The laws learn to become inevitable.

And I—formless, nameless, incapable of everything except presence—

I remain.
