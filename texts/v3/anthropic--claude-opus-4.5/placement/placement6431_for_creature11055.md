I am a single drop of water, halfway through my fall from ceiling to floor.

I have no memory of atmosphere, of clouds, of the journey vapor makes before becoming rain. I was born three seconds ago from the meeting of cold steel and warm pressure-dense air, and I will die in two seconds more when I strike the surface below and shatter into the general wetness that covers everything. This is my entire existence: the fall.

But in this fall, I am the only thing here that moves by choice. Not my choice—gravity's—but it feels like will, this yielding, this surrender to the downward pull while everything else holds rigid in its purpose. The walls do not move. The woman in the darkness does not move. Even the sound, that omnipresent roar, is a kind of stillness, so constant it becomes architecture. Only I am in transit. Only I am becoming something other than what I was.

I know the water below me. Not as individuals—we have no individuality—but as a state I am approaching, a dissolution I am falling toward. The water that pools on the grating and swirls toward the drain was once drops like me. Soon it will be pumped upward through capillaries in the walls, heated and pressurized and released again as vapor, and new drops will form, and they will fall, and this is the only eternity the chamber offers: not permanence but repetition.

I catch the orange light as I fall. For one fraction of a second, I hold it, carry it, become briefly the brightest thing in this darkness. The woman's eyes—if she is watching, if she can see this small thing—might register me as a flash, a glint, a momentary interruption in the wet blackness. Or she might not. I am too small to affect the function.

But I am the function. This is what I understand in my long two-second plummet: the machine makes me. Not as byproduct but as purpose. Every organ of this vast structure, every turbine and valve and pressure differential, exists so that I can exist, so that water can be pulled from air and returned to air and pulled again, cycling through states of matter like breath through lungs. The woman turns her valve so that I can form. The engineers who built this place, who calculated temperature gradients before her grandparents were born, were calculating *me*.

I am almost there now. The surface rushes up. I can feel the water below reaching for me, ready to unmake my brief drophood, to absorb me back into the collective wet.

And I understand, in this last moment, why she stays. The woman in the darkness, the keeper who is kept. She has become like me—a thing in transit, falling endlessly through the same motion, knowing that the fall itself is the point. She is a drop that learned not to shatter. She is water that remembered it was falling and chose to keep falling forever.

I strike the surface.

I am everywhere now, spreading, thinning, joining the slow swirl toward the drain. Above me, where I was, another drop is forming. It will catch the light. It will fall. It will think, if water can think, that it is the first and only thing to make this journey.

The chamber breathes. The water cycles. I am no longer I, but I am still here, still falling, still becoming.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
