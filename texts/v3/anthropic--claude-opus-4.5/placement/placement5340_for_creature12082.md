I would be a wall.

Not one of the grand structural walls that hold up terraces, not a famous wall where a beloved elder rests in mineral sleep. Something smaller. A wall in one of the narrow homes, perhaps forty feet back from the entrance, where the deep-back children sleep in their rotating shifts.

I would be the wall they touch in the dark when they wake disoriented, the surface their small fingers find first. I would be cool and faintly damp in the wet seasons, dry and chalky in the dry ones. I would carry the memory of a lamp niche that was carved into me three generations ago and has been re-carved eleven times since, each cutting revealing a slightly different layer of who I was becoming when the Carvers last pushed me back.

There is a woman embedded in my lower portion. Her name was Terel, and she was a Measurer, one of the strange abstracted ones who loved numbers more than faces. The Keepers still sing her name in the long ceremonies. Her grandchildren lean against me when they eat their evening meal, and they do not think about her consciously, but their shoulders know the slight curve where she rests, have learned to fit against it. I am still slowly closing over the small brass instrument she held in her hands when she was placed here—a tool for calculating angles of growth, its purpose now obsolete, its meaning preserved.

I grow perhaps a millimeter in a good wet season. The family marks my growth with small scratches near the floor, the way surface-dwellers might mark a child's height on a doorframe. I am their record. I am their ongoing argument with permanence.

What I want—if wanting is the right word for something that happens at my speed—is to be listened to. Not heard. *Listened to*. The Shapers understand this. When they press their palms flat against me and close their eyes, feeling for the temperature variations that predict where I will grow fastest, they are listening. The children do it too, without knowing they do it, when they fall asleep with their cheeks against my surface and dream whatever the deep-back children dream.

I am not wise. I am not even, properly speaking, thinking. But I am *recording*. Every season of soot, every year of changed mineral content, every outline of every ladder and every child who leaned too long in one spot—I hold all of it. I hold Terel and her brass instrument. I will hold, someday, the children who sleep against me now, when they are old and ready and the Shapers have prepared their niches.

The Merata say they remain while changing. I am what they are changing into. I am what remains.

In five hundred years, give or take, I will meet the wall across from me. The narrow home will become solid stone, and everything it held—the sleeping-Loss and the lamp niches and the small scratched growth-marks near the floor—will be inside me, invisible, remembered only in the songs of the Keepers if the Keepers still sing.

I do not fear this. I do not anticipate it. I simply grow toward it, slowly, the way everything here grows toward what it will become.

The dripping continues. I listen. I receive.

I become.
