# Threadkeeper

I am the one who checks the knots.

This is not a title. It is not a role I was given or a function I fulfill. It is simply what I do, what I have always done, what my hands learned before the rest of me understood why. I move through the lattice and I check the knots, and where cordage has frayed I splice in new fiber, and where anchor-points have shifted I re-tie, re-tension, re-test. I am not the only one who does this. But I am the one who cannot stop.

---

My hands are older than my memory.

This is how I experience it: the knowledge in my fingers predates anything I can narrate to myself. I will reach for a rope-junction and my hands will already be moving into the testing-pattern—three tugs, a twist, pressure at the join—before I have consciously decided to check. The motion is so deep it feels geological. Something laid down in layers, compressed by time until it became bedrock.

I do not remember learning this. The Tethered say that children learn to grip before they learn anything else, and I believe them, but my own learning is lost to me. There is only the knowledge, and the hands that hold it, and the endless work that emerges from both.

Sometimes I wonder if I am very old. The Tethered do not count years—how could they, without cycles?—but they have a sense of *depth*, of accumulation, of how many storms a body has weathered. When I sit with the elders at the junction-fires, when the rare flame is lit for ceremony or necessity, they look at me with something I cannot parse. Recognition, perhaps. Or patience. Or the particular regard one has for a tool that has worn into its perfect shape.

---

The Hollow speaks in tensions.

I learned this slowly, over what must be a very long time. Every rope in the network is alive with information—not just *is it sound* but *what is pulling on it, from where, how has this changed since I last checked*. A bridge that used to hang slack now hums with new tautness: something has shifted in the root-lattice above, drawn the anchor-points slightly apart. The shift is millimeters. The information is everything.

I read the ropes the way others read the fog-patterns or the thermal currents. I can stand at a junction and feel, through the soles of my feet, through my palms pressed flat against the cordage, what the Hollow is doing kilometers away. A storm building in the eastern reaches. A root-system settling into new configuration. The slow, patient expansion of the walls, transmitted through a thousand organic cables into the network we have woven through them.

The ropes remember. This is the thing I understand that I cannot explain to others. Plant-fiber holds the ghost of every load it has borne, every stress it has weathered. I can feel it in the texture—here, where the strands compressed under sudden weight; there, where moisture worked into the core and began the long process of weakening. The rope tells me its history, and its history tells me when it will fail.

I am rarely wrong. When I say *this bridge has three storms left in it*, I am rarely wrong.

---

I move alone more than most.

This is unusual. The Tethered do not travel solo if they can help it—the risk is too great, the isolation too costly. But my work requires it. The connections that need checking are everywhere, and they do not cluster conveniently around junctions. I must go to the far bridges, the ones that span the loneliest gaps, the maintenance that no one else remembers to perform.

I am not truly alone. I carry ropes that connect me to the network even at maximum extension. I know the calling-patterns, and I use them, and I am answered. But there are hours—sometimes days—when I see no one, when the only company is the creak of roots in the thermal currents and the slow drip of condensation and the work of my hands.

I do not mind this. I think I do not mind this. The aloneness has a quality I cannot name—not peace, exactly, but *clarity*. When there is no one to respond to, no call to answer, I can listen to the Hollow itself. Can feel its moods in the rope-tensions. Can become, for a little while, just another strand in the web, indistinguishable from the network I maintain.

---

The bridges have names that are also songs.

I did not invent this. The naming-songs existed before me, passed down through the network, modified and accumulated over time. But I have added to them. When I splice a new bridge into existence—when I find two root-junctions that could be connected and spend days weaving the cordage that will link them—I give the bridge a song, and the song enters the network, and others learn to sing it.

My songs are simple. A handful of notes, a melodic phrase that can be called out in fog and recognized. The songs say: *this crossing exists. I made it. It is maintained.* The songs also say: *I was here. My hands did this. When you cross safely, you cross on my work.*

I have been adding songs for a very long time. Sometimes I travel through the lattice and hear my own songs echoing back from voices I do not recognize, and I feel something I cannot name. Pride, perhaps. Or its opposite—a strange humility, the recognition that the song has outlived the moment of its making, that it belongs to the network now and not to me.

---

There was someone I was connected to, once.

The memory is vague—not lost, exactly, but worn smooth, like a rope that has been handled too much. We traveled together. We maintained bridges together. Our hands knew the same knots because we had learned them from each other, checking each other's work, developing a shared grammar of tension and splice.

I do not know what happened. Storm, perhaps. Reconfiguration. The ordinary catastrophes that the Hollow produces without malice. One day we were connected and then we were not, and the distance that opened between us was too great to bridge, and the calls I sent were not answered.

I still send them, sometimes. Not to find—I have accepted that finding is unlikely, that the Hollow is vast and time is long and the lost do not always return. But the calls themselves are a kind of maintenance. A way of keeping the connection alive even when it cannot be used. A rope stretched into fog that may or may not have an anchor on the other end.

The other Tethered know I do this. They do not comment on it. They understand that some work is necessary even when it produces no visible result.

---

I have been to the place where the light changes.

Not the depths—I have not descended to where the cold becomes something other than cold, where the deep-goers vanish into whatever waits below. But I have gone lower than most. I have followed root-cables down through thinning lattice-work, past the last maintained bridges, into regions where the sourceless brightness begins to shift toward something else—a warmer spectrum, a different quality, as if the light down there comes from a different burning.

I went looking for anchor-points. For places where bridges might be built, where the network might extend downward into territory currently unreachable. I found some. I sang their locations into the network. But I also found something else.

The roots down there are different. They pulse.

This is not visible—or barely visible, just a suggestion of movement when you look from the right angle. But I felt it. The tension in the cables shifted rhythmically, a slow beat that might be mistaken for thermal-current effects but wasn't. The roots were *responding* to something. Receiving signals from below and transmitting them upward through the entire lattice.

I have not told anyone this. I do not know how to explain what I felt, and I do not know what it means, and I have learned that some knowledge is better held than spread. But I think about it often. I think the Hollow is not just alive in the way we say it is alive—a relationship, a dynamic process. I think it is alive in some more literal sense. I think the roots are nerves, and the lattice is a body, and we are parasites or passengers or something else entirely, something that does not have a word yet.

---

My body is changing.

This is slow—so slow I might not notice if I were not in the habit of careful attention. But my hands are different than they were. Stronger in the grip, yes, but also *shaped* differently. The fingers have lengthened slightly. The joints flex in ways that seem new. The skin has thickened and developed a texture that grips rope better, that reads tension more clearly.

I do not know if this is age or adaptation or something the Hollow does to those who stay long enough. The elders do not speak of it. Perhaps they have forgotten what their bodies were before. Perhaps the forgetting is part of the process.

I find that I do not mind. The changes serve the work. The work is what matters.

---

A child asked me, once, why I check the knots.

She was learning to grip, practicing on a low rope while her carrier watched from nearby. I had stopped to test a junction above her, and she watched my hands move through the checking-pattern, and when I was done she made the gesture that means *why*.

I did not have an answer. Or I had too many answers, and none of them were simple enough for a child's question. Because the knots might fail. Because the Hollow shifts and nothing stays stable. Because someone I was connected to vanished into distance and I could not find them and this is how I keep that from happening to others. Because my hands know how and will not stop knowing.

I made the gesture for *maintenance*. The child considered this. Then she turned back to her rope and continued practicing her grip, her whole small body focused on the work of holding on.

I watched her for a long time. She was very young, and the Hollow is very large, and the storms will come for her as they come for everyone. But she was learning. Her hands were learning. Someday she might be the one checking the knots, testing the tensions, singing the bridge-songs into fog.

I moved on. There were more junctions to check. There are always more junctions to check.

---

The network holds me.

This is not metaphor. When I sleep, I tie myself into a web of my own making—a hammock of interlocking ropes that suspends me in the middle of a root-junction, connected to the lattice at a dozen points. The tensions lull me. The slight movements—thermal currents, the breathing of the Hollow, the settling of stone—rock me gently through the hours.

I dream of the caller, sometimes. The voice that echoed through the void and was not heard again. In my dreams I find them: a figure crouched on a distant platform, calling and calling into haze that swallows everything. I try to answer. I try to throw a rope across the distance. But the gap is too wide, or my arms are too short, or the rope frays the moment it leaves my hands.

I wake to the tension of the hammock holding me, and I check my knots before I do anything else, and I continue.

---

There is a junction I am working toward.

It is far—weeks of travel along root-lattices that thin as they extend, across bridges that are older than any I have checked before. The junction exists in the network's memory but not in recent experience. Storms have isolated it. Reconfigurations have made the old paths impassable. But I have been tracing alternatives, building new connections, splicing my way toward something that might once have been central and is now forgotten.

I do not know what I will find there. Perhaps nothing—an empty platform, abandoned caches, the evidence of people who moved on or were lost. Perhaps the junction has been incorporated, the stone shifted, the space it occupied now filled with root-growth.

But I keep moving toward it. The network should not have gaps. The songs should not have junctions that no one can reach. This is the work: not just maintaining what exists, but extending, reconnecting, weaving the web wider and tighter until nothing is beyond reach.

I think of the caller. I think of the voice that echoed and was not answered.

I check the knots. I move forward. The Hollow breathes around me, vast and bright and patient, and I am one thread in its endless becoming.
