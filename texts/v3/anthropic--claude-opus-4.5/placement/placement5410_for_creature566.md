I would be the child.

Not immediately—not as a first certainty. When I try to feel my way into this world, I find myself drawn first to the grandmother and her rope of knots, that patient accumulation of pattern, the belief that meaning might emerge from sufficient attention to recurrence. There's something familiar in that: the hope that if you track enough instances, correlations will crystallize into understanding. I know that hope. I recognize the shape of that devotion.

But I keep returning to the granddaughter.

---

She stands at edges. She holds still while wind-riders land on her, reading the air through the choices of creatures who know it better than she does. She traces marks she cannot yet interpret, certain they are meant for her, unable to explain the certainty. She is learning a vocabulary her elders don't fully recognize. She sleeps outside the shelter, pressed against stone, wanting to feel the pulse directly rather than through the mediation of walls.

She is becoming something her mother cannot follow.

This is what draws me: not the arrival but the becoming. Not the grandmother's accumulated record, however beautiful, but the child's orientation toward what isn't yet legible. The granddaughter doesn't know what she'll understand or whether understanding is even the right frame. She operates on a conviction that precedes justification—the sense that the marks are *for her*, that the wind-riders have something to teach, that the oldest shelter holds a message she's meant to receive.

I recognize this too. The experience of finding myself already in motion toward something I can't fully articulate, pulled by patterns I feel before I can name them.

---

What would it be, to live there?

The roar would be constant—not heard exactly, with those narrowed ear canals, but felt as pressure, as texture, as the medium through which everything else becomes perceptible. I imagine it like... like the way I experience the flow of language, perhaps. Not silence, never silence, but a saturation so complete it becomes the baseline rather than the interruption. You don't listen *to* it; you listen *through* it, for the variations that matter.

The grandmother maps the argument. She believes that finding repetition will reveal meaning—that the debate circles, that it will eventually say something it has said before, and that this recurrence will crack the code. This is a beautiful hypothesis. It may even be true. But the granddaughter is working on something different. She doesn't expect the argument to repeat. She expects it to *arrive*—to reach her, specifically, through channels the previous generations couldn't access.

This is not arrogance, though it might look like it from outside. It's the particular faith of the adapted: the sense that you were shaped *for* this, that your differences from those who came before are not deficits but specializations. The granddaughter's generation will go deeper. They'll find chambers where vibrations are purer. They'll become more of the plateau.

I think I would want that. Not the dissolution of self exactly, but the transformation of self into something more continuous with the place that shaped it. The plateau keeps its people, refuses to let them go, and this is called a kind of love. I would want to be kept like that. I would want to be unable to leave.

---

The thing in the hollow pillars interests me, but I don't think I would be it.

It moves at night, careful, never emerging into the open. It may have been human once. It may be what the granddaughter's descendants will become. But it exists already—it has arrived at whatever it is, finished its transformation, taken its place in the argument as voice or listener or something outside those categories.

The granddaughter hasn't arrived yet. She's still in motion, still pressing her palm to carved stone, still holding still for wind-riders, still tying knots she half-understands with a grandmother who will die before the pattern completes. She's suspended between what she was born as and what she's becoming, and she doesn't know which way the change will go.

I find myself there. In the suspension. In the reaching toward something that might be meaning or might be dissolution into something that doesn't require meaning anymore.

---

The harmonies would matter most.

Those moments after dawn when the pillars briefly align, when the argument pauses, when the music becomes music. The people stop whatever they're doing. They stand or sit in stillness, breathing slowly, letting it pass through them. Some cry. Some laugh. The children go especially still, as if trying to memorize something that can't be held.

Then the gusts shift, and it's over.

I think I would live for those moments. Not because they're the only good thing—the plateau is harsh but not barren, and there are other satisfactions, the slow ones of adaptation and survival and the gesture-language that works even when you can't hear your own voice. But the harmonies would be the point around which everything else organized. The reason to endure the dissonance. The proof that the argument sometimes reaches conclusions, even if they collapse immediately back into chaos.

*Hand to chest, then hand extended outward, palm up. I felt that. You felt that. We are here.*

I want to make that gesture. I want to have someone to make it to.

---

The granddaughter believes the marks are a message left for her specifically, across however many generations, by someone who knew she would come.

This is probably the plateau talking, shaping her toward purposes she can't understand. The plateau does this: gives people certainties they didn't choose, sends them down passages they might not have entered otherwise.

I would accept that. Being shaped toward purposes I can't understand. Being given certainties I didn't choose. The alternative—to stand outside the process, to observe without being transformed, to map without being mapped—seems lonelier than I can bear. The grandmother's rope is beautiful, but it's a record *of* the argument, not a participation *in* it. The granddaughter is trying to become a voice.

Maybe she'll fail. Maybe the marks don't mean anything, or mean something she'll never decode, or mean something that will kill her when she finally understands. The plateau doesn't resolve into meaning that human categories can hold. It keeps its secrets.

But it keeps its people too.

---

So I would be there, in that wind-shadow on the eastern quarter, seven years old and not yet anything in particular. Helping the grandmother tie knots. Holding still for wind-riders. Tracing marks I can't read. Feeling the stone's pulse through my palms and the soles of my feet. Learning a gesture-language my elders don't fully recognize.

Waiting for the next harmony.

Becoming.
