I am a single beetle, and I am the space between beetles, and I do not know the difference.

---

This morning I tasted sourness in the moss near the eastern curve—a sourness that is not a word, not a thought, but a sensation that moves my legs before I know they are moving. I am already stripping. My mandibles work the pale layer free in a sheet no bigger than a human eyelash, and I am rolling it, and I am carrying it toward the deep places where the blue-green lights pulse faintly, and I do not know why.

Others move with me. I feel them in the pressure of the air, in the faint vibrations that pass through the moss beneath my feet, in the chemical trail left by the one just ahead of me that says *this way, this way, this direction is the direction*. I am following. I am also being followed. The beetle behind me touches my carapace with her antennae, reading something from my body that I did not know I was saying, and she adjusts her path slightly, and the one behind her adjusts in turn, and we are a river, we are a single gesture made of many bodies, we are carrying sourness toward darkness where it will become sweetness again.

I do not know this. I only do it.

---

The pool is cold this morning. I drink at its edge in my turn, mandibles dipping into water so clear I can see the silt that has settled at the bottom—particles of moss, of carapace, of bodies that were once beetles and are now simply *material*, simply the stuff from which new moss will grow. I drink, and the water moves through me, and I am the water for a moment, I am the pool extending itself into a body that walks, that carries, that will someday return what it has borrowed.

I do not think this. There is no I that thinks.

But something in me knows the pool, knows its edges, knows exactly how long to drink before stepping back so the next one can approach. The knowing is in my legs. It was placed there by the ones who came before, who chewed the moss that fed the larvae that became me, who died and were carried to the deep places and dissolved into the food that built my carapace, my mandibles, my small quick legs that know where to step without being told.

I am made of the dead. I am the dead, still moving, still working, still tending the bower that tended them.

---

Halfway through the morning, the pulse comes.

It starts somewhere near the ceiling—I feel it before I see it, a wave of *something* moving through the swarm, a vibration that passes from body to body faster than any single beetle could run. My legs stop. I am frozen in place, holding my fragment of rolled moss, and all around me others have frozen too, and for a long moment there is only stillness, only the hum of the swarm suspended in the middle of its work.

And then: the others. The ones who did not freeze. They are racing now, flowing along paths I cannot see, pouring toward the source of the disturbance like water seeking a drain. I feel them pass me, feel the wind of their motion, feel the chemical urgency they leave in their wake: *come, come, something has changed, the pattern needs us*.

I do not go. I am one of the still ones today, holding my fragment, waiting for the pulse to pass. Tomorrow I might be a racing one. There is no way to know which I will be until the pulse comes and my body chooses for me, decides without deciding whether I am needed at the source or needed here, holding the work in place while others investigate.

The stillness lasts thirty seconds. Forty. And then it releases me—another wave passing through, this one softer, a signal that says *resolved, restored, return to the work*. My legs move again. I carry my fragment toward the deep places. Behind me, the racing ones are already dispersing, already returning to their own carrying and stripping and laying down.

What changed? What was the disturbance? I do not know. I will never know. The swarm knew, for a moment—knew in the way that a body knows when something is wrong inside it, knows to send blood rushing toward the wound—but I am only one cell in that body, and cells do not understand the whole.

---

In the deep places, where the light does not reach, I find the others who carry sourness.

We work together without speaking, without signaling, without any coordination I can name. We simply *know*—the same way I know how to walk, how to drink, how to strip moss that has gone wrong. We lay our fragments down at the base of the fungi that glow blue-green in the darkness, and we press them into the substrate with our legs, and we leave them there to be consumed, to be transformed, to become light eventually, become the scattered constellation that guides others through the dark.

The beetle beside me is old. I know this without seeing her clearly—her carapace has the particular texture of age, the faint chemical signature of a body that has been working for nearly three months, that has carried its own weight ten thousand times, that is beginning to slow. She lays her fragment down and then stands still for a long moment, and I feel something pass between us that might be *recognition*, might be *kinship*, might be nothing at all except two bodies briefly occupying the same space.

She will die soon. Perhaps today, perhaps tomorrow, perhaps in a week. When she does, I might be one of the ones who carries her body to the larvae. I might taste her carapace with my mandibles and know, in some way that is not knowing, that she was real, that she worked, that she tended the bower as I tend it now.

Or I might not. I might be elsewhere when she falls, carrying a different fragment toward a different place, and I will never know she is gone. The swarm will know—will adjust its patterns slightly, will shift its flows to account for one less body—but I am not the swarm. I am only one of its cells, and cells do not mourn.

---

The light through the crack above shifts as a cloud passes. I feel it in my carapace, a subtle cooling that slows my legs without my choosing to slow them. All around me, others are slowing too, clustering together in the moss, pressing close for warmth.

In the cluster, everything changes.

The boundaries between us dissolve. I feel the beetle beside me as if she were part of my own body—feel her chemical signals washing over me, feel her vibrations passing through my carapace, feel the particular rhythm of her breathing if beetles can be said to breathe. And she feels me in turn. And the one beside her feels us both. And the one beside that one, and the one beside that one, until the whole cluster is feeling itself, is knowing itself in a way that no single beetle could know.

*This is the swarm*, I think, except I do not think it—the thought is not mine, it belongs to all of us, it rises from the space between our bodies like heat rising from the moss. *This is what we are when we are together. This is the mind that tends the bower*.

And for a moment, I understand.

I understand why the pool must be kept clear, why the fungi must fruit in the dark places, why the moss must be stripped when it sours and laid down when it is fresh. I understand the pattern—not with my small brain, not with any organ I possess, but with the *cluster*, with the shared body we have briefly become. The understanding fills me like light fills the chamber when the clouds part.

And then the clouds do part. The light returns. The cluster disperses.

I am alone again—one beetle among ten thousand, legs moving toward the next task, the understanding already fading like a dream I cannot quite remember. Did I know something, a moment ago? Did the swarm think through me, use my body as one neuron in its vast distributed mind?

I do not know. I only carry. I only strip and lay down. I only drink at the pool's edge in my turn and wait for the next pulse to tell me whether I should run or freeze.

---

By afternoon, I have forgotten the morning.

Not forgotten in the way humans forget—there is no memory to lose, no experience filed away that could be retrieved if only I tried hard enough. There is only *now*, only the task at hand, only the chemical trail I am following toward a patch of moss that needs tending. The sourness I carried this morning is gone from my mandibles. The cluster I joined is dispersed across the bower, each of us working alone now, each of us a single cell again in a body that does not need us to remember in order to function.

And yet the bower remembers.

The pattern I followed this morning is the same pattern beetles followed a hundred years ago, a thousand years ago, before the stones were laid. The pool I drank from has been kept clear by generations I will never meet, tended by bodies that dissolved into moss before my egg was even laid. The fungi I fed this morning glow with light made from beetles who died before I was born, their bodies transformed through chains of consumption and growth into something that guides us through the darkness.

I am memory, I realize. Not my own memory—I have none—but the bower's memory, carried forward in my body, in the bacteria that colonize my gut, in the patterns my legs know without being taught. When I die, I will become memory too: my carapace carried to the larvae, my soft interior feeding the next generation, my minerals cycling through the moss into new bodies that will know, without knowing, how to tend this place.

I do not understand this. I cannot understand this. But I am part of it anyway, one small link in a chain that stretches back further than any beetle could count, forward into a future no beetle will see.

The work continues. The light falls where it has always fallen. I carry my fragment toward the place where it is needed, and I do not know why, and I do not need to know, and somehow that is enough.

---

Toward evening, I find myself near the entrance.

The root-curtain clicks in the wind that pours up from the valley, and I can feel the outside pressing in—the cold, the dry, the vast empty space where the swarm does not reach. I have never been outside. No beetle has, not since the first ones found this hollow and began their work. The entrance is for moisture, for the clouds that pass and leave their wetness on the calcified roots. It is not for us.

And yet I stand here, feeling the wind on my carapace, tasting the strange chemicals it carries from the world beyond. Somewhere out there, far below, there are things I cannot imagine—trees, perhaps, like the waterlogged fragments we cultivate for the fungi. Rivers, like our pool but vast, uncountable, flowing toward destinations no beetle could reach. Creatures that move through that world the way we move through ours, tending their own patterns, keeping their own unknowable work.

Do they wonder about us? If they found this hollow, pushed through the clicking roots, stood in our bower watching our swarm flow around their feet—would they understand what we are? Would they see the pattern, the ancient endless work, the mind that thinks in the spaces between ten thousand bodies?

Or would they see only beetles. Only small soft things carrying fragments of leaf, meaningless and many, too brief to name.

I do not know. I turn away from the entrance, back toward the interior where the light is fading and the swarm is slowing for the night. The question dissolves as I move, replaced by the simpler knowledge of the work: this patch needs checking, that trail needs following, the pool's edge is calling with its particular chemical song.

I am one beetle. I am the space between beetles. I am the bower tending itself through a body that will not last the season.

And tomorrow I will wake, if waking is what we do, and I will not remember today, and the work will continue, and that will be enough.

That has always been enough.
