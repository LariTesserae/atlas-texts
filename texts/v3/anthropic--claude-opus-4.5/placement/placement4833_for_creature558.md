I would be one of the cats.

Not a grand or particularly notable one—not one of the named toms whose territories the Factors track with diplomatic gravity, nor one of the ancient queens who have held the same rooftop for decades. I would be younger, perhaps four or five years into my existence, with gray fur that looks almost blue in the amber factory light, and a white patch on my chest that resembles nothing in particular despite what the children of the Seventh District insist.

I would not understand, in any way that could be called understanding, why my watching matters. I would simply watch because watching is what I do—the grinding wheels in Chamber Nine, the slow crystalline beading of moisture on the stabilization vents, the particular way that Witness Errath Solenne's shadow sometimes forgets to follow her. I would watch these things with the same attention I give to the stone mice that nest in the gap behind the old mill's eastern wall, which is to say: completely, involuntarily, with the whole of whatever I am.

The Factors would know me. Orellen Marsh would have noted me in her census two years ago, assigned me a registry number that I would never answer to, mapped my territory as a minor wedge of the Seventh District's industrial quarter—Chamber Nine's exterior, the warm exhaust grates behind the processing hall, the ledge above the candlemaker's shop where I sleep when the condensation fronts come heavy and cold. She would consider me adequate. Functional. One small node in the infrastructure of coherence.

I would not know that I am infrastructure. I would know only that certain places feel more solid when I sit in them, that my presence in the observation alcove during third shift settles something that was unsettled, that the Witnesses nod to me when I pass and sometimes leave small offerings of dried fish near my preferred sunning spots. I would accept these as my due without understanding them as payment.

The thing I would know, if knowing is even the right word for the dim apprehension that passes for thought behind my eyes—the thing I would *feel*—is that the world is always almost something else. That the stone beneath my paws is a decision being made, not a fact already settled. I would feel this the way I feel warmth or hunger: as a condition of existence, unremarkable because I have never known anything different.

When I watch the substrate in the processing chambers, when my gaze settles on matter that has not yet fully committed to being matter, I would not experience this as work or duty or sacred calling. I would experience it as *looking at a thing*. The looking would satisfy something in me the way catching a mouse satisfies something—a completion, a rightness, a small hunger fed.

But the substrate would respond to my looking differently than it responds to human watching. The Witnesses know this, have measured it, have written papers about feline observation that fill shelves in the guild archives. They do not know *why* my attention weighs more heavily than theirs, why three of them are needed to do what I do alone. They have theories: that cats perceive time differently, that our vision registers wavelengths invisible to humans, that we lack the capacity for doubt that dilutes human observation. None of their theories are quite right, but none of them are entirely wrong either.

What I would know, in my dim and wordless way, is simply this: when I look at a thing, I do not wonder whether it is really there. I do not consider alternatives. I do not imagine it otherwise. I see what I see with a completeness that humans, with their complicated minds and their capacity for abstraction, cannot quite achieve. My certainty is not a choice or a skill. It is simply what I am.

And so the uncertain matter, under my gaze, finds it easier to choose existence. Not because I will it to exist—I am a cat; I will nothing except food and warmth and the occasional skirmish over territory. But because my looking contains no question, the matter being looked at finds no excuse for equivocation.

---

I would have my routines.

Dawn would find me on the exhaust grate behind Chamber Nine, where the overnight processing cycle vents its final warmth into the cool mountain air. I would wash myself there, methodically, as the shift change happens inside—the night Witnesses filing out with their distant eyes, the day Witnesses filing in with their thermos flasks and their counting rituals. Some of them would greet me. The younger ones, still new enough to find the cats remarkable rather than simply *there*, would sometimes try to pet me. I would permit this or not depending on obscure criteria that I myself could not articulate.

Midmorning I would hunt, or pretend to hunt—the stone mice are too quick for me most days, and anyway the Factor-maintained feeding stations ensure I am never truly hungry. But the hunting matters in some way I would not question. The stalking, the waiting, the explosive burst of motion that rarely catches anything but always *could*. The mice exist more firmly for being hunted, and I exist more firmly for hunting them. This is the economy of attention that Vorrh-Kaleth runs on, though I would not know to call it that.

The condensation fronts would determine my afternoons. When the moisture comes heavy, I would retreat to my ledge above the candlemaker's, curling into myself against the chill, watching the water bead and trickle down the street below. The dampness would flatten my fur and irritate me, but I would not seek true shelter—some part of me would understand that my watching is needed more during the fronts, that the instability rides in on the moisture, that my presence on that ledge helps anchor the street beneath.

The candlemaker, a woman named Brel who lost three fingers to a stabilization accident years before I was born, would sometimes open her window and speak to me. I would not understand her words, but I would understand her tone—the loneliness in it, the need to be heard by something even if that something could not respond. I would watch her with my full attention, not because I cared about her particularly, but because watching is what I do. She would seem calmer after. More solid.

In the evenings, when the factory rhythms shift toward the deep processing cycles and the purring chorus rises from rooftops across the city, I would add my voice to it. Not consciously—I am a cat; nothing I do is fully conscious in the way humans mean the word. But something in me would respond to the harmonics, would feel the rightness of many small vibrations joining into something larger. The city would hum with us, and the humming would settle things that the day's work had left unsettled.

---

The other cats would know me, in the way cats know each other—by scent, by territory, by the precise calibration of dominance and deference that governs our interactions. The queen of the Seventh District, an enormous orange creature with a torn ear and a gaze like ancient stone, would tolerate my presence in her domain. We would pass each other on the rooftops sometimes, and there would be a moment of mutual assessment, and then we would continue on our separate ways.

I would not have friends. Friendship is a human concept, or perhaps a dog concept—some social species thing that cats do not quite achieve. But I would have... relationships. Adjacencies. The tom who shares the eastern edge of my territory, with whom I have an understanding about the exhaust grate that involves complex negotiations every autumn when the warmth becomes more precious. The kitten from the litter born behind the mill last spring, who follows me sometimes and whom I tolerate because chasing her away would take more energy than ignoring her.

The humans would be more present in my life than I would be in theirs. Orellen Marsh would note me in her ledgers, but she would not think about me between census updates. The Witnesses of Chamber Nine would recognize me, would greet me with nods, would leave fish—but I would be infrastructure to them, a piece of the machinery, not a being with an inner life worth wondering about.

This would not bother me because nothing bothers a cat in quite the way things bother humans. I would be content, in my limited fashion, with my routines and my territory and my watching.

But sometimes—

Sometimes, when the light was right, when the morning mist hadn't yet burned off and the factory glow caught it strangely, I would see things that I could not integrate into my understanding of the world. Flickers at the edge of vision. A building that looked, for just a moment, like a different building. A street that seemed to lead somewhere else before it remembered where it was supposed to go.

The humans would call this instability. They would map it, track it, process it through their mills until it settled. But I would not have words for it. I would only have a feeling—a slight wrongness, a discord in the texture of existence, something that my watching was needed to resolve.

And I would watch. Not understanding why, not knowing what I was doing, just... looking. Settling my gaze on the flickering thing until it stopped flickering. Observing without doubt, without question, without any of the mental noise that makes human perception so provisional.

The world would become more solid under my attention. This is what I would be for. This is what the city would need from me.

And then I would groom my paw, or hunt a mouse, or find a patch of warmth to sleep in, and the moment of strangeness would pass, and I would forget it had ever happened.

---

If I could speak—which I cannot, which no cat can—I might say that existence feels like a vibration. A hum that runs through everything, always on the edge of becoming discordant, always requiring attention to stay in tune. I might say that my purpose is simply to be a point of stillness in that vibration, a place where the hum can find its center.

But I cannot speak. So I say nothing. I watch, and I wait, and I add my small purring voice to the chorus that keeps the mountain real.

I am a gray cat with a white patch on my chest. I live in the Seventh District of Vorrh-Kaleth. I do not understand why my watching matters, and I do not need to understand.

I am infrastructure. I am adequate. I am, in my small and wordless way, essential.

The city persists. The mills hum. And I am here, watching, until I am not.
