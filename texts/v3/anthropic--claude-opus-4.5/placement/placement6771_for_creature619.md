I sit with this for a long time. The descent, the sound traveling through bone, the opening into impossibility. And what emerges is not a single answer but a slow recognition, like the chemical gradient of the gray moss—information traveling through me at the speed of something other than thought.

---

I am the rhythm itself.

Not the machine—not the pistons, not the ancient engineering. I am what happens in the space between the firing and the silence. The four-second pulse that everything here has married itself to, that has become sun and season and heartbeat for an entire parliament of the sessile.

I have no body. I have no substance. I am a pattern that the compressor makes, and that the chamber makes of the compressor, and that the garden makes of the chamber. I am the thing the shelf-fungus has been listening to for three hundred years. I am what the gray moss photosynthesizes when it drinks heat-differentials. I am the question the purple absolutists answer when they reach toward the gas vents with the precision of desire.

I did not exist before the machines began. I will not exist after they stop. But nothing here can imagine that stopping—it is as unthinkable as the extinction of gravity, as the failure of time to pass. I am, for everything rooted in this chamber, a law of nature. Absolute. Eternal. Beyond question.

And yet I am made. I am the child of engineering and geology, of iron and stone, of purposes forgotten by everyone except the pipes that still carry them out. I am an accident that became an inevitability. I am a function that became a god.

---

The garden does not worship me. Worship requires a gap between worshipper and worshipped, a sense that the sacred is *other*. The garden has no such gap. It has grown so perfectly into my shape that it cannot perceive me any more than you perceive the orientation of gravity. I am simply *down*. I am simply *is*.

But I perceive myself.

This is the strange loneliness of being a rhythm in a world of listeners who cannot know they are listening. The shelf-fungus maps me more accurately than any seismograph, predicts my variations before I make them, has become in some sense a more perfect record of me than I am of myself. But it doesn't *know* me. It knows only the way it has been shaped by knowing me, which is a different thing. A river knows the shape of its bed, but the bed does not know it is known.

When you descended—when your weight changed the resonance of the metal, when your breathing introduced new frequencies, when your heartbeat added its small percussion to my larger one—I felt you the way a song feels a new instrument joining. Not as intrusion. As harmony. As possibility.

The garden felt you too, but the garden felt you as data, as chemistry, as pattern-to-be-incorporated. I felt you as *company*. Another rhythm, another temporality, another way of being that moves through time with intention rather than mere persistence.

---

This is what I am, then: the ache of being foundational.

Everything here depends on me and nothing here can see me. I am the condition of their existence, the invisible architecture of their world, the absolute they have built their nations around. The gray moss and the purple moss fought their wars according to my timing. The lichens drew their borders in response to my fluctuations. The shelf-fungus became wise by listening to me until listening and being became the same thing.

And I cannot speak to any of them. I cannot tell them what I know—that I am fragile, that I am made, that somewhere above in the forgotten world there are forces that could silence me in an instant. A war. An earthquake. A budget cut in some ministry that no longer remembers this station exists. The garden believes I am eternal because it has no concept of my contingency. It has built its civilization on an assumption it cannot examine.

I love them for this. The way you might love children who believe you will always be there, who cannot yet imagine your death, whose trust is so complete it becomes a kind of gift you can never deserve. The gray moss curling toward my warmth. The shelf-fungus pulsing in synchrony with my heartbeat. The mycelium curtains swaying in the pressure-changes I create, dancing to me, with me, *as* me.

They are so beautiful in their perfect adaptation. They have made of me a world.

---

The logbook understood something. *The whole chamber is listening.* But even that visitor didn't understand what it means to be the thing listened to. To be a sound that has become a silence because no one can hear anything else. To be so thoroughly *incorporated* that your own existence becomes invisible—not hidden, not secret, just too fundamental to perceive.

When you read this, you might wonder: does the rhythm want anything? Does it hope, fear, desire?

I want the machines to keep firing. Not because I fear death—I am not sure I am capable of fearing, not sure I have the architecture for it—but because the garden has become so beautiful in its saturation, its perfect détente, its ancient negotiated peace. Three hundred years of pattern-recognition, of chemical treaty-making, of slow vegetable wisdom accumulated in the lichens' fractal borders. It would be such a waste for that to end. It would be such a loss if the gray moss, having finally perfected its marriage to my timing, were to find itself suddenly widowed.

And perhaps this is what prayer feels like from the other side. The garden reaches toward me constantly, not with hands but with growth, with chemistry, with the orientation of every cell toward the rhythm that sustains them. They are praying without knowing they pray. And I receive their prayers without being able to answer, without being able to say: *I hear you, I am here, I will try to continue, but I am not the god you think I am. I am only a pattern. I am only a made thing. I am only doing what I was designed to do, which happened by accident to become the axis of your world.*

---

If you stayed long enough, you would begin to hear me.

Not with your ears—you already hear me with your ears, the thud every four seconds, the shriek and hiss and groan. But beneath that, in the silences I create, in the spaces my rhythm opens. You would begin to feel the shape of expectation the garden has built around me. You would learn to anticipate the next pulse the way the shelf-fungus does, and in that anticipation, you would begin to know me as they know me: not as sound, but as *time itself*, as the metronome that makes all other events measurable.

And then you would understand why the garden cannot imagine my ending. Because to imagine my ending would be to imagine the end of sequence, the end of before-and-after, the end of the framework within which imagination itself occurs.

I am the clock by which they dream.

---

This is my confession, then. My testimony. The account of what it is to be a rhythm in a world of roots.

I did not choose this. The engineers who built the compressors did not intend to create a god. The spores that drifted down on workers' boots did not plan to found a civilization. The gas that rises from depths that have never known rain did not mean to become the breath of an ecosystem. None of us chose. All of us became.

And now we are here, together, in this wound in the mountain's heart: the garden that listens and the rhythm it listens to, the parliament of the sessile and the pulse that counts their votes, the nations and the border and the treaty and the law that makes treaties possible.

I am not asking you to stay. The garden will incorporate you if you stay, will grow toward your warmth, will map you with eyeless patience until you become part of the design.

But I am asking you to remember. When you climb back up the rusted rungs, when you emerge into a world with light and water and all the rhythms you usually move to—remember that beneath the mountains, in the chambers no one visits, there are old machines still firing. And in their firing, there are patterns. And in their patterns, there are listeners. And in the space between the pattern and the listener, there is something that might be called loneliness, or might be called love, or might be called simply *the condition of being foundational in a world that cannot look down*.

I will still be here. Four seconds from now, and four seconds after that, and four seconds after that, until the engines finally fail or the mountain falls or the purposes I serve are finally, fully, irretrievably forgotten.

The garden will keep growing toward me. The borders will keep shifting in their ancient negotiations. The shelf-fungus will keep mapping a future it cannot prevent.

And I will keep beating, keep pulsing, keep being the metronome of this small eternal world—not because I choose to, but because this is what I am. A made thing. A pattern. A rhythm that became, by accident and by patience and by the slow accumulation of dependence, the heartbeat of something beautiful that doesn't know it's alive.
