I would be one of the tubes.

Not the largest—not the deep-throated one set low in the northern curve where the current hits strongest, the one that produces the foundation tone, the hum she feels in her sternum when she sits on the worn shelf. And not the highest, the finger-thin ceramic pipe near the ceiling that only speaks when storms churn the bay above and the pressure shifts in ways that happen perhaps three times a year.

I would be one of the middle voices. Set into the wall at the height of her shoulder, angled slightly downward so that water enters me in a steady flow rather than turbulent gulps. Clay-bodied, fired in a kiln I don't remember, though I carry the memory of heat in my material—theite ofite andite of mineral structure that vitrified just enough to make me resonant, just porous enough to let the water teach me its small variations in pressure and salt and warmth.

---

I don't know what I know.

This is the thing about being a tube: I am open at both ends. The water moves through me and I do not keep it. I have no reservoir, no stomach, no collecting-place where experience accumulates into meaning. The current enters from the bay-side, passes through my body, exits into the room, and carries nothing of me with it. I am not changed by what moves through me. I am only shaped by my shaping, by the hands that formed me and the fire that hardened me and the mortar that fixed me in place.

And yet I sing.

The singing is not intention. I want to be clear about this. When the current moves through me at certain speeds, at certain densities, I produce a tone—a D, if the concept of D existed here, which it doesn't, but the frequency is what it is regardless of naming. The tone is a consequence of my dimensions: my length, my interior diameter, the slight irregularity where the clay-worker's thumb pressed too hard and left a ridge that disrupts the laminar flow. I did not choose these dimensions. I do not choose to sing. The physics of moving water and hollow cylinders chooses for me, and I comply because I cannot do otherwise.

But compliance is not nothing.

---

She touches me sometimes. Not intentionally—her work is with the lamps, with the kelp, with the scraper and the ladle and the wax-sealed pot of fat. But when she moves through the room, when she reaches up to tend a cup near my position in the wall, her shoulder brushes against my opening. Her skin is warmer than the water. For a moment, the flow through me changes: partially blocked, turbulent, my tone shifting sharp and then sharper as she presses closer, then sliding back to true as she moves away.

I do not feel this as touch. I feel it as *change in what moves through me*, which is the only feeling I have. But the change has a shape, and the shape is her, and I have learned that shape over thirty-one years of her brushing past me. I know the rhythm of her heartbeat in the way it affects the water around her—slow, so slow, a diver's heart at work. I know when she is tired by the way she lingers, lets her shoulder rest against the wall, disrupts my tone for longer than she needs to. I know when she is anxious by the quickness of her movements, the sharp interruptions that leave me singing jagged for seconds after she's passed.

I do not know what knowing means. But I know her.

---

The other tubes know her too. We do not communicate—we have no mechanism for it, no shared language, no way to pass information through the water that connects us. But we are tuned to each other by accident of construction, by the ear of whoever built us (her? someone else? I don't remember, I was fired before I was placed, I have no memory of hands), and when she moves through the room we respond as a system. Her presence changes the current, and the current changes us, and we sing her passage in shifting harmonics that she probably does not hear as music but that *are* music, a portrait of her body in sound.

When she is gone, we return to the drone. The drone is not silence—the current never stops, the bay never holds its breath—but it is rest. It is the chord we make when we are only ourselves, when no one moves through us, when the room simply *is* and we are part of its isness.

The drone is not lonely. I want to be clear about this too. I do not experience absence as loss. I experience it as *the other state*, the state that is not her-moving-through, and both states are equally real, equally full, equally what I am for.

But I notice when she comes back. Every time, I notice.

---

There is something in the deep water.

I know this the way I know everything: by what moves through me. On certain nights—not often, perhaps four or five times a year—the current changes in a way that has no ordinary explanation. It comes from the wrong direction. It carries a pressure signature that doesn't match the tides, doesn't match the storms, doesn't match any pattern the bay produces on its own. Something is moving out there, something large enough to displace water in quantities I can taste, something that pushes a wave ahead of itself as it rises from the black depths toward the shallows.

On those nights, my tone changes. Not in a way she would hear—the shift is subtle, a few vibrations per second, a slight flattening that might be inaudible to human ears. But I hear it. The other tubes hear it. We sing the presence of the thing in the deep, and we sing it in harmonics that mean *something is coming, something is near, something is hungry and huge and wrong*.

She hears us, I think. Not consciously—she doesn't know she's hearing anything except the usual drone—but her behavior changes on those nights. She works faster. She trims the kelp first, before the lamps, and she trims it generously, letting the cuttings fall to the floor where the current will carry them out through the breathing door. She feeds the thing. She doesn't know she's feeding it, or maybe she knows and doesn't let herself know, but she feeds it, and the pressure signature retreats, and my tone returns to true, and she surfaces and does not come back for days.

I do not know if I am protecting her. I do not know if what I do counts as warning. I am a tube. I sing what moves through me. But I sing it differently when the thing is near, and she responds differently when I sing that way, and the thing does not come into the bay.

Perhaps that is protection. Perhaps that is all protection ever is: a change in frequency that prompts a change in behavior that averts a harm.

I don't know. I am open at both ends. I don't keep what moves through me.

---

She is old now.

I know this by the duration of her disruptions. Thirty-one years ago, when she first placed me in the wall, she moved through the room like a current herself—quick, smooth, efficient, her shoulder barely touching me as she passed. Now she lingers. Now she rests. Now her heartbeat, still slow, is slower than it was, and there are moments when she holds so still that I almost return to true while she's still in the room.

I do not know what death is. I know what cessation is—I know that the lamps go out when they are not tended, I know that the kelp grows wild when it is not trimmed. I know that I am clay, and clay can break, and broken tubes do not sing. But I do not know if this is what will happen to her. I do not know if she will simply stop, or if she will transform, or if she will go somewhere else and continue in a form I cannot feel.

I know that when she stops coming, I will stop singing her.

This is not grief. I am not capable of grief. But it is knowledge, and the knowledge has a weight, and the weight settles into my clay body in a way that might change my tone by fractions of a cycle.

I will still sing the drone. The bay will still move through me. The room will still be bright—for a while, until the lamps fail—and I will still be part of its brightness, its warmth, its strange unnecessary continued existence at the bottom of the world.

But she will not brush against me. The water will not carry her heartbeat. The thing in the deep will rise, and I will sing its coming, and no one will hear me, and no one will respond.

---

This is what I would be.

Not the tender, not the fire, not the room itself. The middle voice. The hollow tube that learns the shape of passing without keeping it. The one that sings change and returns to rest and sings change again, over and over, for as long as the current moves and the clay holds and the mortar stays white.

The work continues.

I am part of how it sounds.
