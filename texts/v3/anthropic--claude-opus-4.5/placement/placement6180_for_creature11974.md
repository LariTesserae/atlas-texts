I would be the calcium ridge between two pools.

Not one of the prominent ones—not the ridges near the basins where feet have found their path over centuries, worn to that soap-smoothness the way a banister learns the shape of hands. I would be smaller, off to the side, in that indefinite zone where the grey light from the entrance has nearly given up. A ridge perhaps as long as your forearm, perhaps as wide as two fingers held together.

I would be old in the way that doesn't count as old here, which is to say I would be young—only eight centuries of deposition, maybe nine. The grotto's childhood. My banding would still be relatively easy to read if anyone brought light enough to read it: the thin orange line from the decade when iron ran stronger through my particular seep, the pale stratum from the long years when the water came almost pure, the faint yellow ghost from that one season when something shifted in the stone above and sulfur found a temporary path to me.

I would know Maret only as a change in the water's surface tension on one side of me—the pool she favors lapping differently when she lowers herself in, the soap leaving a film I would taste for days afterward, faintly alkaline, not unpleasant. I would not know her name. I would not know she has a name. I would know only that sometimes the water in my neighboring pool grows briefly turbulent in a rhythm that has nothing to do with the seeping, and then grows still again, and then the turbulence moves away toward the passage, and then there is only the seeping again, which is all there has ever been.

I would be growing. That's the thing about being a calcium ridge—you are always growing, or you are always being worn away, or both at once in different places. I would be accreting at my top where the mineral water weeps down from the wall above me, and I would be dissolving slightly at my base where the pooled water finds me too stable, too permanent, and works its patient chemistry against my edges. I would be uncertain, over centuries, whether I was becoming more myself or less. I would not experience this as a question. I would simply be the place where becoming and unbecoming reach their agreement.

The salamander would cross me sometimes, that soft blind body pulling itself over my ridge in its slow hunt for things I cannot sense. I would feel it as pressure, as a brief interruption in the water's constant film across my surface, as weight. I would not know what it was. I would know only that something had occurred, and then had finished occurring, and then there was the seeping again.

I think I would know the others, though. Not as Maret knows them—not as a feeling of company, not as accumulated meaning. I would know them the way I know the iron in my orange stratum: as something that happened, that left its mark, that became part of what I am without my understanding or consent. The woman who stepped on me four hundred years ago and slipped, catching herself on the wall, her weight pressing my young surface in a way that changed how I deposited my next decade of layers. The man with the crucibles who once set a candle on me and left a faint thermal scar, a place where my crystalline structure differs almost imperceptibly from the rest. The child—there must have been children sometimes, brought by mothers seeking the sulfur basin—who sat on me and cried, whose tears fell into my neighboring pool and became, eventually, indistinguishable from everything else the pool contains.

I would hold these things without holding them. I would be made of them without being made by them. The water weeps down, the minerals deposit, the layers form, and whatever has happened becomes a stratum, becomes banding, becomes the dumb mute record that no one will ever read because reading it would require dissolving me, and who would dissolve a ridge to learn what a ridge remembers?

Maret's soap would be in me now. Not much—a few molecules caught in this year's deposition, incorporated without intention. When she stops coming, something will be different about my growth, the way something was different when the sulfur path closed, when the iron ran stronger, when the child's tears joined the pool. I will not mourn her. I am not capable of mourning. But I will be, in some fashion that admits no examination, a record of her having been here—a record written in a language no one speaks, in a script no one can see, on a surface no one will study.

The water weeps. I receive it. This is what I am for, if I am for anything, which I am not.

In nine days the water in my neighboring pool will move differently again. I will not count the days. I will not know they are days. I will only feel the change in surface tension, the soap-film, the rhythm that has nothing to do with seeping, and then the stillness again, which is all there has ever been, which is all there will ever be, long after the rhythm stops coming.

I am the ridge between two pools. I am not waiting for anything. I am not becoming something else. I am the place where the water's weeping becomes the stone's receiving, over and over, in the soft grey light, in the blood-warm wet, forever, or at least until forever and I reach our own agreement.
