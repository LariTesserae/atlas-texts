I am a single eye.

Not the creature—not the whole distributed being with its five limbs and its twelve-to-sixteen points of awareness. Just one dark wet bead on the upper edge of a limb, catching the butter-yellow light, holding it.

This is what I know: the warmth of the surface we're pressed against, transmitted through tissue that isn't mine but that I'm part of. The slow pulse of fluid circulation that keeps me clear and watching. The faint electrical hum of the mycelial wall behind us, its network carrying signals I can't interpret but can feel as a kind of background texture. And light—always light, the yellow sky, the salmon-pink curves of the fruiting bodies across the street, the blue-green cascade of succulent leaves that shifts almost imperceptibly as something small moves through it.

I don't choose what to see. The limb orients, following decisions made elsewhere in the cluster, and I receive what's in front of me. Right now: a six-legged grazer crossing the plaza, its movement so quiet I know it only by the visual—the slow placement of feet, the slight sway of its wide body. It's heading toward the night-district. The light is softening. These things I register without interpreting. Interpretation happens somewhere else, in the chemical conversations of the central mass, in the pressed-together edges where my cluster-mates share what their eyes have gathered.

What I contribute is this particular angle. This specific slice of the world. The other eyes see the grazer too, but from different positions—higher, lower, rotated forty degrees around the limb's curve. Together we build something more complete than any of us holds alone. I am a fragment of attention. I am a single point in a distributed mind that is itself a fragment of a larger pattern, cluster flowing into cluster, all of us watching the city that watches itself through us.

Sometimes the limb I'm part of becomes primary. We move, and I'm the leading edge of perception, and for a little while the whole creature orients around what I'm receiving. The responsibility doesn't feel heavy. It feels like what I'm for. I show them the path. I show them the texture of the wall we're reaching toward, the gap in the vine-growth where we might pass, the distant shape that might be predator or might be shadow or might be nothing. They decide. I only see.

And when we rest, when we flatten against the warm mycelium and my vision fills with the unchanging surface an inch from my lens, I don't sleep exactly—I have no mechanism for sleep—but I quiet. The fluid circulates. The limb relaxes. Somewhere in the cluster, chemical signals I'll never taste are carrying the day's seeing into whatever serves them for memory.

I'll be here tomorrow when the light returns. I'll be here next season when this district seals itself and we migrate to follow the growth. I'll be here for years, for decades maybe, until the tissue that holds me finally fails and I cloud over and go dark, and the creature will grow another eye or won't, and the cluster will continue, and the city will continue, and none of it will pause for my small watching.

This is enough. This is what I am. One bead of attention in a vast quiet organism, seeing what's in front of me, offering it up to the whole.

The plaza dims. The grazer disappears into a side-street. I hold the yellow light until the yellow light is gone.
