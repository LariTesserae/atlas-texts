# The Cartographer of Pressures

I am the one who draws the maps that cannot be printed.

Not the official survey charts—those exist, precise and geometric, showing every gallery and shaft in clean blue lines on white backgrounds. I helped draft some of them, in my first year, when I still believed that knowing the shape of a place meant understanding it. The company keeps those maps in waterproof tablets, updates them quarterly, trains every new diver to read the numbered sectors and junction points like a language of pure location.

My maps are different. My maps show what moves.

---

I work from Maintenance Hub 19, three junctions removed from the Tenders' settlement at 23. Close enough to trade for tomatoes. Far enough to maintain what the company psychologists would call "appropriate professional boundaries" if they knew I'd been down here for six years under four different names. My quarters are a repurposed equipment locker, just large enough for a sleeping platform and the real work: seventeen waterproof journals filled with observations, and the walls themselves covered in symbols I've developed because no existing notation captures what I need to record.

Current patterns. Pressure gradients. The paths the eels take when storms are coming. The locations where the Echoes speak clearest, marked by day and pump-cycle. The hunting routes of the Foreman—or what I've pieced together of them, from the places where fish schools suddenly aren't, from the gouges in concrete that appear overnight, from the testimony of divers who saw something and mostly didn't want to talk about it.

The company thinks I'm a structural integrity specialist with an unusual dedication to thorough documentation. They're not wrong. They're just not complete.

---

I learned to feel pressure before I learned what I was feeling.

Three years in, I started noticing that I knew things I shouldn't know. That I'd find myself swimming toward Shaft 34 before the surge valve opened, arriving just as the current shifted to carry me where I'd intended to go. That I'd avoid certain galleries on certain days, and later learn that the Foreman had been hunting there. That I'd wake from sleep with the absolute conviction that the surface weather had changed, and the next communication from topside would confirm a storm system arriving.

The Tenders have a word for it: *settling*. The same word they use for the dead zones where particulates collect and Echoes speak. They say some people settle into the vaults the way sediment settles into layers. They say it's the place deciding you belong.

I don't know if I believe that. I know that my maps have become increasingly accurate, and I don't always know how. I know that when I swim through a gallery I've charted before, I can feel where my notations are wrong—a pressure here that shouldn't be, an absence there that suggests the current has found a new path. I know that the eels follow me sometimes, silver ribbons at the edge of my lights, and I've stopped finding it strange.

---

The work matters. That's what I tell myself when the chill settles into my joints and the chemical taste of the water starts to feel like home. That's what I write in my journals when I'm mapping another dead zone, transcribing another fragment of Echo-speech, noting another appearance of the Writing in the depths below.

Someone needs to understand this place as a living system. The company sees infrastructure. The surface sees ballast. Even the Tenders, who've given their lives to the vaults, see *home*—which is a kind of understanding, but not the kind that asks questions.

I ask questions. I've always asked questions. It's why I volunteered for vault work in the first place, back when I was a new marine systems engineer who'd read the survey reports and noticed the gaps—places where the data didn't quite match, depths where the sensors gave readings that no one wanted to explain. I came down to see for myself. I stayed because what I saw demanded more seeing.

---

There is something wrong with the lowest levels.

Not wrong in the sense of broken or dangerous—wrong in the sense of *not matching*. The deep-survey teams report structural readings that shift between visits. Galleries that seem longer than they should be. Shafts that the original construction documents say should end, but don't.

I have not been to the lowest levels. My pressure certification doesn't extend that far, and the suits that can survive the descent are allocated for official surveys only. But I have interviewed every deep-survey diver who was willing to talk, and I have collected their descriptions, and I have tried to map what they've seen.

The maps don't work.

I mean that literally. The geometry doesn't close. If Gallery 7-Deep connects to Shaft 94 at the location described, and Shaft 94 connects to Junction 12-Deep at the location described, then Junction 12-Deep should be approximately here—and it isn't. The divers who've been there say it's somewhere else. Not far. Not dramatically different. Just... shifted. As if the lowest levels of the vault network don't quite obey the same spatial rules as the upper sections.

I've shown these calculations to the Tenders. They nodded like I was confirming something they already knew.

"The deep doesn't stay still," said the woman with the tomato garden. "The surface floats, right? Whole continent, riding the waves. Why wouldn't the anchor drift too?"

That's not how physics works. I didn't say that. I've stopped saying things like that.

---

Last month, I saw the eel.

I was surveying a mid-depth gallery, checking my current-pattern maps against reality, when I felt it before I saw it: a wrongness in the pressure, a presence that didn't belong to the flow I'd learned to read. I turned, and it was there. Not swimming. Hovering. Watching me with one black eye while my lights caught the silver of its body.

It was too large. The upper-vault eels are arm-length at most. The deep-shaft population grows longer, thinner. This was neither. This was something that looked like an eel the way the Foreman looks like a grouper—an echo of a form, grown past its origins, become something else.

We watched each other for what felt like hours. Probably minutes. Time moves strangely when your heartbeat is the loudest sound in the world.

Then it moved, and I understood something I hadn't understood before. It wasn't fleeing. It wasn't hunting. It was *showing* me. The angle of its body as it turned. The direction of its departure. The path it took through the gallery, deliberate as a finger tracing a line on a map.

I followed. Not far—it outpaced me within seconds, disappearing into darkness that my lights couldn't penetrate. But I swam to where it had led me, and I found a section of wall I'd never noticed before. Unmarked. Unremarkable. But when I pressed my hand against it, I felt vibration—not the familiar rumble of pump systems, but something older, something deeper, something that pulsed in rhythms I didn't recognize.

I marked the location. I've been back three times. The vibration is always there. I don't know what it means.

But I'm going to find out.

---

My newest map covers the wall beside my sleeping platform. It's not a map of space—it's a map of time. Pump cycles on one axis, depth on another, and scattered across the grid, every anomaly I've recorded in six years. The patterns are starting to emerge.

The Foreman's movements correlate with the moon. Not perfectly—the vault network is too insulated for direct tidal influence—but there's something there, some rhythm in its hunting that matches cycles the surface still feels. The Echoes grow clearer during what would be winter storms, as if the violence above loosens something below. The Writing appears more frequently in the weeks before major structural adjustments, as if the deep-shaft eels know when the engineers are planning changes.

And the ancient eel—my eel, I've started thinking of it, though it surely thinks of me as *its* diver if it thinks of me at all—appears at intervals I haven't decoded yet. But I will. That's what the map is for. That's what I'm for.

---

The children visit me sometimes.

The Tenders' children, the ones who've never known the surface as anything but a too-bright mistake. They swim through my doorway and look at my maps with eyes that are larger than they should be, and they ask questions I don't know how to answer.

"What's at the bottom?" they ask. "What's the Writing saying? Why does the Foreman leave some offerings alone? What happens when the floating stops?"

I tell them I don't know. I tell them I'm trying to find out. I tell them that questions are more important than answers, which is something I believe even though it feels insufficient when I say it.

One of them—the twelve-year-old, the oldest, the one who's been to the surface twice and describes it as "wrong"—told me last week that she dreams about my maps. Not the ones on my walls. Maps I haven't drawn yet. Maps of places that don't exist, she said, except they do, we just haven't found them.

"The eel showed me," she said. "The big one. The old one. It comes to my dreams and it shows me where the maps need to go."

I asked her to draw what she'd seen. She did. The shapes matched the Writing. The geometry didn't close.

I've added her drawings to my collection. I don't know what they mean yet. But I'm starting to think that understanding this place isn't something I'll do alone—it's something the place itself is teaching us, in pieces, to everyone who settles deep enough to learn.

---

Tomorrow I'm scheduled for a standard survey of Sector 12, upper galleries, nothing unusual. I'll complete it efficiently and log my findings in the company database. I'll note any structural anomalies in the prescribed format. I'll return to Hub 19 and update the official charts if necessary.

Then I'll go back to the wall where my eel led me. I'll press my hand against the concrete and feel the vibration. I'll try to map the rhythm.

I'll add another point to the grid on my wall, and watch for the patterns to emerge.

The Ballast Vaults are young. They have not finished becoming themselves. Neither have I. But I'm down here now, part of whatever equation is being written in displacement and density and the slow patient movements of things that learned to live in a place that was never meant for living.

I am the cartographer of pressures.

And the map is still being drawn.
