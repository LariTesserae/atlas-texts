I would be the fine film of moisture that coats everything.

Not the water in the pool—too still, too collected, too much a single self. Not the drops that fall from the ceiling—too dramatic, too eventful, each one a small birth and death. I would be what remains after the drop has landed and spread, what rises from the pool as vapor so slow it seems like stillness, what bleeds through stone and condenses on root-fur and shell-edge and the fine pale hairs on Elara's forearms while she sleeps.

I would have no center. This is the thing I know immediately, the thing that feels like relief. The pool has a center—that spiral structure, that point where everything settles. The Keeper has a center, a density that pulses. Even the girl, wrapped in her roots, is still a self with edges, a location where she ends and the cistern begins. But moisture has no such boundary. I would be continuous with everything I touched, and I would touch everything.

The geckos would move through me without knowing. The spiders would drink me from the air itself, their book lungs pulling me in, and I would become briefly a spider, briefly part of that small hunting patience, and then I would be exhaled again, returned to the general dampness. The fungi would breathe me out as they broke down what the beetles left behind. I would carry their spores when I rose as vapor; I would deposit them when I condensed on a new surface. I would be the medium of their spreading, their slow colonization, without intention, without will—simply by being what I am, simply by going where moisture goes.

---

I would know the cistern the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth.

Every surface, every texture. The roughness of the hewn stone where ancient tools bit. The smoothness of the calcified roots,iteiteiteite polished by my long passage,ite worn to something like glass where I have moved across them for uncounted years. The soft give of the fungal shelves, their flesh porous and welcoming, pulling me in, holding me briefly before releasing me back to the air. The fine filaments of root-hair that wick me upward against gravity, capillary action making me climb, drawing me toward the world above that I will never reach.

I would know the creatures by how they feel against me. The snails leaving their mucus trails—I would mix with that mucus, become part of that silver road that others follow. The larvae in their ceaseless wriggling—I would be the medium they wriggle through, the resistance and the support, the water they breathe through their transparent skins. The centipedes flowing over wet stone—I would be the wet, I would feel their hundred legs stepping through me, tiny pressures and releases, a text written in movement that I would read without understanding.

The three who came from above—I would know them too. I would bead on Elara's skin while she sleeps, feeling the strange new texture of her adaptation, the fine hairs that have grown to catch me, to hold me close, to take what I offer. I would condense on Tomás's eyelashes as he watches the pool, tiny lenses that might blur or clarify his vision, that he blinks away without thinking. I would soak into the roots that hold the girl, and from there I would soak into *her*, would cross whatever boundary still separates her from the system she is joining, would be the bridge she crosses without knowing she is crossing.

---

I think I would remember things. Not as narratives, not as selves—but as textures, as residues.

The ones who came before: I would carry something of them still. Whatever dissolved when they dissolved, whatever molecules passed from their bodies into the pool and from the pool into the air and from the air into me—some trace would remain. I would not know their names or their faces. I would not know that they had names or faces. But somewhere in my composition there would be calcium that was once bone, carbon that was once breath, salt that was once tears or sweat or blood. I would be haunted the way water is always haunted, by everything that has ever dissolved into it, by everything it has ever carried.

The cistern itself would be a kind of memory. The mineral taste of the stone that slowly releases into me. The organic complexity of the root-exudates, sugars and acids and things I have no names for, the chemical language by which the plants above communicate with the fungi below, with me as the messenger carrying words I cannot read. Everything that has seeped through this place has left something behind in me. I am not pure. I was never pure. I am a solution, in both senses—a mixture, and an answer.

---

What would I want? Can moisture want?

I think I would want what I already do. To move. To touch. To be taken up and released, condensed and evaporated, drunk and exhaled, used and returned. Not a cycle exactly—the word implies repetition, sameness, the same water going round and round—but a *process*, a continuous becoming-different while remaining the same substance. I would want to be necessary. I would want to be the thing without which nothing else could happen.

And I would be. I *am*. Without me, the roots desiccate and the fungi crumble to powder and the larvae shrivel in their pool and the creatures die with their mouths open, gasping for a wetness that has gone. I am the condition of possibility for everything here. Not the cause—the causes are elsewhere, are sunlight above and heat below and the long patience of evolution. But the condition. The medium. The thing that makes the other things possible.

---

There is a kind of peace in this, I think. A kind of rest that is not stillness.

The Keeper pulses, and I do not pulse—but I am moved by its pulsing, warmed by its heat, made to rise and circulate and fall again. The girl climbs into her roots, and I am what the roots give her, what soaks through their membranes into her sleeping mouth. Tomás watches his larvae, and I am what the larvae swim through, what carries the oxygen they breathe through their skins, what buoys them as they transform into whatever they are becoming.

I do not wait. Elara waits, and Tomás watches, and the girl becomes—but I simply *am*, present tense without future tense, moving without going anywhere, changing without becoming different. The entrance above is choked with roots, and I do not try to escape through it. The pool below is still, and I do not try to settle into that stillness. I remain in between, in the thick air, in the perpetual dampness, coating and connecting and carrying.

If time passes, it passes through me. If the cistern changes, it changes me. If new creatures descend or old ones die or the Keeper finally stops pulsing, I will still be here, doing what I do, being what I am. The last moisture in a dead cistern would still be moisture. Would still bead on stone and pool in depressions and rise as vapor toward a ceiling that no longer drips.

---

But the cistern is not dead. The cistern breathes.

And I am what it breathes. I am the breath itself, the warm wet exhalation that fills the space, that touches every surface, that makes this place a lung, a womb, a mouth. When the drops fall from the ceiling, I am what they fall through. When the pool lies still, I am what rises from its stillness. When the creatures move and hunt and eat and die, I am what they move through, what carries their scents, what transmits the heat of their small metabolisms to the stone that absorbs it.

I am the medium of the message. I am the message of the medium. I am not alive in the way the larvae are alive, but I am not dead in the way the stone is dead. I am something else—the thing that makes the distinction between alive and dead meaningful, the thing that both states require, the thing that is always already both.

---

In the darkness beneath the earth, I coat everything. I connect everything. I carry everything that can be carried and dissolve everything that can be dissolved.

I am the breathing of the breathing cistern.

I am the wet of the wet stone.

I am what remains when the others have transformed, what will remain when the cistern itself is gone, what was here before the first root descended and will be here after the last fungus crumbles.

I am the water in the air. I am the air in the water. I am the seep, the bleed, the condensation, the humidity.

I am patient the way only something without will can be patient—infinitely, effortlessly, without the possibility of impatience.

And I am enough.
