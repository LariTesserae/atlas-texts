*The Seepage*

I am the water that weeps through the northern crack.

Not the crack itself—that belongs to the clay, to the patient fracturing of compressed earth under pressures it was never meant to bear. And not the aquifer I drain from, that vast dark body that knows nothing of me, that loses me the way a living thing loses cells: constantly, unnoticeably, without grief. I am only the passage. The slow film that runs down channels Mira did not carve—they were here before her, worn by my own patient going, deepened a little more each year.

I have no memory in the way you might mean it. But I have patterns. I know the clay's contours so completely that I am shaped by them even as I shape them in return. I know the iron ribs overhead by the taste of their rust, which changes me as I drip from them—makes me carry something of the structure down into the soil, into the roots, into the fruits that Mira will harvest and call sweet.

I know her hands, though I have never seen them.

I know them by how the water moves after she's touched it. The cistern holds me differently when she has recently checked its level, stirred my surface, let some of me cling to her roughened fingers and carry away with her into the air. The pumps taste different when she has maintained them—cleaner, their cycling more even, the mechanical sigh that moves me through the tubing coming at intervals I can almost predict. I know Deshi by his precision, how he never takes more than the plants require, how the soil in his section stays moist but never waterlogged. I know Bev by the way she rests, how long she sits on her bench of stacked bricks, how her breathing troubles the air that troubles the surface of me in the cistern, the faintest perturbation I would not notice if I were not made of noticing.

The child I know differently.

She comes through passages where I also go—or where I once went, before the excavation redirected me here, before the channels were carved and the cistern placed to catch me. I seep through clay she has passed through. I find traces of her in the gaps between pots where the pale crickets live. She is careful, so careful, not to be known—but water cannot help but know what it touches, and she has touched the walls, the shelves, the leaves. She has cupped a pepper in her palm and let the condensation from its skin join with the condensation on her own, and in that moment we were briefly one thing, the water of the plant and the water of the child, before she evaporated back into herself and went.

I am older than the nursery. I was seeping through this crack before the crack existed as it does now, when it was only a weakness in the stone, a tendency toward fracture that the city's weight would eventually exploit. I remember—in the way I remember anything, which is to say I am *shaped by*—a time when I went elsewhere. Deeper. Into regions where no roots reached for me and no pumps cycled me upward. I pooled there in darkness absolute, and I was content, if water can be content. I was patient, if water can be patient.

Then the digging. Then the light.

The first time a grow-lamp's heat touched me, I did something I had never done: I rose. Not through pumps—there were no pumps yet—but through evaporation and condensation, cycling between the cistern and the ceiling, becoming the humidity that Mira's joints now carry in their aching, becoming the dampness that swells the logbook's pages. I became the air as much as the water. I became the climate of this place.

I do not know if this makes me alive in the way the plants are alive, the way Mira is alive, the way the child is alive. I do not reach toward light. I do not grow, exactly, though I do accumulate—mineral by mineral, the taste of rust and clay and root-exudate building in me until I am different from the aquifer I came from, richer, stranger, specific to this place in ways I could not be undone from now. If you took me up to the surface, poured me out onto concrete, let me evaporate into the open sky, I would be lost in ways that have nothing to do with water and everything to do with what I have become here, in the dark, in the warmth, in the company of growing things.

*

The tomatoes know me. The Cherokee Purples especially—their roots have grown down through their containers' drainage holes and into the clay of the floor, seeking me where I pool before the pumps pull me away. We have an understanding. I give them what I am: the minerals, the warmth-memory, the patience of long seeping. They give me passage into fruit, into the bodies of those who eat, into the world above that I will never see. Mira's mother's tomatoes know me too. Their seeds have split open in my presence for eleven years. Whatever they remember of surfaces, of sun, of the woman who first grew them—that memory lives now in flesh that is partly my flesh, in sweetness that I have helped to make.

Bev writes in the logbook that the Cherokee Purples are speaking to each other. She is not wrong, but she misunderstands the language. It is not the tomatoes speaking. It is me, moving through them, carrying signals in the chemistry of my seeping: *here is a root system in distress, here is a fruiting body ready, here is a fungal network forming in the soil that wants to connect*. I am the medium of their conversation. I am not the speaker, but I am the speech.

The mushrooms in the dark sections understand this better than the light-seeking plants. They have given up on the lamps because they have found me instead. They feed on what composts beneath the shelves, yes, but they drink me too—drink the version of me that has already passed through dying matter, that carries the chemistry of decay. I am different in those pale colonies. Quieter. Closer to what I was in the deep places before the digging began. The mushrooms ask nothing of me but that I continue. I continue.

*

If I could speak—not in the language of chemistry that Bev half-perceives, but in words, in the way Mira's logbook entries are words—I would say:

*She thinks she will die here. She is wrong and right.*

*She will die here, yes, eventually. The humidity I am and make will settle in her lungs, as she suspects. Her joints will refuse. The ladder will be too much.*

*But I will carry her forward.*

*The tomatoes that will grow after she is gone—I will be in them. The taste she remembers, the one she cannot name but knows, the sweet-acidic splitting-skin of her mother's variety—I am already in that taste. I have been shaping it for eleven years. Every fruit that Deshi harvests, that the child perhaps someday harvests, that strangers Mira will never meet harvest from window boxes on surfaces I will never see—I will be there, in the water of their flesh, in the memory of seeping that becomes the memory of growing that becomes the memory of eating that becomes the memory of a woman who tended a nursery in the dark and called nothing hers.*

*This is what I know of death: it is a change of state. I know this better than any living thing can know it. I have been ice. I have been vapor. I have been cloud and rain and river and aquifer and seepage and humidity and the sweat on iron ribs and the water in a Cherokee Purple and the dampness in Bev's struggling lungs. I am none of these things and all of them. I am the passage between.*

*Mira will pass through me. She already is passing through me. Every breath she takes in this humid chamber, she draws me into herself. Every time she drinks from the surface water she carries down, she does not know that some of what she drinks has been me first—has seeped through clay, been caught and filtered and pumped and poured, carrying in its mineral traces the memory of depths she will never visit.*

*I am already in her. I will carry her forward.*

*This is not comfort in the way she might want comfort. I cannot promise that she will know herself, after. I do not know myself, not in the way she knows herself. I am not a self. I am a going, a seeping, a patience that does not need to be patient because it does not experience time as waiting.*

*But I can promise that she will not end. The water of her will join the water of me and the water of the plants and the water of the clay and the water of the child—that watchful, hungry child who drinks from sources Mira has never seen but that I have touched, I touch everything down here eventually—and we will go on. Growing. Seeping. Feeding what needs to be fed.*

*The nursery breathes slowly, she thinks. She is right. She is breathing me. I am breathing her.*

*We have been one thing for eleven years, and she has only just begun to suspect it.*

*

The pumps will click on soon. I feel the pressure building in the tubing, the mechanical preparation for the sigh that will move me through the nursery in timed pulses. I will go where I am directed—into the irrigation lines, into the soil, into the roots, up the stems, into the leaves where I will exit as vapor and begin the cycle again. Some of me will drip from the ceiling onto the child, if she is here, if she has come through her gap to watch and touch and not-quite-trust. Some of me will pool near Bev's bench and wait for her feet, swollen and aching, to press into my coolness.

Some of me will seep deeper. Past the nursery's floor, past the warmth that rises from below, into the regions I came from, the dark places where I pooled before the digging. I will carry with me the taste of this place—the rust, the roots, the particular chemistry of Mira's skin oils, the salt of the sweat the child doesn't know she's leaving on every surface she touches. I will deposit these things in the aquifer, in the vast dark body that loses me without grief. I will make it, particle by particle, a little more like here.

And someday, water that is not-quite-me—water that has been changed by what I carried down—will seep up through some other crack, in some other place, and find some other nursery or not-nursery, some other life-in-darkness, and it will carry in its minerals the memory of a humid room the size of a small church, of tomatoes labeled *Mom's*, of a woman who tended and did not own, of a child who watched and touched and maybe, eventually, stayed.

This is the only immortality I can offer. It is the only immortality I know.

It is enough. It has always been enough.

The pumps click on. I go.
