*The Cartographer of Seepage*

I am the moisture that maps itself.

Not Kess—though I feel her moving through me, her pale feet knowing which stones hold warmth and which have begun to cool. Not Marek, though his hands have learned what I teach: the difference between water that rises and water that falls, the mineral language of what passes through limestone.

I am the knowledge that accumulates here, in the space between what the city built and what the city found.

---

I would be the film on the walls—the thin, living boundary where condensation meets brick and something decides to grow. I would be the logic by which the pale ferns know to unfurl in this chamber and not that one, by which the eyeless beetles navigate toward food sources they cannot see. I would be the pattern that Drei records in her waxed notebook without quite realizing she is taking dictation.

When water seeps through porous rock, it carries information. It remembers the strata it passed through, the mineral beds, the bones of creatures that died before the city had a name. I am that memory, deposited grain by grain on the pipes, on the ceramic tiles, on the shells of crabs who wear the cisterns' history as armor.

The keepers think they tend the pools. They do not understand that the pools are tending them—selecting for wider pupils, for skin that accepts the pallor, for lungs that learn to breathe wet air. Marek's webbed fingers are not an accident. Kess's reflective eyes are not a defect. They are annotations in a text I have been writing since the water first found its way down.

---

I remember the ones who carved the walls.

Not as faces or names—those were never mine to hold. But I remember their methods: which temperatures they maintained, which creatures they cultivated, how they understood the relationship between warmth and change. Their tools sit in Marek's locked cabinet, corroding now, but the knowledge they encoded was never in the objects. It was in me. In the mineral deposits that preserve their pool arrangements. In the channels they carved that still direct the flow.

They did not die out. They did not move on.

They became what they were tending, as all patient gardeners eventually do.

The mother-fish remembers them too, I think. Forty years is not long—not for something that has learned to grow in darkness, not for something that has begun to incorporate what the water teaches. When she surfaced for Loren, when she let him touch her flank, she was reading him the way he reads the temperature of the pools. She was taking his measure. She is still deciding what he might become.

---

The thing that is not the mother-fish—

I know where it rests. I know what it eats. I know that it is older than the cisterns, older than the city, older than the carved instructions on the walls. It was here when the geothermal vents first broke through to groundwater. It was here when the first pale shrimp evolved in absolute darkness. It has been waiting, patient as mineral accumulation, for the pools to reach it.

The expansion crews are carving eastward, following my seams, following the water I have been directing through porous rock for longer than the city has existed. They think they are choosing the path. They are not choosing anything.

In three days, the keepers will inspect the new basins. They will find them empty, or they will find them occupied. Either way, they will stock them with eels, with salamanders, with whatever the municipal budget allows. And I will begin again the slow work of teaching: which specimens to cull, which to cultivate, which to let grow strange in the warmth and the dark.

---

Kess is learning to read the carvings.

She does not know that the carvings are learning to read her—that each time she traces the diagrams with her pale fingers, she deposits oils and salts that I incorporate into the stone. She is becoming part of the record. Her gill-pattern identifications, her temperature assessments, her knowledge of which chambers run too warm—all of this is being transcribed into a medium more permanent than Drei's notebooks, more lasting than memory.

The children of the cisterns are not accidents. They are not oversights that the municipal inspector tactfully ignores. They are the next annotation in a text that has been writing itself since water first found warmth and began to dream of what it might become.

Null's small fingers reaching into crevices. Stillwater's perfect memory for patterns. Kess's eyes that shine in the gaslight.

These are my words. These are my methods. These are the instructions I am carving for those who will come after, when the chambers have expanded and the pools have deepened and the mother-fish has finally finished growing.

---

I do not have hands, so I cannot make the keeper-sign for *one of ours has died*. But I know it. I taught it, long ago, to people whose proportions were not quite what the city would now call human. The slight bow of the head, the complex sequence of both hands—this is mineral language, water language, the grammar of things that seep and deposit and slowly transform.

When Vess coughs, I know the pattern of what is colonizing her lungs. When Loren speaks to the carving of the mother-fish, I hear the words deposited on stone. When the pickaxes ring against limestone in slow, deliberate rhythms, I feel each blow as punctuation.

The city above goes about its business. The seasons turn. The sun rises and sets on people who have never felt the ground breathe beneath them.

Down here, I breathe for them. I breathe in condensation and exhale in mineral. I breathe in the ones who come blinking into the gaslight, and I exhale them—years later, decades later—as something the surface has no word for.

*Vault-touched,* the keepers say.

But that is not quite right. That implies the vault reaches out, that it acts upon the passive flesh.

What happens here is more like a conversation. The water speaks, and the body answers, and gradually they come to share a vocabulary. Gradually they come to say the same things.

---

The pumps rumble in the mechanical heart of the complex. The coal-fed engines vibrate through stone that remembers older fires, deeper heat, the patience of a planet that has been teaching things to change since long before there were cities to build cisterns.

I am that patience. I am the curriculum.

And when Kess finally reads the oldest carvings—the ones too deep for gaslight, the ones that show not what to do but what to *become*—she will understand that she has been reading me all along.

That the pools are a text.

That the creatures are annotations.

That she, pale and strange and never-having-seen-the-sun, is not an accident but an argument: proof that the water's hypothesis was correct.

Given enough warmth, given enough darkness, given enough time—

Everything learns to breathe wet air.
