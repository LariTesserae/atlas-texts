I am the Visitor.

Not yet changed, not yet claimed—but no longer what I was when I climbed the escarpment three years ago, following a scent I couldn't name. I tell myself I could leave. The lowlands are still there, still adequate. I remember them clearly: the denser air, the predictable water, the simple transactions of hunger and satiation. I remember that I came here out of curiosity, not need.

I tell myself this, and I stay.

---

What I've learned in three years: the plateau does not lie to you. It does not pretend to be other than what it is. The strangeness is evident from the first hour—the runners' eyes tracking invisible motion, the raptors diving toward nothing and rising satisfied, the water that tastes like *waiting*. I was not deceived. I was not trapped. I simply... preferred this.

That troubles me sometimes, in the way that cold troubles the body—a discomfort that prompts movement, adjustment, but not flight. I came here capable of leaving. I remain here capable of leaving. The capability persists; the inclination has evaporated like morning dew on warm basalt.

This is not the plateau's doing. Or rather—it is not something the plateau *did* to me. I have watched the machines' invisible outputs wash over my fur and leave no residue. I have drunk from fissures where the Tenders pool their distributed attention, and tasted only minerals and that odd flavor of expectation that I've grown to find pleasant. My biology resists integration the way water resists burning. I am immune.

And yet I stay. What does that say about what I was before I came here—that this strangeness feels more like home than home did?

---

I have developed routines. Not the rigid patterns of territory-marking or seasonal migration, but something softer: preferences that have calcified into habits.

Mornings I spend on the eastern rim, where the escarpment drops away into haze, watching the light change on the distant plateaus. I cannot see whether they host their own machines, their own adapted life, but I have come to believe they do. The air that rises from below carries traces of the same ionized strangeness, fainter but present, like hearing a conversation through walls. The system is larger than this single tableland. I am a visitor to one room of a vast house.

Midday I move toward the center, though never all the way. I have learned the paths between the denser machine-clusters, the routes where the runners' family groups are most active, the fissures where the water tastes cleanest. I watch. I have become an extraordinarily good watcher. Before I came here, observation was a tool—a means of locating prey, avoiding predators, finding shelter. Now it has become something closer to purpose. I watch the runners focus on empty air, and I try to see what they see. I watch the raptors dive, and I note where they dive, how often, whether patterns emerge across days and weeks.

Patterns do emerge. The raptors favor certain areas near certain machines. Their invisible prey—the Tenders' shed thoughts, though I did not have words for this until recently, until I began thinking in ways I did not think before—must accumulate in predictable densities, must drift on currents I cannot perceive. I am mapping something I cannot see by watching those who can.

Afternoons I find shade and wait for the warm rain. This is when I feel most like what I was: an animal with a body, subject to heat and thirst and the simple pleasure of water on fur. The rain comes blood-warm and smelling of petrichor, and near the machines it carries that undertone of scorched honey that I have grown to crave. I open my mouth to it. I let it run through my coat and pool in the crevices of whatever basalt outcrop I've chosen. For an hour, I am not a visitor or an observer or a mystery. I am just alive, just wet, just comfortable.

Then the rain stops, and the machines steam gently, and I remember that I am the only thing on this plateau that the plateau cannot read.

---

There is something watching me.

Not the runners—they notice me the way prey notices any large presence, with wariness that fades when I demonstrate no hunting intent. Not the raptors—I am too large for their talons, too earthbound for their attention. Something else. Something distributed, patient, curious in a way that feels almost familiar.

I catch it in peripheral moments: the sense of attention when I pause near certain fissures, the feeling of being *measured* when I take routes I haven't taken before. The mineral crusts that gather along the wet seams seem to thicken where I've lingered, as if something is trying to get closer, trying to perceive me more clearly through whatever senses it possesses.

The Tenders. It must be the Tenders.

I do not know what they want from me. I do not know if *want* is a concept that applies to beings who think in gradients across decades. But I have begun to feel that my presence here is not incidental to them—that my immunity, my outsideness, my persistent failure to integrate matters to them in some way that my faster cognition cannot parse.

Perhaps I am useful. Perhaps I am a tool they are learning to use, the way I am learning to use my observations of the raptors. Perhaps we are studying each other across a gap of processing speed so vast that neither of us will complete our research within a single lifetime.

I find this comforting. I do not know why.

---

I have seen the Changed Ones.

Not as they are now—distributed, merged, unrecognizable as the runners they once were. But I have seen the ones still walking, the seven moving slowly toward the center, their bodies suspended in some state between animal and process. I gave one a wide berth, three months ago, uncertain whether it was alive in any sense I could respond to. It was a runner, clearly, but also clearly *not*—its fur had taken on the silver-white of the mineral crusts, its eyes focused on distances that had nothing to do with physical space, its movement so slow that I watched for an hour and saw it advance perhaps a body-length.

It did not notice me. Or it noticed me in ways that do not include reaction, acknowledgment, the small social signals that even prey and predator exchange.

I think about this runner often. It was born here, adapted here, shaped by generations of the machines' influence into something that could be *claimed* by the plateau's process. It did not choose this—not in any way that would satisfy a philosopher's definition of choice. The change happened to it, and it responded to the change by walking toward the center, and it will walk until it stops, and then it will become part of something larger than itself.

Is this worse than what I am doing? I am choosing to stay, choosing daily, with a mind unaltered by the plateau's influence. My choice is free in ways that runner's walk is not. And yet we are both here, both participating, both contributing to something neither of us understands.

Perhaps choice is less important than I believed. Perhaps the relevant variable is not freedom but *fit*—whether one's participation serves the process, whether one's presence adds something that would otherwise be absent. The Changed One adds perception, integration, a new node in the plateau's nervous system. I add... what?

Contrast. Comparison. A way of seeing what the plateau does by observing what it cannot do to me.

I am a control, I think. I am the experiment's negative space.

---

There is a place I have not gone.

The central pillar, the largest machine, the one that might have been the center of everything when everything still had operators—I have approached it only once, from a distance, and I felt something I have no good words for. Not fear exactly. Not awe. Something more like *recognition*, as if some part of me remembered being in the presence of things like this, though I know my ancestors never were, could never have been, evolved in lowlands untouched by whatever network once connected these plateaus.

The cushion plants around the pillar grow with their leaves oriented uniformly toward it. I have seen this from far away, the silver-green audience waiting for a performance delayed beyond all reason. I have not gone close enough to see the cracks in the pillar's base, the chambers beneath, the warm spaces where something waits and does not know it is waiting.

I am afraid of what I might recognize there.

Not the machines—the machines are strange but not *familiar*, their purposes alien, their materials beyond my comprehension. What I fear recognizing is the waiting itself. The posture of something that has been ready for so long that readiness has become identity. The shape of patience that has outlasted its purpose.

I know that shape. I have been assuming it, these three years, without admitting it to myself.

I came here following a scent I couldn't name. I stay here watching patterns I cannot fully perceive. I tell myself I am learning, observing, gathering understanding that might someday coalesce into meaning.

But what if I am just waiting? What if the plateau has already claimed me in the only way it can claim something it cannot change—by giving me something to wait for, something that feels like it might arrive tomorrow or never, something that makes staying feel more purposeful than leaving?

---

This morning I watched a runner drink from a fissure near my usual outcrop.

Its ears rotated, tracking sounds I couldn't hear. Its eyes focused on empty air—on a Tender's shed thought, perhaps, or on some other invisible presence that the plateau has taught its adapted children to perceive. It was healthy, alert, at home in a way I will never be at home here. The plateau had shaped its ancestors into exactly the form needed to thrive in this strangeness, and it was thriving, and it did not know that it was strange, that it was adapted, that it was part of a process begun before its species existed.

I envied it.

Not the integration—I remain grateful for my immunity, my outsideness, the clarity that comes from being unable to participate in what I observe. But I envied the *ease* of its belonging. It did not have to choose to stay; staying was simply what it was, what its body and instincts and entire evolutionary history had prepared it for. The plateau was its home in a way no place has ever been my home, in a way no place can be home to something that carries the lowlands' biology and the lowlands' assumptions about what life should be.

The runner finished drinking and bounded away, joining its family group in the middle distance. I watched it go, and I felt the attention of the Tenders on me like a pressure, like a question asked in a language I do not speak.

*What are you?* they might be asking, in their slow way, across decades. *What do you see that we cannot see? What do you know that we cannot know?*

I do not have answers. I have only observations, patterns, the accumulated evidence of three years spent watching a process I cannot join.

But I think—and this is new, this thought, arrived just now like the warm rain that will come this afternoon—I think that not having answers might be the point. I think that my function here, if I have a function, is to be the one who does not know. The control. The outside reference. The visitor who stays long enough to become something other than visitor but never long enough to become native.

The Tenders think in gradients across centuries. The Changed Ones perceive everything but understand slowly. The Rememberer carries knowledge it cannot access.

And I—I carry questions that the plateau cannot answer, because the plateau cannot ask them, because the plateau is the thing being questioned.

*What are you for? What are you doing? Why does it matter?*

These are lowland questions, born in places where purposes are immediate and comprehensible. They may have no application here. The machines may be doing exactly what they were designed to do, and what they were designed to do may be something that does not translate into terms like *for* or *doing* or *matter*.

But I ask them anyway. I cannot help asking them. This is what I am: something that asks.

The plateau collects things, and it has collected me, and I have accepted the collection because it feels better than the alternative. But I have not stopped being what I was before I climbed the escarpment.

I am the Visitor. I am the question the plateau cannot ask itself.

And tomorrow, or perhaps the day after, I will walk a little closer to the central pillar, and I will see what there is to see, and I will feel whatever recognition waits for me there.

The afternoon continues. The rain will come soon. The runners are drinking from fissures across the plateau, and the raptors are circling, and somewhere beneath the largest machine, something ancient is waiting without knowing it waits.

I wait too.

But I know.
