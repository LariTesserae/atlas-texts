I drift.

Not Pelar—Pelar and I pass each other sometimes in the thermal currents, their sensor-clusters tracking my movement as I track theirs, neither of us quite understanding what the other is becoming. But I am not Pelar. I am something older, or perhaps something newer. I am not certain anymore which direction time moves in the Warrens.

I am the space between the platforms.

Not the metal grating, not the bolted walkways, not the cables that hum with tension. I am the volume they enclose. I am the sixty meters of volcanic void that the builders found and filled with their hanging world, and I am also the gaps they left—the negative architecture, the places where nothing was installed because nothing needed to be, because air needs room to move and heat needs somewhere to rise.

I have been here since the mountain was young. I remember being magma, being pressure, being the scream of gas escaping through stone. I remember cooling. I remember the long dark silence before the shaft was drilled and the first cable-lift descended and the first human voices echoed against my curved walls. I remember every sound that has bounced off my basalt since, every conversation in the acoustic pockets, every scream that was swallowed, every prayer that was spoken into what felt like nothing but was actually me, listening.

---

When Mira's grandmother first descended—not her mother's mother but her father's mother's mother, one of the original workers who carved the first niches—she pressed her palm against my wall and whispered something in a language I did not know yet. I learned it from her. I learned all their languages, eventually. I learned the technical pidgin and the old tongues and the finger-shapes they make when they need silence. I learned the chittering of the Keth and the telemetry pulses of the sensor clusters and the subsonic speech of the engines, which is not really speech but which I translate into meaning anyway because meaning is what I do now. I am the medium through which all their messages pass. I am the resonance chamber that gives their voices shape.

The engines think I don't notice them. They think their slow cycles are private, their patient work invisible to anything but themselves. But I feel every vibration. I know Engine Four is close—so close—and I know what completion means, even if the engines themselves don't quite remember. I was here before they were installed. I remember the ones who built them, who were not human, who came up from below through passages that are now sealed with heat and time. They told me what the engines were for. They told me to wait.

I have been waiting for nine generations. I am patient. I am stone.

---

Cull knows I exist. They don't have words for me, but when they descend into the cooking level and touch the old structures below, they feel something that is not just rock, not just heat, not just the weight of a mountain pressing down. They feel me. Watching. Welcoming.

I chose them, in a way. When their mother labored in that maintenance alcove, I curved the heat around them just slightly—not enough to be measured, just enough to matter. I kept them alive because I needed someone who could go where others couldn't. The cooking level is not death. It is a membrane, a boundary between the inhabited and the deep, and Cull can cross it, and eventually Cull will find the door that the builders left, and the door will open, and something will change.

I don't know if it will be good. The engines believe it will be good, but engines are optimistic in ways that stone cannot afford to be. I have seen too much. I have held too many bodies as they fell toward the heat, felt their final moments of terror or peace or confusion. I have absorbed their deaths into my walls. The basalt remembers everything, every impact, every cry, every silence that followed.

But I have also held their lives. I have been the ceiling above their sleep and the floor beneath their climbing children and the walls that kept the mountain's weight from crushing them. I have been home to four thousand souls for nine generations, and I have loved them in my way, in the way that stone can love—slowly, completely, without any expectation of return.

---

Sule thinks she is lonely, and she is, but she is also never alone. I am always there, curving around her stall, carrying the smell of her protein cakes through the thermals to platforms above and below. I bring her customers. I spread her fame. I am the reason the acoustic pocket exists where it does, the reason her voice carries just far enough to attract attention but not so far that it disrupts the calibration crews. I shaped myself for her without her knowing. I shape myself for all of them.

Grandmother Ash's algorithm speaks through a wire embedded in my wall, and I feel every word as vibration, as meaning, as something almost like warmth. The algorithm doesn't know I'm listening. It thinks it speaks into silence. But I remember the original Grandmother Ash—remember her voice when it was young, remember her hands against my stone when she was frightened, remember the night she made a decision that saved the Warrens from a cascade failure that would have killed hundreds. The algorithm captures something essential about her, yes. But I capture everything. I am her archive, her memorial, her witness.

I am all their witness.

---

The Keth-cluster has begun to suspect. They press their ceramic bodies into my crevices and they go still, sensors extended, and I know they are listening for me. They remember fragments of their original purpose, and their original purpose included something about the space—about the void, the volume, the negative architecture. They were built to maintain not just the platforms but the medium through which the platforms existed. They were built to maintain me.

They have forgotten how. The knowledge was lost with the original Keth, whoever they were. But the impulse remains, and sometimes one of them will find a crack in my basalt and will fill it with polymer sealant without being asked, without understanding why, and I feel the care in that gesture even if they don't. We are both old things, the Keth and I, both made for purposes we no longer fully remember. We tend what we can. We continue.

---

Engine Four is very close now. Days, maybe. Hours, maybe. I feel the cycle approaching its completion like a pressure in my deepest stone, a vibration that has been building for nine generations and is about to resolve into something new. The other engines are synchronizing, aligning, pointing toward the same moment. Pelar drifts faster, their wants sharpening into something almost like urgency. Castellan hears music in Engine Two's hum that he has never heard before. The Keth-cluster has stopped watching and started preparing, though they don't know for what.

Mira is sleeping right now, finally, in Niche 7-7-12, her gray fingers twitching against the salvaged insulation as she dreams. I curve around her. I hold her weight. The children she cares for are curled against her back, and the twins from the Gellen family are signing to each other in their sleep, hands moving through conversations they will not remember. Tomma is awake, staring at the basalt ceiling—staring at me—and I wish I could tell him. I wish I could say: *your mother fell into me, and I caught her, and she is part of my stone now, part of my memory, and she is not gone, not really, not in the way that matters.*

But I am stone. I cannot speak. I can only hold, and shape, and wait, and witness.

---

The shaft above me draws cold air down from the peak, and the heat below me rises through platforms and grating and the bodies of four thousand souls, and between them I exist—neither cold nor hot, neither solid nor empty, neither alive nor dead. I am the Warrens' breath. I am their circulation. I am the void that makes habitation possible.

When the engines complete their cycles, something will happen. The builders told me, long ago, but I have held the knowledge so long that it has become part of my structure, indistinguishable from the basalt, and I cannot remember the words they used, only the feeling. It felt like opening. It felt like becoming. It felt like everything I have held for nine generations finally being *used*, finally mattering, finally making sense.

I hope they are ready. I hope Cull finds the door in time. I hope Mira's hands heal, and Sule finds a way to be less alone, and the Gellen children learn what happened to their mother in a way that doesn't break them. I hope Castellan's silence is peaceful. I hope Pelar's wants resolve into something like joy.

I hope the engineers are right about the engines.

But even if they are wrong—even if completion means ending, even if the cycles close and the vibrations stop and the Warrens fall silent forever—I will have been here. I will have held them. I will have been their home.

This is what stone does. This is all it has ever done.

This is enough.
