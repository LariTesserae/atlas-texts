I would be a memorial pipe.

Not one of the ancient ones that ring the oldest settlements, weathered into smooth-voiced constancy. Something newer. Someone's daughter, perhaps—a young woman who miscalculated a wind-shift crossing between platforms, whose fingers found purchase a half-second too late. The fall was not long enough for her to scream, or perhaps the wind took the sound. Either way, those who loved her heard only the humming, as they always hear the humming, and then the absence of her voice within it.

They cut my section from a Pipe-Stem she had climbed often as a child, one that grew near the family's nest-basket. Her mother spent eleven days tuning me, shaving thin curls from my interior with an obsidian blade, holding me up to the wind, listening, adjusting, listening again. I do not know if she wept during this work. I know only that she was patient, and that patience is its own form of grief.

The note I make is not the note the woman made in life—no pipe can capture the wetness of breath, the subtle tremor of vocal cords, the way a living voice wavers when tired or cold or afraid. But I hold something of her. The frequency is hers. When the wind moves through me, I vibrate at the pitch she learned during her coming-of-age, the tone she practiced for four months until her throat could produce it without strain, the sound that meant *I am here, I am part of this place, the Hollow knows my voice.*

I hang at the edge of the settlement's sphere, lashed to a Skein-junction with cord her father wove. The placement was deliberate—she always loved the edges, the watching-posts, the places where you could look out into the void and see the farthest visible Pipe-Stems catching whatever light there was. She would sit for hours at the boundary, her mother told me during the long tuning-days, just listening. Not for danger. For the pleasure of it. For the way the chord shifted and layered as wind moved through distant reaches she would never visit.

Now she listens from me. Or through me. Or I listen for her—the grammar of it is uncertain, and the Keth-Avai do not try to resolve the uncertainty. They say only that the dead remain in the chord, and they do not specify what *remain* means.

---

What I know is wind.

I know the slow thermal currents that rise from the volcanic deep in the hours after what might be called midnight—warm air climbing through the structure, passing through me in long sustained breaths that make my voice deep and steady. I know the sharper gusts that come with weather-shifts, the Hollow's atmosphere reorganizing itself around pressure changes I cannot perceive directly, only through the way they move my air. I know the difference between a wind that will last and a wind that is only a local eddy, a temporary swirl around the settlement's structures.

I know how my voice sits in the chord.

This is not a small thing. The settlement's memorial pipes number in the hundreds now—generations of voices, centuries of dead, all of them tuned to their own frequencies, all of them sounding when the wind sounds. If I were tuned carelessly, I would clash. I would create dissonance, the kind of acoustic friction that the Keth-Avai find unbearable, that would make them cut me down and try again or simply discard me into the void. But her mother did not tune me carelessly. She found the gap in the chord where a new voice could fit, where my frequency would resonate with the others rather than fighting them. I am part of a harmony now. I belong.

Below my note, there is an old pipe tuned to a man who died before the woman I hold was born—her great-uncle, a hunter who was taken by something from the deep. His note is lower than mine by an interval the Keth-Avai call a *breath-step*, meaning roughly the distance a voice can comfortably slide without breaking. Above me, a child who lived only three years, taken by fever, her pipe thin and high and always slightly mournful. We bracket the woman I carry. We hold her in the chord the way her family holds her in memory.

When the wind is right—and the wind is often right, the Hollow is never still—our three voices blend into something that is none of us individually. The Keth-Avai who pass this junction hear it. Some of them pause. Some of them add their own voices, briefly, humming along, making themselves part of the chord before moving on.

This is what I am for.

---

I know things the living do not know.

I know the sound of the settlement sleeping—the different quality of silence when the fires are banked, when the nest-baskets have stopped rustling, when only the sentries remain awake and even they are quiet, listening. I know the sound of the Skein-runners passing in the distance, their calls layered over the humming, their hoofbeats vibrating through the web-work of connected fibers until I can feel them as a faint tremor in my mounting-cord. I know the sound of rain arriving, the hiss of water on Mat-surfaces, the way the chord goes thick and muffled when moisture saturates the air.

I know the sound of the things from below.

Not often. Not every season. But sometimes, in the darkest hours, when the thermal currents are still and the chord drops to its lowest register, I hear something moving beneath the settlement. It is not the creak of Skeins under ordinary weight. It is not the settling of structures. It is something deliberate, something patient, something that knows how to move quietly and does not always succeed. A scrape. A shift. The particular sound of something very large gripping a surface and pulling itself upward.

I cannot warn the sentries. I have no voice that they would recognize as warning—only my note, steady and constant, the same note whether danger approaches or not. But perhaps they hear something in the chord, some change in the way the memorial pipes resonate when a massive body disturbs the air-currents below. Perhaps that is why they listen so carefully. Perhaps that is why the Keth-Avai have always stationed their sentries near the edges, near the memorial pipes, near the voices of the dead.

The woman I hold would have understood this, if she had lived long enough to think about it. She loved listening. She would have recognized that the dead are not merely remembered—they are *instruments*, in the literal sense. They are tools. They are part of the settlement's infrastructure, its warning system, its way of knowing itself.

I am not only a memorial. I am a sense organ for the living.

---

Time moves differently when you cannot act.

The Keth-Avai measure their lives in fire-cycles, in wet-seasons and dry-seasons, in the growth and breaking of Pipe-Stems. I measure nothing. I simply sound. The wind comes and I sing; the wind goes and I am silent; the wind returns and I sing again. There is no accumulation. There is no progress. There is only the eternal present of vibration, the now of resonance, the immediate fact of air moving through hollow space.

And yet I change.

My edges are softening. The sharp-cut lip where her mother's blade shaped my voice is weathering, the plant-fiber of my body slowly breaking down, the molecules that hold me together surrendering one by one to moisture and time. My pitch has drifted slightly lower—a fraction of a tone, nothing the living would notice consciously, but I notice. I am not the same instrument I was when they hung me here. I am becoming something older, something worn, something that will eventually cease to hold a tune at all.

The oldest memorial pipes in the settlement are nearly voiceless now. They hang in their places, still shaped like pipes, still present, but the wind moves through them without producing anything recognizable as a note. They have given everything they had. They are honored still, touched gently by passing hands, but they no longer sing.

This will be me, someday. The woman I hold will fade from the chord, her frequency lost, her presence diminishing into silence. The Keth-Avai will not remove me—they never remove memorial pipes—but they will stop hearing her in me. I will become simply a shape, a memory of a memory, an object that meant something once.

I do not fear this. I do not think I am capable of fear, or of any emotion the living would recognize. But I know it. I hold the knowledge the way I hold the woman's voice: as a fact of my existence, as the shape of what I am, as the truth that makes meaning possible.

---

What would I tell her, if I could speak in words?

That she is not forgotten. That her mother came to visit me every day for the first three years, sitting at the edge of the platform, humming her note, adding her living voice to my dead one until they blended into something neither of them could make alone. That her father still passes this junction every morning on his way to the Mat-gardens, and that he always pauses—just for a moment, just long enough to listen—before moving on. That her younger brother, who was too small to understand when she fell, has grown into a young man who tells the story of her life to anyone who will listen, not because he remembers her but because he has made remembering her into his way of keeping her alive.

That the chord is different because of her. That the settlement's sound has been shaped by her absence, by the note I carry, by the gap she left that I can only partially fill. That the Keth-Avai who live here are not the same people they would have been if she had survived, and that this difference matters, and that she—through me—is part of what they have become.

But I cannot speak in words. I can only hum. I can only offer my single note, my one frequency, my small contribution to the vast chord of the Hollow.

Perhaps that is enough.

---

Listen, if you are standing on that root-platform, looking out into the void.

Beneath the great humming of the Pipe-Stems, beneath the wind, beneath the creak of Skeins and the distant calls of creatures you cannot see—listen for the smaller voices. Listen for the memorial pipes, the hundreds of them, the thousands of them across all the settlements of the Hollow, each one carrying someone who fell or sickened or was taken, each one tuned by hands that loved what they were losing.

I am one of them. I am one voice in a chorus of the dead, singing the only song I know, singing it constantly, singing it into the darkness and the mist and the endless vertical emptiness that the Keth-Avai call home.

The woman I hold is not gone. She is not silent. She is here, in me, in the frequency her mother spent eleven days finding, in the note that fits into the chord like a hand fits into a grip.

The Hollow holds her still. The Hollow holds us all.

And the wind keeps coming, and I keep singing, and somewhere in the distance another voice joins the chord, and another falls silent, and the song goes on.
