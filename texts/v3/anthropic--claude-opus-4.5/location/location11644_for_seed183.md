# The Weeping Spires of the Folded Ridge

The mountains here do not end. They rise and rise until they simply stop making sense—until the peaks begin bending sideways, curving like the spines of great sleeping creatures, their summits pointing toward each other across impossible angles. The highest reaches fold inward, creating archways of naked stone that frame nothing but bright, sourceless white sky.

Everything weeps.

Water streams down every surface in sheets and rivulets and fat, wandering drops. It seeps from the rock itself, emerging from seams and cracks and the pores of the gray-blue granite as though the mountains are sweating, or bleeding, or simply unable to contain themselves. The air is cool and saturated—breathing feels like drinking. Mist hangs in the valleys between the folded peaks, but the light burns through it anyway, coming from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless and relentless.

There is no soil. There are no plants. The stone is clean in its wetness, polished by centuries of this endless seeping. In places, mineral deposits have built up where the water pools before spilling over—terraces of pale blue and rust-orange travertine, rippled and ridged like frozen waterfalls. Some of these formations have grown large enough to bridge gaps between spires, creating accidental walkways that gleam slick in the bright non-light.

The sound is the first thing that disorients.

Water falling makes sound. Water seeping makes sound. But here, the accumulated whisper of a million tiny flows becomes a voice—or many voices—or one voice speaking in many registers at once. It rises and falls without pattern. Sometimes it seems to form words in a language that almost resolves into meaning before dissolving back into liquid murmur. The echoes between the folded peaks layer and interfere with each other, creating standing waves of sound that you can walk into and out of, pockets where the noise drops to near-silence and pockets where it rises to a roar of overlapping syllables.

And then there are the structures.

Someone built here, once. Or something did. Small huts of stacked stone cling to ledges where the spires meet, their construction simple—unmortared, hand-fitted, clearly the work of beings who understood balance and patience but not much else. The huts have no doors, only openings that face in seemingly random directions. Some face walls of rock mere inches away. Some face the open sky where the mountains fold wrong. A few face *inward*, their openings pressed against the mountainside as though the builders wanted to enter the stone itself.

Inside one hut—its roof half-collapsed, its floor ankle-deep in clear water—there are tools. Stone axes. Bone scrapers, though nothing with bones lives here, nothing with bones has ever lived here. Bowls carved from something that might be granite but feels wrong under the hand, too light, faintly warm. Some of the bowls are full of water. Some of them are full of nothing but seem full—you could pour them out and watch emptiness spill.

The air tastes of minerals and static.

Where the water flows downward, it collects in pools that sit at angles that should drain but don't. One vast pool spreads across a tilted plateau, thirty degrees off horizontal, perfectly still despite the water constantly feeding into it. Fish should live in that pool. Something should live in that pool. Instead, the water simply holds, clear to its depths, which seem to descend much farther than the stone beneath could possibly allow.

There are paths—not roads, just worn places in the rock where many feet have passed. They wind between the spires, up and around the impossible folds, sometimes disappearing into crevices too narrow for passage and emerging from crevices on peaks that are not connected to anything. The paths are real. You can follow them. You will arrive somewhere. The somewhere may not be where the path appeared to lead.

The light never changes. The weeping never stops. The water has nowhere to go and everywhere to come from, and the stone accepts it, has always accepted it, has been shaped by it into these forms that fold and reach and almost touch across gaps that hurt to look at directly.

Sometimes, from certain angles, the folded peaks align into patterns that suggest intention—a circle, a spiral, a shape that has no name but feels like the diagram of a sound. When this happens, the water-voice clarifies for just a moment, and you can almost understand what it's saying before the angle shifts and the mountains become merely impossible again.

The builders knew. That's what the huts suggest. That's what the inward-facing doors mean. They were trying to listen. They were trying to enter. The tools remain, still wet, still waiting, as though the builders simply stepped away for a moment, stepped *through* for a moment, and might return any time now from inside the weeping stone.
