# The Calving Plains

The city is being born.

You stand at the edge of a platform—one of thousands scattered across this immense frozen tableland—and watch the construction happening below, above, around you, in every direction until the curve of the world swallows the distance. The plains stretch for what must be eight hundred kilometers before the horizon takes them, a pale expanse of new-formed ice and the dark geometric shapes of structures still emerging from it.

The machines work in silence, or near enough. Swarms of them, each no larger than a grain of rice, moving through the ice like thought through dreaming. Where they pass, the frozen water reorganizes itself—crystalline lattices shifting, reforming, becoming architecture. You can see a tower taking shape three kilometers to the north, its translucent walls thickening as the nanoscale constructors migrate upward through its structure. It will be complete in a few days. It will house no one for years yet. The city builds itself in anticipation of inhabitants who haven't been born, on a world that's still deciding what it wants to be.

---

The cold is significant but not punishing—enough to make your breath visible, to keep the ice stable, to slow biological processes to a contemplative pace. The air carries moisture from the warming vents that punctuate the plain at regular intervals, each one a subtle exhalation of geothermal energy tapped from far below. Around these vents, the ice weeps. Meltwater pools in shallow depressions before refreezing into glass-smooth lenses, and it's in these transitional zones that you find the life.

The herdbeasts came first—introduced, adapted, then left to find their own way. They move across the plain in loose aggregations of dozens, maybe hundreds, their bodies low-slung and heavily furred, their wide feet spreading their weight across the ice. They eat the frost-lichen that grows on any surface the constructors aren't actively using, and they've learned to avoid the building zones by some combination of instinct and bitter experience. When a swarm is active, the ice beneath it hums at frequencies the herdbeasts can feel in their bones. They give those areas wide berth.

Hunting them—or following them, or simply sharing their migration routes—are the glass-wolves. You've seen four packs today, which the monitoring systems tell you is unusually high activity. They're not wolves in any biological sense; their ancestors were something between a large mustelid and a patience-based predator from the original colony's specimen banks. But they hunt in coordinated groups and they howl, so wolf is what people call them. Their fur is pale to the point of translucence, and when they stand still against the ice, they become nearly invisible—just a shimmer, a suggestion of movement that might be wind across the surface or might be death waiting.

There are birds, too, though that word also fails to capture them. They ride the thermals that rise from the warming vents, hundreds of them spiraling upward in loose columns before dispersing across the plain to scavenge, to hunt the smaller ice-crawlers, to perform whatever other errands their small fierce minds have invented for themselves. Their calls are thin and high, the only sound besides the wind that carries across the plain.

---

The scale is the thing that never stops being strange.

You can see weather forming to the west—a system of low pressure pulling moisture from the vents into a long gray band of cloud that will eventually become snow. It's two hundred kilometers away and you can watch it grow, watch the way it interacts with the structures already built beneath it, the towers and platforms and covered walkways that alter wind patterns in ways the original terraforming models didn't fully anticipate. The city is changing its own climate by the fact of its existence. The constructors adapt, adjusting their building patterns to account for snowdrift accumulation, for ice-stress from thermal cycling, for the way the herdbeasts' migration routes are shifting as the built environment expands.

Somewhere beyond the weather system, beyond the visible horizon, the construction continues. The monitoring networks extend for thousands of kilometers in every direction, and they report that sixty-two percent of the planned urban zone is currently in some stage of emergence. Foundations being laid in the molecular structure of the ice. Transportation corridors forming as tubes of reinforced crystal. Power distribution networks growing like root systems through the frozen substrate. All of it happening at the pace of patience, the pace of a civilization that has learned to think in centuries because it finally can.

---

The platform you're standing on is old, relatively speaking—one of the first permanent structures, built before the constructors were sophisticated enough to work unsupervised. Its walls are thicker than they need to be, its systems redundant in ways that newer construction finds unnecessary. But it's stable, and it's warm inside, and the observation deck gives you a view that the newer, more elegant towers somehow lack. From here you can see the way the plain breathes—the subtle rhythms of construction and weather and animal movement, the way the glass-wolves circle a herd at distances that seem random until you watch long enough to see the pattern.

A flock of the not-birds passes overhead, briefly darkening the gray sky. The wind shifts. Somewhere to the north, a tower finishes forming and goes still, its constructors departing in streams too small to see, dispersing into the ice to begin their next project.

The city is being born, and it is taking its time, and the plain stretches away in every direction holding everything it will eventually become.
