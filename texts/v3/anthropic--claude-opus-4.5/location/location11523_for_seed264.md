# The Resonance Flats

The sound reaches you before understanding does—a deep, unwavering drone that seems to emanate from the ground itself, from the air, from the space behind your thoughts. It never changes pitch. It never wavers. It has been exactly this loud, exactly this frequency, for longer than anything has existed here to hear it.

You stand on a plain of dark glass that extends to every horizon, interrupted only by the slow undulations of its own frozen topography. The glass is volcanic in origin but ancient beyond reckoning—not the sharp obsidian of recent flows but something worn to a kind of smoothness by ages of contact with warm, wet air. Its surface holds a permanent film of condensation, never quite drying, never accumulating into pools. Your footsteps leave brief dry prints that fog over within seconds.

The landscape rolls in long, low waves—swells of glass perhaps three meters high at their crests, with troughs between them that collect slightly more moisture, enough to create darker ribbons across the plain. These formations repeat with an almost mathematical regularity, as though the original eruption followed laws more precise than geology usually permits. Or as though something shaped them afterward, and then stopped, and then nothing else ever happened.

The drone comes from columns.

They rise from the glass at intervals of perhaps two hundred meters—metal cylinders, each roughly fifteen meters tall and four meters in diameter, their surfaces marked with regular patterns of vents and apertures. They are the color of weathered bronze, though closer inspection reveals materials you cannot name. Each column vibrates at a frequency you can feel in your teeth, in your joints, and together they produce the great chord that fills the Flats.

They are not buildings. They were not built. You understand this immediately, though you cannot say how. They grew here, or precipitated, or emerged from some process that has industrial characteristics without requiring industry—like crystals that happen to form turbines, like geology that arrives at engines. Their vents intake the humid air and exhale it warmer, drier. Their vibration is the sound of this respiration, this metabolism of atmosphere.

Whatever they are doing, they have been doing for so long that the system has reached perfect equilibrium. The moisture they process returns as condensation on the cooling glass. The warmth they generate maintains the temperature that keeps the condensation cycle active. Nothing accumulates. Nothing depletes. The Flats exist in a state of dynamic stasis, every process exactly balanced by its inverse.

No plants grow here. The glass admits nothing; the columns emit nothing that seeds could use. But in the deeper troughs, where moisture gathers thickest, you occasionally see movement—something like a flat ribbon of gray tissue, perhaps a meter long, sliding across the wet surface with muscular undulation. These creatures—if they are creatures—seem to feed on nothing, produce nothing, simply move in slow circuits through the troughs. When you approach, they do not flee. They have never needed to flee from anything.

The light is the amber-gray of permanent overcast, though no clouds are visible—only a kind of luminous haze that the columns seem to exhale along with their processed air. It casts no shadows sharp enough to navigate by. The horizon blurs into the sky at every edge.

Walk for an hour in any direction and you will find more columns, more glass waves, more ribbon-creatures in their endless loops. Walk for a day and nothing will be different. The Flats extend for distances that would take weeks to cross, and every part of them has been exactly like this since before the concept of change had meaning here.

The drone enters you and stays. Even if you could leave—and the Flats have edges, somewhere, they must—you suspect some part of you would keep vibrating at this frequency forever. The columns do not notice you. They do not notice anything. They simply continue their ancient respiration, processing moisture and time in equal measure, converting the possibility of change into the certainty of permanence.

Somewhere in the haze, another ribbon-creature slides through its trough, following a path it has followed ten thousand times, will follow ten thousand more. The glass holds its condensation. The drone holds its note. The Flats hold their breath, and have always been holding it, and will never exhale.
