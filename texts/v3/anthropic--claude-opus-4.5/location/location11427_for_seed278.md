# The Crawling Plateau

The cold comes first. It seeps through everything here—through the grey air, through the stones that have forgotten warmth, through the very concept of shelter. This is a high place, a vast tilted shelf of world that rises above cloud-lines and continues rising, imperceptibly, toward horizons that never arrive. The plateau stretches in all directions with a patience that predates the idea of patience. Walk for days and the view barely shifts: the same weathered basalt, the same shallow depressions where meltwater pools and freezes and pools again, the same dim sky the color of old pewter.

There is no sun visible. There hasn't been for longer than anyone could measure. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere—a distributed luminescence that the air itself seems to emit, sourceless, casting no shadows or casting shadows in every direction at once. It is enough to see by, barely. Enough to make out the shapes that move.

---

They cover the plateau. Not densely—there is space between them, sometimes miles of space—but there are so many, and the plateau is so vast, that their numbers become incomprehensible. They move slowly, almost too slowly to perceive, like watching hour hands. Massive things. Industrial things.

The nearest one is perhaps three miles away, and it fills a significant portion of the visible world. Its body is segmented, armored in plates of something that looks like oxidized bronze but behaves like chitin, each segment the size of a city block. It has no discernible head. Instead, at its leading edge, a cluster of grinding mechanisms work perpetually at the stone beneath it—great rotating drums studded with teeth of unknown alloy, chemical nozzles that spray acids or solvents, articulated scoops that carry powdered rock into intake ports along its underside. Behind it, the plateau is slightly lower than it was before. This is erosion. This is how erosion happens here.

The creature—if creature is the right word—makes almost no sound. A low subsonic hum travels through the ground, felt in the bones more than heard. Occasionally a faint grinding, like distant thunder, when it encounters a particularly dense vein of rock. But mostly silence. The vast silence of a cold high place where even wind moves slowly, reluctantly, as if the air itself has grown tired.

---

More of them are visible from any vantage point, if you have the eyes to find them. Some are larger—segmented shapes that wind across the plateau like living mountain ranges, their bodies disappearing into the dim distance before curving back, perhaps, or perhaps simply continuing forever. Some are smaller, merely the size of warehouses, working in clusters around geological features that interest them. Their forms vary: serpentine, radial, configurations that suggest centipedes or millipedes built at architectural scale, others that resemble nothing evolved but rather manufactured, designed by principles of efficiency that have nothing to do with biology.

Yet they are not machines. Not exactly. They breathe, in their way—great vents along their flanks that open periodically to release plumes of processed minerals, metalite compounds, gases that the plateau absorbs back into itself. They reproduce, though the process takes centuries and no observer has ever witnessed it begin or end. When one dies—if they die—its body becomes part of the plateau itself, indistinguishable within a millennium from any other ridge or depression.

They are the fauna here. The overwhelming, patient, nearly silent fauna of the Crawling Plateau.

---

Moisture collects in their tracks. The channels they carve become temporary rivers when the temperature shifts, when whatever passes for seasons in this place brings brief thaws. Ice forms in the deeper grooves, accumulates in the cavities where grinding mechanisms have worked for decades at the same stubborn outcrop. Some tracks are ancient enough to hold permanent glaciers, ribbons of grey ice that wind across the plateau like frozen rivers—which, in a sense, they are.

Nothing grows here except in these wet places. Lichens the color of dried blood cling to stones at the waterline. Fungal mats, white and faintly luminescent, spread across the undersides of ice shelves. In rare deeper pools, something like algae clouds the water with pale green—the only color, the only true color, in this entire landscape of greys and rusted bronze and pewter sky.

The vegetation, sparse as it is, exists only where the great crawlers have been. They are the plateau's gardeners without intention, its farmers without purpose. The minerals they process, the moisture their passages trap, the sheltered microenvironments their trails create—all of this permits life, however marginal, to persist.

---

The plateau has no edges that anyone has found. It may wrap around a world. It may be a world—a single continuous surface that curves back on itself through dimensions not meant for comprehension. The sky offers no clues: no stars visible through the perpetual dim glow, no moons, no variation in light that might suggest planetary rotation. Time passes—the pools freeze and thaw, the crawlers move imperceptibly forward, the lichens grow in rings that might mark years—but it passes strangely, at its own pace, as if the plateau keeps its own time and shares it reluctantly with visitors.

The cold deepens in what might be winter. The air grows sharp enough to ache. The crawlers continue regardless, their internal furnaces—if that's what they are—burning fuel drawn from the stone itself. Their trails steam faintly in the deepest cold, warm enough to melt the frost for a few feet on either side before the plateau reclaims it.

This is a place of slow processes. Of patience measured in geological time. Of creatures—or machines—or something between—that have been wearing down this high cold plateau for longer than mountains elsewhere have existed. The erosion they perform is ancient, and it continues, and it will continue, and the plateau bears it with the resignation of all stone everywhere, which is not resignation at all but simply the way things are, the way they have always been, the way they will always be until the last crawler grinds to a halt and becomes the ridge it was always destined to become.

Walk carefully here. The ground looks solid but sometimes isn't—sometimes a crawler passed through centuries ago and left hollow spaces, chambers of processed air, pockets where the worked stone has different properties than the stone around it. Step wrong and you might fall into darkness that hums with residual warmth, that smells of metal and something almost biological, that might shelter you from the cold or might simply swallow you into the plateau's slow metabolism.

The silence is not absolute. Listen long enough and you'll hear it: the subsonic grinding, the faint whisper of moisture freezing, the distant exhalation of a crawler venting processed gases into the dim air. Listen longer and you might hear something else—a rhythm to it all, a pattern too slow and too vast to perceive directly, the heartbeat of a plateau that is, in some way no one could ever prove, alive.
