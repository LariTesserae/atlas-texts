# The Canopy Plateau

The first thing you understand is that the ground is not ground.

You stand on a surface that gives slightly beneath each step, springy and warm, woven from ten thousand years of root-mat and accumulated growth. This is the crown of something older than nations—a forest that grew upward through geological time, climbing the volcanic plateau until the trees themselves became the terrain. The actual stone lies somewhere far below, buried under centuries of living architecture, glimpsed only where ancient sinkholes have opened passages into the hollow dark beneath.

The plateau rises nearly three kilometers above the distant lowlands, its edges worn to soft undulations by winds that have blown since before anything with a spine drew breath here. What erosion could not flatten, the forest absorbed. Cliffs became root-walls. Ravines filled with the patient accumulation of growth. The landscape has settled into its final form—not dead, but complete, a system that found its equilibrium and kept it.

From where you stand, the canopy extends to every horizon.

---

It is not dense. That's what strikes you, once the scale settles in. The trees are massive—trunks wider than village squares, bark textured like weathered canyon walls—but they are spaced apart like columns in a cathedral built by something that thought in centuries. The understory is sparse, filtered to a haze of epiphytes and hanging gardens. Light falls in broad shafts, warm and gold, catching the moisture that drifts through everything. The air tastes green. It tastes alive.

But the space between the great trees is *open*. You can see for kilometers through the forest, the trunks receding into blue-green distance, the canopy a continuous ceiling so high above that weather happens beneath it. Mist forms in the mid-levels. Rain falls from leaves hundreds of meters up—not from clouds, but condensation gathering on the architecture of the forest itself, dripping down in localized showers that sweep through like slow curtains of wet light.

The temperature holds steady, blood-warm, never cooling even as the sun angles toward what passes for evening up here. The forest breathes. You can feel it—a slow rhythm in the humidity, the pressure, the way the air moves.

---

And then there is the sound.

It begins before you're ready for it, and it does not stop.

The fauna of the Canopy Plateau exists in such density, such overwhelming presence, that their voices merge into a single continuous roar. Birds—thousands of species, millions of individuals—call from every level of the vertical world. They are the size of sparrows and the size of cattle. They move through the open air in flocks that take minutes to pass, their wingbeats a subsonic thrum beneath the screaming of their cries. Some are iridescent. Some are camouflaged so perfectly they exist only as movement. Some have developed calls that resonate through the hollow trunks of the great trees, emerging as bass notes you feel in your chest.

The insects are louder still. Cicadas the size of your palm, their songs drilling into frequencies that blur thought. Beetles that drum on bark with specialized limbs, communicating in rhythms too complex to follow. Things with too many wings that hover in clouds, their collective hum rising and falling like breath.

And in the deeper architecture of the forest—on branches as wide as roads, in the hollows where trees have fused into labyrinths—the larger things move. Primates with hands adapted for the rope-bridges of aerial root, their territorial calls echoing for kilometers. Gliding mammals that bark as they leap. Predators that have learned to move in the gaps between the noise, their silence more frightening than any cry.

You cannot think clearly here. The sound is not background; it is medium. It fills the space the way water fills a lake. Your own voice, if you tried to use it, would vanish into the chorus without ripple.

---

The built structures appear in the middle distances—never close, never far. Platforms of woven branch and sinew lashed to the great trunks. Rope-ways strung between them, some sagging, some taut, some clearly abandoned to the slow patient reclamation of the trees. The materials are hand-worked: stone axes, bone awls, plant-fiber cordage, nothing that requires fire beyond what the sun provides. Whatever people live here—and you catch glimpses of them sometimes, small figures moving along the branch-roads, pausing to watch—they have built nothing that the forest cannot eventually absorb.

Smoke rises from one platform, kilometers away. A figure stands at its edge, motionless, looking out over the canopy. You cannot tell if they have seen you.

The forest does not care. It holds you all—the builders, the beasts, the accumulated millennia—in the same warm, deafening, breathing embrace. It has reached its final form. It is waiting for nothing. It simply continues, vast and complete, while the plateau wears down by millimeters and the creatures scream their unceasing songs into the green cathedral air.
