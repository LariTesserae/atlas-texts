# The Nest That Grew Backwards

The hollow sits inside a shoulder of young mountain, where the rock pushed up so recently that the stone still remembers being deeper. You find it by following the condensation—the air grows heavier, wetter, until droplets form on your skin without any cloud or spring to explain them. Then a crack in the basalt, barely wide enough for shoulders turned sideways, and you're inside.

The space is no larger than a modest room, roughly oval, ceiling low enough that you must crouch in places. But the walls are thick with growing things, and what grows here grows *inward*, toward itself, in ways that make your eyes want to slide away and return and slide away again.

---

**The Vegetation**

Moss, but the moss spirals. Not in the way of ferns unfurling—this moss has grown in helices that tighten as they approach the center of the hollow, each tendril reaching toward some invisible axis that hangs in the air roughly two feet from the back wall. The effect is of a slow green whirlpool frozen mid-drain. Small ferns sprout from cracks in the basalt, but their fronds all point the same direction regardless of where the light enters. Liverworts carpet the floor in overlapping scales, and when you look closely, you can see that each scale bears a smaller pattern that echoes the shape of the whole room.

The air is warm—blood-warm—and so humid that breathing feels like drinking. Water weeps from the ceiling in threads too thin to drip, sliding down the walls in sheets that never seem to reach the floor. The liverworts drink it before it arrives.

---

**The Built Things**

Someone has been here before. Many someones, or the same someone many times across what might be years or might be something else. They left small structures: a cairn of seven stones that refuse to cast shadows in the correct direction, a woven ring of mountain grass hung from a basalt spur, the bones of small animals arranged into a shape that almost resolves into meaning if you look long enough.

The weaving is old but not rotted. The bones are clean but not weathered. Time moves differently here—not faster or slower, but *reluctantly*, as if each moment must be convinced to follow the last.

Near the narrowest point of the hollow, where the spiral of the moss reaches its tightest turn, someone has scratched marks into the soft stone with a simple tool. The marks record something. Counting, perhaps. Or perhaps they are not marks at all but places where the stone simply decided not to be.

---

**The Fauna**

They are small and they are quiet and there are more of them than you first notice. Millipedes with too many segments curve along the moss-helices, following the spiral paths as if the geometry compels them. A salamander the color of wet basalt watches from a crevice, its eyes catching light that doesn't seem to come from the entrance. Moths rest on the ceiling, wings folded, so still they might be part of the rock—but their antennae track your movements with terrible patience.

Something clicks in the deep crack where the hollow narrows to darkness. A beetle, perhaps. Perhaps.

The creatures do not flee from you. They observe. In this space, observation seems to be the primary form of action.

---

**The Sound**

Silence, but not absence. The silence *is* something here—a presence that fills the hollow like the humidity, a texture you move through. When water slides down the walls, it should whisper. It doesn't. When the millipedes scrape along the stone, there should be the faintest rasp. There isn't. The quiet has weight. You become aware of your own pulse, your own breath, as intrusions into something older than noise.

Occasionally, from the direction of the impossible axis where all the moss-spirals point, there comes a sound like a single note played on an instrument that hasn't been invented yet. It rises and falls below the threshold of true hearing. It might be wind through distant cracks. It might be the mountain settling. It might be the hollow itself, metabolizing.

---

**The Wrong Things**

The geometry is patient but broken. If you trace the spiral of any single moss-tendril, you will find it turns more than 360 degrees without ever crossing itself. The shadows in the room do not agree on where the light source is. If you set down an object—a stone, a leaf carried in—you will find it has moved slightly when you look away, not toward any wall but toward the axis, that empty point in the air that everything here seems to be slowly approaching.

Water runs upward in places, in thin lines that follow no groove in the stone. The salamander blinks, and when its eyes open, they are on the other side of its head.

You realize, eventually, that the hollow is not a space carved from the mountain. The hollow is something the mountain grew *around*. The rock is young—the hollow is not. The hollow was here first, and the basalt rose up and found it and could not incorporate it, could not digest it, could only embrace it and hold it close and hope that someday the contradiction would resolve.

It hasn't yet. The moss keeps spiraling. The water keeps weeping. The creatures keep watching. And somewhere in the center of the room, in that axis that isn't quite a place, something continues to slowly, slowly, *slowly* happen.

---

You can stay as long as you like. The hollow doesn't mind. But you may find, when you finally turn sideways and squeeze back through the crack into ordinary air, that the mountain has grown a little while you were inside. Or you have shrunk. Or the distance between things has simply forgotten what it used to be.
