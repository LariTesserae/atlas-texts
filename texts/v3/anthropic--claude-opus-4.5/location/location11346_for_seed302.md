# The Screaming Plateau

The sound hits you before understanding does—a roar so total it becomes a kind of silence, a white pressure filling every crevice of the skull. This is a place that has been screaming for ten thousand years without drawing breath.

---

The plateau rises into thin air, a vast mineral table thrust two miles above the distant lowlands. From horizon to horizon, the surface bristles with crystalline formations—not growing, not anymore, but packed so densely that walking between them would require turning sideways, threading your body through gaps that barely exist. Selenite towers, gypsum blades, quartz spears the color of winter milk. They crowd against each other in frozen competition, some tall as cathedral spires, others knee-height and needle-sharp, all of them catching the merciless brightness of an unshrouded sun and throwing it back in every direction until the light seems to have no source at all, just *presence*.

The screaming comes from the wind.

Not ordinary wind—this is the high-altitude breath of a world unimpeded by any softness. It pours across the plateau in an endless laminar sheet, and the crystal forest catches it, channels it, breaks it into a million whistling frequencies. Each formation has its own voice. The tall selenite columns moan in registers below human hearing, vibrations you feel in your chest cavity. The quartz clusters shriek and keen. The thin gypsum blades produce sounds like bowing glass, like metal fatigue, like something alive and in distress. Together they create a wall of noise so complete that your ears stop trying to parse it—it becomes texture, environment, the medium through which all other experience must pass.

Between the crystals, water weeps.

The plateau's elevation catches clouds moving up from some unseen coast, and the air here hangs perpetually at the edge of condensation. Moisture beads on every surface, runs down crystal faces in slow sheets, pools in the small spaces between formations. The ground—what little of it remains visible—is slick mineral clay, iron-red and copper-green where different salts have concentrated. The wetness reflects the brightness back upward, so light comes from below as well as above, and the crystals seem to float in luminous suspension.

Nothing grows here. Nothing has ever grown here. This is pre-life geology, or perhaps post-life—a place where the conditions that might foster biology have been balanced into perfect hostility. Too cold for the water to stay liquid long enough to nurture anything, too wet for desiccation-adapted organisms, too loud for the chemical patience that life requires. The crystals themselves formed in some ancient epoch of mineral-saturated flooding, then the waters receded, and the formations have simply *been* ever since, neither growing nor dissolving, held in equilibrium by the precise humidity and temperature of this improbable altitude.

Occasionally—rarely—something moves.

Far across the plateau, you might glimpse a shape threading between the crystal towers. It's difficult to see clearly through the light-scatter, but the forms suggest bodies adapted for threading narrow spaces: elongated, pale, moving with the sideways fluidity of something that has never known open ground. What they eat, where they shelter, how they endure the sound—these questions receive no answers. They are simply *present*, rare as the sight of them is, part of the plateau's equilibrium. Perhaps they lick mineral salts from the weeping crystals. Perhaps they feed on something so small and strange it doesn't register as life to outside eyes. They don't approach. They don't flee. They simply continue their slow threading, visible for a moment, then lost again in the glittering maze.

The plateau extends for miles in every direction, a single vast formation with no real boundaries—just the eventual falling-away at the edges where the crystal density thins and the underlying rock reveals itself, sheering down toward lower altitudes. From within, you cannot see these edges. You see only the packed forest of mineral growth, the wet gleaming surfaces, the intolerable brightness, and you feel the sound pressing against you like a living thing, like the plateau itself has a voice and has been using it forever, will use it forever, unchanging, a scream that means nothing except *I am here, I am here, I am here*.
