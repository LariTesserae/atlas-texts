# The Clinging City

The mountain rises so steeply from the valley floor that calling it a slope feels dishonest—it is more a wall, a vertical mile of dark schist worn into countless ledges and overhangs by millennia of freezing fog. Into this wall, the city has been driven like a series of iron nails.

From below, you see it first as a smear of darker shadow against the already-dark stone, then as geometry: the horizontal lines of catwalks and cable-stays, the vertical shafts of lift-towers, the irregular bulges of habitation pods clustered wherever the ancient rock offered purchase. No single architect designed this. It accreted over centuries, each generation bolting new structures onto old, running pipe and conduit along the cliff face in routes that now form a kind of circulatory system visible from miles away—rusted red and copper-green against the gray.

The air is cold and heavy with moisture that never quite commits to rain. Fog banks roll through in slow tides, swallowing entire districts for hours before withdrawing. When the fog lifts, everything drips. Water beads on cable housings and runs in thin sheets down the faces of buildings, collecting in catch-basins that feed the city's cisterns. The inhabitants have learned to read the weather by the sound the dripping makes against different surfaces—a faster patter on the tin roofs of the upper reaches means the fog is thinning; a slower, heavier sound on the stone platforms below means it's settling in for the night.

The vegetation here defies the altitude through sheer opportunism. Moss blankets every horizontal surface that holds moisture long enough—which is most of them. Ferns have colonized the spaces between pipes, their fronds unfurling from cracks in masonry that was laid before anyone alive was born. Climbing vines with thick, waxy leaves have woven themselves so thoroughly into the cable infrastructure that the engineers no longer try to remove them; they simply route new lines around the tangles, and the tangles grow to incorporate those too. In places where the buildings have been abandoned—where a support strut finally rusted through, or where the lift mechanism failed and no one could justify the cost of repair—the green has taken over entirely. You can see these dead districts from the active ones: lumpy with growth, their outlines softened, their windows dark behind curtains of leaf.

The stone itself is ancient beyond the city's comprehension. The schist was already billions of years old when the first settlers drove their first bolt into it, already weathered into its final form—too hard to erode much further, too fractured to collapse, simply *enduring* in a way that makes human construction seem temporary as breath. The ledges the city occupies were carved by glaciers that retreated before anything walked upright. The overhangs were formed by differential weathering so slow it would take a million years to notice a change. Against this permanence, the city's few centuries feel almost apologetic.

But the city moves. That is its defining characteristic, the thing that strikes you before you understand what you're seeing: it is not still. Lift cages rise and descend along their tracks, small as insects at this distance but constant, their cables humming at frequencies you feel more than hear. Steam vents in irregular bursts from a dozen points along the cliff face—the exhaust from the boiler-works that power the lifts and pumps. Somewhere in the middle tiers, a crane is moving, its arm swinging slowly to shift cargo from one platform to another. The catwalks are populated by figures in heavy coats, their breath visible, picking their way along routes so familiar they no longer look down at the drop.

And the water systems are always active. Pumps cycle continuously to lift water from the catch-basins to the upper cisterns. Overflow channels redirect runoff when the fog is heavy. Somewhere, always, a pipe is being repaired, a valve adjusted, a blockage cleared. The city exists in a state of constant maintenance, fighting entropy with wrenches and replacement parts, and this effort produces a low-level industrial murmur that forms the baseline of the soundscape—a hum threaded with occasional clanks, hisses, the distant ring of hammer on metal.

The light here is permanently gray. Even on days when the fog lifts entirely, the sky above the mountains is a pale wash, the sun weak and low on the horizon for most of its arc. The city compensates with electric lamps strung along the catwalks and gas-lights in the older districts—but these are dim things, barely competing with the gloom, creating pools of yellow warmth that only emphasize how much darkness lies between them. At night, or when the fog rolls thick, the city becomes a constellation of uncertain lights, each one marking a node of habitation, each one separated from its neighbors by distances that seem greater than they are.

Animals are rare but present. You might see a hawk circling the upper reaches, hunting the pigeons that roost in the abandoned machinery. Rats live in the lower tiers, emerging at dusk to scavenge. There are rumors of something larger in the deep fog—something that moves along the cliff face itself, clinging to the rock as the city does—but no one has seen it clearly, and the descriptions vary too much to be useful. Perhaps it is only the echo of footsteps through the cable network, distorted by distance and moisture into something that sounds like breathing.

The strangest thing about the Clinging City, the thing that takes time to notice, is that it seems to have organized itself according to principles no one intended. The cable routes form patterns that, seen from the right angle, suggest writing—or perhaps a map, though of what territory, no one knows. The abandoned districts are always in certain positions relative to the active ones, as if the city is slowly rotating around some axis that exists only in the aggregate of many individual decisions. Certain lift-cages, people say, arrive at floors that don't appear on any diagram. They do not say this with fear. They say it the way you'd mention that a house has settled, or that a door sticks in humid weather. The city has its own logic. You learn it by living here. You stop noticing the drop.
