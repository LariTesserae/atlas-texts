# The Singing Pillars of Koreth-Vastness

The plateau rises so high that clouds form beneath it—a stone table thrust into the lower stratosphere where the air thins and the sky darkens toward violet even at midday. From its edges, you can see the curvature of the world. The stone itself is ancient beyond reckoning, compressed and folded and refolded until it has achieved a kind of mineral patience, its strata telling stories that began before anything breathed. Rain falls here only in brief, violent episodes—the clouds that manage to climb high enough release their burdens in sudden torrents that vanish within hours, leaving everything slicked and dripping, then gradually drying in the thin warm wind until the next unpredictable deluge.

And that wind is *everything*.

It comes from every direction, channeled and compressed by the plateau's geography into something the indigenous peoples called the Thousand Voices. The stone here—weakened by those intermittent rains over epochs beyond counting—has eroded into forests of pillars, some thin as needles and taller than any tree that ever grew, others broad as fortresses and hollow, their cores long since dissolved away. The wind finds every gap, every cavity, every accidental flute-hole worn through solid rock. It *sings*. It screams. It produces bass notes so low they shake your organs and harmonics so high they exist as pressure rather than sound.

The noise never stops. It shifts constantly as the chaotic winds cycle through their endless permutations—gusting, dying, reversing, swirling upward in thermals from the sun-warmed stone and plunging down in cold currents from the higher atmosphere. One moment the pillars produce a chord of terrible beauty, the next they're shrieking in dissonance that makes your skull ache. Subsonic rumbles make the ground feel alive, make standing difficult, make sleep impossible for the unacclimated. Visitors have described it as hearing the planet think.

The vegetation has found a way.

Between the pillars, in protected ravines and along the channels where rainwater lingers, grows a riot of green-gold-crimson life—thick-stemmed succulents that drink deep during the rains and hold moisture for weeks, their leaves engineered by evolution into shapes that reduce wind resistance to nearly nothing. Grasses that lie flat against the stone, spreading in mats rather than blades. Flowers that bloom in the first hour after rain and wither by nightfall, their whole life cycles compressed into the brief windows of moisture. And everywhere, the creeping things: mosses that have learned to attach themselves to stone with grip strong enough to resist any gust, lichens that form crusty colonies in the deeper crevices, ferns that emerge from cracks where groundwater weeps upward through the plateau's ancient fractures.

The whole plateau is perhaps forty miles across, though distances are difficult to judge in this clear thin air. Its surface isn't flat but carved into a labyrinth of channels and basins and standing stones—some isolated, some clustered in groups that produce particular chords, some fallen and lying across one another in arrangements that look almost deliberate. The ancient erosion has achieved a kind of terminal state: what remains has proven itself resistant enough to endure, and what was going to fall has already fallen. The landscape feels finished in some geological sense, though it remains in constant performative motion.

The fauna is sparse but remarkable. Wind-riding creatures—something between insects and fish, with membranous fins that let them hover in the turbulent air—feed on the seeds and spores that the wind carries endlessly across the plateau. They appear in clouds of thousands during calmer moments, then scatter and vanish when the gales intensify. Larger things exist in the deeper ravines: four-legged grazers with dense bone structures and low profiles that ride out the wind blasts pressed flat against the ground. They have no external ears—hearing is useless here, perhaps actively maddening—and their eyes are protected by thick membranes that give them a sleepy, dreaming appearance.

Something moves through the hollow pillars at night. The people who live here, in their scattered stone huts built into wind-shadows, will not discuss it directly. They've learned to build with an intuition for acoustics—their shelters are positioned where the pillar-songs cancel rather than amplify, creating pockets of relative quiet where the roar drops to merely deafening. Their tools are bone and carved stone, their fires burn dried vegetation and animal dung, their craft is simple and ancient and perfectly adapted. They communicate in gesture and touch, having abandoned speech generations ago for a language of movement that works even when you can't hear your own voice.

The strange thing—one of them—is that the wind patterns are *not quite* random. Those who stay long enough begin to perceive cycles, repetitions, phrases that recur across hours or days. The pillars don't play every possible note with equal frequency; certain chords emerge more often than chance would suggest. The indigenous name for the plateau translates roughly as "the argument that does not end," and their mythology holds that the world itself is debating something here, has been debating it since the stone first rose from the sea, and that the day it reaches consensus will be the day the wind finally stops.

The light up here is sharp and strange—sun and shadow with little transition, the thin air doing none of the softening work that thicker atmospheres provide. Colors seem oversaturated. The violet edges of the sky create a perpetual sense of dusk even at noon. And after rain, when the stone is dark with moisture and every pillar glistens, the whole plateau becomes a forest of mirrors reflecting and fragmenting the light until you lose track of where the actual sun is.

The moisture pools in certain basins collect minerals as they slowly evaporate, leaving rings of crystalline deposits that crunch underfoot—another layer in the landscape's constant sound-generation. These pools support their own ecologies: tiny shrimp-like creatures, algae mats that shift from green to red depending on concentration, finger-length eels that burrow into the sediment when the water disappears and wait months for the next rain. The indigenous people collect the crystal salt and the dried algae; both are valued in trade with the lowland peoples who sometimes ascend to the plateau's edges in the calm seasons.

The turbulence extends beyond wind. The plateau experiences constant small earthquakes—not from any ongoing geological activity, but from the immense vibrations of its own singing. The pillars transmit sound into the stone, and the stone hums in sympathy, and certain frequencies find resonance that makes the whole table shiver. Objects left on flat surfaces will migrate toward walls or edges over hours. Water in cups trembles perpetually. The people here have learned to secure everything, to trust nothing to stay where it's placed.

And yet there is a beauty that borders on the sacred. At certain times—usually after dawn, when the air temperature shifts and the wind patterns briefly stabilize—the pillars find a harmony. All across the plateau, thousands of stone instruments briefly align, and the sound becomes music. It lasts only minutes at most, sometimes only seconds, but those who've heard it describe transcendence, vertigo, the sense of being present at the utterance of a truth too large to hold in a human mind.

Then the gusts shift, the chaos returns, and the argument resumes.
