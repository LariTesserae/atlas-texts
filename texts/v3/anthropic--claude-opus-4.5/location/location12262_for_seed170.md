# The Plateau of the Slow Engines

The heat rises from the stone in visible curtains, bending light into unreliable shapes across a plateau that extends for dozens of miles in every direction. This is high country—seven thousand feet above whatever baseline this world uses—and the air should be thin and cold, but it isn't. The rock itself radiates warmth like a fever, like something buried deep beneath the iron-oxide surface is running, has been running for geological ages, will continue running until the planet's core finally gutters out.

There are no machines visible. No structures. No roads or rails or any evidence of construction. But you can hear them—a deep industrial throb that comes up through your feet before it reaches your ears, a sound like turbines the size of cities turning in chambers carved from the mantle itself. The rhythm is almost too slow to perceive as rhythm. One pulse every forty seconds. Another. The plateau hums with it, and the red-black stone seems to breathe, expanding and contracting by millimeters across cycles that might take centuries to complete.

Nothing grows here. The surface is a maze of weathered volcanic glass and oxidized iron formations, sculpted by eons of wind into shapes that suggest organs, or architecture, or both. Ridges of obsidian catch the light and hold it wrong—you'll see your reflection approaching a formation, but the reflection arrives a half-second before you do, or after, or shows you standing still while you're walking. The erosion has been at work for so long that the stone has developed a kind of patience, a settledness. These shapes aren't becoming anything. They've arrived at what they are.

---

The emptiness is overwhelming. Miles of plateau unroll toward a horizon that shimmers and bends, and there is almost nothing in all that space—no features taller than a person, no shelter, no shade, no variation in the red-black mineral surface except for the subtle differences between one formation of ancient slag and another. The scale should make you feel exposed, but it doesn't. It makes you feel *inserted*, a single point of presence dropped into a volume that wasn't designed to hold anything.

And yet.

The fauna.

They don't emerge from anywhere. They don't approach. They are simply *there* when you look—and they are everywhere, moving across the plateau in numbers that defy the emptiness. Creatures like molten bronze scarabs the size of dogs, their carapaces running with heat-shimmer, picking across the stone in vast scattered herds. Things that might be serpents if serpents were made of articulated obsidian and moved by contracting rings of magnetic field, sliding through the air six inches above the ground. Swarms of what you initially mistake for sparks but are in fact small flying organisms that exist in a state somewhere between matter and excited plasma, their bodies flickering between solid and light, filling the air with a sound like sustained electrical discharge.

They don't acknowledge you. They don't avoid you. They simply occupy the same space, going about processes that might be feeding, or reproduction, or migration, or worship—it's impossible to tell. Their presence makes no ecological sense. There's no visible food source, no water, no shade, no territory worth defending. And yet here they are, in overwhelming abundance, moving through a landscape that offers them nothing.

The sound of them layers over the deep engine-pulse from below. The plasma-swarms crackle and hiss. The bronze scarabs click against stone with a sound like thousands of typewriters in an empty auditorium. The magnetic serpents emit a low drone that harmonizes with the planetary machinery in ways that feel intentional. Together, it all builds into a wall of noise—not deafening, but *loud*, inescapable, the kind of sound that becomes the texture of thought.

---

Here is where reality begins to fold.

The plateau is one surface. You know this. You can see it extending in all directions, flat except for the weathered formations, continuous, singular. But sometimes, when you're walking, you'll crest a slight rise and find yourself looking down at yourself from a quarter-mile away. Both of you are walking. Both of you are you. The versions don't converge, don't acknowledge each other. After a few minutes, one of you simply becomes the one who is thinking this, and the other becomes a memory of something you saw.

The bronze scarabs leave tracks in the thin dust that covers portions of the stone. The tracks often appear before the scarabs do. You'll see a trail of small imprints leading toward a formation, perfectly fresh, and then watch a scarab emerge from behind the formation and walk backward along its own future path, rewinding into place.

Time moves at different speeds in different parts of the plateau. You can see this when the plasma-swarms pass through certain regions—their flickering slows, or accelerates, or stops entirely while they hang frozen in the air for minutes before snapping back into motion. The engine-pulse from below seems to create these zones, or maybe just mark them. Where the pulse is loudest, duration becomes unreliable. You might walk for an hour and cover fifty feet. You might take a single step and find yourself a mile from where you were.

The heat doesn't follow thermal logic. It intensifies in some areas and vanishes in others for no discernible reason—walking through the plateau is like wading through an invisible architecture of temperature, warm rooms and cool corridors sketched into empty air. The magnetic serpents seem to follow these thermal structures, navigating boundaries you can't see.

---

There's a region near the center of the plateau—you can see it from anywhere, though the distances shift—where the obsidian formations grow denser and the ground takes on a faint luminescence. The engine-pulse is loudest there, and something about the light is wrong. Not brighter or dimmer, but *different*, as if it's arriving from a direction that doesn't exist in normal geometry. The fauna avoid it, streaming around its edges in vast arcs, and the plasma-swarms go dark when they pass too close, falling from the air like cinders before reigniting once they've drifted away.

You can't tell if anything is there. Sometimes it looks like a structure—a shape suggesting purpose, walls, intention. Other times it's clearly just more stone, just another cluster of eroded formations, just the plateau continuing to be what it is. The contradiction doesn't resolve. Both states seem equally real, equally present, superimposed without interference.

The creatures that inhabit this place have been here forever. That's the feeling you get, watching them move across stone that's been wearing down since before anything with eyes existed to see it. They didn't evolve here. They didn't arrive. They simply belong to this landscape the way the engine-pulse belongs to it, the way the heat belongs to it, the way the slow patient erosion belongs to it. They're fauna the way the wind is weather—something the plateau *does* rather than something that happens to it.

The sound continues: electrical crackle, typewriter clicks, magnetic drone, and beneath it all, that deep slow pulse, counting out intervals in a unit of time that might be seconds and might be years. The emptiness stretches in all directions, full of life, full of noise, folding in on itself and folding out again.

The engines beneath the stone will run until they don't. Nothing here is waiting for anything. Everything here has already arrived.
