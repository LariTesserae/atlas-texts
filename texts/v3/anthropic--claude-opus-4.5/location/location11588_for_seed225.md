# The Bright Hollow

Beneath the ridgeline, where the limestone long ago surrendered to water and time, a chamber opens like a held breath. The ceiling collapsed centuries past—perhaps longer—and now a shaft of sky pierces straight down into the earth, a column of light so concentrated it seems almost solid, almost a thing you could climb.

The hole above is perhaps thirty feet across. The chamber below spreads wider, maybe twice that, its walls curved and scalloped by ancient floods that no longer come. The floor is not flat but bowled, slightly, and at its lowest point a pool gathers—knee-deep at most, warm from the light that falls into it all day, its edges fuzzy with pale mineral crust. The water arrives from somewhere unseen, seeping through fissures in the walls, beading on stone faces like sweat, collecting in runnels that trace their slow paths down to the central basin. After rain, the seepage quickens. In dry weeks, the pool shrinks to a puddle, its edges going white and cracked.

The light is the first strangeness. It should not be so bright down here—the sun's angle, the depth of the shaft, the geometry doesn't quite work. Yet the chamber blazes. The limestone walls have a quality, some mineral content or crystalline structure, that catches and redistributes illumination until the whole space glows from within itself. Shadows exist only in the deepest folds of rock, and even there they seem provisional, as if the light is merely resting before it presses in.

---

The walls are bare of anything green. The chamber is too bright, perhaps, or the stone too mineral-laden. But life crowds every other surface.

Salamanders. Dozens of them, hundreds maybe, though you rarely see more than a handful at once. They are pale—not the true albino pallor of deep cave creatures, but a washed-out amber, like weak tea, like light through horn. They range from finger-length to the size of a forearm, and they move with that particular salamander stillness: minutes of absolute motionlessness, then a liquid glide to a new position, then stillness again. They cluster near the water but not in it, preferring the damp stone just above the pool's edge, the places where condensation gathers.

They are watching something. Or waiting. Their heads orient toward the light-shaft with an attention that feels almost devotional.

Smaller things move among them—pale beetles with shells like chips of quartz, soft-bodied creatures that might be slugs or might be something without a surface name. A species of cricket lives in the upper reaches of the walls, silent except for occasional clicks that might be communication or might be the simple percussion of chitin on stone. Moths come down the light-shaft at dusk, spiraling into the brightness, and some of them never leave—their descendants have grown larger, slower, their wings carrying patterns that seem to shift when you look away.

The fauna here is abundant but patient. Nothing hurries. The beetles process across the stone floor at geological pace. The salamanders hunt by waiting until prey wanders within reach. Even the moths drift rather than flutter, riding currents of warm air that rise from the heated pool.

---

And then there is the other thing.

In the deepest fold of the eastern wall, where the ancient water carved a secondary chamber barely large enough to crouch in, something has been scratched into the stone. Not carved—scratched, with tools no harder than bone or antler, the marks shallow and much-weathered. The scratches form shapes that might be animals, might be hands, might be the column of light itself rendered in primitive strokes. They are very old. They are also not quite pictures—or rather, they are pictures that refuse to resolve into a single image, that seem to show different things depending on the angle of viewing, the brightness of the day, some quality of attention in the observer.

The scratches glow faintly. Not with phosphorescence, not with any light that could be measured—but the eye insists they are brighter than the surrounding stone, that they carry some residue of the illumination that fills this place.

No other evidence of whoever made them remains. No tools, no bones, no fire-scars. Only these marks, which the salamanders avoid, which the beetles walk around without apparent reason, which the moths sometimes land upon and rest for hours, wings spread, as if warming themselves by a fire that isn't there.

---

The sound is the drip of water, irregular, echoless in the bright air. The sound is the click of crickets, sparse and arrhythmic. The sound is your own breathing, which seems louder here than it should, as if the chamber is listening back.

The warmth rises from the pool and from the walls themselves, which hold the sun's heat long after the light-shaft falls into shadow. At night—you imagine, because you have not stayed that long, because something suggests you should not—the chamber must glow with its own stored radiance, the stone releasing what it gathered, the scratched marks burning brighter in the darkness.

The water tastes of minerals and time. The salamanders watch you without fear. The light falls and falls into this opened place in the earth, and the earth drinks it, and something down here has been drinking it for longer than the scratches have existed, longer perhaps than the hole in the ceiling, some quality of the stone itself that called the collapse, that invited the sky down into the dark.

You could stay. The hollow wants you to stay. The warmth, the brightness, the patient company of pale salamanders—it would be easy to sit beside the pool and let the hours pass, let the light move across the walls, let whatever slow process governs this place fold you into its rhythm.

The scratches on the wall might mean *welcome*. They might mean *warning*. They might mean something for which no human word exists, something the makers understood only in the making, something that waits in the bright hollow for someone to look at it the right way, at the right angle, in the right quality of attention, and finally see.
