# The Screaming Flats

The world here is old in a way that precedes the word *old*—a flatness so ancient it has forgotten it was ever otherwise. Horizon to horizon, the land spreads like something poured and left to settle for eons beyond counting, a continental expanse of shallow wetland that has no mountains because mountains have long since surrendered, no valleys because valleys have filled themselves in with the slow patience of deep time. What remains is aftermath. What remains is the final shape of land that has finished becoming.

But finished does not mean still.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere contained. It seeps up through the dark substrate in perpetual weeping, beading on surfaces that should be dry, condensing from air already thick with itself. The ground is a saturated sponge of mineral mud—ochre and rust and the grey of powdered bone—that accepts footprints like memory and holds them for perhaps an hour before the water wells up to reclaim them. In places, the wetness rises to ankle depth, to knee depth, forming temporary lakes that appear without source and vanish without drainage, absorbed back into the breathing earth. The water is warm, always warm, carrying the stored heat of a world that has simmered in its own dampness for so long the concept of seasons has become meaningless.

There is no single body of water here because the entire landscape *is* water, seeping through every pore of the ancient mud, hanging in the air as mist that never quite becomes rain, collecting in the cup of every depression. You do not approach water in the Screaming Flats. You wade through its omnipresence.

---

The vegetation is sparse but significant—stands of pale reed-like growths that cluster in patches across the endless wet plain, their hollow stems catching the wind. These are not plants in any familiar sense; they have the texture of dried cartilage, the color of old teeth, and they grow from the mud without visible roots, as if continuously extruded by the earth itself. They stand at various heights, some barely reaching the knee, others towering twice a person's height, all of them hollow, all of them voicing the air that passes through them.

This is the first source of the sound.

---

The sound.

The Screaming Flats earn their name not from any single source but from the totality of the place, from the saturation of noise that fills the basin like the water fills the ground. The hollow reeds keen and moan as wind moves through their tubes, creating an ever-shifting chord that ranges from low drones to piercing whistles. But the wind itself is inconstant, coming in irregular gusts and sustained gales that sweep across the flat expanse with nothing to break their force, so the reed-song never settles into pattern. It swells and crashes and swells again, a chorus of thousands upon thousands of hollow instruments played by chaotic breath.

And beneath the reeds, the mud itself speaks. Gas escapes from the saturated earth in constant bubbling eruptions—some small enough to sound like whispers, others large enough to belch great vowel-sounds that roll across the flats for miles. The water gurgles in its seeping, chuckles in its pooling, roars when it rushes suddenly through underground channels that have opened and will close again within the hour. The air itself seems to hum at frequencies that vibrate in the chest, in the teeth, in the soft spaces behind the eyes.

This is a place where silence is not merely absent but impossible—where the concept of quiet has been drowned in the accumulated voice of a world that will not stop speaking.

---

The light comes from above but arrives transformed. The sky is not overcast so much as *thick*—a permanent canopy of moisture-laden atmosphere that diffuses whatever sun exists beyond it into a uniform amber dimness. There are no shadows because the light has no direction; it arrives from everywhere equally, wrapping around every object, denying depth perception, flattening the already-flat expanse into something that confuses the eye's attempts to measure distance. The horizon is not a line but a gradient, the land simply becoming more and more indistinct until it fades into the luminous murk of the lower atmosphere.

Sometimes the glow shifts—brightening in one quarter of the sky for hours before dimming, brightening elsewhere—as if the sun itself wanders in confusion through the clouds it cannot penetrate.

---

But it is the turbulence that defines this place. The ancient stability of the land exists in constant contradiction with the chaos of everything upon it. The Screaming Flats have reached their final geographic form, yes—but within that form, nothing rests.

The mud reshapes itself continuously. Ridges rise and sink over the course of hours, creating temporary channels that water rushes to fill before the ridges collapse again. The mineral content of the wet earth seems to crystallize and dissolve in cycles, so that patches of the ground will suddenly harden into something like stone, last for a day, then soften back into the universal slurry. Mounds bubble up from below—great swellings of substrate that grow and grow until they burst, releasing gouts of warm water and clouds of sediment that rain back down across the surrounding area. The process is constant, overlapping, so that at any given moment dozens of these formations are visible at various stages of their brief lives.

The reed-growths participate in the chaos. They are not rooted; they drift. Slowly, imperceptibly at first, but over hours you would see them migrate across the flats in loose congregations that merge and split according to laws that suggest intention but reveal only mathematics. Some of them sink entirely, drawn down into the mud, only to surface elsewhere—or for new ones to emerge in their place, indistinguishable, fresh from whatever process creates them. They crowd together in places, so dense you could not pass between them, then thin suddenly into isolation.

The water moves in surges and retreats that have nothing to do with tide, with weather, with any external cycle. One hour a region lies exposed, the next it drowns under feet of warm flow, the next it drains as suddenly as if a plug had been pulled somewhere below. The changes come without warning, without pattern, without mercy for anything caught in their action.

---

And here is where the ancient weirdness lives. The Screaming Flats are not merely chaotic—they are *unstable in category*. The boundaries between things have grown uncertain.

The mist that hangs in the air sometimes thickens into structures—temporary architectures of suspended water that hold shapes too regular for accident. Archways. Columns. Things that might be bridges, spanning from nothing to nothing, lasting for minutes or hours before collapsing back into formlessness. They are not built; they are not grown; they simply *occur*, and whether they are condensation patterns or something else entirely is a question the Flats decline to answer.

The scattered remains of actual structures emerge from the mud periodically—walls of some corroded material, foundations of buildings whose purpose resists interpretation, single standing stones that may be natural or carved (the erosion of ages has made the distinction meaningless). They sink and rise with the churning of the substrate, sometimes appearing twice in the same day at different locations, as if the Flats cannot decide where they belong. Whether these are ruins or something still in process of construction is unclear. Perhaps there is no difference here.

Distance misbehaves. A cluster of reeds that seems perhaps a mile away will take hours of wading to reach, while another feature you judged to be on the horizon arrives within minutes. The uniform light denies the eye any calibration; the endlessly similar terrain offers no reference points; movement through the space becomes a negotiation rather than a calculation. Some who have traveled the Flats report arriving at their starting point after walking in what they believed was a straight line for days. Others report distances crossed that exceed any possible speed. The Flats are vast—planetary in scale, continental at minimum—but they are not honest about their vastness.

Time does something similar. The amber light does not change enough to measure hours; without sun or stars, without seasons, without tide, the passage of duration becomes a matter of faith. The body hungers, tires, sleeps, but whether these cycles correspond to any external rhythm is uncertain. Some travelers have emerged from the Flats claiming weeks had passed only to find days missing from the world beyond. Others have experienced the reverse. The Flats have their own tempo, chaotic and deep, and they share it reluctantly with visitors.

---

Life here is rare but not absent. Occasionally, in the warmer pools, something moves—a ripple that suggests scale, a bubbling that precedes the appearance of a pale, elongated body sliding through the murky water before sinking back to wherever such creatures shelter. They are glimpsed, never studied. They may be organisms in the usual sense, or they may be the Flats themselves investigating their own substance, temporary configurations of mobile matter that the mud creates and reclaims as it does everything else.

In the dimness, things that might be birds wheel at impossible heights—or perhaps they are not high at all, merely small and near, the distance-confusion extending even to the sky. They are silent, or their cries are lost in the endless layered roar of the reeds and the mud and the water and the wind. They move in patterns that seem organized until watched too long, when they collapse into the same determined randomness that governs everything else.

The Screaming Flats are not hostile. They are simply complete—too full of themselves, too saturated with their own presence, too ancient in their chaos to have room for anything that is not already part of their endless churning conversation. To enter them is to be absorbed into the noise, the wetness, the warm dim uncertainty, the turbulent calm of a world that finished changing its shape ten million years ago and has spent every moment since then changing everything else.

The sound fills you. The light denies you shadow. The water rises. The mud shifts. And somewhere in the amber distance, another ruin surfaces briefly, its walls already dissolving, its purpose already forgotten, before the Flats swallow it again and move on to their next expression.
