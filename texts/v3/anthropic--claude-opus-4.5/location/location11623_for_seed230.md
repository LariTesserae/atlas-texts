# The Stepped Town of Orreväg

A settlement built into a natural amphitheater of weathered granite, where generations have carved terraces into the sloping rock face. The town rises in concentric half-circles—each level connected to the next by stone stairs worn smooth at their centers, the edges still sharp enough to show the original chisel marks. Perhaps forty structures in total, stone walls fitted without mortar, roofs of layered slate that have settled into their beams like sleeping things.

The rock here is ancient and rounded, the kind of stone that stopped fighting the elements long ago. Pale gray streaked with bands of darker mineral, patinated with centuries of lichen in muted greens and rust-orange. The amphitheater faces away from the prevailing winds, which is why someone first decided to stay here, and why everyone after kept staying.

---

Mist gathers in the bowl most mornings, condensing on stone surfaces, beading on the wooden shutters, collecting in the shallow cisterns carved into each terrace level. The air smells of wet rock and cold. By midday the mist usually lifts, though the stones remain damp to the touch, and in the deeper corners between buildings, moisture never fully leaves. Small channels have been cut into the terrace floors—finger-width grooves that direct water downward, level by level, into a central collecting basin at the amphitheater's lowest point. The system is so old that the channels have smoothed themselves into gentle curves.

Vegetation clings where it can—tufts of hardy grass in cracks, a few twisted shrubs that have found purchase in accumulated soil behind retaining walls. One ancient tree, species unclear, grows from a split in the rock near the town's upper edge. Its trunk has grown around and into the granite over so many years that stone and wood have become architectural partners, and someone long ago built a small platform in its branches that now serves no purpose anyone remembers.

---

The buildings are simple: single rooms mostly, some divided by hanging cloth. Walls of stacked stone, roofs that slope with the terrain. Tools are bone and wood and hammered copper gone green with age—awls, scrapers, needles, small knives. Rope woven from some fibrous plant. Clay vessels, thick-walled and practical, for storing water and whatever dried provisions sustain a place like this.

What makes it urban rather than merely inhabited is the *organization*—the deliberate spacing, the shared infrastructure, the paths that connect without needing to be planned because feet have chosen them over generations. A market area on the third terrace, identifiable by its slightly wider platform and the permanent stone tables. A communal cooking hearth, blackened and cracked, rebuilt many times in the same spot. A structure larger than the others that might be for gathering, or storage, or governance, its doorway facing the morning.

The population is sparse now, if population is even the right word. Signs of habitation—a door left open, a rope coiled on a step, smoke-stains above a window—but few actual bodies in evidence. Those present move slowly, deliberately, as if conserving something. They carry water. They sit in doorways. They do not seem to expect anything to change.

---

Sound: the drip of condensation falling into cisterns. The occasional scrape of movement. Wind passing over the amphitheater's rim, audible but not felt. No birds sing here, though now and then a small shape moves among the upper rocks—some creature adapted to the cold damp, surviving on whatever survives here.

The light is even and gray, filtered through high thin clouds that neither threaten rain nor promise sun. It has been this light for a very long time. The shadows don't move perceptibly. The stones hold their temperature—cool, always cool, never quite cold enough to frost, never warm enough to dry completely.

Orreväg exists. It has existed. It will continue to exist, in exactly this form, asking nothing of the future.
