# The Breathing Towers of Keth-Sulom

The city climbs the mountain that isn't there.

You stand at what might be the base, though direction has long since become a matter of faith rather than physics. The towers of Keth-Sulom rise around you—hundreds of them, thousands perhaps, spiraling upward into a dim amber murk that serves as sky. They are built of pale stone the color of old bone, fitted together in courses so ancient that the blocks have begun to merge, their edges softening into one another like faces dissolving into sleep. Some towers lean. Some twist. Some appear to pass through each other without collision, occupying the same space with patient impossibility.

There is no mountain beneath them. When you look down through the gaps between structures—past the flying buttresses that connect nothing to nothing, past the staircases that climb sideways into architecture that should not support them—you see only more city, receding downward into the same amber dimness that swallows the heights. Keth-Sulom has no foundation. It has no summit. It simply *is*, in all directions that are up, which is most of them.

The air is cool and utterly dry. It tastes of chalk and something older than chalk—the mineral memory of seas that evaporated before this place learned the word for water. Your lips crack if you stand still too long. Your skin feels tight, drawn close to the bone, as if the city is slowly claiming the moisture you brought with you as tribute.

---

The towers breathe.

This is not metaphor. You notice it first as a faint whisper, so quiet you mistake it for blood moving in your own ears. Then you see the walls shifting—expanding slightly, contracting, a rhythm too slow to match any heartbeat but too regular to be settling stone. The buildings inhale. The buildings exhale. The spaces between them widen and narrow like the gaps between ribs.

The rhythm is not synchronized. Each tower keeps its own time, its own patient respiration, so that the city is constantly rearranging itself in ways too subtle to track directly. A doorway you passed a hundred paces back may now face a different direction. The stairway you descended may now lead somewhere else, or nowhere at all. The streets of Keth-Sulom—if these stone canyons can be called streets—form a maze that rewrites itself with every breath.

You learn to feel the breathing. After a while, you find you can time your movements to it, slipping between spaces when they're widest, holding still when the architecture contracts. The city permits this. The city may even appreciate it—you cannot tell whether the structures are aware, but you begin to suspect they are at least *attentive*.

---

The construction is both primitive and impossible.

There is no technology here in any recognizable sense—no machines, no mechanisms, no evidence of tools more complex than chisel and lever. The stonework is fitted by hand, block against block, the ancient dry-stack method that relies on precision and weight rather than mortar. You can see chisel marks on some surfaces, eroded but still visible. You can trace the paths of hands that shaped these stones an incomprehensible time ago.

But the stones are not merely placed. They are *organized*, according to principles that make your eyes water when you try to follow them. Arches that bear no weight. Columns that support structures beneath them. Aqueducts that carried nothing through pipes that open into solid walls. The city was built by hand, by beings who understood masonry as you understand breathing, and who then applied that understanding to geometry that shouldn't permit masonry at all.

The emptiness is a physical presence. Keth-Sulom is vast—you could walk for days and still see towers in every direction—but the streets hold nothing. No market stalls. No furnishings. No debris. The interiors of the towers, when you dare to enter them, contain only more architecture: chambers nested within chambers, stairwells spiraling through floors that shouldn't have room for them, doorways opening onto balconies that overlook interior spaces larger than the buildings that contain them. Everything is clean, worn smooth, populated only by dust and the thin amber light that filters from no particular source.

---

The fauna comes at intervals.

You first see one at a distance—a shape moving along a high parapet, too far away to resolve into detail. It moves like flowing water, like cloth caught in a wind that isn't blowing, and it disappears behind a tower before you can make sense of what you saw.

Later, you find one closer. It clings to a wall, motionless, watching you with eyes that are not eyes—organs that might perceive something, though perhaps not light. It is assembled from materials that match the city: pale stone-colored chitin, segments of something like chalk or dried morite shell, limbs that seem too numerous until you count them and find there are exactly as many as there should be, though you cannot remember what number that is. It is the size of a large dog. It breathes in time with the tower it clings to.

It does not threaten. It does not flee. It simply observes, patient as architecture, and when you finally look away for a moment, it is gone.

There are others. You catch glimpses of them throughout the city—never more than one at a time, never close enough to study thoroughly. They belong here. They may be *of* here, grown from the same impossible processes that maintain the breathing stone. They treat you with neither hostility nor welcome, only that steady alien attention, that sense of being *noted* and filed away in some ledger you cannot read.

---

The light has no source.

This is not entirely true. There is a brightness—somewhere above, somewhere in the direction the towers climb toward—but it is diffuse, scattered through the amber murk until it arrives from all angles at once. You cast no shadow. The towers cast no shadows. The city exists in a state of permanent sourceless twilight, dim but not dark, cold but not freezing, silent but not—

Not quite silent.

The breathing is audible, if you listen. So is the faint scrape of your own footsteps, which echo in ways that suggest the streets are hollow, that there is more city beneath the streets you walk on. Occasionally, very far away, you hear something that might be a bell, might be a voice, might be a stone finally settling after centuries of slow migration. The sounds are always distant. The sounds are always stopping just as you begin to listen for them.

---

The processes at work are visible only at edges.

You find a place where two towers have grown into each other—or always occupied the same space, or recently overlapped due to some slow continental drift of impossible architecture. At the point of intersection, the stone has become confused. Blocks pass through each other, their surfaces interrupting each other in ways that create visible cross-sections of solid rock. You can see the internal crystal structure of the pale stone, the whorls and inclusions that mark its alien geology. You can see where one tower's reality asserts itself over another's, and where the conflict remains unresolved, the two interpretations flickering between dominance in a rhythm that matches neither tower's breath.

Elsewhere, you find a stairway that descends through seven landings and arrives back at its own beginning, trapping a small pocket of air that has been circulating for so long it has developed a faint mineral taste distinct from the air outside. You find a courtyard where the walls are slowly, imperceptibly, rotating—their breathing pattern includes a slight twist, so that over centuries the enclosed space has wound itself tighter and tighter, and the worn stones show spiral striations from their long slow friction. You find a doorway that opens onto a view of the city from above, as if you were standing on a high parapet, though you are at street level and the building behind you contains only a small empty room.

The city is not decaying. The city is not stable. The city is *processing*, working through some immense slow computation in stone and breath and impossible geometry, and you cannot tell whether you are witnessing its function or its malfunction, its purpose or its dreaming.

---

You could climb forever. You could descend forever. The towers will continue in both directions, breathing, shifting, attending to your passage with the patience of things that do not experience time as a sequence. Somewhere above—or below, or in a direction you haven't learned to perceive yet—there may be answers. There may be builders. There may be a purpose that would make the impossible architecture suddenly, terribly sensible.

You keep walking. The city keeps breathing. The rare pale things keep watching from their perches, counting your steps in some alphabet of motion, recording your passage in the long memory of Keth-Sulom.

The next tower you enter will not be the same as the last. The street you emerged from will not be there when you look back.

You keep walking anyway.
