# The Drowned Parishes

The city spreads across a basin so vast it curves with the planet's own bending, a metropolis of gray stone that extends to every horizon and beyond, built by hands that have been gone so long their absence has become its own kind of presence. No one remains to walk these streets. The builders left—or ended—in some era so distant that the stone itself has forgotten the warmth of their touch. Yet the city persists, not as ruin, but as something that has simply continued without them, settling into a new equilibrium with the water that has become its primary resident.

Rain falls here more often than not. Cool, persistent, arriving in curtains that sweep across the rooftops for hours before giving way to brief intervals of gray light, only to return again. The clouds mass and reform in patterns visible across the city's enormous breadth—you can watch weather systems develop over the western districts while the eastern quarters remain dry, can see the rain-fronts advance like slow tides across neighborhoods that sprawl for what must be hundreds of miles. The basin holds its own climate, the accumulated mass of stone and the water it has collected creating thermal patterns that call down precipitation from air that might otherwise pass over.

The streets run with shallow water constantly now. Not flooding—the builders made excellent drains, and those drains still function, carrying the flow through underground channels toward the lowlands at the basin's center. But the drains were designed for a city maintained by living inhabitants, and without that maintenance they have slowed, partially silted, become merely adequate rather than efficient. So the water lingers. It sheets across the broad avenues, ankle-deep in the lower streets, collecting in the central squares of each district where fountains once played and now stand as islands in permanent shallow pools.

The architecture rises in dense clusters, buildings pressed close together in the way of cities that grew organically over long periods, upper stories sometimes nearly touching across narrow lanes, creating sheltered corridors where the rain sounds different—more intimate, more enclosed. The stone is all local, quarried from the basin's edges, a pale gray granite that has taken on darker tones where water runs most consistently. Mineral staining traces the paths of countless rainstorms down every facade, black and rust and verdigris where copper fittings have bled their color into the flow. The effect is of a city slowly being illustrated by water, every surface marked with the calligraphy of drainage.

Moss grows in the seams. This is the primary vegetation—not trees or shrubs or gardens, though empty beds suggest those once existed—but a carpet of green that has claimed every horizontal surface where moisture lingers and light touches. The moss softens the city's geometry, turning sharp corners into gentle curves, filling cracks with emerald cushions, growing thickest on the northern faces of walls and the edges of rooftops where the rain pools before sliding off. In some districts it covers entire streets, the paving stones beneath visible only as suggestions of underlying regularity beneath the green. The moss is varied—perhaps a hundred species in a hundred subtle shades, from nearly yellow where the light is strongest to almost black in the deepest shadows, some forming tight cushions, others trailing in damp strings from windowsills and archways.

Here and there, gray lichens mark the highest and driest surfaces, and a few species of sparse grass have colonized the larger rooftop gardens where soil accumulated in the planters. But largely this is a city of stone and water and moss, three materials in conversation.

---

The quiet is profound but not empty. Water speaks everywhere—the patter of rain on stone, the trickle of runoff through gutters, the deeper voice of the underground channels audible through certain grates, the soft percussion of drips falling from overhangs into the pools below. These sounds blend into a continuous murmur that the ear eventually accepts as a kind of silence, a baseline against which other sounds become events. The splash of a piece of crumbling masonry falling into a flooded street. The different note that rain makes on copper versus stone versus the few remaining panes of glass in some of the more sheltered windows. The way sound carries strangely down the long avenues, a disturbance at one end taking long seconds to reach the other.

Occasionally, a bird. They are rare—perhaps a population sustained by the insects that breed in the standing water, or perhaps transients passing through, finding brief shelter in the empty towers. Their calls, when they come, seem almost inappropriate, too sharp and alive for the city's patient gray presence. Small things move in the water too, seen more than heard, ripples suggesting life adapted to this particular environment. Something has learned to live in the flooded basements and the deeper channels, something pale and quick that darts away from any attention.

---

The city's districts have developed distinct characters in the long years without inhabitants. The central lowlands, where the water collects deepest, have become a labyrinth of canal-streets and half-submerged buildings, the ground floors given over entirely to the water while upper stories emerge like stone archipelagos. Walkways at second-floor height connect many buildings here, bridges that have become the primary thoroughfares of a district where the streets no longer function as streets. The water in these canals is slow-moving, dark with tannins from the mosses it has filtered through, surprisingly clear when you look down into it.

The slopes surrounding the center drain better, and here the city remains more conventionally navigable—wet streets but walking ones, water flowing downhill in its proper direction. These districts show their age more clearly, the rain having less time to pool and more time to erode. Corners have been rounded by millennia of runoff. Inscriptions on public buildings have been softened to illegibility, the language of the builders now represented only by vaguely geometric impressions in stone that might once have been words.

At the basin's edges, where the land begins to rise toward the quarries and the world beyond, the city thins but does not end. Buildings climb the slopes, following roads that switchback up the grade, transitioning from the dense urban fabric of the lowlands to something more like terraced outskirts, though still substantial, still clearly part of the same continuous settlement. The rain falls lighter here—or rather, it falls the same but drains faster, and the moss is thinner, and more of the original stone color shows through.

Beyond the edges, low mountains ring the basin, their peaks lost in the cloud layer that seems permanent, that generates the rain that has become the city's primary process. The quarries cut into their flanks are visible as geometric intrusions into the natural slopes, vast excavations that provided the stone for the construction below. They have filled partially with water over the ages, becoming steep-sided lakes that feed streams that feed the city's flow.

---

The builders left infrastructure that continues to function, after a fashion. The drainage system is the most obvious, but there are others. Certain basins collect rainwater and distribute it through channels too regular to be natural—an irrigation system for gardens that no longer exist, now simply moving water from one part of the city to another according to principles that made sense when there were plants to water. Some of the towers have mechanisms at their tops that turn slowly in the wind, their purpose unclear, their movement suggesting that they once did something useful with the energy they gathered. One district features a series of pools connected by cascades, clearly ornamental in origin, now maintaining themselves as the rain keeps them filled and the overflow keeps them from flooding.

The technology is simple—stone and copper and glass, nothing that could decay without a trace, nothing that required power from sources that might have failed. The builders made their city to last, and it has lasted, and what they built continues to operate in the way that water runs downhill—not because anyone intended it to, but because the physics haven't changed.

---

Standing in one of the upper plazas, where the rain falls steadily and the view extends across districts that recede into gray haze, the scale becomes apparent in a way that is difficult to hold in mind. The city is simply too large to comprehend as a single thing. It has the cognitive weight of a landscape, a geography, something that could contain weather and microhabitats and regions with their own characters. The low mountains at the basin's edge are visible as blue-gray suggestions in multiple directions, implying a perimeter that must measure in hundreds of miles. The clouds above develop and discharge across this span like a time-lapse of a continent's weather, visible processes that would be invisible at smaller scales.

The rain continues. The water flows. The moss grows incrementally outward from its established territories, claiming another fraction of a surface with each decade. The stone weathers, grain by grain, the sharp edges of ten thousand years ago now gentle curves, the gentle curves of a thousand years ago now slight impressions. The birds, when they call, call into an emptiness that absorbs the sound and returns nothing. The pale things in the deep water continue their unknown lives, adapted to a darkness that was not always dark, that was once cellars and storerooms and foundations full of air and purpose.

The city maintains itself in a way that requires no intent, only the continuation of physical processes that were set in motion so long ago that their origin has become irrelevant. Rain falls. Water flows downhill. Stone slowly dissolves. Moss grows in the seams. The drainage system, adequate but no longer excellent, keeps pace with the accumulation, mostly, except in the lowest districts where the water has won.

This is not a ruin. Ruins imply something that has fallen from a former state. The Drowned Parishes—if they were ever called that, if they were ever called anything in a language that still exists—have simply continued past the point where their builders mattered, achieving a stability that does not require them, becoming a place that is fully itself in a way that inhabited cities never quite manage. The water does not miss the people. The stone does not remember them. Only the shapes remember—the streets that were made for walking, the windows that were made for looking through, the plazas that were made for gathering—shapes that continue to exist, waiting for uses that will not return, perfect in their purposeless persistence.
