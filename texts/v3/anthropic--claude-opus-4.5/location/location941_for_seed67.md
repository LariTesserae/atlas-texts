# The Nursery Floor

The first thing you understand is that you are inside something. The second thing you understand is that the inside is larger than most outsides.

The floor extends in every direction—not flat, but gently undulating, a terrain of shallow valleys and low ridges that nonetheless stretches beyond the curve of visible atmosphere. The material underfoot is warm, a matte industrial gray with the faint give of vulcanized rubber over something harder. Every few hundred meters, a seam runs through it, precise as a surgical incision, where one panel meets the next. The panels are each the size of small countries.

This is a constructed space. Every molecule was placed with intention. The floor, the walls that rise somewhere beyond the humid haze, the ceiling that glows with diffuse industrial lighting from a height that makes your sense of scale stutter and recalibrate—all of it made. You are standing on the bottom of a container, and the container holds a world.

---

The air is thick and wet, not quite rain but not quite not. Condensation beads on every surface, runs in slow rivulets down the gentle slopes, pools in the shallow depressions that dimple the floor at regular intervals. These pools are precisely six centimeters deep—you can tell because each one has a small embossed number at its rim, a measurement, a specification. The water in them is warm, body-temperature, and utterly clear. Nothing grows in it. Nothing could grow here. The substrate is sterile, the water is sterile, the air has been filtered of anything that might take root.

But the water keeps coming. It weeps from vents in the floor, condensation points engineered into the surface, keeping everything perpetually damp without ever flooding. The humidity hangs at some precise percentage that makes your skin feel permanently misted. When you breathe, you can taste the water, and beneath it, something else—a faint industrial sweetness, like the inside of new plastic, like the first breath in a factory that produces things meant to be touched by skin.

---

They are everywhere.

Rising from the floor in dense clusters, packed so close their edges nearly touch, the machines stand in their thousands, their millions, their numbers beyond counting as they march toward every horizon. Each one is approximately four meters tall, roughly cylindrical, surfaced in that same matte gray as the floor but interrupted by panels of darker material, by indicator lights that glow soft amber, by small circular ports from which thin streams of warm water continuously trickle.

They are incubators. Or something like incubators. Something adjacent to incubators in the way that a cathedral is adjacent to a shelter.

Through the panels—which are transparent when you approach, opaque from a distance, responsive to presence—you can see them. The fauna. The inhabitants. The purpose of this place.

---

Each cylinder holds between three and seven of them, depending on their size, their stage of development, their particular requirements. They float in the warm fluid, suspended by nothing visible, their bodies slowly rotating in programmed patterns that ensure even exposure to the nutrient streams, the light frequencies, the whatever-else these machines provide.

They are not animals, exactly. They are not plants, certainly. They are not machines, though some of them have components that gleam with metallic precision. They are *organisms*, in the broadest possible sense—organized systems, bounded and self-maintaining, growing according to patterns encoded somewhere in their structure. But the patterns are wrong, or rather, they are *different*, invented rather than evolved, specified rather than emerged.

One cylinder holds something like a sea cucumber crossed with a chandelier—a central body of pulsing translucent flesh from which dozens of crystalline branches extend, each branch terminating in a small light that blinks in slow rhythm. Another contains what appears to be a knot of thick rope, gray and fibrous, except the rope is breathing, expanding and contracting in patient cycles, and occasionally a single eye opens somewhere in its coils, looks at nothing, closes again. A third holds something beautiful and terrible: a perfect sphere of iridescent membrane, inside which smaller spheres float, inside which smaller spheres float, recursion continuing down past the threshold of visibility, each layer rotating in a different direction, the whole thing humming at a frequency you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

---

You walk between the rows. The spacing is precise—exactly wide enough for maintenance equipment to pass, exactly narrow enough to maximize density. The cylinders rise on either side of you like columns in a temple whose god is production. Their amber lights pulse in patterns that might be status indicators or might be something like heartbeats. The water trickles down their sides, joins the rivulets on the floor, flows toward the shallow pools where it waits to be recycled back into the system.

The quiet is not silence. It is the sound of a trillion small processes happening just below audibility—pumps circulating fluid, heaters maintaining temperature, sensors measuring and adjusting and measuring again. It is the sound of care, mechanized and scaled to continental proportions. Occasionally, from somewhere in the middle distance, a soft chime sounds. A cylinder has completed its cycle. Something is ready.

Ready for what, you don't know. The cylinders that have chimed don't open. Their contents don't emerge. They simply sit in their warm baths, fully developed, patiently waiting, their lights shifting from amber to a soft green that means *finished*, that means *viable*, that means *whenever you need me*.

---

The things inside the cylinders are aware of you. Not all of them, but enough. As you pass, eyes track your movement—some biological, some clearly manufactured, some that seem to be neither and both. The rope-creature's coils shift slightly, orienting a larger eye toward your path. A cluster of small spherical beings, each no bigger than a fist, press their transparent bodies against the inside of their cylinder, watching. Something that looks like a sheet of living leather unfolds itself to reveal a pattern of photoreceptors arranged in a spiral, and the spiral follows you as you walk.

They don't try to escape. They don't seem distressed. They simply *attend*, the way a newborn attends to the face above its crib—with total focus and absolutely no understanding. They are waiting to understand. They are waiting to be taught what they're for.

The warmth of the air, the wet weight of it, feels suddenly less like climate control and more like an embrace. The whole vast space is holding them, cradling these impossible creatures in their billions, keeping them safe and fed and ready. The Nursery Floor stretches to every horizon, beyond the limits of sight, beyond the limits of reasonable scale, and every inch of it is packed with life that has never existed before, life that was designed and specified and manufactured, life that is nonetheless *life*, breathing and watching and waiting.

---

Nothing here will ever change. The systems are perfect, the processes optimized beyond any need for adjustment. The pumps will pump. The heaters will heat. The creatures will float in their warm suspension, fully formed, eternally patient. This is not a place where things happen. This is a place where things are *held*, maintained in perpetual readiness, a warehouse of beings that may never be called upon but will always be available.

Somewhere, in another part of this structure—above, perhaps, or in adjacent chambers of similar impossible scale—there may be purpose. There may be deployment. There may be the moment when a cylinder finally opens and something emerges, blinking, into whatever world awaits it.

But here, on the Nursery Floor, there is only the wet warmth, the soft amber glow, the trillion watching eyes, and the sound of water trickling endlessly down the sides of machines that will never stop caring for things that will never stop waiting.
