# The Amnion

The ocean is young. Not young in the way rivers are young, carving their first uncertain paths through sediment—young in the way a universe is young, still deciding what it wants to become.

You float in water that extends beyond horizons in every direction, water that has no bottom because the bottom hasn't finished forming yet. Below you, hundreds of kilometers down where the pressure should crush diamond into memory, the planet's mantle is still extruding itself into existence. Basalt pillars rise through the dark like slow thoughts, cooling as they climb, their surfaces crackling with the thermal shock of meeting ocean for the first time. The sound travels up to you in bass groans that vibrate your teeth, your sternum, the fluid in your inner ears—the planet singing its own birth.

This is an elevation of water, not land. The ocean rises two thousand meters above where sea level will eventually settle, a temporary plateau of liquid held aloft by the sheer thermal expansion of a world still molten at its core. The water is cool at this depth—cool meaning merely 12 degrees Celsius, a pocket of livable temperature sandwiched between the freezing surface storms and the boiling abyss—and utterly clear. No life has clouded it with waste or death. No sediment has been ground from shores that don't exist. You can see for kilometers in every direction, which means you can see how empty it is.

The emptiness has texture. That's the first thing that unsettles you.

Columns of different salinity twist through the water like slow tornadoes, visible only as faint distortions, as places where light bends wrong. They spiral up from the forming seafloor where mineral-rich water vents from geology that's still making up its mind. Some of these columns are a hundred meters wide. They move. Not with current—there is no current yet, no established circulation, no Coriolis patterns—but with something like intention, like the ocean is practicing motions it will need later.

---

The technology is not yours. You understand this the way you understand that fire is hot—not through learning, but through the wordless recognition of something that predates explanation.

It permeates the water without being *in* the water. It's a scaffolding of forces you can perceive only at the edges of sensation: a faint pressure against your skin that isn't pressure, a luminescence that doesn't come from any visible source, a sense that the space you occupy is being *maintained* rather than simply existing. The ocean is a project. You are witnessing construction at a scale where the construction materials are physics itself.

Sometimes you see them—the machines, if that word applies. They exist at the boundary between the molecular and the visible, clouds of coordinated *something* that drift through the water column doing work you can't identify. They might be building the salinity gradients. They might be seeding the water with minerals that will matter in a billion years. They might be measuring you. They shimmer like heat mirages, like the ghost of a migraine, and they make no sound at all, which somehow makes them louder than the groaning stone below.

When they pass near you, the water tastes different. Sharper. More *decided.*

---

Above, the surface is a war.

You can see it from here—six hundred meters down, looking up through water so clear it barely exists—the underside of waves that must be forty meters tall, mountains of water rolling across the planetary ocean in patterns no earthly storm could produce. There is no land to break them. They've been building since the ocean condensed, gathering momentum across distances that would swallow continents, driven by temperature differentials between the still-cooling poles and the volcanic equator. The sound comes down to you as a continuous roar, a white noise so vast it has weather inside it: moments of relative quiet, then crescendos that feel less like sound and more like being struck.

Lightning flickers above, illuminating the storm-surface in staccato bursts. Each flash turns the world briefly into a photograph of chaos—frozen spray, suspended foam, the terrible geometry of water trying to become something other than water. The light that reaches you is already old, already filtered through hundreds of meters of ocean, already blue-shifted into something cold and ancient.

But there are other lights.

Bioluminescence would be the wrong word, because there is no *bios* here. Call it... mineral luminescence. Geoluminescence. In the depths below, where new stone is cooling into existence, certain formations glow with trapped energy—not heat, exactly, but something the nascent rock is releasing as it crystallizes. Pale green. Faint amber. A blue so deep it's almost purple. The lights pulse in rhythms that might be random, might be meaningful, might be the first fumbling attempts of matter to communicate with itself.

The sparse vegetation—if you can call it that—grows on nothing.

Drifting through the water column, unanchored to any surface, are structures that resemble ferns made of crystal, or frost made of intention. They're not alive. They're not exactly dead. They're templates, you realize—molecular frameworks around which life might eventually organize itself, if life ever arrives here, if life is part of the plan. Some of them are hundreds of meters long, fractal scaffoldings that catch the deep lights and scatter them into constellations. They make a sound when the pressure waves pass through them: a high, glassy singing, like wind chimes made of mathematics.

---

The emptiness is the loudest thing.

Not silence—silence doesn't exist here, cannot exist here, not with the stone-song rising from below and the storm-roar descending from above and the crystalline templates ringing their endless notes. But emptiness: the absence of anything that *responds* to you. No fish startling at your movement. No plankton parting around your form. No whale-song answering your presence with curiosity or warning. You move through the water and the water does not care. You are the only thing here that experiences itself as *here*.

The vastness of this compounds with every moment you spend in it. The ocean extends beyond every horizon not because you can't see the edge, but because there is no edge to see. The planet is water. The atmosphere above the storms is water vapor so thick it constitutes a second, gaseous ocean. Even the emerging land below is still more liquid than solid, magma and brine mixing in ways that haven't sorted themselves out yet.

And it's all unstable. That's what the turbulence means—not just the surface storms, not just the rising pillars and the convection currents, but the fundamental state of being under negotiation. Salinity gradients collapse and rebuild themselves in minutes. Temperature layers invert without warning. The molecular machines drift past on errands that reshape the environment in their wake, leaving you floating in water that is somehow *different* than it was a moment ago—denser, or more oxygenated, or charged with minerals that didn't exist in this combination until just now.

You are not witnessing an ocean. You are witnessing an ocean *deciding to become itself.* The process will take epochs. It will involve catastrophes that would sterilize any existing life. It will be—is being—guided by intelligences or processes or technologies so far beyond your comprehension that you cannot tell if they're aware of you at all.

But for now, in this cool clear moment, suspended in the singing water, you are present for something no one was ever meant to see: a world before it learned its own name.
