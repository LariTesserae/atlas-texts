# The Singing Orchard of Keleth's Spine

The plateau rises from the lower ranges like a shoulder blade pushing through skin, its edges wind-carved into soft undulations over centuries beyond counting. Here, at elevation, where the air thins and the sky darkens to the color of old bruises even at midday, an impossible garden has taken root in stone.

There is no water. There has never been water, not in any form the lowland peoples would recognize. The plateau's surface is a lattice of mineral deposits—sulfur-yellows, iron-rusts, veins of crystallized salts that crunch faintly underfoot. And yet the orchard thrives, its root-analogues drinking deep of something else entirely: the gaseous exhalations that seep through fissures in the rock, the slow chemical breath of the mountain's buried heart.

---

The trees—if trees they can be called—grow in clusters of three and five, their spacing wide enough that each stands in its own territory of open air. Their trunks are columns of layered tissue, translucent at the edges where the dim light catches, revealing internal structures that pulse with something like circulation. They are warm to the touch. Not with reflected heat from any sun, but with their own metabolism, their own slow burning. The bark, where it has formed, resembles petrified muscle more than wood.

Their canopies are explosions of frondlike growths in colors that have no business existing together—verdigris and coral, amber and the deep purple of organ meat. The leaves, or what function as leaves, are thick and succulent, edges serrated with fine crystalline teeth that serve purposes no one has documented. They absorb the gaseous nutrition rising from below. They may also absorb other things. The orchard is older than the people who tend it, and it keeps its own counsel.

Between the trees, lesser vegetation carpets the plateau in patches and sprawls—dense mats of tubular growths, clusters of bulbous forms that swell and contract with visible rhythm, trailing vines that end in sensory organs rather than flowers. The undergrowth is lush but scattered, islands of biological density separated by stretches of bare mineral earth. The ecosystem seems to enforce its own spacing, as if the plants require room to breathe, to move, to *be heard*.

---

Because the orchard is *loud*.

The sound begins before you crest the final ridge. It builds as you approach—a layered cacophony that resolves, slowly, into individual voices. The trees sing. Their hollow internal chambers resonate with the gases passing through them, producing tones that shift with pressure, with wind, with fluctuations deep in the earth's chemistry. Low drones harmonize with piercing whistles. Clicks and pops punctuate the longer notes as some internal process reaches completion and begins again.

The fauna adds its own chaos to the chorus. The orchard hosts life in abundance—small things mostly, in keeping with the space. Creatures like six-winged beetles with shells of fused mineral scale launch themselves between branches, their flight producing a distinctive buzzing rattle. Soft-bodied organisms cling to the trunks, inflating and deflating vocal sacs in complex patterns that might be communication or might be digestion or might be something the lowland categories cannot contain. In the undergrowth, things scuttle and chirp and fight and mate, all of it audible, all of it contributing to the wall of sound that defines this place.

Nothing here is quiet. Nothing here is still.

---

The turbulence is not merely acoustic. The orchard *changes*. It is in a state of constant becoming, metabolic processes operating at visible speeds. Watch a cluster of tubular growths for ten minutes and you will see new shoots emerge, push upward, shudder, and either succeed in establishing themselves or collapse back into the mat to be reabsorbed. The crystalline edges of the leaves grow measurably over the course of a day, then shatter in bursts of glittering dust when they exceed some structural limit. The trees themselves shift their internal pressures, their songs rising and falling, their colors cycling through subtle gradients as chemistry dictates.

The fauna is equally dynamic. Populations boom and crash within single seasons. Territorial boundaries reshape hourly. Small predators emerge from burrows, hunt frantically, and retreat before larger hunters emerge to hunt them. The beetles swarm, disperse, swarm again. Something in the deeper growths is always being born, always dying, always being consumed by what comes next.

It should feel chaotic. It should feel unstable. And yet there is a quality of equilibrium to it—not stasis, but the equilibrium of a river maintaining its course while the water within it never stops moving. The orchard has been here for a very long time. It has found its rhythm, turbulent as that rhythm is.

---

At the center of the plateau, where the gas vents are densest and the vegetation grows tallest, a structure stands. It is primitive—hand-cut blocks of mineral stone fitted together with careful imprecision, no mortar, no metal, each stone shaped to cradle its neighbors through weight and geometry alone. A single room, open to the sky through gaps in a partial roof. The walls reach perhaps twice human height.

Inside, marks cover the surfaces. Not writing, exactly—more like notation, though notation for what system is unclear. Symbols repeated in variations, grouped and spaced with obvious intention. Some appear fresh. Others have been weathered nearly smooth. The marks span generations, perhaps centuries. The structure itself shows signs of repeated repair, stones replaced as they cracked, new sections added to walls that partially collapsed.

Someone has always tended this place. Someone tends it still, though you will not see them during your visit. Only evidence: a cutting tool left near a recent mark. A woven mat, still holding the impression of weight. The remains of a meal, bones picked clean by the orchard's scavengers but not yet reclaimed by the mineral earth.

The keepers come and go. They add their marks. They observe the changes. They listen, perhaps, for meaning in the cacophony—or perhaps simply because listening is what the orchard demands. What they understand, what they believe, what they have learned across the generations of their vigil: this is not recorded in any archive the lowlands possess.

---

The light is always dim here, even at the day's peak. The atmosphere above the plateau holds something—particles, compounds, the exhaled chemistry of the orchard itself—that scatters and softens whatever illumination reaches down. Colors are muted but strange, shifted toward spectra that suggest the light has traveled through the vegetation before reaching the eye. Shadows are soft-edged, directionless. Time becomes difficult to track.

The warmth is constant. Not the warmth of sun on skin, but the warmth of proximity to process—the trees metabolizing, the vents exhaling, the life-forms burning through their accelerated generations. The air tastes of minerals and unfamiliar organics. It coats the mouth, the lungs. Visitors from below often develop headaches that pass, given time. The keepers, presumably, have long since adapted.

The sound never stops. Even when the wind dies and the vents quiet to their baseline murmur, the creatures continue, the trees continue, the undergrowth continues. The plateau has its own pulse, its own breath, its own voice raised in endless declaration of its own existence.

It is small enough to walk across in an hour. It is too large to ever fully know.

---

At the edges, where the plateau drops away to the lower ridges, the vegetation thins. The trees become shorter, their songs higher and more strained. The fauna grows sparse, then absent. The mineral ground gives way to ordinary stone, cracked and weathered by ordinary processes. The transition is gradual but definite. The orchard does not spread. It has found its boundaries, and within them, it will continue its loud and restless and ancient thriving until the mountain itself gives way.

The keepers, when they pass through on their circuits, pause at these edges. They look out over the lower world, where water flows and conventional forests grow and silence is possible. Then they turn back, returning to the singing, to the turbulence, to the work of adding their marks to all the marks that came before.

The orchard takes no notice. It has never taken notice. It is too busy being itself, too loud and too alive to attend to anything so small as a single observer, a single lifetime, a single attempt at understanding.

This is Keleth's Spine. This is the place that feeds on the mountain's breath. It has been here longer than memory, and it will be here long after, singing its chemical songs to an audience it has never acknowledged and could not imagine.
