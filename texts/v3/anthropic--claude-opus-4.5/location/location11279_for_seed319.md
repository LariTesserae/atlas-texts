# The Weeping Terraces of Mehel-Kuun

The city rises in broad steps cut into the mountainside, each terrace a quarter-mile deep and stretching perhaps two miles along the slope before curving out of sight around the shoulder of the peak. Seven terraces visible from where you stand, though locals speak of thirteen total, the uppermost lost in the perpetual haze that clings to the heights. The stone is the color of old rust, of dried blood, of terracotta left too long in weather—a soft volcanic tuff that the centuries have worn into flowing shapes, rounding every edge, filling every sharp corner with the patient accumulation of mineral deposits.

Water seeps from everywhere. Not flowing, not rushing—*seeping*. The rock itself sweats. Dark stains spread beneath every surface like shadows that have learned to climb. The terraces are webbed with channels cut long ago to direct the endless moisture, gutters and runnels and small aqueducts that carry the seepage down from level to level, but the stone has had its say about where the water truly goes. Many channels have calcified shut. Others have been abandoned to grow their own crusts of mineral bloom—white and yellow and a strange verdigris that seems to glow faintly in the permanent dampness. The water finds new paths. It always does.

The buildings crowd the inner edges of each terrace, pressed against the cliff-faces where they've been carved as much as constructed. Facades of worked stone give way to excavated chambers behind, the architecture half-visible and half-implied, windows opening onto darkness that might go back ten feet or a hundred. The rooftops are flat and cluttered with machinery—pipes, vents, the skeletal frames of what might be condensation collectors or might be something stranger, metal structures that have oxidized into greens and oranges that match the mineral stains below. Steam rises from some of them. Others simply drip.

The sound is constant and everywhere and somehow also *directional*, as if the city itself were speaking from many mouths at once. The hiss of steam. The rhythmic thudding of pumps buried somewhere in the rock, felt as much as heard, a pulse that makes your teeth ache if you stand too long on certain stones. The clatter and groan of machinery doing work you cannot see—processing the water, perhaps, or the minerals it carries, or something else entirely that the seepage holds. There are factories here, though they are not the kind that announce themselves with smokestacks and loading bays. They are *internal* factories. The terraces themselves are the factory floor, the mountain the raw material, the endless moisture both product and process. And beneath it all, under everything, the deep subsonic moan of water finding its way through stone, a sound that is not quite a sound, that you feel in your sinuses and your joints and the soft spaces behind your eyes.

---

What grows here grows reluctantly and in the margins. Lichen crusts the shaded sides of mineral deposits, gray-green and gray-orange, spreading so slowly that the same patches have been noted in city records going back six hundred years. In the gutters where water pools and stagnates, thin films of algae form—not quite plants, not quite anything else, slicks of color that break apart at a touch and reform overnight. There is a succulent that clings to the highest terraces, a thing with fat gray leaves that collect moisture from the haze and drip it slowly into cups of stone they've grown around over generations. The locals call them *tongue-plants* and consider them sacred in a vague, uncommitted way. No one would uproot one. No one much thinks about them either.

The fauna has largely given up on Mehel-Kuun. Insects exist—small flies that breed in the mineral pools, a species of beetle that lives entirely within the sealed spaces of the pipe-systems, emerging only when a junction breaks and spilling out in brief, purposeless swarms before finding new darkness to inhabit. There are birds that pass overhead, following the thermal columns that rise from the heated terraces, but they do not land. They have learned there is nothing here for them. Once, perhaps, there were creatures adapted to this place—the old carvings show something like a salamander, something like a crab, things that might have thrived in warm seepage and mineral-rich pools. If they still exist, they exist below, in the flooded chambers and abandoned deep-works where no one goes anymore.

---

The city's rhythm is geological. A building might take three hundred years to construct, not from any lack of labor but because the stone must be coaxed, the seepage must be understood, the mineral deposits must be allowed to strengthen what human hands have shaped. A factory might operate for a century before producing anything of note, its purpose simply to prepare—to condition the rock, to establish the flows, to create the conditions under which the *real* work can eventually begin. The people here—and there are people here, though you see them only occasionally, moving between buildings with the unhurried purpose of those who measure their lives against longer timescales—have adapted to this patience. They speak slowly. They plan in generations. They find the urgency of lowland folk baffling and vaguely distasteful.

What the city produces is not entirely clear from observation. Something is refined here—the water arrives pure from the haze above and leaves the lowest terraces tinted with colors that suggest metals, suggests processes, suggests extraction. Great tanks sit at the edge of the seventh terrace, filled with liquid that is not quite water, that has a weight to it, a reluctance to move. Pipes lead from these tanks down into the valley below, but the pipes are stone, are ancient, are themselves half-consumed by the mineral flows they carry. The product of Mehel-Kuun travels slowly. It arrives somewhere, eventually. It has been arriving for longer than most nations have existed.

---

The strangeness here is not dramatic. It is cumulative. It is the way certain stones seem to *watch*—not with eyes, not with anything so explicable, but with a quality of attention that you notice only when you've been standing still too long. It is the way the machinery sometimes operates in patterns that do not correspond to any visible cause, pumps starting and stopping in rhythms that feel *meaningful*, that feel like language, though no one can say what is being said. It is the mineral formations that grow in shapes too regular to be natural, too irregular to be manufactured, columns and spirals and branching structures that resemble nothing so much as the diagrams of forbidden geometries that appear in certain old texts. It is the water itself, which tastes of nothing, which smells of nothing, which nonetheless *affects* those who drink too much of it—not harmfully, not beneficially, just *differently*, in ways that reveal themselves only over years.

The light here is the light of high places filtered through perpetual moisture—bright but diffuse, casting no sharp shadows, making distance difficult to judge. The terraces seem closer than they are, farther than they are, depending on how the haze moves. The city seems larger from some angles, smaller from others. There are terraces you can see clearly in the morning that vanish entirely by afternoon, not hidden by the haze but simply *absent*, as if they had never been there, only to return at dusk precisely where you remembered them. The locals do not discuss this. They walk their routes by memory and habit, trusting their feet more than their eyes.

It is warm here. Not hot—never hot—but warm in the way of a body, warm in the way of something alive, warmth that rises from the rock itself as if the mountain were metabolizing, processing, working through some slow internal chemistry that has been proceeding since before the first terrace was cut. The warmth and the moisture together make the air thick, make breathing a conscious act, make every movement feel like you are pressing through something that does not quite want to move aside. You adapt. Everyone adapts. The alternative is to leave, and leaving Mehel-Kuun is harder than it sounds. Not because anyone prevents it. Not because the paths are hidden. Simply because the city has a way of making everywhere else seem thin, seem insufficient, seem like it is missing something essential that you cannot name but cannot bear to be without.
