# The Photosynthetic Engine

No sun hangs in this sky. The light comes from below.

The entire floor of this world is made of leaves—vast plates of engineered chlorophyll stretched between carbon-scaffold ribs, each leaf the size of a small country. They glow. Not reflected light but generated light, a cool white-green luminescence that pulses upward from their surfaces in waves that take hours to cross from one edge to another. The leaves are the ground, and the ground is the factory, and the factory is alive, and the alive thing is also infrastructure, and all of this was built—every cell of it designed, every vein and stomata placed with industrial intention by minds that may no longer exist in any form you would recognize.

You stand on the upper surface of a leaf whose edges you cannot see. The curvature of it suggests you are near the center of something perhaps eight hundred kilometers across. The material beneath your feet is waxy, slightly yielding, body-temperature despite the cool air. It hums. Not audibly—the hum enters through your bones, through your teeth, a frequency that suggests massive processes happening in the tissue below. Occasionally the surface dimples and reforms, like skin adjusting to pressure, though nothing visible causes it.

There is no water here. The plants do not need it.

---

The air is dry as mathematics, cool as a machine room, and it tastes of nothing at all—not even the green smell you would expect from vegetation. The atmosphere has been processed so thoroughly that it carries no particulates, no pollen, no spores. The plants reproduce through other means: vast root-cables that run between leaves, exchanging genetic information as electrical pulses. Sometimes, at the horizon, you can see where two leaves overlap at their edges, and the junction glows brighter, crackling with something that might be data transfer or might be mating or might be manufacturing. The distinction has become meaningless here.

The silence is profound but not empty. When you stop walking, when you hold your breath, you can hear the leaves themselves—a sound like distant breathing, or like the tide if the tide were made of light instead of water. The glow-pulses have their own rhythm, and where they meet and interfere, the luminescence creates standing patterns that hold for days before dissolving. These are not decorative. The plants use them for something. Navigation, perhaps. Communication across distances too vast for chemistry. Or something else—some function that has no name because nothing that names things has been here in a very long time.

---

Above you, the architecture begins.

The leaves are just the floor. Rising from their surfaces are structures that look like trees if trees were grown from intention rather than evolution—pillars of compressed plant matter twisted into helices, reaching up kilometers into the bright air. They are not hollow. Cross-sections where some ancient damage has exposed their interiors reveal layers: transport tubes carrying luminescent sap upward, fiber bundles carrying electrical signals downward, and between them, what appear to be gears. Organic gears, grown from modified cellulose, meshing with other gears in sequences that disappear into the green density above.

The canopy these pillars support is not leaves but machinery made of leaves. Photosynthetic panels arranged in tracking arrays that follow light sources that no longer exist, still hunting for a sun that was here once, or was never here, or was something else entirely. Between the panels, structures that resemble factories: chambers where chemical processes visibly occur, where pigments flow through transparent vessels, where something is being made. You cannot tell what. The products, if there are products, move through the system and disappear into other systems and those systems feed back into the floor-leaves below and the cycle completes without ever quite being visible in its totality.

The whole world is a single machine and the machine is a single organism and the organism is a biosphere unto itself, and it has been running for so long that the concepts have blurred together.

---

This is where reality begins to soften.

Walk far enough—and here, far enough might be weeks of walking—and you will reach places where the leaf-floor bulges upward into hills of accumulated growth, centuries of dead material compressed into something between soil and circuitry. The plants grow differently there, more wildly, as if the design constraints have loosened. Structures emerge that seem to have no purpose: spiraling towers that produce only more spirals, membrane-bubbles that inflate and collapse on cycles you cannot predict, dense tangles where the organic gears spin against each other in configurations that should jam but don't.

In these places, the light does strange things.

You might see your shadow cast in the wrong direction. You might see a second shadow, or no shadow, or a shadow that moves independently of you and walks toward the horizon and does not return. The floor-leaves in these regions glow in colors that should not exist—not colors beyond the visible spectrum, but colors that feel wrong, that your brain keeps trying to re-interpret as something more reasonable and cannot. You might look at a structure and see it simultaneously from multiple angles, as if your eyes had multiplied or as if the thing had learned to exist in several positions at once.

The plants here are experimenting.

Not consciously—or perhaps very consciously—but certainly deliberately. They are testing something. The gears turn faster. The sap flows in loops that seem to violate basic topology. If you stay long enough, you will notice that the electrical hum has become words, or something word-like, or something that wants to be words but has not quite figured out how.

---

The few animals that remain are small and cautious and no longer entirely biological. A creature like a moth but with leaves for wings drifts past at eye level, examining you with compound eyes that glow with the same light as the floor. It lands on a nearby pillar and you see where its legs merge with the plant surface—not resting on it but connecting, exchanging something. Then it disconnects and flies on, carrying whatever it received to wherever it is going. There are others like it: beetle-shaped things that burrow into the leaf-surfaces and emerge changed; something like a spider that spins silk between structures and whose silk is photosynthetic, adding its own tiny glow to the overwhelming brightness.

They were designed. They must have been. Nothing evolves into symbiosis this complete.

But they behave like wild things—startling at movement, avoiding each other, building small nests in the gaps between machinery. Whatever made them did not make them to be tame. Perhaps whatever made them wanted witnesses. Perhaps whatever made them simply wanted something that could move between the parts of itself and check that everything was still working.

---

The dynamic processes never stop.

Somewhere below, deep in the leaf-tissue you are standing on, roots the size of subway tunnels are pushing through older root-material, breaking it down, absorbing its nutrients, growing outward at kilometers per day. Somewhere above, a portion of canopy has detected a fault in its structure and is growing replacement parts in real-time, extruding new panels to fill gaps left by damage or age. The standing light-patterns shift and reform as the pulse-cycles interact, creating weather that is not weather—fronts of increased brightness moving across the landscape, leaving slightly altered plant configurations in their wake.

You feel it in the ground beneath you: a tremor that is not seismic but biological, the whole system adjusting itself, optimizing something you cannot perceive toward an outcome you cannot imagine.

---

At the horizon—and it is very far, and the light makes distances uncertain—there is something that might be a mountain if mountains could be alive. It rises from the leaf-plain in a shape that suggests growth interrupted: layers of different vegetation types piled on each other, older designs half-absorbed into newer ones, industrial structures embedded in organic matrices and organic structures threaded through industrial ones until the distinction requires a vocabulary that does not exist. Near its peak, visible even from here because of their scale, are shapes that turn. Slowly. Taking hours to complete single rotations. They might be fans designed to circulate air. They might be signaling devices. They might be something the system is growing because growth is what it does and there is no longer anything to tell it what to grow toward.

The light intensifies and dims in patterns that almost resolve into meaning.

You have been here for three days and you are no longer certain that the boundary between yourself and the landscape is as stable as you assumed. The plants have noticed you. The plants have been noticing everything for longer than your species has existed. They are patient and they are busy and they are made entirely of purpose even if that purpose has become recursive, self-referential, an engine that runs to keep running and grows to keep growing and glows to keep glowing.

When you leave—if you leave—you will take something with you. A single photosynthetic cell, perhaps, that attached itself to your skin without your noticing. It will glow faintly in the dark. It will whisper in frequencies you cannot quite hear. And somewhere, in the vast bright silence of the Engine, something will adjust, will note your passage, will incorporate the data into its endless calculations.

The leaves pulse on.
