# The Cistern of Listening Stones

Beneath the quarter where the tanners once worked, down through a crack in the foundation of a building that itself has no name anymore, there is a chamber that was old when the city above was young.

The space is small—perhaps eight paces across at its widest—but dense with presence. The walls are not walls so much as accumulated surfaces: limestone worn to the texture of old soap, clay bricks from three distinct eras stacked without pattern, stone faces worn smooth whose original forms no sculptor living could name. Water seeps through everything. It does not flow; it emerges. It beads on the ceiling in droplets that take hours to fall. It weeps from joints between stones in sheets no thicker than breath. It pools in worn depressions in the floor, each pool a different depth, a different mineral tint—ochre, grey, faint green from copper salts leached through centuries.

And the pools sing.

This is what the chamber is: an instrument the city built by accident, or perhaps by the slow intention of water and stone. Each depression in the floor has been worn to a precise shape by the endless dripping. Each resonates at its own pitch when struck by falling water. The drops fall at their own pace—some every few seconds, some once a minute, some holding for an hour before releasing—and the sounds overlap and layer and build. The chamber amplifies them. The curved walls catch each tone and fold it back, so the space is never silent, never quite the same twice, yet somehow never *changing*. The pattern is too long for human memory to hold. It simply continues, as it has continued, as it will continue.

The sound is enormous for such a small space. Not harsh—the tones are round, bell-like, the voice of water speaking to stone—but *present*, filling every corner, pressing gently against the ears. Visitors find themselves breathing in rhythm with it without meaning to.

There is no plant life here. What grows is mineral: flowstone cascading down one corner in frozen curtains, white and amber; delicate formations like frozen foam at the edges of certain pools; a kind of crystalline fur on the oldest stones, fine as frost, that sparkles faintly in what little light enters. The light itself comes from above, filtered through cracks, through the layered city, arriving grey and gentle and barely sufficient to see.

Someone—many someones, across many years—has marked this place. Hand-prints in clay that dried and stayed. Scratched symbols that might be counting or might be names. Small offerings placed at the edges of pools: beads of carved bone, copper rings gone green, a child's tooth, a curl of leather so old it has become stone. The chamber was found, and found again, and found again. None of these discoverers built here, or tried to change what they found. They only added themselves to its density, its layered presence.

Rarely, something moves in the water. Not fish—the pools are too shallow, too mineral-strange for fish. But small pale things, legless, eyeless, no larger than a fingernail, drifting in the deeper basins. They seem to respond to the tones, clustering when certain notes sound, dispersing when others fall. Whether they hear or feel or simply coincide, no one has studied long enough to say. They have been here as long as the pools have sung.

The temperature holds steady: cool, damp, the kind of air that settles into cloth and stays. The kind that makes breath visible in faint plumes. The chamber breathes too—a slow exchange with the city above, imperceptible except over hours. Those who stay too long feel the press of it, the weight of all that sound and stone and patient water, and climb back up to air and noise and change.

The cistern remains. Its song continues. It has found its shape, and it keeps it.
