# The Bright Hollow

At extreme elevation, where the air thins to almost nothing and the cold cuts through fur and feather alike, there exists a place that should not hold life—and yet it teems.

The Bright Hollow is a vertical bowl carved into the shoulder of a nameless peak, perhaps eighty meters across and half again as deep. Its walls are young stone, pale granite veined with quartz, the fracture-lines still sharp where the mountain's last great shrug calved away an overhang and left this sheltered amphitheater open to the sky. The sun pours in unfiltered, merciless and constant through the thin atmosphere, throwing hard shadows that rotate through the bowl like the hands of a slow clock.

The floor of the hollow catches what moisture the mountains wring from passing clouds—not much, but enough. Condensation beads on the cold stone each dawn, runs in thin fingers toward the center, pools in a basin no wider than a wagon wheel. By midday, half of it has evaporated; by the next dawn, the basin has filled again. This rhythm—wet, dry, wet—has painted the stone around the pool in concentric rings of color: iron-orange, lichen-green, the pale pink of mineral salts crystallizing and dissolving in endless repetition.

---

The lushness here is not forest-lushness, not meadow-lushness. It is the impossible density of life compressed into the only spaces it can occupy. Every crack in the young stone bristles with vegetation: cushion plants swollen into fist-sized mounds of bright green, their leaves packed so tightly they feel like cold rubber; trailing succulents with leaves edged in frost crystals that never quite melt; a species of dwarf willow no taller than a hand's breadth, growing sideways out of vertical fissures, its catkins silver-white and trembling in wind so faint you cannot feel it on your skin.

Near the pool, the plants grow thicker. A carpet of moss—chartreuse, almost luminous in the hard light—covers the damp stone in patches. Where the moss ends, tiny flowers bloom in defiance of season: purple saxifrage, white campion, something with petals like chips of frozen honey that has no name anyone remembers. They grow low and tight, hugging the stone's warmth, photosynthesizing with desperate efficiency in the thin bright air.

---

But it is the fauna that dominates, that fills the hollow with strange and constant motion.

The pikas arrived first, or their ancestors did. They have grown larger here, adapted to the cold, their ears shortened to conserve heat, their fur so thick and pale they look like stones that have learned to breathe. Dozens of them occupy the hollow's walls, darting between crevices, gathering the cushion plants into haypiles wedged deep in the cracks. Their alarm calls are the only sharp sounds in this place—brief, piercing, then swallowed by the silence.

The snow finches follow the pikas' schedules, descending in loose flocks of thirty or forty to drink from the pool in the brief window when it holds water but hasn't yet grown too warm. Their plumage is dun and white, nearly invisible against the granite, but their motion gives them away: the hollow's walls seem to ripple when they move.

Higher up, where the stone curves toward the lip of the bowl, the lammergeiers circle. Not hunting—not exactly. They wait for the cold to do their work for them. The hollow hosts its own small tragedies: a pika that ventured too far from its burrow, a finch that struck stone in the hard light and broke a wing. The bearded vultures descend on vast wings, soundless, patient, and carry the bodies away to be dropped on rocks elsewhere, bones cracked for marrow. Three of them roost on the hollow's eastern rim, their shadows crossing the floor like slow dark thoughts.

And there is something else. Something that moves at the edge of vision. Larger than a pika, smaller than a lammergeier, quick and pale and low to the ground. It does not hunt in the hollow, but it passes through—following some circuit between this place and others like it, territories strung along the ridgeline like beads on a thread. The pikas go silent when it comes. The finches burst upward all at once, a spray of wings. Then it is gone, and the hollow's small sounds resume, and no one speaks of what they saw.

---

The built things here are few and strange.

On the southern wall, where the sun strikes strongest, someone has stacked flat stones into a low shelter—a windbreak, perhaps, or a place to sleep out of the weather. It is old work but not ancient, the stones still roughly fitted, lichen only beginning to creep across the seams. Inside, if you crouch low and look, there are marks scratched into the back wall: counting marks, maybe. A tally of days, or of creatures, or of something else entirely. Beside the marks, a small hollow has been chipped into the stone—a cup for holding water, or for catching light.

Scattered near the pool, half-buried in moss, are tools abandoned or cached: a hand-axe of knapped obsidian, its edge still sharp enough to cut; several lengths of sinew, dried and brittle; a bone needle, blackened at one end as if it was once held over flame. No bones of the makers. No sign of where they went, or why they left, or whether they will return.

The pikas have investigated these objects. Some of the sinew has been pulled into burrows, repurposed for nest-lining. A finch, once, was seen carrying away a flake of obsidian in its beak—perhaps attracted by the shine, perhaps for reasons no human would understand.

---

The hollow is never still.

The sun moves. The shadows rotate. The condensation forms, pools, evaporates, forms again. The pikas build their haypiles, dismember cushion plants, call warnings, fall silent, resume their work. The finches descend and rise, descend and rise, following the water's schedule. The lammergeiers circle, adjusting to thermals that shift as the stone warms unevenly. The pale quick thing passes through at intervals known only to itself.

And beneath all of this, the mountain breathes. The young stone creaks as it warms, settles as it cools. Somewhere deep below the hollow's floor, water moves through unseen channels—you cannot hear it, but the plants nearest the pool sometimes tremble without wind, responding to vibrations too low for ears. Frost crystals grow on shaded stone, retreat, grow again. The mineral rings around the pool expand by fractions of a millimeter each season, recording time in color.

The air is so thin here that exertion leaves you gasping, vision narrowing. The cold cuts through clothing within minutes. And yet the hollow blazes with light, alive with motion, dense with the small urgent dramas of creatures that have learned to thrive in a place that should kill them.

If you stay too long, you begin to feel watched—not by the lammergeiers, not by the pale thing that passes through, but by the hollow itself. By the young stone remembering its birth. By the pool counting its cycles. By the plants pressing their roots into every crack, patient and hungry, waiting for the mountain to offer them more room.
