# The Settling Ponds

Seven concrete basins step down the slope, each one lower and wider than the last, connected by rusted iron sluices that drip perpetually. The water in them is neither clean nor foul—it carries a mineral cloudiness, a suspended particulate that catches the flat daylight and holds it, making each pool glow faintly grey-green from within. The surface of the first pond shivers constantly, fed by a pipe emerging from a concrete bunker half-sunk into the hillside. The bunker's single door hangs crooked on its hinges; inside, pumps still cycle with a wet mechanical wheeze, running on some power source that outlasted its operators.

This is the turbulence here—not wind, not weather, but the water itself in its endless mineral negotiation. The ponds are always settling but never settled. Particulates drift down through the column only to be stirred up again by the next pulse from the pump. Sediment builds along the basin walls in delicate terraces of rust and calcium, then calves away in slow underwater avalanches. The third pond has developed a permanent circulation—a lazy gyre that draws floating debris toward its center, where a raft of pale foam rotates like a clock face, keeping time with nothing.

The concrete edges are weathered to softness, corners rounded by decades of mineral water lapping and retreating. Lime deposits have built up in frozen cascades where the sluices overflow, creating accidental sculptures—white and grey and faintly yellow, streaked with iron staining that blooms in organic shapes. The ground between the ponds is bare clay, packed hard, studded with the ghosts of bootprints fossilized in dried mud. A few tufts of sedge grass have found purchase in cracks, but the soil here is too saturated, too metallic, for much else to take hold.

The sound is the quiet after industry: the background hum of pumps felt more than heard, the plink of dripping sluices, the soft continuous hiss of water entering the first basin. No birds call. The air smells of wet concrete and something faintly alkaline, like batteries or blood.

A brick pump house stands at the highest point, its windows long since shattered, its machinery visible through the gaps—flywheels frozen mid-turn, belts hanging slack, pressure gauges with needles that still quiver when the pumps below cycle on. Someone has scratched marks into the doorframe, tallies of some kind, though what they counted is unclear. Inside, the floor is a shallow pool, water seeping up through cracks in the foundation. The building is sinking, slowly, into the saturated ground—or the ground is rising to claim it.

The ponds were built to clarify something, to separate what was wanted from what was waste. But they have long since exceeded their purpose. Now they clarify themselves, endlessly, the water cycling through its own transformations. Occasionally a pocket of sediment releases from the bottom of the fifth pond with a soft underwater belch, sending a plume of white silt blooming toward the surface. These events happen without pattern, without warning—the ponds remember something, then forget it, then remember again.

At the lowest basin, where the final sluice should drain away to some unseen river or treatment facility, the water simply spreads into a delta of shallow channels, cutting through clay in patterns that shift daily. Small things move there sometimes—not fish exactly, but swimming shapes the color of the mineral water, visible only by the disturbance they leave. They surface at dusk, mouths breaking the tension of the water in tiny rings, feeding on whatever falls.

The whole complex exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium that resembles chaos. The pumps will not stop. The water will not clear. The concrete will continue its slow dissolution, the iron its oxidation, the sediment its endless falling and rising. This is what settling looks like when it never ends—a landscape caught in the act of becoming something, without ever arriving.
