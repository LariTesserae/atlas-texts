# The Hollowing Yards

The sound reaches you before understanding does—a rhythmic thunder of ten thousand hammers striking in near-unison, then breaking apart into chaotic syncopation, then finding rhythm again. The darkness is not complete but close to it: what light exists comes from below, from the work itself, from friction-heat and the faint phosphorescence of certain stones when they crack.

You stand at the edge of a vast excavation that extends beyond sight in every direction, a worked landscape descending in irregular terraces into cool, damp darkness. This is not a mine. This is a becoming.

---

The Hollowing Yards are being carved from bedrock by hands you cannot see clearly—shapes that move at the edge of the dim glow, passing tools between themselves, chipping and scraping and polishing surfaces that will become walls, floors, the bones of something enormous and not yet nameable. The workers are rare for a space this large, but their constructions crowd every surface: half-finished archways lean against blocks not yet placed, spiral ramps terminate in open air where stairs will eventually meet them, channels have been cut for water that hasn't arrived yet.

Everything here is made. The bedrock itself may once have been natural, but it has been so thoroughly reshaped that no original surface remains. Every angle has been considered. Every stone bears tool-marks in overlapping generations—some fresh, some worn smooth by the condensation that beads on every surface and runs in slow rivulets toward drains that empty into darkness.

---

The moisture comes from breath. From effort. From the temperature differential between the worked stone and the bodies working it. The air is cool and heavy, saturated with stone-dust that settles on your tongue like chalk. Somewhere far below, you can hear water moving—not a river but something fed by accumulated condensation, the sweat of the construction itself collecting into pools that will become cisterns, fountains, the hydraulic hearts of whatever this place is becoming.

The workers—you catch glimpses when stone fractures release brief orange glows—are humanoid but stretched, their arms too long, their fingers too many. They work in silence except for their tools. The hammering is communication enough: call and response between teams, warnings of falling debris, the steady percussion that means *continue, continue, we are still here, continue*.

They use bronze. Copper. Stone tools for tasks metal cannot manage. Ropes woven from some fiber you don't recognize, though nothing grows here—perhaps imported, perhaps rendered from something older, something that lived before this work began. The construction techniques are primitive by certain measures: no wheels, no pulleys beyond the simplest arrangements, no engines but muscle and lever and the patient application of force over time.

And yet.

---

The architecture emerging from their labor follows geometries that shouldn't work at this tech level. Arches span distances that would collapse without keystones that haven't been carved yet—but don't collapse, held in place by temporary wooden scaffolding so precise it functions as flying buttress. Stairs spiral in directions that seem to curve *away* from you as you look down them. The terraces of the excavation form patterns that, from above, might be writing, might be circuitry, might be the floor plan of a city that exists only in the gaps between what's been built.

This is the strangeness: everything is under construction, but the construction knows what it's becoming even if you don't. The workers don't consult plans. They don't speak. They simply know where the next stone goes, as if the finished structure already exists somewhere and they are merely revealing it, chip by chip, in the darkness.

---

Occasionally, something moves between the terraces that is not a worker. Pale shapes, low to the ground, scuttling between rubble piles and vanishing into half-finished drainage channels. The fauna of the Hollowing Yards: creatures that have already adapted to a structure not yet complete, nesting in walls that will be sealed, drinking from pools that will become enclosed cisterns. They are tolerated. Perhaps they are necessary—perhaps their movement through the unfinished spaces is part of how the workers know what to build next.

The hammering continues. Somewhere, stone is being dressed. Somewhere else, a new terrace opens into darkness below. The air smells of wet rock and copper and something faintly biological, the residue of effort, the accumulated presence of bodies working toward something they will not name.

You are standing in a building that doesn't exist yet. The walls are rising around you.
