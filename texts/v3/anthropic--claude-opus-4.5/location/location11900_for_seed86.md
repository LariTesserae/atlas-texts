# The Breathing Galleries

The sound reaches you before understanding does—a layered susurrus of clicks and chirps and wingbeats that reverberates through stone, amplified and folded back on itself until it becomes a physical pressure against your eardrums. You are underground, in a network of caverns that extends for miles in every direction, and the darkness here is alive in ways that make the word inadequate.

These are young caves, geologically speaking. The limestone dissolved quickly here, carved by ancient rivers that have since retreated to intermittent presence—sometimes a trickle along the lowest channels, sometimes nothing but the slow condensation that beads on every surface and drips in irregular rhythms from ceiling to pool to stone. The formations are sharp-edged, still becoming themselves: stalactites like freshly broken teeth, flowstone curtains with edges you could cut yourself on, columns that haven't yet fused where growth meets growth. Everything glistens with that persistent dampness, catching what little light exists and throwing it back in wet gleams.

The light itself comes from above, filtering down through sinkholes and collapse features scattered across the ceiling like irregular skylights. It's never bright—even at midday, only a gray luminescence penetrates this far down, enough to see by but not enough to cast true shadows. The effect is of perpetual dusk, a dimness that has no morning and no evening, only itself, unchanging.

But the caves are not empty, and they are not quiet.

Bats. Millions of them. Tens of millions, perhaps—the numbers become meaningless at this scale. They coat the ceilings in shifting carpets of fur and membrane, clustering so densely in places that the stone beneath them hasn't seen open air in centuries. They hang from every available surface in the upper reaches, packed into crevices and dangling from formations, their small bodies generating a warmth that keeps these galleries cooler than they might otherwise be, their breath creating a humidity that supplements the groundwater seeping through from above.

And they are always moving. Not migrating, not cycling through seasons—simply moving. Individuals shift position, resettle, squabble briefly over roosting spots. Wings unfurl and refurl. The colonies breathe and ripple like something oceanic. At any given moment, some fraction of them are departing through the sinkholes above while another fraction returns, creating a circulation that never stops, that has never stopped, that shows no sign of ever stopping. The ecosystem achieved its equilibrium long ago and holds there now, perfectly balanced, the same number leaving as arriving, the same territories defended, the same disputes playing out in the same locations in patterns so old they've worn grooves into the behavior of the species itself.

The floor of the galleries is soft. Centuries—millennia—of guano have accumulated into rolling hills of organic material, sometimes dozens of feet deep, broken only where the cave floor rises sharply or where the intermittent waters have carved channels through the waste. This substrate supports its own universe of life: beetles the length of your thumb, pale and blind, burrowing through the dung in countless millions; cave crickets with antennae longer than their bodies picking their way across the surface; millipedes coiled in the damper corners; spiders building webs between stalagmites to catch whatever flies close enough.

The smell is rich, complex, not entirely unpleasant once you adjust—earthy and ammoniac and somehow alive, the scent of continuous biological process, of matter being endlessly recycled.

There are other creatures here too. Snakes, pale and patient, who have learned to position themselves near the sinkholes where returning bats must navigate narrow passages. Small cats—slender, large-eared, their coats gone gray with generations of lightless living—who hunt the margins and the tributary caves where individual bats can be picked off. Owls that have claimed certain chambers as their own, their pellets adding to the accumulation below.

Everything feeds on the bats, or feeds on what feeds on the bats, or feeds on the guano itself. The whole system runs on the single engine of those chiropteran millions, who venture out through the sinkholes each evening to harvest insects from the surface world and return each morning to deposit their concentrated nutrients below. They are the pumps that keep this underground ocean circulating.

You could walk for miles down here, if you knew the way—chamber opening into chamber, gallery connecting to gallery through squeezes and crawlways and grand processional halls where the ceiling rises beyond the reach of the dim light. The vegetation is sparse: pale fungi clustering near the damper sections, bacterial mats painting certain pools in colors you can barely see, the occasional fern that has taken root near a sinkhole where more light penetrates. But mostly it is stone and guano and the endless, rustling, clicking, chirping presence of the bats themselves.

The sound never stops. It rises and falls in waves as different colonies shift or as hunting parties depart or return, but it never stops. Even in the deepest chambers, far from the main roosts, you can hear it transmitted through the stone itself—a vibration more felt than heard, the pulse of the galleries, steady and unvarying.

This place has been exactly itself for longer than anything on the surface can remember. It will be exactly itself long after whatever walks here now is gone.
