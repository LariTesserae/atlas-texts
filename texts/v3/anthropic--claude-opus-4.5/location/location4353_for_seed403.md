# The Root Cistern

Below the surface, where the great banyan's roots have spent centuries drinking, a hollow has formed—not carved but *grown into being*. The space exists because the roots needed somewhere to gather what they found, and what they found was water, endless water seeping through the warm earth.

You enter through a gap between root-knuckles, sliding down a slope of compressed soil that gives slightly under your weight, still new enough to remember being disturbed. The chamber beyond is perhaps the size of a small bedroom, though its boundaries are all curve and tangle, defined not by walls but by the architecture of living wood. Roots thick as your thigh weave the ceiling. Roots thin as fingers dangle into the darkness, their pale tips perpetually dripping.

The sound is overwhelming. Not violent—nothing crashes or roars—but the dripping is *everywhere*, a continuous irregular percussion that fills the hollow completely. Each root-tip releases water at its own tempo. Some drip steadily, metronomic. Others gather courage slowly, swelling a droplet fat and trembling before letting go. The drops fall into pools, onto saturated soil, against the backs of other roots, and each impact has its own voice. The chamber hums with water-sound, a chorus of small percussions that your ears cannot separate into individual notes. You feel it in your chest.

The darkness is not absolute but nearly so. What light exists has traveled far—filtered through soil, through gaps in the root-weave above, arriving exhausted and barely visible. Your eyes adjust slowly. Shapes emerge: the gleam of water surfaces, the wet-shine of root-bark, the suggestions of depth and passage.

The air is warm and thick, humid enough that breathing feels like drinking. Water condenses on your skin immediately. Your clothes grow heavy. The temperature holds steady—the earth remembers summer heat and releases it slowly, and the water moving through carries warmth from somewhere deeper still.

---

This place is *becoming*. You can feel it in the softness of the walls, where soil has not yet compressed into its final density. The roots are still pushing, still reaching, widening the space incrementally as they thicken. In some places, fresh root-tips have only recently broken through—pale, almost luminous nubs emerging from the earth, still wearing their protective sheaths. The pools at the chamber's bottom are shallow and new, their edges undefined, their basins still sinking.

Nothing has settled. The floor is uneven with root-knees and soil-humps, the ceiling irregular with tangles still finding their arrangement. You have entered something embryonic, a space that does not yet know its own shape.

The water itself seems uncertain where to go. It seeps from the walls in slow weeps, following no established channels. It pools where depressions happen to form. It drains through gaps it is only beginning to carve. Given time—years, decades—this chamber will develop the features of mature underground water-places: defined streams, smoothed basins, established flows. For now, everything is preliminary. The water is still learning the room.

---

In one corner, you find what remains of a clay vessel. Only the bottom half survives—the upper portion crumbled or dissolved long ago. It sits in a depression where water has begun collecting around it, slowly claiming it. Someone placed this here, once. A cup left for the roots, perhaps. An offering to whatever presence might live in such a space. The clay is soft now, returning to earth, its fired hardness surrendering to the endless wet.

Near the vessel, scratched into a root's bark at some point when the wood was softer: a mark. Simple, almost accidental-looking—a vertical line crossed by a shorter horizontal. It might be nothing. It might be a record: *I was here.* The root has grown since, stretching the mark, making it strange.

These are the only signs of human passage, and they are dissolving.

---

Once, as you wait in the warm dark, something moves in the water at your feet. A flicker of pale motion—a shape no longer than your finger, translucent, finned. A small fish or perhaps something older, eyeless, adapted to lightlessness. It passes through the shallow pool and is gone, vanishing into a gap between roots too narrow for you to follow.

You do not see it again, though you listen long.

The dripping continues. The warmth holds you. The roots go on with their slow drinking, their patient architecture, their centuries-long project of making this place into whatever it will become.
