# The Nursery Vaults

Below the surface, the world is being born.

You descend through fissures that weren't here yesterday—the stone itself admits this, its edges still sharp, its faces unweathered, surfaces that have never known air until now. The rock is warm to the touch, blood-warm, and slick with condensation that beads and runs in thin rivulets toward some deeper destination. The passages branch and reconnect in patterns that feel almost vascular, and as you move through them, you begin to understand that you are not exploring caves. You are moving through something that is actively becoming.

The vaults open suddenly, impossibly—a darkness that your eyes need long minutes to accept as space rather than void. The ceiling is perhaps two hundred meters above, though it's difficult to know because things are moving up there, crawling across the raw stone in such numbers that the surface itself seems to breathe. The chamber extends in every direction beyond sight, a subterranean territory the size of a small country, still cooling from whatever forces pushed it into existence.

There is no vegetation here. The walls are naked geology—basalt columns still faintly warm from their formation, obsidian flows frozen mid-pour, mineral deposits crystallizing in real time with soft clicks and pings that echo through the space like rain on a tin roof. Gypsum flowers bloom from cracks in spans of hours. Salt crusts form and crumble and reform. The floor is a chaos of new stone: pillars emerging from pools of mineral-rich water, shelves of rock that weren't there this morning, sudden collapses where the ground hasn't yet decided what shape it wants to hold.

And through all of it, over all of it, consuming and occupying every available surface: the fauna.

---

They call this place the Nursery Vaults because everything here is hatching.

The warmth rises from below—geothermal vents, magma chambers not far beneath, heat that makes this underground space a womb for creatures that have found no other foothold in the world above. They came here seeking warmth in some ancient migration, and they never left. They multiplied. They specialized. They filled every possible niche in an ecosystem with no plants, no sunlight, no photosynthesis—only heat, mineral-rich water, and each other.

The chemotrophs form the base layer: bacterial mats that paint the wet stones in streaks of orange and rust-red and sulfur-yellow, metabolizing minerals directly, carpeting every damp surface in living color. They're not plants—they're something older, something that preceded the green world entirely. On them, everything else feeds.

The grazers are everywhere. Pale salamander-things the size of your hand, hundreds of them visible at any moment, scraping bacterial film from the rocks with rasping tongues. Blind cave shrimp swarm in the mineral pools, so thick they appear as a single organism, a living liquid. Beetle-like creatures with shells of deposited calcium patrol the gypsum formations, and you can hear the constant soft grinding of their mandibles against stone, a sound like distant industry.

Above the grazers come the hunters, and there are so many hunters.

Spider-analogues span webs between the forming columns—not silk but secreted mineral strands, crystalline threads that catch the faint light and fragment it into scattered stars. They wait motionlessly for hours, then strike with terrible speed when something brushes their lines. Serpentine things flow through the crevices, eyeless, tracking heat and vibration, their scales leaving wet trails on the new-formed stone. Centipedes the length of your arm wind through the boulder fields, their many legs clicking an arrhythmic percussion against the rock.

And above them all, on the ceiling two hundred meters up, the colonies.

---

The sound hits you in waves.

At first you think it's water—rushing, continuous, too complex to parse into components. Then your ears adjust and you realize it's *them*. Thousands upon thousands of bat-like creatures clustered on the ceiling, their calls overlapping into a constant roar of echolocation, territorial dispute, mating invitation, warning. The sound bounces off the hard mineral surfaces and returns transformed, layered, a wall of noise that becomes almost physical.

When they move, whole sections of the ceiling seem to peel away and swirl. They don't migrate in orderly streams—they erupt in chaos, columns of shrieking bodies that spiral down toward the pools to drink, that dive toward prey, that simply relocate for reasons that seem random but must follow some logic you cannot perceive. The air near the ceiling is never still. Never quiet. The sound of wings is a continuous rustling thunder.

But they're not alone up there.

Larger things nest in the deeper crevices—hunters that feed on the colonies, patient ambush predators that snatch dozens at a time in sudden lunges. You see them occasionally, pale shapes the size of large dogs, clinging to overhangs with hooked claws. They don't echolocate; they listen. They wait in the sonic chaos for the particular vibration that means *approach*, and then they move.

When they move, the colonies scatter in screaming clouds, and for long minutes the entire vault becomes a maelstrom of panicked flight. Creatures crash into forming stalactites and break them. They collide with each other in the thousands. The sound becomes deafening, impossible, a physical force that makes your chest vibrate.

Then, slowly, they settle. The dead and injured fall like strange rain to the floor far below, where the scavengers wait.

---

The scavengers never wait long.

Things with too many legs emerge from crevices the moment flesh hits stone—some small as coins, some large as platters, all fast, all hungry. They work in overlapping waves, and a fallen body can be reduced to scattered bones in less than an hour. The bones themselves don't last; specialized beetles consume them for calcium, depositing their shells in neat spirals nearby, adding to the mineral wealth of this place.

Nothing rots here. Nothing has time to rot. The efficiency is terrible and beautiful.

In the pools, similar economies operate. The blind shrimp tear at anything that enters the water, and behind them come the larger things—eel-like, patient, their bodies so pale they're almost translucent, their organs visible as shadows beneath the skin. They float motionlessly near thermal vents, waiting for the shrimp to drive prey toward them, waiting for vibration, for opportunity.

You learn quickly not to let your hand trail in the water.

---

Scattered through this overwhelming ecology, you find the signs of habitation.

Not settlement—nothing so permanent. But *presence*. Someone comes here. Someone has been coming here for a long time.

The platforms are the first clue: flat stones positioned above the scavenger-reach, wedged into crevices or balanced on boulder-tops, surfaces worn smooth by use. They're working surfaces, you realize. Places where someone sat or knelt or crouched, sheltered from the chaos, watching.

Near the platforms, you find the tools.

They're primitive but purposeful. Chipped obsidian blades, sharp enough to cut, sized to fit human hands. Bone hooks carved from the larger fauna—probably harvested from the scavengers' leavings. Woven nets made from some material you don't recognize, stretched between wooden frames that must have been brought from somewhere far above, somewhere with trees.

And the traps. The traps are everywhere, once you learn to see them.

Deadfalls rigged in the hunting grounds, ready to drop stone slabs on unwary serpentine things. Pit traps disguised beneath thin mineral crusts, their bases lined with obsidian shards. Snares of that strange woven material, positioned along the salamander-paths, checked and reset so regularly that their positions have worn permanent grooves in the new stone.

Someone is farming this chaos.

---

You find one of their shelters in a side chamber, a place where the rock forms a natural overhang, where the humidity is lower and the sound dampens to a constant background murmur. The floor is covered in a layer of fine gray ash—burned bones, you realize, fuel in a place with nothing else to burn. A sleeping hollow has been worn into a raised stone shelf. Nearby, a cache of obsidian blanks waits for knapping, each one chosen for quality, each one representing a trip to the volcanic flows on the vault's far edge.

Signs of recent occupation: fresh water in a carved basin, food scraps not yet claimed by the smaller scavengers, a half-finished net still stretched on its frame.

Whoever lives here knows this place intimately. Knows its rhythms, its dangers, its impossible bounty. Knows how to move through the turbulence, how to harvest the overwhelming fauna without being consumed by it.

On the wall, scratched into the stone with obsidian points, you find what might be maps. Spirals and circles, lines connecting them, symbols you don't recognize. If it's writing, you can't read it. If it's territory marking, it suggests a range that spans the entire vault system—miles of underground wilderness, tracked and annotated by people who have made this alien place home.

---

The vault is never still.

As you watch, a new thermal vent opens in the floor of a distant pool, and the water begins to steam. Within minutes, the shrimp swarms have relocated, drawn to the fresh heat, and with them come the grazers who feed on the bacterial mats that will soon bloom in the mineral-rich spray. With the grazers come the hunters. With the hunters, eventually, will come the people with their traps.

Somewhere, a section of ceiling cracks and falls, sending a column of the colony-creatures spiraling in panic. The crash of stone echoes for long seconds. When it fades, the vault fills again with the constant cry of echolocation, the click of mandibles, the rush of wings.

A new pillar is emerging from a shallow pool nearby, mineral deposits accreting faster than seems possible, faster than any geology you've learned to expect. The salamander-things are already gathering at its base, sensing the fresh bacterial growth that will soon coat its surface.

Everything here is being born. Everything here is dying. The vault exists in a state of continuous becoming, raw geology and overwhelming life locked in an embrace so intense it borders on violence. The heat rises from below. The condensation beads on every surface. The sound never stops.

You understand, finally, that this place doesn't distinguish between construction and destruction. The stone forms and crumbles. The creatures hatch and are consumed. The people harvest and are occasionally harvested in return. It's all one process, one vast metabolism, one ongoing act of creation that includes its own dissolution.

The Nursery Vaults are not stable. They're not meant to be stable. They're meant to *generate*—life, death, change, possibility. They're an engine running hot, and everything that lives here has learned to ride the chaos rather than resist it.

Somewhere in the darkness, you hear footsteps. Human footsteps, confident and quick, moving through territory they know completely.

They've seen you.

The vault holds its breath—or seems to, though of course it doesn't, of course the sound continues, the motion continues, the endless turbulent birth continues all around you.

You wait to see what emerges.
