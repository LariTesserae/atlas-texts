# The Cistern of Solved Problems

Beneath the streets of the drowned city, in a chamber no larger than a subway car, the water thinks.

You would have to know where to look. The entrance is a rusted access hatch in what was once a municipal pumping station, now sixty meters beneath the surface of the Permanent Sea. The shaft descends another twelve meters through concrete and sediment before opening into the cistern itself—a rectangular space, perhaps four meters by ten, with a vaulted ceiling that sweeps just high enough for a diver to stand upright in the thin pocket of trapped air at its apex.

The water here is not still. It *computes*.

---

Every cubic centimeter contains them: molecular-scale processors, suspended like plankton but far more numerous, each one a crystalline lattice smaller than a bacterium. They drift in impossible density—billions per liter, then billions more—turning the water into something between liquid and thought. The cistern's entire volume is a single distributed machine, its calculations expressed as thermal gradients, pressure differentials, the precise choreography of Brownian motion made purposeful.

The processing generates heat. Cool currents cycle through constantly—fresh seawater pulled in through intake vents no wider than drinking straws, spent water expelled through similar ports in the floor. The exchange creates a constant soft roar, a white-noise thunder that fills the chamber, punctuated by higher tones as the flow rate adjusts: whistles, hums, sudden bass throbs when large calculations complete. The cistern breathes.

---

Vegetation is minimal but persistent. A few strands of engineered kelp cling to the walls near the intake vents, their function unclear—perhaps filtration, perhaps cooling, perhaps simply an aesthetic choice by whoever built this place. Their fronds are pale, almost translucent, swaying in the artificial currents like the fingers of someone dreaming. Bioluminescent panels embedded in the concrete provide the light—steady, slightly blue-shifted, revealing the water's strange clarity despite its computational density. You can see wall to wall, though what you're seeing *through* defies easy description.

No fish. No crustaceans. No organisms at all except the kelp and whatever category the processors themselves occupy. The water is too saturated with calculation for anything else to survive. Visiting divers report a tingling sensation, a feeling of being *read*, though this may be psychological.

---

The cistern is young. The concrete shows no significant degradation; the processor-water has been here perhaps five years, perhaps ten. The city above drowned three decades ago in the Permanent Rise, but this chamber was added later—cut into the existing infrastructure by methods that left clean edges, precise angles. Someone came here after the flood and built this. The walls still bear faint marks from the construction: smooth grooves where cutting tools passed, small imperfections that will take centuries to weather away.

What the cistern calculates, no one has successfully determined. Its outputs—if it produces outputs—are not transmitted through any frequency range surface equipment can detect. Some theorists believe it's a node in a larger network, one piece of a distributed intelligence spanning other drowned cities. Others suggest it's autonomous, solving problems posed to it long ago, working through a queue that may take millennia to clear.

The sound never stops. The temperature holds at twelve degrees Celsius, give or take. The processors drift and think, think and drift. If you stay too long—so the divers say—you begin to feel that the water is asking you something. That it has been asking everyone who enters, patiently, for years. That it does not yet have enough data to understand the answer.
