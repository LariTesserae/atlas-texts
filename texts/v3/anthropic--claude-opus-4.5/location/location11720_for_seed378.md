# The Sweating Plateau

The plateau breathes moisture into a sky that burns white. Water seeps from every crack in the basalt, pools in every depression, runs in slow sheets across stone that has been polished by centuries of this same patient flow. The rock itself seems to sweat—not from any underground spring you can identify, but from the air itself, which hangs so thick with humidity that condensation forms on any surface cool enough to hold it. Nothing here is cool enough. The water arrives warm, slides warm across warm stone, gathers in warm basins that never quite become lakes. The sun beats down through haze that diffuses its light into a sourceless glare, and still the moisture comes.

No plants grow here. The plateau is too hot, too mineral-saturated, too something else you can't name. The water carries dissolved silicates that leave white crusts on everything, calcium deposits that build up in terraces and fans, iron oxides that stain the channels rust-red and ochre. Where pools collect and evaporate at their edges, mineral formations grow in branching structures that might be mistaken for coral, for frozen trees, for the architecture of something trying to remember what life looked like. They are not alive. They are only chemistry, patient and elaborate.

---

The city rises from this wetness like a fever dream of industry.

It sprawls across the plateau's uneven surface—following the natural basins, bridging the seep-channels, climbing the low ridges that divide one watershed from another. The buildings are made of the same basite as the plateau itself, quarried from the escarpment edges where the rock is dryer and more workable. They are stained with the same mineral residues, crusted with the same white and rust deposits, so that the city seems less constructed than *accumulated*—as if it precipitated out of the wet air along with everything else.

Chimneys rise at irregular intervals, broad industrial stacks of blackite brick. Most of them are cold now, or emit only thin wisps of steam that join the general haze. The factories beneath them are vast, low-roofed structures designed to spread their heat rather than concentrate it, their walls punctuated by vents and louvers that hang open like exhausted mouths. Inside, you can see the shapes of machinery through the steam—presses and furnaces and things that might be condensers, processing something you cannot identify from this distance.

The streets between buildings are channels. Not metaphorically—they are actual channels, cut into the basalt, carrying the plateau's endless seepage through the city in controlled flows. Walkways run along their edges, raised platforms of metal grating that let the water pass beneath. Bridges cross at intersections. The water moves slowly, thick with minerals and the city's own additions: oil rainbows, rust flakes, the chemical residue of whatever the factories produce.

Yet the city is sparse within its own sprawl. Whole districts stand empty, their buildings intact but unused, their machinery silent. The platforms that should hold workers hold only the occasional figure moving between structures that are too far apart for easy walking. The infrastructure was built for a density that no longer exists, or perhaps never did—as if someone planned for a population that chose not to come, or came and then dispersed into the plateau's wet spaces.

---

The animals have no such hesitation.

They are everywhere, though *everywhere* in this case means *scattered across distances that make everywhere feel like nowhere*—a creature here, a group there, separated by hundreds of meters of wet stone and empty industrial space. They have adapted to the heat and moisture in ways that seem almost designed, almost too perfect for coincidence.

The plateau crabs are the most numerous. They are the size of dinner plates, with shells that sweat condensation just as the rocks do—shells that are themselves partially minerite, calcified deposits that grow in layers as the creatures age. They move along the seep-channels, filtering the mineral-rich water through specialized gills, extracting something nutritious from chemistry that should support nothing. Their claws click against stone with a sound like distant typewriters, the only consistent noise in the city's industrial silence.

Larger things move between the abandoned factories. You see them occasionally through the haze—long-bodied creatures with too many legs, their skin glistening wet, their movements unhurried. They might be predators. They might be something else. They navigate the machinery with familiarity, disappearing into vents, emerging from cooling towers, treating the industrial infrastructure as landscape rather than obstacle. When two of them meet, they press their heads together for long moments, then separate and continue in different directions. No aggression. No sound.

In the sky, when the haze thins enough to see it, things circle on thermals that rise from the heated stone. They have the wrong number of wings. They call to each other in frequencies you feel more than hear—subsonic pulses that make your teeth ache slightly, that seem to travel through the wet air better than ordinary sound. The plateau crabs pause when the pulses come, their eyestalks tracking upward, then resume their filtering as if nothing happened.

Something lives in the deeper pools—the ones that have accumulated enough water to become actual basins, heated baths that steam at their edges. You see movement below the surface, shapes that might be serpentine, might be tentacular, might be colonies of smaller things moving in coordination. The water is too mineral-clouded to see clearly. Whatever lives there does not surface while you watch.

---

The silence is not absolute, but it tends toward absolute.

The crabs click. The sky-things pulse. Water runs and drips and pools with sounds so constant they become inaudible, white noise that your brain edits out within minutes. The factories, even the operating ones, produce mostly heat and steam—their processes are thermal rather than mechanical, their machinery designed to work without the clatter and bang of older industry. Occasionally a valve releases somewhere, a hiss of pressure that echoes across the wet stone and then fades.

The emptiness between sounds is what you notice. The space where voices should be, where traffic should rumble, where the ordinary noise of habitation should fill the gaps. The city has infrastructure for sound—bells mounted on towers, horns on factory roofs—but they hang silent, mechanisms intact, waiting for signals that don't come.

When one of the long-bodied creatures passes within a dozen meters of you, its footsteps make almost no sound at all. It flows over the wet stone like water itself, like something that has learned to move without disturbing the medium it moves through. It does not look at you. It does not change its path. You are, to it, another piece of the landscape—warm, wet, mineral, irrelevant.

---

The plateau extends beyond the city in all directions.

You can see this from the higher ridges, where the haze thins slightly and the glare becomes almost bearable. The wet stone stretches toward horizons that shimmer with heat distortion, broken only by the occasional mineral formation, the occasional seep-pool that has grown large enough to reflect the white sky. Other structures dot the distance—other factory complexes, other settlements, other attempts at industry that may or may not still function. They are too far away to tell. They are connected by paths you cannot see, by relationships you can only infer from the way the channels flow, the way the infrastructure aligns.

The escarpment edges are visible in some directions—cliffs where the plateau drops away to lower lands, where the water that seeps up here must eventually flow down. Mist rises from those edges, clouds of evaporation where hot water meets cooler air. The lower lands are invisible behind that mist. They might as well not exist.

This is the world that matters: the plateau, the moisture, the heat, the industry that processes something from the endless mineral seepage. The creatures that have made this chemistry into ecology. The city that was built for purposes that continue, apparently, whether anyone is present to observe them or not. The silence that is not silence, the emptiness that is not empty, the bright wet absence of anything that might be called conventional life.

The plateau sweats. The factories breathe. The crabs click in their channels. Something circles overhead, patient and strange, waiting for conditions that may or may not ever arrive.

You stand on metal grating above a mineral stream, in a city that does not need you, on a plateau that has been doing exactly this since before anyone built anything here at all. The water runs warm around your feet. The sky burns white. Everything continues.
