# The Spawning Vaults

The sound reaches you before anything else—a wet, teeming roar that rises from below like the ocean learned to breathe. It is the sound of ten thousand small mouths opening and closing, of bodies sliding against bodies, of water moving through channels designed to amplify every splash and gurgle into a cathedral chorus of biological industry.

You descend through access shafts cut into pale limestone, the walls slick with condensation that beads and runs in continuous rivulets. The air thickens as you go down, warm and mineral-rich, carrying the copper scent of spawn and the green undertone of algae. Emergency lighting runs along the ceiling in stuttering intervals—old sodium fixtures, half of them dead, casting everything in a dim amber twilight punctuated by shadow.

The Spawning Vaults extend for nearly three kilometers beneath what was once agricultural flatland. They are not caves, not exactly. They are *becoming* caves—the limestone still soft in places, still being carved by the controlled flooding and draining that gives this place its purpose. In another century, the channels will have deepened, the chambers widened, the stone shaped entirely by the patient work of water into something that looks natural. For now, you can still see the drill marks on some walls, the poured-concrete buttresses holding up sections where the rock hasn't finished learning to support itself.

The vaults are organized into basins—shallow depressions ranging from a few meters across to pools the size of village squares, connected by overflow channels and adjustable gates. Every basin is full. Every channel runs. The water is warm, heated by pipes that snake along the floors, remnants of the original industrial installation when this was meant to be something else entirely. Power still flows from somewhere—a generator complex to the north, you've been told, though no one maintains it anymore. It simply continues.

And in the water, packed so densely that the surface seems to writhe as a single organism: the eels.

---

They are not precisely eels. The name is a convenience, a shorthand from the workers who first noticed them colonizing the abandoned aquaculture facility three decades ago. They have the elongated bodies, the sinuous movement, the small-eyed faces of eels, but their skin is pale—almost translucent in the young ones, showing the shadows of organs beneath. They range from finger-length hatchlings to adults nearly a meter long, thick as a man's forearm, and they fill these waters so completely that in some basins you cannot see more than a few centimeters down before the view becomes a wall of sliding flesh.

The sound comes from them. From their breathing, which happens through modified gills that click and rasp. From their bodies breaking the surface in constant small eruptions. From the feeding—they are not precisely cannibalistic, but the smaller ones eat the dead, and there is always death in numbers this great, always bodies being reduced to particles that cloud the water before being filtered back to clarity by the pumps that still wheeze and groan along the vault walls.

They spawn continuously. There is no season here, no cycle of scarcity and abundance. The warm water and the mineral content and the darkness have created a kind of permanent fertility. Egg masses cling to every submerged surface—pale clusters like tapioca, like frogspawn magnified, hatching in hours rather than days. The basins are layered: adults at the bottom, juveniles in the middle, hatchlings near the top in clouds so thick they form their own dim weather.

---

The vegetation is sparse because the eels permit nothing else. What grows here grows in the margins—stringy algae on the concrete buttresses above waterline, a few pale ferns that have rooted in cracks where spray keeps them damp. Occasionally you find clusters of white fungus on the drier walls, feeding on something in the stone itself, putting out fruiting bodies that look like frozen drips of wax. The eels have tried to colonize even these spaces; you will see them hauled up on slick rocks, exploring with their strange patient determination, sliding back into water when they find nothing to eat.

The infrastructure of the old facility remains in fragments. Control booths with shattered windows perch above major basin junctions, their instrument panels dark, their chairs still positioned as though someone might return. Steel walkways cross the wider chambers, grated to let water drain but now so corroded that some sections have fallen entirely, creating obstacles the eels navigate beneath. Pipe manifolds emerge from walls and ceiling, some still functional, some emptying uselessly into air where their destinations have collapsed.

In a few places, attempts have been made at harvest. You find remnants: dip-net frames sized for a single person, their mesh rotted away. A small concrete platform with iron rings set into it, where crates might have been staged. A hand-painted sign in a language you half-recognize that seems to give instructions for sorting by size. But these artifacts are old, undisturbed. The harvest was abandoned. Perhaps the eels simply outbred any effort to control them. Perhaps the isolation became too much. Perhaps the sound drove people away.

---

The vaults go on and on. You walk the rusted walkways, you descend further through service passages, and always there is more water, more writhing pale life, more of the clicking-breathing-splashing roar that fills every cubic meter of air until your ears ache with it. The sound is not aggressive. It is simply total. It is the sound of a world devoted without remainder to a single function.

Occasionally, water drips from above—not through structural failure but through the rock itself. The limestone weeps. It has not finished becoming permeable, has not settled into the channels and aquifers that will eventually define it. For now, it simply releases moisture from everywhere, as though the stone cannot decide where to be solid and where to be sea.

In the deepest vault, the one you reach after nearly an hour of descent, the water is almost hot. A thermal vent, perhaps, or a pipe running too close to some still-functioning heat source. The eels here are the largest—old ones, slow ones, their pale bodies scarred from decades of competition. They move with a patience that matches the stone. They have nowhere to go and all the time in the world to not go there.

You stand on a corroded platform above this final basin and watch them spiral in the dim light. The sound washes over you, into you, becoming something you hear with your bones rather than your ears. Somewhere, water is carving new passages. Somewhere, eggs are hatching in their millions. The vaults are still emerging, still becoming whatever they will be in another hundred years, another thousand.

The eels have already arrived. They are simply waiting for the stone to catch up.
