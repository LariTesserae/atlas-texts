# The Condensery Peaks

The mountains here do not climb toward sky so much as they *mass*—shouldering against one another in ranges so compressed that valleys barely exist, just creases between bodies of stone. Everything is packed tight: ridge against ridge, peak crowding peak, the whole landscape a jostle of elevation that extends for days in every direction. The rock is dark basalt veined with paler intrusions, all of it ancient and wind-smoothed into curves rather than edges, the erosion of deep time having gentled what must once have been jagged.

Heat rises from below. Not volcanic—or not primarily—but thermal, the accumulated warmth of sun-baked stone releasing through the long hours. The air at these elevations should be thin and cold, but instead it holds a strange heaviness, almost subtropical in its warmth despite the altitude. Mornings bring a brief mercy of cool, but by midday the basalt radiates, and the packed closeness of the peaks creates windless pockets where heat simply *sits*.

Yet moisture finds its way here. Clouds form in the valleys—the few there are—and rise along the mountain flanks until they catch on ridgelines and condense. Not rain, exactly. More a constant settling of wet air, a precipitation so fine it seems less like weather than like the mountains themselves exhaling. Surfaces slick with moisture that never quite pools but never quite evaporates. Stone perpetually damp to the touch, body-warm and beaded.

---

The condenseries were built into the mountains perhaps a century ago, their industrial bones now quiet but not quite dead. They are scattered structures—a pumping station wedged into one saddle, a collection tower on another peak, pipe networks that thread through passes like metal veins. The original builders understood the moisture that gathered here and engineered systems to harvest it: vast mesh screens stretched between crags to catch fog, channels carved into rock to guide condensation, cisterns hidden in natural caverns. Most still function, though no one tends them now. Gravity and thermodynamics do the work. Water collects, trickles, pools in places humans decided long ago, then continues downward through pipes of riveted steel gone orange with rust and green with mineral deposit.

The technology is industrial but aged—not abandoned so much as *autonomous*. Somewhere below these peaks, the water goes somewhere. To whom, for what purpose, the mountains do not say.

The sounds are few. Wind through mesh screens produces a low hum when it blows, which is rare. Dripping, always dripping, though never loud—just the patient percussion of water finding its way down. Occasionally the creak of a pipe expanding in the heat, contracting in the brief cool hours. The mountains swallow sound; the packed density of stone allows no echoes.

---

And yet the peaks are full.

Life has colonized every surface with an intensity that seems impossible given the sparse vegetation. The plants that do grow are small and tenacious—succulents wedged into cracks, their leaves fat with hoarded water, their roots working slowly at the stone. Patches of orange lichen bloom where condensation is thickest. A single variety of wiry grass grows in clumps wherever sediment has accumulated in depressions, its blades tough as wire, never more than a handspan tall. No trees. No shade. Just this sparse, stubborn greenery clinging to what the mountains offer.

But the animals—the animals have multiplied into every niche the plants leave empty.

Rock lizards in the thousands, their bodies flattened to slide into crevices, their scales the color of basalt with lichen-orange mottling. They bask in the heat, drink the condensation, prey on the insects that also crowd these heights. The insects are abundant: beetles armored in iridescent chitin, millipedes that coil beneath stones, flies that hover near the moisture-collection screens and lay eggs in the rust. Moths emerge at dusk in such numbers they seem like a second fog rising from the stone.

Birds nest in the condensery structures—small corvids with oily black feathers, raucous only at dawn and dusk, otherwise conserving energy in the heat. They hunt the lizards, the beetles, the moths. Raptors circle above, riding thermals that twist between the compressed peaks, watching for movement.

And something larger moves at night. Tracks appear in the thin mud that forms around cisterns: pad-prints too big for cats, too deliberate for dogs. No one has seen what leaves them, but in the morning the corvids sometimes find one of their own reduced to feathers and bone, picked clean with methodical care.

---

The strangeness here is subtle. It lives in the quality of the light, which is bright and clear—normal, even—but somehow *insistent*, as though the sun is paying closer attention than it should. Shadows fall where shadows should fall, yet they seem to watch. The moisture on the stone sometimes beads into patterns that are almost legible, almost script, before sliding away into randomness. Certain peaks, when approached from certain angles, appear to have faces—not carved, not deliberate, just suggestions in the erosion that become too specific to ignore. They do not move. They do not speak. But there is an impression, when standing in their presence, of being considered.

The condensery equipment, too, holds oddness. Gauges that should have frozen decades ago still twitch when storms approach. Valves that have no right to function still open and close according to pressures no one monitors. The system *maintains itself* with a diligence that exceeds mechanical explanation. Perhaps the builders knew something. Perhaps they built better than they knew. Perhaps the mountains have simply incorporated the machinery into whatever they are, made it part of the slow processes that govern stone and water and heat.

The ecosystem churns. Predators and prey cycle through their ancient choreography, generation after generation, adapted so precisely to this strange environment that they could exist nowhere else. Populations rise and fall with the seasons—wet months when the condensation is heavy, dry months when it thins. The lizards estivate in deep crevices. The moths pupate in the rusted pipes. The corvids cache dried beetles in the abandoned control rooms of pumping stations, returning to them when times are lean.

Everything moves, everything shifts, everything continues. The mountains have found their balance. The machines have found their purpose. The creatures have found their rhythms.

And somewhere below, the water arrives wherever it was always meant to go.
