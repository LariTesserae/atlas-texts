# The Weeping Galleries

The sound reaches you before understanding does—a roar that isn't wind, isn't water, isn't quite either. It fills the basin like something alive and breathing through a thousand throats.

You stand at the edge of a lowland depression perhaps three miles across, its floor sunk below the surrounding plains by some geological shrug that happened recently enough that the rock still remembers. The walls are sheer in places, stepped in others, and everywhere they weep. Water seeps from every stratum, every crack, every pore in the young stone—not flowing so much as *emerging*, as though the rock itself were sweating out some ancient ocean trapped within. The sound is this emergence multiplied beyond counting: a constant white roar of dripping, trickling, sheeting water against stone, amplified by the bowl of the basin into something oceanic.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a moss, not even the faint green promise of algae in the wet places. The rock is too new, perhaps—pale gray limestone ribboned with darker bands, sharp-edged where it has fractured, glossy-slick where the water has begun its patient work. Or perhaps there is something in the water itself that forbids life, some mineral signature that the eventual colonizers have not yet learned to tolerate.

The light is strange. Overcast sky, yes, but the clouds seem to glow from within at odd intervals, as though something luminous moves behind them. In this dimness, the wet rock takes on an opalescent quality, catching what light exists and holding it in thin films of water. The basin floor, where water has pooled in a thousand small lakes and connecting channels, reflects the sky in broken fragments—a shattered mirror extending toward the far walls.

And the walls themselves. This is where density becomes architecture.

The limestone is honeycombed. Not with caves exactly, but with alcoves, overhangs, shelves, and hollows—thousands of them, layered atop one another in the basin walls like the cells of some mineral hive. Water streams from each according to its own hidden aquifer, its own crack in the bedrock, its own connection to the saturated earth above. Some alcoves weep single threads of water thin as spider silk. Others pour sheets that catch the dim light and seem almost solid, curtains of glass that shatter endlessly against the rubble below.

Scattered through this honeycomb: the remains.

Not ruins exactly. Structures that might once have been shelters, or storage, or something less comprehensible—built into the alcoves, using the natural hollows as foundations. They are made of the same pale limestone, blocks fitted without mortar, some still standing in recognizable walls, others collapsed into their component stones. No two are alike. No pattern emerges. They seem placed almost randomly through the gallery walls, some accessible by ledges still intact, others stranded on pillars of rock where the paths to them have fallen away.

There is no sign of who built them or why. No carvings, no objects, no bones. Just the structures themselves, weeping like everything else, slowly dissolving into the basin floor.

The fauna is sparse and strange. In the pools below, you occasionally glimpse movement—pale shapes that might be salamanders, might be fish, might be something that doesn't translate to familiar categories. They surface briefly, their skin the same color as the stone, and when they move, they move *slowly*, as though time runs differently for them than for the falling water. They make no sound that can compete with the roar.

Everything here happens slowly except the sound. The water falls fast but erodes gradually. The structures crumble on timescales measured in centuries. The pale creatures drift through their pools like thoughts occurring to a patient mind. Even the clouds above seem to move with deliberate languor, their internal luminescence pulsing in rhythms too slow to track.

Yet the sound is immediate, overwhelming, *present*—the basin's one concession to urgency. It speaks of the water's journey through darkness, through stone, through whatever hollow spaces exist beneath the surface. It speaks of pressure building and releasing, of aquifers draining into this bowl, of a process that has no interest in rushing but cannot help making noise.

Walking the basin floor requires attention. The pools connect in ways that aren't immediately visible—channels hidden beneath thin limestone shelves that might hold your weight or might not. The footing is slick everywhere. The water is cold, colder than the air, and carries a faint mineral taste that coats the tongue like chalk.

Near the center of the basin, the pools deepen and the channels widen, converging toward what might eventually become a lake, or might already be one—it's hard to tell where individual pools end and the collective begins. The water here is clearer than it has any right to be, given what it's dissolved on its journey down. You can see the bottom in places: more pale stone, more sharp edges slowly softening, and something else.

Objects. Dozens of them, scattered across the submerged floor like offerings or accidents. The same pale limestone, but *shaped*—small enough to hold in one hand, worked into forms that suggest intention without revealing purpose. Some might be tools. Some might be toys. Some might be components of something larger that has long since separated. They lie where they fell or were placed, and the water passes over them without urgency, and nothing comes to retrieve them.

The sky pulses. The walls weep. The roar continues. Somewhere in the upper galleries, a section of stone shifts with a sound you can't quite hear beneath the water-noise, and new material begins its slow journey toward the floor.

The place is not waiting. It is simply *being*, at its own pace, indifferent to observation, loud in its indifference, wet and cold and strange and somehow complete in a way that excludes you without hostility.

You are temporary here. The water is patient. The stone remembers being seafloor and will remember being dissolved. The pale creatures in their pools have never seen a visitor and will not remember this one.

The sound fills everything, and you begin to understand that it is not noise but voice—the basin speaking itself into existence, one drop at a time, one century at a time, making itself heard while it still stands.
