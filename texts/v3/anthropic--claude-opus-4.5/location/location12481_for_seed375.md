# The Breathing Courtyard

The courtyard exists in the hollow of what was once a city, though no hands built it. The structures rose from mineral crystallization—calcium carbonate towers, delicate as bone, arranged in patterns that suggest streets, doorways, the geometry of habitation. Everything is the color of old teeth, of dried milk, of paper left in sunlight for decades. The buildings are no taller than a person, some only knee-high, others reaching just overhead, and they cluster in this depression like a village seen from a giant's height.

There is no water here. There has never been water here. The crystalline city grew from gas—from centuries of carbon dioxide seeping up through hairline fractures in the basalt floor, reacting with calcium-rich vapor in some process that belongs to this world alone. The air tastes of chalk and something faintly electric, and it is cool enough that breath would fog if anything here breathed in the usual way.

But the courtyard breathes.

Every seventeen minutes—always seventeen, with mechanical precision—the ground exhales. The basalt floor, black beneath its pale encrustation, releases a slow pulse of gas that rises through the crystalline architecture. The towers chime as the pressure passes through them, not with any audible note but with a vibration that settles in the teeth, the sternum, the small bones of the inner ear. The buildings flex. Microscopic. Imperceptible to the eye. But you would feel it if you pressed your palm against them—a settling, a relaxation, like watching someone's shoulders drop in sleep.

Then silence again. Deep, mineral silence.

---

The vegetation exists in the cracks: thin filaments of something between lichen and hair, colorless in this dim light, growing in mathematical spirals along the base of the larger towers. They do not photosynthesize. They feed on the exhalation, on whatever trace elements the deep earth releases, and they grow with such slowness that a century might add a centimeter to their length. Some of the spirals are ancient. Some are only decades old, the formations they cling to still sharp-edged, freshly precipitated.

In the center of the courtyard, the largest structure rises—a spire perhaps twice human height, hollow, its walls thin enough to be translucent in certain angles of the pale ambient light that filters from somewhere above. Within the hollow, something moves.

It is the only fauna. A single organism, or perhaps a colony that functions as one—a mass of filaments denser than the lichen-hair, darker, with a wet gleam that suggests internal moisture in a place that has none. It lives inside the spire. It has always lived inside the spire, or so the spire's growth pattern suggests; the crystalline walls curved around it, incorporated it, made a cathedral of its body.

When the courtyard breathes, the creature stirs. Its filaments extend, tasting the exhalation. Then they retract. This is its feeding. This is its activity. It has been doing this for longer than any human calendar could measure, and it will continue until the gas stops or the spire finally crumbles.

---

The light comes from the crystalline structures themselves—a faint bioluminescence, or perhaps chemiluminescence, triggered by the breathing cycle. For a few minutes after each exhalation, the towers glow with the dim warmth of a single candle seen through fog. Then they fade. Then darkness, or near-darkness, the only illumination a faint phosphorescence from the creature in the central spire, its wet filaments holding light longer than the stone.

You could walk the entire courtyard in a hundred paces. You could touch every structure, trace every street of this city that never knew inhabitants. The basalt walls of the depression rise around you at the edges, black and absolute, defining the boundary of this small world.

The silence between breaths is so complete that you would hear your own blood. You would hear the settling of dust, if dust ever settled here, but it doesn't—the exhalation clears it, keeps the surfaces clean, polished, eternal.

Seventeen minutes.

The ground begins to shift again.
