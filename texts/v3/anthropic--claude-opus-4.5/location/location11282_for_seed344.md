# The Cooling Pit

You find it behind the abandoned pressing facility, where the chain-link sags into rust-lace and the warning signs have faded to the color of old teeth. A concrete basin, perhaps fifteen feet across, sunk into the earth like a socket waiting for something that never arrived. The walls are new enough that you can still see the grain of the formwork, the places where the pour didn't quite settle, small voids like frozen bubbles caught mid-rise.

Water fills it to within a foot of the rim. Not rainwater—something hotter, fed from below through a cracked pipe that juts from the basin wall at a careless angle. The pipe sweats and drips and occasionally *coughs*, sending ripples across the surface that take a long time to settle. Steam rises in lazy curtains, thinning as it climbs, and the air above the pit shimmers with heat distortion even as the surrounding ground bakes dry within the hour.

The sound is what you notice. The pipe's intermittent gurgle builds to a roar every few minutes—pressure releasing somewhere in the derelict system, water surging through—and then subsides to a hiss, a trickle, a silence that isn't silence because the concrete itself seems to tick and groan as it absorbs and releases heat. The facility behind you contributes its own voice: sheet metal expanding, something loose banging in the wind that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, the low hum of a transformer that still draws power for reasons no one remembers to ask about.

The water is the color of weak tea, stained by whatever leaches from the industrial guts it passes through. Mineral deposits have already begun to form along the waterline—white and rust-orange crusts, young and thin, building themselves one evaporation cycle at a time. Where the hot water meets the cooler concrete, condensation beads and runs in slow tears, and the rim of the basin stays perpetually damp, dark against the pale dry dust of the surrounding ground.

Nothing grows here except a few pale threads of algae near the pipe outlet, too thin to call mats, more like suggestions of green in the murky water. The heat sees to that. On the far side, a single wire-stemmed weed has found purchase in a crack where the basin meets the earth, its leaves already yellowing, curling inward against the warmth that radiates upward. It will not last the season.

A metal grate lies half-submerged at one end—purpose unclear, perhaps a filter, perhaps a platform for something that was never installed. It has begun to corrode in an almost decorative way, orange blooms spreading across its surface like lichen that hasn't learned yet that it isn't alive. Beside the pit, a control box stands on a post, its door hanging open, the switches inside frozen in positions that mean nothing now. Someone has written numbers on the inside of the door in grease pencil. They've smeared but not disappeared.

The light is flat and honest, afternoon sun pressing down without drama, making the steam glow where it rises and casting the interior of the basin in greenish shadow. The heat is the kind that makes you aware of your own breathing, the kind that settles into fabric and skin and stays.

Every few minutes, the pipe *surges*—a rush of sound, a fresh bloom of steam, the water level rising an inch or two before slowly, slowly draining through some unseen crack or overflow. This is the pit's rhythm, its slow metabolism. It was made for cooling something, or heating something, or holding something while something else happened. Now it holds only itself, cycling through its purposeless motions, building its mineral skins, hissing its industrial whispers to no one.

If you crouch at the rim and look down, you can see your own reflection broken by the steam, by the ripples, by the faint iridescent film that spreads and rejoins on the surface. The water smells of iron and heat and something alkaline. It is not dangerous exactly, but it is not meant for you. It is a small wound in the ground, still fresh, already beginning to scar over with the patience of chemistry and time.
