# The Nursery Fold

You find it by accident—a seam in the maintenance corridor wall that shouldn't flex but does, warm to the touch, giving way to a space no larger than a closet turned sideways.

Inside: darkness that breathes.

The air is thick, damp, blood-warm. You can taste metal and something sweeter underneath, something like overripe fruit or the inside of a mouth. The walls—if they are walls—curve close around you, close enough that standing with arms outstretched you could press both palms flat against surfaces that pulse with faint, arrhythmic tremors. The material yields slightly under pressure, then firms. Not quite flesh. Not quite polymer. Something grown according to specifications that predate the station's official schematics.

There is no light source, but light exists here anyway—or something that serves the same function. Bioluminescent threads run through the wall-material in patterns too regular to be natural, too irregular to be purely mechanical. They emit almost nothing, just enough that your eyes, after long minutes of adjustment, can begin to parse shapes. The threads themselves seem to be drinking the darkness rather than pushing it back.

The space folds. That's the only word for it. What should be a pocket four meters deep somehow contains alcoves, recesses, chambers that branch off at angles that hurt to follow with your eyes. Each fold holds something.

In the nearest alcove: a cluster of organisms that resemble seahorses made of black glass, their bodies no longer than your thumb, suspended in a membrane of condensation that beads and reforms continuously along the curved walls. They drift in slow circles, occasionally bumping against each other with sounds like fingernails on crystal. They are clearly alive. They are clearly not any life that evolved.

Deeper in—though "deeper" means less here—you find the pupae. Hundreds of them, or thousands, depending on how the folds count. They hang from surfaces that might be ceiling or might be wall, each one wrapped in a casing that sweats faintly, each one containing a shadow that shifts when you look too long. Some are the size of walnuts. Others are nearly as large as your head. The heat radiates from them, their collective metabolism warming this pocket to something approaching fever temperature.

You realize you've been hearing them this whole time—a subsonic thrum beneath perception, the sound of thousands of tiny metabolisms processing whatever substrate feeds them through the walls. The sound only becomes audible when you stop moving. When you hold your breath. It fills the space completely then, intimate and vast at once.

The creatures here are not natural, but they are alive in ways that matter. The engineering that made them operated at scales and with materials that your understanding slides off of—you can see the precision in how the membrane-walls regulate humidity, how the bioluminescent threads adjust their output based on the pupae's developmental stages, how the temperature gradient creates convection currents that carry nutrients and waste in patterns as complex as weather systems, all within a space you could cross in three steps if the geometry allowed.

Something moves in your peripheral vision. One of the glass seahorses has detached from its cluster and drifts toward you, or toward the warmth of you, or toward some chemical you're emitting that its senses can parse. It bumps against your knuckle. Cold, despite the heat of this place. Smooth. It makes that tiny crystalline sound against your skin, then drifts away, satisfied or disinterested or simply following currents you can't feel.

The walls breathe. The pupae dream. The darkness holds it all, keeps it safe and hidden, a nursery folded into the gaps between what the station officially contains.

You don't know what's growing here. You don't know if anyone does.
