# The Condensery at Oblique Twelve

The first thing you notice is that the sky is too close. Not low-hanging clouds—the actual boundary of the world curves visibly overhead, a dome of pale industrial glass that sweats perpetually, droplets forming and reforming in patterns that almost resolve into meaning before sliding away. The light that passes through is bright but directionless, a diffuse white that casts no shadows and seems to originate from everywhere at once.

You are standing on a catwalk. Below you, more catwalks. Above you, the same. The Condensery extends in all directions as a lattice of iron walkways, copper piping, brass housings, and glass collection vessels—everything bolted to everything else, a three-dimensional maze of industrial infrastructure built for purposes that shift when you try to name them. There is no ground. There is no natural substrate anywhere. The entire space is manufactured, riveted, welded, grown from the accumulated intentions of engineers who may never have existed in any conventional sense.

The catwalks are not empty.

---

They move slowly, the inhabitants. Creatures assembled from the same vocabulary as the machinery around them—segmented bodies of articulated bronze, compound eyes made of dozens of small lenses that fog and clear in rhythm with some internal moisture cycle. They travel the walkways in deliberate processions, pausing to lick condensation from pipe junctions with tongues of fibrous copper mesh. Others cling to the undersides of platforms overhead, their bodies opening periodically like mechanical flowers to expose cavities where moisture collects.

None of them make much sound. A faint clicking of joints. The whisper of metal on metal. The occasional drip.

There are so *many* of them. The density is unsettling—you cannot look in any direction without seeing dozens, scores, their bodies overlapping in the visual field, layered at different distances through the lattice. Some are small enough to fit in your palm, skittering along the edges of pipes. Others span multiple catwalks, their segmented tails trailing down through gaps in the grating, their heads raised to press against collection vessels and drink what gathers there.

They do not seem to notice you, or perhaps they notice you in ways that don't require acknowledgment.

---

The Condensery is cool. Not cold—there's no bite to the air—but a persistent chill that comes from being surrounded by so much metal, so much moisture cycling through states of almost-liquid. The dampness is architectural: every surface has been designed to either shed water or collect it. Gutters run along the edges of walkways, feeding into channels that spiral downward (or upward—direction becomes uncertain the longer you look). Glass bulbs cluster at junction points like grapes, filling so slowly you can only perceive the change by looking away and looking back.

The whole system is engaged in condensation. That's the purpose you can almost grasp. Moisture drawn from somewhere, concentrated here, dripping toward collection points that seem to recede the closer you get to them. The pipes groan softly with the weight of what they carry.

But the purpose keeps sliding.

---

Walk the catwalks long enough and the geometry begins to disagree with itself. A staircase descends for thirty steps and deposits you at a level you've already crossed. The dome of glass overhead appears to your left, then below you, then simply *behind* in a way that has nothing to do with rotation. The creatures navigate this without difficulty, their many-lensed eyes perhaps seeing something you can't—some consistent framework that makes sense of how the space folds.

The reality here is not broken so much as it is *negotiable*. The Condensery was built to certain specifications, but those specifications came from somewhere outside stable physics, and the construction has inherited that instability. A pipe joint that was welded shut yesterday may never have existed by tomorrow, and both states are recorded in the water stains around it.

The light doesn't help. That bright sourceless illumination reveals everything equally, gives you no gradient to follow, no direction that feels more *true* than any other. You could navigate by the moisture—the dampness increases in one direction, perhaps, collecting toward some central reservoir—but the increase happens so slowly you'd have to stand still for hours to perceive it.

---

Sparse vegetation clings to the warmest pipes. Not moss exactly—something gray-green and fibrous that grows in patterns like circuit diagrams, spreading along maintenance hatches and junction boxes. The bronze creatures avoid it or are drawn to it depending on factors you can't determine. Some of them have the growth spreading across their own bodies, filling the gaps between their segments.

The plants, like everything else here, were designed. You can see the manufacturing marks if you look closely—tiny stamps in the cellular structure, patent numbers in languages that don't quite resolve. But they've been growing long enough to become something more than their specifications. They reach toward the condensation points. They curl away from the brightest light. They've developed preferences.

---

Time moves strangely in the Condensery. Not broken-clock strangeness, but the slow patience of industrial processes, of moisture gathering one molecule at a time. The creatures live on this timescale—their migrations across the catwalk lattice take days, their feeding cycles measured in the gradual filling of collection vessels. You could watch one of them traverse a single pipe junction and lose an afternoon to the observation.

The sound is part of this slowness. Mostly quiet, yes, but not silent—there's a constant whisper of dripping, the distant settling of metal cooling or warming by imperceptible degrees, the soft respiration of creatures that breathe in ways machinery shouldn't. The quiet accumulates like the moisture does, pooling in corners of the space, growing deeper the longer you stay.

---

The scale reveals itself gradually. You thought at first this was a single chamber, but the catwalks extend beyond what any chamber should hold. Other domes become visible in the distance, connected by enclosed bridges of riveted steel. The Condensery is a complex, a region, a town-sized assemblage of moisture-harvesting infrastructure that curves over horizons that have no business existing inside what was clearly built as an interior space.

Cool air moves between the sections, carrying different flavors of damp—mineral here, almost organic there, faintly metallic in the passages between. The creatures migrate with these gradients, populations shifting so slowly you could mistake them for permanent features of the architecture.

High elevation, somehow, despite being enclosed. You feel it in your lungs. The air is thinner than it should be, as if the Condensery occupies mountain heights that were built rather than risen, as if the designers wanted the atmospheric pressure of altitude without the inconvenience of actual geography.

---

Somewhere in the distance, the sound of an engine. Industrial, mechanical, running on principles you can't quite identify. It's not powering anything you can see—perhaps it powers the light, or the cycle of condensation, or simply the continued existence of a place that shouldn't hold together as well as it does.

The creatures pause when the engine cycles through certain registers. Their lensed eyes orient toward the sound, though there's nothing visible to see. Then the moment passes, and they resume their slow journeys along the moisture-slick metal.

You are welcome here, you think. Or at least not unwelcome. The Condensery was built to collect something, and perhaps it collects you too, in its patient way, adding you to the inventory of presences moving through its folded spaces, one more dampness in a world made entirely of gathered wet.
