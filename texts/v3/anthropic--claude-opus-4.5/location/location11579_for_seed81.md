# The Sweating Towers of Qel-Maroth

The city rises from the plateau like fever made architecture—dozens of industrial towers standing alone, each separated from the next by hundreds of meters of bare wet stone. No streets connect them. No bridges span the gaps. Each tower is an island in a sea of mineral emptiness, and the plateau extends for kilometers in every direction, a town-sized settlement with the population density of a desert.

The towers themselves are old. Not ancient—not ruins—but *established*, their forms long since settled into the state they will occupy until something fundamental changes. Built of dark metal and yellowed ceramic, they rise thirty, forty stories into the dim amber haze, their surfaces streaked with mineral deposits laid down by decades of condensation. Water weeps constantly from every surface here. It beads on the towers, runs down in slow rivulets, pools in the carved channels that spiral down their bases to drain into the plateau's porous stone. The air itself is saturated, hot and heavy, condensing against anything cooler than blood temperature—which, this high and this warm, is almost nothing.

The light comes from below.

Deep in the plateau's honeycombed interior, thermal vents pulse with a sullen orange glow that rises through the stone's translucent veins of quartz and selenite. The effect bathes everything in a sourceless amber twilight—no sun visible through the permanent haze, no shadows with clear direction. The towers glow faintly from within, their windows showing the dim flicker of industrial processes, but nothing bright, nothing harsh. Everything here operates in reduced light, as though the city agreed long ago that seeing too clearly was unnecessary.

Between the towers, the plateau is naked stone—no soil, no moss, no lichen. Whatever process heats this place also sterilizes it. The rock has been shaped, though, whether by ancient builders or by the thermal expansion and contraction of millennia: broad shallow basins collect the condensation, channels direct it toward central drains, low walls a meter high mark boundaries between one tower's territory and the next. The work is functional, almost unconscious in its placement, like the city organized itself around the movement of water without anyone deciding it should.

The distances create a particular loneliness. Standing at one tower's base, you can see the next clearly—see its weeping walls, its lit windows, the slow rhythmic motion of whatever mechanisms operate inside—but the several hundred meters of wet stone between you feel like several hundred kilometers. Sound doesn't carry well in the saturated air. The industrial hum of each tower reaches only partway across the gap before dissipating, leaving the center of each empty zone in relative silence, just the tick and plink of water drops, the occasional groan of cooling metal.

Sometimes things cross those gaps.

Not often. You might watch for an hour and see nothing, then catch movement—something low and pale scuttling between the drainage channels, something that doesn't like the open space any more than you would. They look like engineering equipment that learned to be afraid: multi-jointed, ceramic-shelled, moving in bursts of speed between long frozen pauses. Whether they were made by the towers or emerged from the plateau's thermal depths, no one can say with certainty. They avoid each other. They avoid everything.

The towers themselves are alive with process. Not biological—mechanical, thermal, hydrological. Pipes run up their exteriors carrying heated water from below, carrying cooled water back down. Vents release steam in scheduled intervals, adding to the haze. Inside, glimpsed through fogged windows, things *work*: pistons cycling, wheels turning, fluids moving through transparent tubes. The purpose is unclear from outside. Production of something, transformation of something else. The towers have been doing this for long enough that the question of *why* has been smoothed away like the rounded edges of their drainage channels.

The whole plateau breathes. Slow thermal convection moves the haze in vast lazy currents, carrying the wet heat in unpredictable eddies. Sometimes a tower will be shrouded for hours, invisible to its neighbors; sometimes the air clears enough to see halfway across the settlement, a momentary vista of dark spires standing alone in amber emptiness, all of them sweating, all of them working, none of them touching.
