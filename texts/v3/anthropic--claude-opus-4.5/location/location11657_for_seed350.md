# The Drowning Engines

The flood came up thinking.

That's the only way to understand it—the water itself developed intentions. Not consciousness exactly, nothing so coherent as a mind, but *wants*. The ocean that swallowed the industrial lowlands began to desire things, to reach for them, to manufacture them from its own substance in endless churning production.

You are suspended in the warm murk at what was once the factory district's heart, where the water is thickest with purpose. The pressure here isn't crushing—the flood operates by different rules—but it presses against you with the insistence of a crowd, hundreds of soft bodies jostling for space. The water is dense with suspended particles that might be rust, might be pollen, might be something the flood is growing from nothing because it saw the shape once and cannot stop replicating it.

Light filters down from somewhere impossibly far above, amber and insufficient, scattered by the particulate density into a permanent golden twilight. You can see perhaps thirty feet in any direction before the murk becomes absolute. Within that sphere, everything moves.

---

The kelp forests rose within weeks of the submersion—that's what the surviving records claim. Not kelp, not really. The flood wanted vegetation, wanted the lush impossible gardens it found in images of tropical shallows, so it grew them. Massive fronds of something like seaweed spiral up from the factory floors, their surfaces textured with patterns that recall leaf venation but also circuit traces, also the grain of rusted steel. They're green and brown and copper-red, some of them twenty meters tall, swaying in currents that change direction every few seconds with no apparent cause.

Between and beneath them: a secondary growth of smaller plants, if plants is the word. Fern-analogues with fractal geometries that hurt to follow. Flowering structures that open and close arhythmically, their petals thick as leather, releasing clouds of spores or pollen or microscopic machinery—the distinction has ceased to matter. Creeping vines that colonize the old factory walls, growing through shattered windows, wrapping around support columns, incorporating industrial debris into their structure until you cannot tell where pipe ends and stem begins.

The vegetation is impossibly lush, layer upon layer of growth competing for whatever light reaches this depth. It should be lightless here. It should be dead. Instead the warm water teems with photosynthetic impossibilities, each plant generating enough of its own dim bioluminescence to feed its neighbors, the whole system bootstrapped into existence by the flood's insatiable want.

---

The factories remain, partially. The flood didn't destroy them so much as *adopt* them.

To your left, rising from the silt-choked floor, the scaffolding of what was once a textile mill. The building's walls are gone but the iron skeleton persists, festooned with vegetation, its beams bent into curves that follow no structural logic. The old machinery inside still moves—looms shuttle back and forth, drive shafts rotate, flywheels spin—but they're connected to nothing, powered by nothing visible. The flood wanted them to work, so they work. They produce no textiles. The motion is the point.

Further out, glimpsed through the murk, a smokestack rises toward the amber glow above. It's still smoking, impossibly, belching clouds of something darker than the surrounding water, something that disperses upward in roiling plumes. The smoke, too, is warm. The smoke has texture, almost presence. You have learned not to swim through it.

Scattered across the seafloor between the major structures: smaller artifacts. A workbench, its surface thick with barnacle-like growths that incorporate the tools left upon it. A bicycle frame, perfectly preserved, standing upright in the silt as if waiting for a rider. An entire section of brick wall, three stories tall, that simply *ends* at its edges, the bricks giving way to water with no rubble, no explanation. The wall is covered in the creeping vines. Something like moss glows faintly in the mortar gaps.

---

The sound is the hardest part.

It comes from everywhere, from the flood itself, from the water's constant agitation. The factories that still run produce a grinding, rhythmic industrial percussion—metal on metal, the complaint of gears forced to turn forever. The vegetation adds its own layer: the kelp-analogues rubbing against each other in the shifting currents, producing a deep groaning harmonic like whale song slowed down, like tectonic plates grinding. The water itself hums at frequencies you feel in your bones, a standing wave of pressure that rises and falls on no predictable schedule.

And beneath all of it, threading through the mechanical and organic noise: a sound like voices. Not words, not language, but the cadence of speech. The flood trying to remember something it heard once. Trying to reproduce conversation from first principles. The voices come from no single direction. They are everywhere. They say nothing. They never stop.

The combined assault is physical. It presses against your eardrums, fills your sinuses, vibrates your teeth. There are moments when individual sounds resolve—the clear ring of a hammer falling, the creak of a door swinging in currents—and these moments of clarity are somehow worse than the chaos. They suggest attention. They suggest the flood is trying to communicate, and failing, and trying again louder.

---

The weirdness operates on a gradient.

At the edges of your vision, where the murk begins to swallow details, reality is merely unstable. Objects flicker, occupying two positions at once. The kelp fronds bend in directions that don't correspond to any current you can feel. Light sources appear and vanish—the amber glow from above interrupted by pulses of deep blue, of sickly green, of colors you have no name for.

Closer, within the thirty-foot sphere of relative clarity, the breakdowns become specific. The flood is *trying* here, putting effort into consistency, and mostly succeeding, but not always. You watch a section of factory wall unspool itself into component bricks, the bricks hanging suspended in the water, then reassembling in a different configuration—the window has moved, the doorframe is wider, the pattern of moss has changed. This takes perhaps four seconds. The wall now has a chimney it didn't have before.

The floor is less reliable than it looks. The silt is deep in some places, solid stone in others, and the distinction shifts. Swimming paths that were clear minutes ago are now blocked by new vegetation, by factory walls that have extruded from nowhere, by sudden upwellings of water so dense with particulate matter that they might as well be solid. Other paths have opened—structures collapsed or retracted, the kelp parted by currents that serve intentions you cannot parse.

The flood is building and destroying continuously. The industrial instinct it absorbed from the factories has merged with something older, something tidal, and the result is a landscape that manufactures itself in real time. New plants sprout from the silt, growing to full height in minutes, then dissolving back into the murk to be replaced by something different. Machinery assembles from scattered components, runs for a while, disassembles. The walls of buildings creep outward, colonize new territory, then retreat.

---

There are no animals here. The flood wanted vegetation, wanted industry, wanted *presence*, but it did not want fauna. Perhaps it couldn't figure out the shape of them. Perhaps it found something wrong in the concept, some inconsistency it couldn't resolve.

The absence is louder than the presence would be. Among all this dense, layered, desperately generative chaos—no fish. No crustaceans. No microscopic swimmers in the particulate clouds. The water is crowded with growing things, with working things, with things that move, but nothing that *lives* in the animal sense. The plants are not alive either, not really, but they are closer. They are reaching toward life and not quite arriving.

You are the only animal here, and the flood knows it. The water presses against you with a curiosity that feels almost hungry. The kelp fronds turn toward your passing, tracking your movement with nothing so crude as eyes. The factory machines pause, briefly, when you draw near, as if listening. The voices beneath the noise seem to converge when you hold still, to circle around the shape of you, trying to learn what makes you different from everything else in this generative chaos.

You are a template the flood has not yet found the courage to copy.

---

In the distance—fifty meters? a hundred? scale is difficult in the murk—something massive is being born.

You can see the water roiling around it, clouds of particulate matter spiraling inward to a central construction. The amber light from above is brighter there, concentrated somehow, focused on the event. The vegetation bends toward it. The factory sounds have synced to a new rhythm, supporting whatever the flood is attempting.

It will be large. It will be strange. It will not be alive, but it will move, and want things, and perhaps the flood is finally learning enough to make something with eyes.

The water warms by another degree. The sound rises toward pain. The kelp forest trembles with anticipation.

You swim closer.
