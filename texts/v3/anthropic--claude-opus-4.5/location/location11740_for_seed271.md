# The Cistern Garden

The ceiling drips. It has always dripped, will always drip—the same drops falling from the same places into the same shallow pools, each impact a small sound in a space that holds small sounds carefully.

This was a cistern once, or something like one. The walls are old brick, curved inward overhead in a low dome maybe thirty feet across at its widest. Whoever built it built it deep: the floor sits well below the surface, reached by worn stone steps that descend from a square opening above. Dim light filters down through that opening, gray and constant, neither brightening nor fading. The light has no obvious source. It simply is, the way the dripping simply is.

The bricks have aged past redness into something closer to rust and char, their surfaces pocked and softened by centuries of mineral-laden moisture. White calcium deposits trace paths down the walls like frozen rivers, like the memory of water made solid. In places the brick has given way entirely, leaving dark gaps that lead nowhere—just pockets of earth, roots that probed through and then died, leaving behind hollow tubes that sometimes catch the dripping and redirect it into new channels.

The floor is covered in a thin film of water, perhaps a quarter inch deep, perfectly still except where the drops disturb it. The water is not quite clear—it carries a faint mineral cloudiness, a soft opacity that makes the submerged brick floor seem further away than it is. Here and there, the floor rises slightly, creating islands of damp brick that support small colonies of pale moss. The moss grows in tight cushions, gray-green and silver-green, barely alive by any vigorous standard but persisting nonetheless. It neither spreads nor retreats. It simply occupies its territory.

Three clay vessels sit against the far wall, large amphorae with broken handles and cracked lips. They've been here so long that calcium has begun to claim them too, painting their bases white where they meet the perpetual water. One has tipped onto its side; nothing spills from it. They are empty of everything except the same damp air that fills the rest of the space.

The warmth here is strange—not the warmth of sunlight or fire, but a kind of thermal steadiness, the temperature of deep earth asserting itself. The air sits heavy with moisture, not oppressive but present, coating the skin in a fine dew that neither dries nor accumulates. Breathing feels like drinking something very thin.

No insects circle the still pools. No frogs call from the shadows. No fish move beneath the clouded surface—there is not enough water for fish, not enough life for frogs, not enough movement to attract anything that feeds on movement. The cistern exists in a biological pause, populated only by the moss and by whatever invisible processes keep the moss alive.

The dripping follows a pattern. Three drops from the center of the dome, falling into the deepest part of the floor-pool in quick succession—*tap tap tap*—and then a longer silence. Then a single drop from somewhere to the left, a softer sound, landing on moss. Then nothing for what feels like thirty seconds but is probably closer to fifteen. Then the cycle begins again. The pattern has not changed in a very long time. It will not change.

The steps that lead down are worn in their centers, smoothed by feet that no longer come. At the top of the steps, where the light enters, a stone lintel shows marks that might once have been carved symbols, now weathered into suggestions. The stone has been rained on from above, wind-scoured in some earlier era when wind could reach it, and whatever meaning the marks held has been distributed back into the mineral anonymity from which it came.

There is a smell: wet stone and old clay and something faintly vegetal, the slow exhalation of moss performing its quiet chemistry. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of patience, of matter that has stopped trying to become anything else.

The walls hold the sound of dripping and give back only the faintest echo, the dome too low for drama, the space too small for resonance. Sound here is immediate and local, each drop speaking only to the water it strikes, the silence between drops thick and complete but not heavy. It is a comfortable silence, the silence of a place that has finished becoming itself and now simply continues.

The cistern breathes—if that word can apply to something so still—in the way that all enclosed damp spaces breathe, a slow exchange with the world above that maintains equilibrium without ever disturbing it. The moisture in the air is the moisture in the walls is the moisture in the pools, all one system, all balanced, all still.

At the edge of the largest pool, a single pale root has emerged from a gap in the brick, descended into the water, and stopped. It is not dead—some faint life persists in it—but it is not growing. It has reached the water and found it sufficient. There is nothing more it needs to do.
