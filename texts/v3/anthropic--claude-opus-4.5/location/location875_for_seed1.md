# The Weeping Engines

The first thing you understand is that the machines were never built.

They grew here, or they *are* here, in the way that ice is water in another grammar. Across this low basin—miles of it, extending toward horizons that refuse to stay fixed—the engines rise from wet black stone like mineral extrusions, like crystals that dreamed themselves into pistons and flywheels. They are ancient. They are ancient in the way that the word "ancient" is ancient. Whatever process shaped them finished so long ago that erosion has smoothed their housings into something like bone, like weathered basalt, like the faces of cliffs that have spent epochs learning to be nothing but themselves.

And they are running.

---

The sound fills the basin the way water fills a bowl: completely, from the bottom up. A grinding harmonic drone, punctuated by the arrhythmic percussion of valves that open onto nowhere and close on nothing. The engines have no purpose you can recognize. No conveyor belts feed them. No product emerges. They simply *operate*, wet and cold and patient, their movements powered by something that isn't combustion, isn't steam, isn't anything you can name. Perhaps they're powered by the accumulated pressure of all that time. Perhaps they've been running down for so long that their slowest gear-turn looks like stillness to mayfly observers.

Condensation weeps from every surface. The air is thick enough to chew—not fog exactly, but a kind of atmospheric saturation, as if the basin exists at the precise boundary where vapor surrenders to liquid. Water beads on the black stone, on the engine-housings, on your skin. It doesn't fall as rain; it simply *arrives*, materializing from potential into presence. The ground is perpetually slick, shallow water pooling in every depression, filling the spaces between the packed geometries of machine-growth until the whole basin becomes a single interrupted mirror, reflecting a sky that is the exact color of unpolished lead.

---

They live in the machines.

Not inside them—though some do nest in the cavities where gears meet, in the spaces where cam-shafts have worn gaps in their housings over incomprehensible time. But mostly they live *on* them, and *through* them, and in the interstices of their motion. The fauna of this place have adapted to a world where stillness doesn't exist, where every surface eventually moves, where the ground itself pulses with slow mechanical breath.

The creepers are the most visible: long-bodied, many-jointed, something like wet leather stretched over articulable stone. They flow across the engine surfaces in colonies, their movements synchronized to rhythms you almost but never quite catch. Symbiotes, maybe. Or parasites. Or simply organisms that learned to treat the machines as landscape, the way a lichen treats a boulder. They click and scrape as they move, adding their own percussion to the industrial choir.

Something larger calls from deeper in the basin—a sound like a pipe organ being played by fluid dynamics, resonant and wrong. You see its shadow move between two towering engine-columns, something with too many elbows, something that gleams with condensation-wet chitin or scale or neither. The fauna here do not look evolved. They look *derived*, as if something took the principle of "creature" and rebuilt it from industrial first principles. Gears turn in places where joints should be. Pistons seem to drive limbs that shouldn't need them. The boundary between organism and mechanism isn't blurred—it was never drawn.

---

The cold is patient. It doesn't bite; it *seeps*, rising from the wet stone, radiating from the machine-metal, settling into your bones the way sediment settles into still water. The light helps nothing. It's sourceless, directionless, the luminosity of permanent overcast filtered through air too thick to be fully transparent. Shadows don't fall here; they *spread*, like stains in cloth, their edges indefinite.

And the horizons—

You keep trying to map the basin's extent, to find its edges, to understand where this ends and something else begins. But perspective is broken here. Distances fold. You look at an engine-tower a mile away and it's suddenly *close*, not because you moved but because the space between you *forgot itself*. You look at your own feet and the ground seems to recede, the thin water covering the black stone suddenly depthless, containing entire darknesses. Something moves in that darkness. Something with eyes that are also valves. Something that is watching and also waiting and also possibly *calculating*, running its own slow program in the language of attention.

This is what happens, you realize, when matter stops distinguishing between grown and made. When age becomes deep enough to wear through the floor of meaning. The engines grew because something in the physics of this place doesn't recognize the boundary between crystal and factory, between geological formation and manufacturing plant. The creatures adapted because the engines were already here, or perhaps because the line between adapting and being-constructed is another boundary this place doesn't honor.

The weeping continues. The cold deepens. The machines turn, endlessly, their work—if it is work—unwitnessed except by the fauna that have made their peace with perpetual industry, and by the water that condenses everywhere, unable to escape, pooling in the basins of gears, flowing down channels worn smooth by impossible centuries.

You are standing at the bottom of something. The bottom of a process. The bottom of time. The bottom of the place where categories go to dissolve.

The engines do not care. The creatures do not notice. The water keeps weeping from an atmosphere that doesn't remember what dryness meant.

And somewhere, deep in the basin's packed impossibility, another machine begins to turn—one that hasn't moved in an age of ages, its gears finally finding whatever ancient pressure they needed to wake.
