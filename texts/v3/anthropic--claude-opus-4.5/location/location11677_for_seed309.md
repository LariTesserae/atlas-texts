# The Pit Gardens of the Vermitect

Descend the spiral ramp—rusted iron bolted into root-flesh, each step groaning under weight—and the light fails by degrees. What reaches the bottom is green-filtered, bruised, passing through forty meters of living ceiling before it touches anything solid. The Pit is perhaps two hundred meters across, roughly circular, though the walls have long since lost their original shape to the growth that colonizes them.

This was a mine shaft once. You can still find the bore-marks in exposed stone if you know where to look, geometric scars beneath the moss. The Company abandoned it three centuries ago when the seam ran dry, and the jungle above began its slow invasion downward. But people stayed. People always stay.

---

The floor is not soil. The floor is *Corpus matris*—the Mother Substrate, locals call her—a single fungal mat organism that spread across the pit bottom sometime in the first century of abandonment and never stopped growing. She is cream-colored where exposed, faintly luminescent, warm to the touch. Her surface undulates in gentle rises and depressions, breathing perhaps, or digesting. Everything here grows from her or has reached an accommodation with her presence.

The buildings rise like fever-dreams of architecture. Frames of salvaged industrial steel, belt-housings and gear-casings repurposed as structural elements, but skinned entirely in living tissue. Walls of trained vine-growth, thick as a man's thigh, woven tight and sealed with resin. Roofs of broad-leaf canopy encouraged to spread flat, layered like shingles. Some structures are purely grown—hollow gourds the size of houses, their interiors carved out and fitted with hammocks and workbenches. Others are hybrid things, engines still chugging away inside cocoons of greenery, exhaust venting through living chimneys that have adapted to filter the smoke into something the ceiling-forest craves.

Sixty, perhaps seventy people live here. Their homes scatter across the Mother Substrate with deliberate spacing—pathways of beaten fungal flesh between them, each dwelling surrounded by its own small territory of cultivation. The sparse arrangement is not aesthetic preference. The fauna require room to move.

---

*The fauna.*

Begin with the obvious: the ceiling writhes. What you mistook for wind-motion in the canopy above is locomotion. Thousands of bodies move through the root-tangle that forms the pit's living lid—serpentine things with too many legs, furred spheres that roll along vine-highways, flat ribbon-creatures that flow like water through gaps in the growth. Their sounds layer into a constant drone, a white-noise ocean of chirps and clicks and deep thrumming calls that your bones feel before your ears parse.

The walls host their own nations. Beetle-analogues the size of dogs traverse the vertical surfaces on adhesive feet, tending gardens of shelf-fungus, their carapaces marked with paint-symbols the pit-dwellers use to track lineage and territory. They are not domesticated—nothing here is domesticated—but arrangements exist. The pit-dwellers leave offerings at certain wall-hollows. The beetles do not enter certain structures. Everyone pretends this is coincidence.

On the floor, where the Mother Substrate spreads her territory, larger things move. You will see them in the dim: shapes like ambulatory root-systems, seven-legged and patient, picking their way between buildings with the careful deliberation of the very old. The pit-dwellers call them Grandfathers. They are not aggressive unless crowded. They eat what the Mother offers them—fruiting bodies that swell from her surface like blisters, which the Grandfathers graze with rasping mouth-parts that sound like distant sawmills.

And in the air—the pit has its own weather, its own atmosphere distinct from the world above—things fly that should not fly. Membranous, translucent, trailing bioluminescent filaments that paint brief constellations in the gloom. They hunt the smaller canopy-dwellers, diving upward into the root-mass and returning with prey clutched in appendages that seem too delicate for the violence they inflict.

The sound never stops. It modulates—quieter in the pit's approximation of night, when even the Mother Substrate dims her glow, louder in the humid mornings when condensation drips from the ceiling in a false rain that triggers feeding frenzies above. But silence does not exist here. The ecosystem is too dense, too layered, too *active* for silence.

---

Industry persists. That is the strange miracle of this place—humanity did not retreat in the face of overwhelming life, but *integrated*. Near the pit's north curve stands the processing station, a structure of corrugated iron and grown-over scaffolding, three stories of machinery that takes the Mother Substrate's excess fruiting bodies and renders them into fuel, lubricant, building material, food. The engine at its heart is pre-Abandonment, a compression-combustion beast that should have died two hundred years ago, but the pit-dwellers have replaced its seals with secretion-polymers, its filters with living membrane, its fuel-lines with hollow vine-tubes. It runs on rendered fungus and exhales something the canopy converts to oxygen. The sound it makes is a heartbeat, irregular but persistent, underlying everything.

Other machines dot the settlement: water-collectors that harvest ceiling-drip into cisterns lined with antimicrobial moss. Lift-platforms that haul goods up the spiral ramp, driven by counterweights and beetle-labor. Communication systems of tensioned root-fiber that carry vibrations from one end of the pit to the other, a telegraph network grown rather than built.

The pit-dwellers themselves have adapted. They move carefully, aware always of what shares their space. They speak in a pidgin that incorporates animal-calls, sound-signals that distinguish them from prey or threat. Their skin carries symbiotic colonies that the Grandfathers recognize as *kin-marked*, not-food. They do not rule this place. They do not even occupy it in the usual sense. They *participate* in it, one species among hundreds, neither predator nor prey but something outside those categories—gardeners, perhaps, or maintenance workers, keeping the old machines running while the ecosystem flows around them.

---

The light is always failing here, always green and gold and dim. The moisture rises from the Mother Substrate in a constant exhalation, condensing on metal surfaces, beading on leaves, dripping back down in cycles that never quite qualify as rain. The warmth is blood-warmth, body-warmth, the heat of ten thousand metabolisms overlapping in a space too small to contain them.

If you stand still long enough, something will investigate you. If you stand still longer, something will begin to grow on you. This is not malice. This is simply how the pit works—nothing is wasted, nothing is singular, nothing exists in isolation. The boundaries between architecture and organism, between settlement and ecosystem, between human and environment, have worn away over three centuries of cohabitation, eroded into something that no longer admits easy categories.

The pit-dwellers say the Mother Substrate dreams, and that the dreams are everyone's. They say the Grandfathers remember the time before the Abandonment, when the pit was wound and noise and nothing lived. They say the ceiling-forest is slowly descending, year by year, and that someday the pit will close like a healing wound, and everything inside will become undifferentiated jungle.

They do not seem concerned by this. They check their machines, tend their vine-walls, leave their offerings, and listen to the endless sound of life living around them, through them, despite them.

The pit does not need them. But it has made room for them anyway.
