# The Rooted Engines of Veth Ascendant

The city grew upward from a plateau that had been old when the first mountains were young—a tableland worn so smooth by deep time that it had become almost theoretical, a geometric abstraction of stone barely distinguishable from the living mass that consumed it. Here, at elevation where the air thinned and clouds passed below like slow rivers, an entire civilization had been built not *on* the vegetation but *within* it, *through* it, *as* it.

The trees—if that word still applied—rose three hundred meters and more, their trunks wider than city blocks, their bark not bark at all but something between wood and stone and flesh, grown so dense over millennia that it had developed its own geology. The Veth called them the *Pillar Mothers*, though they were neither pillars nor mothers in any conventional sense. They were the city itself. Their branches interlocked overhead in a canopy so total that the sky existed only as rumor, glimpsed through gaps that opened and closed with the slow breathing of growth. Their root systems plunged through the ancient plateau and emerged somewhere below the cloudline, drinking from aquifers that condensed out of the very air.

Between these living towers, the Veth had built their infrastructure—not against the plants but in negotiation with them. Bridges of woven fiber thick as ship's cables connected trunk to trunk, strung with the copper and iron of industrial ambition. Steam pipes ran along branch-highways, their condensation feeding the endless small gardens that sprouted from every surface. Factories clung to the middle heights like shelf fungi, their smokestacks threading up through gaps in the canopy, their machinery powered by generators that burned the endless fall of dead leaves, of shed bark, of the vegetable matter that the Pillar Mothers discarded in quantities sufficient to fuel a civilization.

The sound was immense—a layered roar that never ceased. The factories contributed their mechanical thunder: piston-rhythms and boiler-sighs, the shriek of saws processing the carefully harvested secondary growth, the grinding of mills that turned plant-matter into paper, into fuel, into the thousand products of botanical industry. But beneath that ran the deeper sound of the city-forest itself. The Pillar Mothers grew audibly. Their wood creaked and groaned with expansion. Sap moved through channels wide enough to echo. And when the wind rose—a wind that moved through the canopy like tide through kelp—the entire city swayed and sang, every branch a resonating chamber, every leaf a small voice in a chorus of millions.

The spaces between these sounds were what made Veth strange.

Because something had happened here. Something was still happening. The ancient plateau, worn down to its final form, had achieved a kind of stability that attracted instability—as if the universe, finding a place that had stopped changing, felt compelled to remind it that change was mandatory. The Veth called it the *Seeping*, and it manifested in gaps.

Not physical gaps. Conceptual ones.

You would walk along a branch-road, the fiber-cables humming with the passage of cargo-lifts, and suddenly find yourself walking along the *memory* of the branch-road, a version that existed only in the accumulated experience of everyone who had ever walked it. The factory smoke would part to reveal not the canopy but an *idea* of the canopy, a pure concept of green-coverage that had no individual leaves, only the essence of foliage. Sound would drop away—not into silence but into the *potential* for sound, a pregnant acoustic space where every possible noise existed in superposition until you listened and collapsed it into something specific.

The Veth had adapted. Their city was built to account for the Seeping, with anchor-points of extreme sensory intensity—bells that rang on the hour, braziers that burned aromatic resins, painted walls of violent color—anything to keep reality stitched to its expected pattern. Their factories ran loud and obvious partly from necessity, partly as existential assertion: *this machine is real, this production is happening, this smoke rises and this noise exists*.

The moisture came from the clouds that passed through and below, from the condensation that formed on every cool surface in the pre-dawn hours, from the occasional soft rains that fell *upward* from the cloud-sea below, carrying water that had evaporated from lowland rivers into the cool heights where it re-materialized. The Pillar Mothers drank it all. Every drop that touched the city was claimed eventually, filtered through root and trunk, emerging as sap, as moisture on leaf-edges, as the slow weeping of cut bark that the Veth collected for medicine and lamp-oil.

Few animals survived here. The vegetation had grown so total, so all-consuming, that it had absorbed the roles that animals usually filled. Pollination happened through wind and through the Veth themselves, who carried pollen on their clothes and breath. Seed dispersal happened through the industrial processes—factories that processed plant-matter inevitably spread viable seeds in their waste streams. Even decomposition was handled by specialized fungal networks that were, technically, still part of the plant-mass, symbiotic partners so integrated they had lost any independent identity.

What animals remained were small and strange: beetles with shells of living bark, moths that pollinated only during reality-gaps when flowers bloomed in conceptual space, blind fish that swam through the sap-channels and were harvested by the Veth for protein in a diet otherwise entirely vegetable. Birds had existed once—the old records mentioned them—but they had disappeared generations ago, possibly into the Seeping, possibly simply outcompeted by a forest that had learned to do everything they did.

The city sprawled across the plateau-top in a pattern that made sense only from above: a rough circle of Pillar Mothers, connected by their branch-infrastructure, surrounding a central space where no trees grew. The Veth called this the *Wound*, and they did not build there. It was a kilometer across, a perfect circle of exposed ancient stone, and it was the source of the Seeping. Something had struck there, long ago—long enough ago that the event pre-dated the Pillar Mothers themselves, which were themselves older than recorded history. The stone at the Wound's center was glassy, fused, wrong in ways that defied specific description. Light fell on it normally but did not leave normally. Sound entered it and emerged changed, shifted into adjacent frequencies. The Pillar Mothers' roots grew toward it and stopped at its edge, unable or unwilling to proceed.

From the residential platforms that ringed the Wound at safe distance, you could watch the reality-failures migrate outward like ripples in still water—visible as moments when the ancient stone flickered between states, when the exposed plateau showed briefly the mountains it had been before erosion claimed them, when the glassy center reflected not the sky (which was canopy) but some other sky entirely, violet and ribboned with unfamiliar stars.

The Veth worked their industries and raised their children and maintained their anchor-points and lived around the wrongness as people always live around wrongness: by incorporating it into routine. The morning bell that rang from the central factory tower was the signal to check the anchor-braziers. The evening whistle that announced shift-change was the cue to renew the painted walls, to keep the colors bright. The constant mechanical noise was a blessing as much as a burden, a sonic fence against the silence that seeped from the Wound.

When the leaf-fall came—a seasonal process that buried the lower platforms in a meter of dead foliage every three months—the entire city mobilized to process it. For those weeks, Veth Ascendant was nothing but industry: the howl of shredders, the hiss of digesters, the productive transformation of death into fuel, into fiber, into the raw material that kept the factories running. The smoke rose and the canopy creaked and the sound was so loud, so gloriously present, that even the Wound seemed to quiet, as if the assertion of *reality* through *noise* was somehow effective.

And always, above and around, the Pillar Mothers continued their ancient growth—adding a ring to their impossible trunks, extending a branch another meter into the endless green, drinking the cloud-moisture and shedding their surplus mass and singing their slow wooden song into the cool thin air of the heights, indifferent to the wrongness at their roots, patient as only something that old and that total can be patient, growing toward a completion that might take another thousand years or might already be finished, depending on how you defined the terms.
