# The Root Warrens of Kethvel

Beneath the permafrost, where no melt has touched in living memory, the city grows downward into itself.

You enter through a frost-crack barely wider than your shoulders, descending hand over hand along a braided ladder of pale rootstock. The cold is absolute here—a stillness that settles into bone—but the roots themselves pulse with something like warmth, a metabolic patience that has nothing to do with fire or sun. They are white as old paper, thick as human torsos where they cluster, branching into capillary networks so fine they blur into mist at their edges. And they are everywhere.

The warrens are carved into the gaps between root-masses, spaces hollowed out where the pale tendrils chose not to grow. Or perhaps the roots arranged themselves around these absences from the beginning—no one can say which came first, the hollow or the rootwork that frames it. The chambers connect through passages that spiral and recurve, following the logic of growth rather than architecture. You learn to navigate by texture: this junction where three roots have fused into a ridged column, that alcove where a root-tip died and left its impression like a wax seal in the substrate.

The substrate itself is not quite stone. It has the density of stone, the coldness, but it yields slightly under pressure and springs back when released. Pale grey shot through with darker veining, it smells of nothing at all—which is itself strange, in a place so dense with organic presence. The roots push through it without cracking it, without leaving seams. They belong to each other, root and medium, in some way that predates the arrival of people.

---

The people of Kethvel—though they do not call themselves that, do not call themselves anything you've ever heard a people call themselves—have been shaping these warrens for twelve generations. Their tools are bone and rootite, a material they derive from deceased root-segments through processes they consider sacred and will not explain. It takes on a glassy hardness while remaining light enough to carry comfortably. They have not discovered metalwork, and when asked about it, they look puzzled. Why would they need metal? The roots provide.

Every dwelling is a negotiation. Families maintain relationships with particular root-clusters the way surface peoples maintain relationships with trees or wells. They speak to them—not in the manner of prayer, but in the manner of neighbors discussing a shared fence line. The roots do not respond in words, but over years, over decades, they shift. They open new passages and seal old ones. They fruit, sometimes, producing pale nodules that the Kethveli process into a starchy meal with their grinding-stones. They withdraw from spaces that have been neglected, and the substrate there hardens, becomes brittle, eventually crumbles into a fine grey powder that the wind-that-is-not-wind carries away through channels too small for people to follow.

The wind-that-is-not-wind is one of the strangnesses here. Air moves through the warrens with no apparent source, maintaining breathability, carrying sounds from distant chambers with selective fidelity. Sometimes you hear a conversation from three levels down as clearly as if it were beside you. Sometimes a shout from the next room arrives as a whisper, delayed by heartbeats. The Kethveli have built their social structures around this acoustic strangeness—secrets are kept not by silence but by speaking in certain chambers whose words go nowhere, while public announcements are made from nodes where sound carries unpredictably far.

---

In the deeper warrens, where the density becomes almost oppressive—root-walls so close you must turn sideways, chambers that hold perhaps three people at a time—you begin to see the emergence.

New roots are forming. You can watch it happen if you're patient, if you're willing to sit in the cold for hours, breathing the still air and waiting. The substrate softens in patches, takes on a translucent quality, and then—not bursting, not pushing, but *arriving*—a pale filament extends into the space. It thickens over days. Over months it will become a true root, will fuse with its neighbors, will reshape the geometry of the warrens in ways the Kethveli will incorporate into their mental maps without comment.

This is why the warrens feel under construction despite their age. They are always becoming. Always in a state of just-beginning. The oldest chambers are perhaps a century settled, but the roots remember no age, know no completion. The city elaborates itself with vegetable patience, and the people shape themselves to its elaboration.

---

Light comes from the root-tips. Not all of them, and not constantly—it moves through the network in slow waves, a luminescence that takes most of a day to travel from the upper warrens to the depths and back. The Kethveli time their activities by these waves: the brightening is for work, the dimming is for rest, the complete darkness that sometimes falls—rarely, unpredictably—is for the ceremonies they will not discuss with outsiders.

During normal light, you can see the full strangeness of the space. Roots arch overhead in cathedral formations, their surfaces textured with fine ridges that might be growth-rings or might be something else. Where they intersect, they sometimes form shapes that suggest intention—curves that frame doorways, spirals that create natural shelving, lattices that filter light into patterns. The Kethveli decorate these natural formations with carvings, adding to them in styles that make it difficult to distinguish what the root created from what human hands contributed.

Their carvings do not depict people or animals. They are all abstract: nested circles, branching lines, patterns that repeat at different scales. When you ask what they represent, the carvers look at you with an expression you cannot read. *They don't represent*, one of them says finally. *They continue*.

---

Fauna is sparse here. Small pale creatures move through the gaps between roots—something like millipedes, something like worms, segmented and blind and silent. They process the dead root-material, the crumbled substrate, the waste that the Kethveli are careful to deposit in designated chambers. They are not domesticated, exactly, but they are understood. Children know not to disturb them. Their presence in a dwelling is considered healthy—their absence, a sign of imbalance.

Once a generation, something larger moves through the deepest passages. The Kethveli speak of it obliquely: the Pale Walker, the Long Guest, the One Who Passes. It leaves traces—smoothed walls, reshaped junctions, chambers that weren't there before. No one has seen it directly. No one seeks to. It is part of the system, part of the slow becoming that defines this place.

---

The quiet here is layered. There is the silence of absent water, absolute. There is the near-silence of the roots themselves, which creak sometimes, groan sometimes, but mostly hold their tension without sound. There is the murmur of Kethveli life: conversations conducted in low voices, the scrape of rootite tools against substrate, the soft footfalls of people navigating their brachiating city. And beneath it all, felt more than heard, a kind of hum that might be the collective metabolism of the root-network, or might be the vibration of the deep cold itself, or might be nothing at all but the ear straining against too much quiet.

You adapt to it. After a few days, surface sounds would seem violent, intrusive. The warrens teach patience not through instruction but through immersion—everything here happens slowly, and you slow to match it, and in the slowing you begin to perceive processes you would have missed in the tempo of the upper world.

---

The cold never relents. The Kethveli wear layers of woven root-fiber, sleep in nests of the same material, press close to each other in the deeper chambers where the root-walls hold some fraction of metabolic warmth. They do not long for fire—fire would change the air, change the roots, change everything. The cold is part of the system. The cold preserves. The cold maintains the slowness that allows root and person and substrate and wind-that-is-not-wind to exist in their patient negotiation.

When you finally leave, climbing hand over hand up the braided ladder toward the frost-crack and the stark white surface above, you feel the warmth of upper air as a kind of violence. The roots end abruptly at the permafrost ceiling, their pale tips pressed against the frozen boundary, growing no further.

But they are growing. Imperceptibly. The city extends upward by a finger's width each century, and in time—time measured in generations of Kethveli, in hundreds of those slow light-waves, in the gradual replacement of entire root-networks—in time, perhaps, it will breach the surface.

What it will become then, no one can say. The roots know, perhaps. They are patient enough to find out.
