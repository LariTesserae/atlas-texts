# The Hollow Engine

You enter through what was once a valve—rusted iron petals frozen mid-spiral, each the size of a door. Beyond it, the factory opens downward into itself, and you understand immediately that something has gone wrong with the relationship between inside and outside, between machine and flesh.

The space is a single vast chamber, perhaps two hundred feet across, sunk into marshy lowland. But *vast* is the wrong word, because the chamber is almost entirely full—choked with growth that has replaced the factory's original purpose with its own. Vines thick as industrial piping climb the interior walls. Moss colonies have consumed entire catwalks, transforming them into hanging gardens that drip with condensation. Ferns erupt from every seam where metal plates once joined, their fronds unfurling in the perpetual twilight.

And yet—and this is the strangeness—the interior *feels* empty. Cavernously, achingly hollow. You could walk for what seems like miles between one breathing thing and the next, though the distance is only feet. Space has become unreliable here. The geometry folds in ways that shouldn't accommodate the density of green life you can see with your eyes. You reach toward a cluster of shelf fungus on a corroded beam, and your hand travels through nothing for longer than it should, arriving eventually at a surface that seems surprised to be touched.

---

The factory was built for something. The great turbines still exist—three of them, arranged in a triangle at the chamber's sunken center, each one a cathedral of gears and pistons standing forty feet tall. But the turbines have been *replaced* cell by cell, the way a ship is replaced plank by plank until nothing original remains. Where there were iron teeth, there are now interlocking woody growths that mesh and grind with vegetable patience. Where there were steam chambers, there are now bulbous structures like enormous seed pods, their skins translucent enough to show dark shapes moving within. The turbines still turn—slowly, so slowly you might miss it—driven by no fuel you can identify.

The animals live in the turbines.

They pour from the seed-chambers in cycles you cannot predict: sleek things like wet shadows, creatures with too many limbs that fold in dimensions the eye slides off, small furred bodies that phase between solid and suggestion. They emerge, they traverse the living machinery in complex patterns, they return or they don't. The turbines seem to *produce* them, though whether the animals are fuel or product or simply inhabitants of the mechanism, you cannot say.

The light here comes from bioluminescent growths scattered across the upper reaches—pale blue-green patches that pulse with no discernible rhythm, providing just enough illumination to make the darkness feel intentional. In their glow, you can see that the factory's original infrastructure survives in fragments: a control panel fused to a tree trunk, its gauges still twitching; a conveyor belt that has become a horizontal trellis for climbing vines; a crane arm reaching from the shadows, its hook replaced by a pendulous flower that drips some slow nectar into the abyss below.

---

Sound enters the space reluctantly. Your footsteps on the plant-carpeted floor make almost no noise—the moss absorbs impact, the ferns cushion your weight, the factory's original metal flooring has been composted into something soft and yielding. Occasionally you hear the turbines: a deep, grinding exhalation, the creak of wooden gears engaging, a wet *pop* as another creature emerges from gestation. Water drips somewhere in the permanent distance. Something moves through the hanging gardens above you with a rustle that takes too long to fade.

The air is cool and damp, carrying the smell of mulch and rust and something sweeter underneath—fermentation, perhaps, or the breath of the seed-chambers. Condensation beads on every surface, including your skin. The moisture seems to come from everywhere and nowhere: no rain falls, no streams flow, but the wetness persists, as if the factory is sweating, or breathing, or slowly digesting the humidity of the marsh it was built upon.

---

The laws here are still forming. You notice this in small ways at first: a dropped object falls too slowly, then too fast, then normally; shadows point in directions unrelated to light sources; the vine you brushed against a moment ago is now in a different location, though you never saw it move. The animals seem unbothered by these inconsistencies—they have adapted to instability, learned to flow through the fluctuating rules like water finding its level.

The factory is young, geologically speaking. The marsh beneath it is barely settled, the stone still soft in places, the industrial foundation sinking incrementally into substrate that hasn't decided what it wants to become. And the thing that has replaced the factory—the living engine, the hollow machine—is younger still. It is still learning what it is. The turbines experiment with new configurations. The creatures adapt and re-adapt. The geometry tests its boundaries, pushing against the edges of what space can contain.

You are standing inside something being born. The process is quiet, active, and utterly unconcerned with your presence. The hollow engine will continue its becoming whether you witness it or not. But for now, in this moment, you are part of its ecology: another moving thing in the vast empty fullness, another variable in the calculation of what this place might eventually mean.

Above you, in the darkness near the ceiling, something large shifts its weight among the hanging gardens. The motion disturbs a cloud of small winged creatures—not quite insects, not quite birds—that scatter in silence before reforming into a shape that resembles, briefly and impossibly, the factory's original floor plan.

Then they disperse, and the hollow engine continues its slow revolution, grinding toward a purpose that hasn't been invented yet.
