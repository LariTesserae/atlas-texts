# The Nursery of the Moth-Shepherds

The settlement clings to a shelf of pale volcanic rock, barely a hundred paces across, jutting from the shoulder of a young mountain still rough with the scars of its own emergence. Sharp ridgelines above catch clouds and tear them into ribbons of mist that drift down through the hours, condensing on every surface, beading on the underside of leaves, gathering in the shallow stone basins the inhabitants have carved into the natural contours. There is no river here, no stream, only this slow breathing of moisture from the air itself, a dampness that comes and goes with the mountain's moods.

Yet from this intermittent wetness, lushness pours.

The shelf is enclosed—almost cradled—by walls of stone that rise on three sides, their surfaces colonized by cascading ferns, their crevices thick with moss that glows a faint bioluminescent blue when the mist thickens. Epiphytes hang from the few twisted trees that have found purchase here, their roots drinking fog, their leaves broad and cupped to catch condensation. The vegetation grows dense but selective, following the paths of moisture, leaving open spaces of bare stone between. Walking through feels like moving between rooms in a garden designed by something not quite human, each pocket of green separated from the next by thresholds of rock still sharp-edged, still young.

The cold sits in the bones of this place. Not bitter, not biting, but persistent—a cool that seeps through wool and skin and settles in the joints. Breath fogs in the mornings. The stone never fully warms.

---

And everywhere, in the dim gray light that filters through the perpetual haze, the moths.

They are not like the moths of lower lands. Some are small enough to mistake for drifting motes of ash. Others spread wings wider than outstretched arms, moving with a slowness that makes the air feel thick. They cover the stone walls in overlapping layers, wings folded, breathing imperceptibly. They cluster on the undersides of the carved basins, drinking the water that collects there. They drift between the fern-grottoes in loose spiraling clouds, and when the mist rolls in heavy, they rise in columns, thousands upon thousands, their wing-beats too soft to hear but somehow felt, a pressure in the skull, a hum below hearing.

The people here call themselves *kerathin*—a word that means something like "those who sleep standing." There are perhaps thirty of them on the shelf, living in structures that are half-carved into the stone and half-built from woven plant-fiber frames stretched across the gaps. Their homes have no doors, only hanging curtains of dried moss that the moths pass through freely. Inside, the walls are alive with resting wings.

The kerathin do not speak above a whisper. They have learned, over generations, that loud sounds agitate the swarms, send them spiraling in confusion, disrupt the delicate choreography of breeding and feeding and migration that sustains the settlement. Their tools are stone and bone and hardened resin, shaped by patient hands. Their craft is the reading of moth-behavior: which species' larvae produce the finest silk, which arrival signals coming rain, which patterns of flight warn of rockslides from the unstable peaks above.

---

Because the mountain is still becoming. You can feel it in the sharp-edged joints between stone faces, the fresh fractures where slabs have recently sheered away. Once in a dozen days, sometimes more often, a tremor passes through the shelf—not violent, but definite, a reminder that this place is young, still settling into itself. The kerathin mark each tremor with a small pile of stones in the central plaza, building a slow record of the mountain's restlessness.

The fauna does not end with moths. Pale salamanders slip through the moss-forests, their bodies translucent enough to see the slow pulse of organs within. Something larger moves in the vertical fern-jungles above the settlement—glimpsed only as a shadow crossing a gap, heard only as a faint disturbance of leaves. The kerathin call it *the witness* and leave offerings of moth-pupae at the base of the cliff walls. Whether it is predator or guardian or something else entirely, they cannot say.

And in the deepest moss-grottoes, where the blue glow is strongest, there are structures that the kerathin did not build. Stone arrangements too precise for erosion, too weathered for any living memory. Moth-cocoons hang from them in clusters like strange fruit. Something was here before. Something understood this place differently.

The silence here is not empty. It is full of wing-rustle, of condensation dripping, of the soft footfalls of careful people moving through a world where attention is survival, where the great tapestry of small lives requires a patience that has become indistinguishable from reverence.
