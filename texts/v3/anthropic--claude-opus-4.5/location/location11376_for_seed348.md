# The Sorting Yards of Chitin Mill

The sound reaches you first—a continuous rattling hum, like ten thousand dry seeds shaken in a gourd the size of a warehouse. Then the clicking, the scraping, the whistle of segmented legs against metal grating. The noise never changes pitch or intensity. It has been exactly this loud for longer than anyone can calculate.

---

You stand at the edge of a low basin, perhaps three hundred meters across, where an industrial complex has grown into—or perhaps always was—a single organism of rust-brown iron and living shells. The buildings are squat and utilitarian: sorting sheds with corrugated roofs, conveyor housings, chutes and hoppers and loading bays. Steam rises from vents in thin, constant plumes that neither strengthen nor fade. Condensation beads on every metal surface, pools in the channels between flagstones, drips from the undersides of walkways in a perpetual slow rain that never accumulates and never dries.

But the structures are almost incidental. What moves through them, over them, beneath them—what *is* the place in every meaningful sense—is the fauna.

---

They are beetles, or something that evolved parallel to beetles, or something that preceded beetles and will outlast them. Their carapaces range from thumb-sized to the dimensions of draft horses. The smaller ones flow in rivers along designated channels cut into the stone floor, their bodies a clicking carpet that parts around your feet with mechanical precision. The larger ones haul loads along overhead rails, their legs hooked into guide-tracks, their shells fitted with iron brackets and numbered tags that have worn to illegibility over centuries of use.

None of them are alive in the way you understand life.

They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They do not die. Each one has been performing its specific task—carrying, sorting, climbing, waiting—since before the condensation first formed on these walls. Their movements are not autonomous; they respond to stimuli, to pressure plates and chutes and the presence of specific materials, but they do not vary their responses. A beetle that carries slag from Hopper Seven to Furnace Three has always carried slag from Hopper Seven to Furnace Three. It will do so until the heat death of causality.

The overwhelming weight of their presence is not in their numbers, though the numbers are staggering—easily millions within this basin alone, layered three and four deep in the processing trenches, clustered on every vertical surface like barnacles of chitin. It is in the *completeness* of their occupation. There is no surface, no corner, no shadow that does not contain some carapaced form performing some task you cannot identify. You realize that what you mistook for machinery—the conveyors, the sorting arms, the steam-valves—are also beetles, beetles that have grown into their functions, their bodies shaped around the work they do, their shells extending into pipes and gears and levers.

---

The ground beneath your feet is smooth basalt, worn to a dull polish by the endless passage of chitinous feet. No plant has ever grown here. The stone is too dense, the chemistry too alkaline, the light too filtered through the perpetual steam-haze to allow for photosynthesis. The beetles do not require oxygen in the way that would demand vegetation. They are powered by something else—geothermal vents, perhaps, or some crystalline metabolism that draws energy from the mineral substrate itself. Small pools of condensation gather in depressions, reflecting the grey-amber light, and in these pools you see the smallest beetles—no larger than rice grains—walking calmly across the surface tension, their legs dimpling the water without breaking through.

The air is cool and damp, carrying the smell of wet iron and something faintly sulfurous, the exhalations of deep forges that have burned at the same temperature for eons. The light comes from panels set into the walls of the larger structures—not electric, not flame, but some bioluminescence cultivated in the shells of specialized beetles pressed flat against glass housings. They glow with the steady amber of afternoon sunlight filtered through clouds, never brightening, never dimming.

---

There is a strange peace here, despite the noise. The clicking and rattling has no variation, no surprise. Your ears adjust to it as they would adjust to a waterfall, finding the silence beneath the sound. The beetles flow around you without curiosity or fear. You are not a threat. You are not anything to them. You are simply an obstacle with a particular shape, to be routed around according to protocols established before your species existed.

You understand, standing here, that this place was not built. It emerged. The beetles were first—were always—and the structures grew up around their patterns like coral around current. The sorting sheds exist because the beetles needed to sort. The furnaces exist because the beetles needed to smelt. What they sort, what they smelt, what purpose any of this serves—that knowledge has been lost, or was never known, or is the wrong question entirely.

The work is the purpose. The purpose is the work.

And it continues, and continues, and continues, as the condensation drips and the steam rises and the million million legs click against stone and iron in their eternal, unchanging industry.
