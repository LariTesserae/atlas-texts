# The Breathing Ranges

The mountains here do not simply exist—they metabolize.

You stand on a ridge of dark volcanic glass, worn smooth by millennia into something almost comfortable underfoot. The cold is immediate and penetrating, the kind that settles into joints and stays, but the air itself feels alive against your skin—humid despite the altitude, carrying a faint mineral wetness that coats the inside of your lungs. Below you, and above you, and extending in all directions until the darkness swallows distance, the peaks rise and fall like the vertebrae of some continent-sized creature caught mid-breath.

There is almost nothing here. That is the first true thing about this place. The emptiness is architectural, deliberate-seeming—vast cirques and hanging valleys holding only shadow and the occasional gleam of ice melt pooling in depressions. No structures. No trails. No cairns marking the way. And yet the vegetation that does exist grows with an almost aggressive lushness, as if compensating for its isolation.

---

The plants cling to the mountain flanks in thick, ropy mats—bioluminescent in ways that shouldn't work at these temperatures, giving off a dim blue-green phosphorescence that provides the only light in this perpetual twilight. You cannot see the sky clearly; something up there diffuses what little illumination reaches this place, turning everything into gradients of deep purple and charcoal. The glow-moss (you have no better name for it) spreads in arterial patterns along the cliff faces, following seams of moisture that weep from the rock itself. In the deepest cold, it contracts, pulls inward, dims. When the intermittent warm fronts roll through—and they do, every few hours, arriving with a whisper of humid air that feels almost tropical by comparison—the moss expands, brightens, exhales something that smells like copper and crushed herbs.

The moss is not the strangest vegetation. In the valleys between peaks, where meltwater collects in shallow temporary lakes, forests of something like kelp grow upward through the air. They have no water to suspend them, yet they sway as if submerged, their fronds reaching thirty or forty feet toward the invisible sky. Their surfaces glisten with condensation. Things move among them.

---

The fauna here is abundant in the way that nightmares are abundant—not crowded, but *present*, felt even when unseen. You hear them rarely: a distant clicking that might be echolocation, a low subsonic thrum that you feel more in your chest than your ears. Mostly there is silence, the kind of silence that is actually thousands of small sounds too quiet to distinguish individually, layering into a texture of almost-nothing.

But the animals are real. You catch glimpses. Something pale and many-jointed scaling a cliff face with mechanical precision, each leg finding purchase with an audible *tick* against the glass-stone. Flocks of creatures that might be birds if birds were flat and membranous and moved by rippling their entire bodies, drifting on thermals that rise from vents in the mountainsides. In the kelp-forests, larger shapes—you see them only as shadows against the moss-glow, moving with the slow deliberation of deep-sea things.

They do not approach you. Not yet. But you feel observed.

---

The mountains themselves are ancient and settled into their current form, but nothing here is still. The thermal cycles drive everything—warm fronts arriving, triggering blooms of activity, then cold returning and the whole ecosystem contracting, dimming, slowing. The wet air condenses on cold stone, trickles downward, feeds the moss, which exhales moisture back into the air, which condenses again. Water moves through this system like blood through capillaries, never pooling long, always in transit.

And beneath it all, barely perceptible: a rhythm. A pulse. Every few minutes, the ground vibrates slightly, as if something enormous far below is cycling through some biological process. The mountains themselves seem to inhale, hold, exhale. The kelp-forests sway in time with it. The clicking fauna pause, wait, resume.

Here is the strangeness you cannot explain: the vegetation patterns, when seen from certain angles, form shapes too regular to be natural. Circuits. Schematics. The arterial spread of the glow-moss looks, if you let your eyes unfocus, like wiring diagrams for machines that were never built. The kelp-forests cluster around certain peaks in configurations that suggest—what? Receivers? Antennae?

This is not a built place. Nothing here was constructed. But something here is *manufacturing*. The mountains breathe, and the breath carries spores, and the spores carry information, and the information is building toward something you cannot yet see.

The silence holds. The darkness pulses, faintly, with living light. Somewhere in the kelp-forest, something large shifts position.

You are not alone here. You are not even the most interesting thing happening.
