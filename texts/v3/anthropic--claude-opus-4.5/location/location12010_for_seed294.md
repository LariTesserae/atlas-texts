# The Drowned Columbarium

The city was never meant to hold an ocean.

Seventeen miles of corridors and halls, built by something that understood geometry in ways that make your inner ear protest, now filled entirely with water so cold it burns. Not flooded—that implies accident. The water was *invited*, drawn in through channels that spiral up from depths no probe has measured, and now it churns through these ancient passages like blood through a vast stone heart.

The architecture rises in tiers, a constructed mountain of basalt-black material that isn't quite stone, isn't quite metal, occupies some uncomfortable middle state that reflects no light but somehow *holds* it, releasing a dim phosphorescence hours after any illumination touches it. Everything here was made. Every surface, every angle, every impossible corner where three walls meet at orientations that shouldn't connect. The builders shaped this place from base elements, fused it into being, left no natural substrate—not a single unworked grain.

And then they left. Or ended. Or became something else.

The water remembers none of this. The water has its own concerns.

---

It moves in ways that shouldn't be possible in an enclosed space. Currents rip through vaulted chambers with the force of flash floods, then die to nothing between one breath and the next. Vast spiraling movements sweep through the main arterial passages—what might have been processional routes, if processionals could travel vertically—while in the smaller cells and alcoves, the water sometimes *reverses*, pushing upward against every principle, draining toward ceilings that aren't ceilings.

The temperature hovers just above freezing. In some chambers, crystals of ice form and dissolve within seconds, the water's violence generating just enough friction-heat to prevent them solidifying, then the stillness returns and they begin again. The cold is architectural, built into the water itself. Something in the city's design keeps it this way, pumping thermal energy somewhere else, somewhere you cannot follow.

There is no plant life. Nothing photosynthetic could survive here—no light to feed on, no stability to root in. But the alcoves along the walls, hundreds of them, thousands, hold things that were never plants at all.

---

They look, at first, like anemones.

Pale fronds extended into the current, bodies anchored in niches that seem *made* for them, sized precisely to their dimensions. They pull nutrients from water that carries almost nothing organic, filtering through volumes that would take terrestrial filter-feeders centuries to process. They do it in hours. Their metabolisms run on principles that have nothing to do with chemistry as you understand it—something in the city's construction provides for them, feeds them, though you cannot identify how.

These are the sessile ones. The ones that stay.

The others move.

Eel-like forms, colorless and translucent, slip through the corridors in scattered congregations—never schools, never organized, each one tracing its own path through the chaos. They are abundant in the way that stars are abundant: many, but separated by such distances that each one occupies its own private void. Sometimes the currents bring them together, tumbling past each other in the violence, and they neither touch nor acknowledge. Their eyes, if those are eyes, perceive something other than light.

Larger things patrol the deep arterials. Shapes like mantas, like kites, like folded origami given mass and motion. They ride the worst of the turbulence, banking through corridors that would crush anything less adapted, and when they pass the frond-creatures retract into their alcoves, waiting, waiting, until the pressure-wake subsides.

Nothing here is natural. But nothing here is unnatural either. The creatures belong to this place, were perhaps *made* for this place, have spent so long within it that the question of origin has become meaningless.

---

The sound should be deafening. The weight of water moving at these speeds, slamming through stone passages, spinning in vortices the size of cathedrals—it should roar.

Instead: a low hum. A vibration more felt than heard. The city's construction absorbs impact, dampens chaos into something almost peaceful. You feel the turbulence in your teeth, your joints, the small bones of your ears. But what you *hear* is closer to silence than storm. A held breath. A dream of drowning where the water makes no noise.

Occasionally, from somewhere distant—always distant, no matter where you are—comes a sound like a bell being struck underwater. A single tone, sustained impossibly long, then fading. The frond-creatures sway toward it. The swimmers pause. Then the chaos resumes.

---

The builders left no writing, no images, no bodies. The alcoves might have held urns, once. Reliquaries. The whole structure might be memorial or morgue, a city-sized monument to something that ended long before water filled these halls. The geometry suggests ritual. The repetition suggests importance. The scale suggests grief on a level that borders worship.

Now the columbarium holds only water, only cold, only the pale things that have made it home. The currents will not still. The bells will not explain themselves. The dim glow from the walls will continue, faithful, illuminating passages where nothing remains to see them.

Seventeen miles of built ocean, cycling through itself, alive in ways that have nothing to do with intention.

The water was invited.

It has no plans to leave.
