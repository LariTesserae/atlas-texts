# The Hatchery Cisterns

Beneath the old port district, where the storm drains empty into something older, the municipal incubation vaults spread across three city blocks in a maze of brick chambers and sweating iron pipes. The ceiling is low—perhaps twelve feet at its highest—supported by columns wrapped in mineral deposits from decades of humid air. Gas lamps burn at every fourth junction, their mantles dimmed by the moisture that beads on every surface, casting the brick in shades of amber and rust.

The heat rises from below, from geothermal vents that the city founders tapped generations ago. It radiates through iron grating in the floor, through the walls of the breeding pools, through the very air until your lungs feel heavy with warmth and wet. The temperature never drops below blood-warm, even in the deepest winter above. Down here, seasons mean nothing.

They raise eels in the long channels that run between the walkways—thousands of them, juveniles no thicker than a finger, sliding over one another in water that seeps up through cracks in the foundation. The pools are shallow, perhaps ankle-deep, and the eels move in slow ribbons beneath the surface, their bodies catching what light there is. When you walk past, the water seems to shiver with them.

But the eels are only the beginning.

In the older vaults, where the brickwork gives way to rough-cut stone, the city keeps its more ambitious projects. Salamanders the length of your forearm drift in warmer pools, their gills fanning slowly, their eyes milky and adapted to the dark. Freshwater crabs cluster in the corners where mineral-rich water drips from calcified pipes, their shells encrusted with the same pale deposits. Something larger moves in the deepest pool—they call it the mother-fish, though no one quite agrees on what species she is anymore. She was introduced forty years ago. She has grown patient and strange in the dark.

The newest chambers are still being carved. You can hear the workers sometimes, distant and muffled, their pickaxes ringing against limestone in slow, deliberate rhythms. They're expanding eastward, following a seam of porous rock that sweats groundwater even without pumping. The walls there are raw and pale, not yet darkened by decades of humid air, and the breeding channels end in rough-cut basins still waiting to be lined with ceramic tile.

The sound down here is the sound of water and life in aggregate: the drip of condensation, the whisper of thousands of small bodies moving through shallow pools, the clank and groan of the pump system that circulates fresh water from the aquifer. The pumps are old—coal-fed engines that rumble somewhere in the mechanical heart of the complex, their vibration felt more than heard. Occasionally a valve releases with a hiss of steam. Occasionally something surfaces and breathes.

The keepers move through the chambers in rubber boots and leather aprons, checking temperatures, monitoring the hatch rates, culling the weak. They work in near-silence, communicating in hand signals refined over generations. They know every pool, every channel, every quirk of the subterranean currents. They know which chambers run too warm and which breed fungus on the walls if the airflow fails. They know the mother-fish by the pattern of ripples she makes when she rises.

In the margins between pools, where the walkways widen into small plazas, sparse vegetation clings to existence. Ferns, mostly—pale things that unfurl in the humid air, their fronds beaded with moisture. Liverworts creep across the older brickwork in patches of gray-green. Someone planted watercress in the overflow channels decades ago; it still grows there, bitter and mineral-tasting, harvested occasionally for the workers' meals.

The water comes up through the floor. It seeps through the walls. It condenses on the ceiling and falls back in a perpetual cycle, the cisterns breathing with their own weather. The whole complex exists in a state of becoming—new chambers being carved while old ones slowly transform under the patient work of mineral and moisture. The stone grows its own architecture. The life adapts to the dark.

Above, the city goes about its business, unaware of the teeming world beneath its feet.
