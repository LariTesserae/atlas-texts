# The Greenhouse Heart

Fifty fathoms down, nestled in the calcified ribcage of what was once a continental drilling platform, there beats a room of impossible green.

The structure sank three centuries ago—or was it four? The engineers who built it are sediment now, their names corroded from every surface. But something they made still works. A single chamber, no larger than a chapel, sealed against the crushing black by walls of industrial glass twelve inches thick. The panels are old beyond reckoning, their edges fused to iron frames that have oxidized into permanent shapes, metal that has forgotten it was ever anything but this rust-orange lattice. The whole thing has settled into the seafloor at an angle, one corner buried in silt, the opposite lifted slightly as if in perpetual genuflection.

Inside: warmth. Heat, actually. The thermal exchangers still cycle, drawing from vents in the seabed below—volcanic breath that travels through corroded copper pipes, warming the water that fills the lower third of the chamber. Steam rises constantly. The glass sweats. Everything drips.

And in that humid envelope, the plants have gone feral.

---

They were brought here by someone, once, for some purpose—food production, oxygen generation, psychological comfort for workers who would never see the sun. Now they climb and tangle and reach. Vines thick as wrists embrace the support struts. Broad leaves press against the glass like hands. Roots have cracked the floor plates and descended into the machinery below, drinking from condensation traps, wrapping around gears that still turn with patient, grinding purpose.

The growth is dense but strangely sparse in its distribution—a jungle compressed into pockets, leaving large swaths of the floor visible where the substrate is merely rust and pooled water. Here, a thicket of something like monstera, leaves the size of serving platters, split into fractal fingers. There, three feet of bare corrugated metal. Then a spray of epiphytes cascading from an overhead pipe, their roots trailing into nothing, catching moisture from the air. The vegetation clusters where the warmth concentrates, avoiding the cold spots near stressed seams where the ocean presses close.

The light comes from bioluminescent tubes mounted along the ceiling—ancient industrial fixtures that someone, at some point, filled with engineered algae instead of replacing the burned-out bulbs. They glow a watery blue-green, bright enough to read by, casting everything in the colors of shallow lagoons. The plants have adapted. They photosynthesize in this strange spectrum, and their leaves have taken on a bluish undertone, as if they've been steeped in the light for so long it became part of them.

---

The fauna arrived uninvited.

Crabs, first. Pale things with translucent shells, small as thumbnails, that scuttle through the leaf litter and congregate on the glass walls where condensation beads. They click against each other—a sound like someone nervously tapping fingernails—and their collective movement produces a constant low patter, rain without rain.

Then the eels. Slender, almost white, they inhabit the flooded lower section, weaving between submerged roots, hunting the crabs' larvae. When they surface to gulp air—the water is warm and holds less oxygen—they make a sound like kissing.

Something larger visits. You can see the sucker-marks on the exterior glass: circles the size of dinner plates, arranged in rows. Whatever makes them comes at irregular intervals, pressing against the panes, perhaps attracted by the light, perhaps by the warmth, perhaps by curiosity. The glass holds. It has held for centuries. But each visit leaves new marks, and if you look closely at the oldest panels, you can see where the surface has begun to dimple inward, molecules slowly rearranging under patient pressure.

Inside, there are moths. This should be impossible. But they flutter in the upper reaches, near the lights, dusty-winged and silent. They emerged from somewhere—pupae buried in soil that came aboard as contamination, perhaps, generations removed from any moth that knew the sky. They have never seen the moon, but they spiral toward the glowing tubes with the same desperate faith.

---

The sound is what strikes you first.

The machinery beneath the floor never stops. Pumps cycle. Valves open and close with pneumatic sighs. The thermal exchangers produce a deep, rhythmic thrum that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. It's the heartbeat—the reason locals call this place what they call it.

Layered over that: the crabs clicking, the eels surfacing, water dripping from condensation points in complex polyrhythms. Steam hisses from a cracked pipe in the corner, a sound so constant it becomes a texture of the air. And outside, transmitted through the structure itself, the voice of the deep current—a low moan that rises and falls as water masses shift above the trench.

When the large thing presses against the glass, everything goes quiet for a moment. Then the structure groans, metal complaining, and the groan travels through every beam and panel, a sound like something ancient sighing.

---

The chamber is tilted, remember. So the water pools at one end, and at the other end, there is a dry shelf where someone built a platform from welded salvage. On that platform: a chair, bolted down. A table, also bolted. A series of hooks on the wall where tools once hung—most empty now, but three still hold: a wrench, a knife with a blade oxidized beyond use, and something that might have been a pen.

No one has sat in that chair for a very long time. But the seat shows wear in the pattern of someone who sat there often, and there are scratches on the table that might be tally marks or might be writing in a language that rust has made illegible.

The plants have not claimed this corner. Perhaps the substrate is wrong. Perhaps something was spilled there that they cannot tolerate. The metal is bare and dry, and when you stand on it, you can feel the vibration of the pumps more clearly, transmitted up through your feet, into your bones.

---

The rules of this world are simple:

What still works, works. What has broken has been broken for so long that its brokenness is now a feature. The pumps cycle because they were built to cycle, and nothing has stopped them, and so they continue. The glass holds because it was made to hold. The light glows because the algae eat whatever nutrients leach from the rust and give back this blue-green radiance in return.

But nothing is maintained. Nothing is repaired. The system runs on momentum and luck and the strange resilience of things built too well for their purpose. One day a seal will fail, or the thermal vents will shift, or the large thing outside will press with slightly more conviction. And then the water will enter, and the warmth will flee, and the plants will die in the sudden cold, and the crabs will scatter into the dark, and the greenhouse heart will stop.

But not today.

Today the steam rises. The leaves unfurl toward light that is not sunlight. The eels hunt. The moths spiral. The pumps beat their patient rhythm. And somewhere in the abyssal black, the large thing circles back, drawn by something it cannot name, pressing its ancient body against the only warmth for miles, leaving its marks on glass that has held for centuries and may hold for centuries more.

The chamber does not know it is miraculous. It only knows how to continue.
