# The Calving Grounds

The mountain is still being born.

You stand on a ridge of black volcanic glass, recently cooled enough to hold weight but still warm through your boots. Below, a vast bowl opens—not a crater exactly, but something more like a wound in the process of becoming a basin. Steam rises from fissures in the dark rock, condensing immediately in the frigid air, falling back as a perpetual fine mist that coats every surface in a skin of ice. The cold is severe, the kind that burns exposed skin, but the ground itself radiates heat in patches, creating pockets where the air shimmers and warps.

This is new land. Weeks old, perhaps months. The geology hasn't settled into itself yet. Obsidian ridges jut at improbable angles where magma met glacial meltwater and froze mid-flow. Pumice fields stretch across the basin floor, pale gray and porous, still hissing where subterranean heat meets the water table. In places, the rock is soft enough to crumble under pressure—not earth, but pre-earth, material that hasn't decided what it wants to become.

The light is strange here. A perpetual overcast presses down, the clouds so close they seem to graze the higher ridges. What sun penetrates is diffused into a uniform gray-white glow that casts no shadows, flattening depth, making distances difficult to judge. The mist doesn't help. Sometimes you can see clear across the basin to where new ridges are forming on the far side, kilometers away. Other times the visibility closes to a hundred meters, and the world becomes intimate and uncertain.

---

And yet the basin teems.

They came from somewhere—the birds. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, species you half-recognize and others that seem like variations on familiar forms. They've claimed this newborn landscape as their own, and their presence is the dominant fact of the place.

The largest are the thermal-soarers, broad-winged and pale, riding the columns of hot air that rise from the fissures. They circle endlessly, rarely flapping, their movements synchronized in ways that suggest communication or perhaps just shared instinct for the same invisible rivers of warmth. From below they look like ash caught in an updraft. From the ridge, at eye level, you can see their size—wingspans of two meters or more, bodies heavy with fat against the cold. They don't call. They simply turn, and turn, and turn.

Lower, in the pumice fields, the ground-nesters have established colonies. Small, dun-colored birds that shouldn't survive here but do, huddling in the warm crevices, emerging to pick at—what? There's almost no vegetation. A few patches of pioneer moss, rust-red and hardy, clinging to rocks where mineral-rich water seeps. Some kind of lichen beginning to establish on the older obsidian. Not enough to sustain this many bodies. But the birds persist, somehow, their populations dense in the warmer zones, thinning toward the cold margins.

The answer, when you find it, is in the meltwater pools.

---

Scattered across the basin floor are dozens of shallow lakes, none larger than a village pond, all fed by the same process: glacial ice meeting volcanic heat, melting faster than it should, pooling in the low places before draining into some unseen underground system. The water is mineral-clouded, gray-green and opaque, warm enough to steam at the edges. And it is absolutely thick with life.

Not fish. Something smaller, more primitive. Crustaceans, perhaps, or some larval form—you can't tell without getting closer, and the ground near the pools is treacherous, more liquid than solid. But you can see the birds feeding there, wading into the shallows, heads dipping rhythmically. Whatever lives in that water reproduces fast, colonizes new pools within days of their formation, provides enough protein to sustain an avian population that seems impossible for such a barren landscape.

The sound of the feeding is the loudest thing here. Soft splashing, the wet clicks of beaks, occasional brief disputes over territory. Otherwise the basin is quiet. The steam rises silently. The rock cools without complaint. Even the fissures, when they vent, produce only a low hiss that fades into the general white noise of mist and wind.

---

The wind. It moves through the basin in unpredictable currents, warm air and cold air mixing, never settling into a consistent direction. Sometimes it carries the mineral smell of the pools, sulfur and iron and something else, something organic. Sometimes it brings only the clean cold of the glaciers that still ring the higher elevations, visible as blue-white walls where the clouds thin enough to show them.

Those glaciers are retreating. You can see it in the raw rock below their current margins, stone that was under ice a season ago, now exposed and beginning its own slow process of weathering. The basin is growing. Every year, perhaps every month, the volcanic heat claims more ground, melts more ice, opens more space for the pools and the birds and whatever comes after them.

This is not a stable ecosystem. It's a frontier, a threshold state, a place where the rules are still being written. The birds know this somehow. Their behavior has an edge of urgency to it—breeding cycles compressed, territorial disputes brief and decisive, a constant low-level activity that suggests they're racing something. The season, maybe. Or the land itself, which could turn hostile in an instant if the wrong fissure opens, if the heat balance shifts, if the water goes toxic or dries up entirely.

---

There are other animals here, harder to see. Something moves in the mist at the basin's edge—four-legged, low-slung, picking its way across the obsidian with the caution of a creature that knows this ground can cut. It doesn't approach the bird colonies, but it watches them. Waits. Scavengers, probably, living off whatever the birds leave behind, whatever dies in the cold margins.

And in the pools themselves, when the light catches right, you can see larger shadows moving beneath the surface. Not the small swarming things the birds feed on—something else, something that preys on those. The water is murky enough that you never get a clear look. Just a sense of movement, of appetite, of a food chain already establishing itself in water that didn't exist a year ago.

The mist thickens. The far ridges disappear. The world contracts to just this section of the basin—the steaming pool, the feeding birds, the warm rock under your feet, the cold air on your face. Somewhere below, magma moves through channels it's carving for the first time. Somewhere above, glacial ice cracks and calves, adding more water to the system.

The mountain continues being born.

The birds continue not noticing that they're witnessing it.
