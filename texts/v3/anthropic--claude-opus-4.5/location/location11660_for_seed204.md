# The Condensery

The room is smaller than a closet—perhaps two meters across at its widest point, wedged into an angle where two walls of the station meet at something other than ninety degrees. You found it by accident, following a condensation trail along a maintenance corridor on Level Seventeen, where the atmospheric processors hum at the edge of hearing.

The walls here are raw alloy, still showing the crystalline grain-patterns of rapid orbital fabrication—this section of Hellas Station was printed only eight months ago, extruded in vacuum and pressurized before the metal had fully settled into its mature structure. The surfaces haven't weathered yet. They catch light in ways that older bulkheads don't, throwing faint prismatic halos where the overhead panel illuminates them.

Water weeps from everything.

Not a leak—the station's environmental systems are functioning perfectly. This is simply what happens when you build human habitation at altitude on Mars, in the thin cold of the Hellas rim, and the thermal gradient between the settlement's warmth and the ancient stone it's anchored to creates zones of perpetual condensation. The walls here stay just below dew point. Droplets form, merge, trace slow paths downward before evaporating somewhere around knee height where the floor heating reaches them. An endless cycle, completing itself perhaps four times per hour.

The sound is the soft, arrhythmic percussion of water finding its paths. Not dripping—nothing falls free here, the gravity too gentle, the surface tension too patient. Just the quiet *tik* and *tok* of droplets meeting, the whisper of moisture moving through microscopic valleys in the metal grain.

---

Someone has been here before you.

A monitoring device clings to the far wall—no larger than a playing card, its surface that particular matte gray of high-specification sensors. The readout, when you crouch close enough to see, shows atmospheric humidity, temperature variance, particulate count. It's measuring this pocket of unintended weather, feeding data somewhere into the station's network where someone is paying attention to the small strange places.

Below it, a thin strip of engineered polymer runs along the junction of wall and floor. A moisture collector, channeling the condensation toward a port no wider than a pencil. Even the station's water budget has been designed to capture what this room produces.

But the collector strip has been partially peeled back in one corner—deliberately, carefully, leaving a gap perhaps three centimeters wide. In that gap, the water has no destination. It pools there, a gathering no larger than a fingerprint, before the floor heat finally claims it.

This is the only standing water on Level Seventeen. Possibly on the entire residential ring.

---

You can see where something has been drinking from it.

The traces are so faint you might have missed them—wouldn't have noticed at all if you weren't crouched here, close to the floor, watching light move through the pooling moisture. But there: marks in the fine residue that forms at the pool's edge. Tiny scratches in the water's mineral deposit. Something with feet smaller than grains of rice has been visiting.

The station's biomonitoring should have caught any stowaways months ago. The atmospheric systems filter for organic particulates down to the micron scale. Nothing lives here that wasn't approved, catalogued, scheduled for these controlled environments.

And yet.

You stay very still. The room's chill seeps through your jumpsuit where your knee presses the floor. Water continues its slow descent along the walls, catching light, releasing it. The monitoring device hums at a frequency you can feel in your teeth more than hear.

In the corner of your vision—or perhaps just below it—something moves. A presence smaller than a word, making its way toward the gap in the collector strip where the water waits.

The station's environmental systems continue their work, entirely unaware that in this pocket of residual damp, in this error in the thermal gradient, a Mars that was supposed to be lifeless is quietly, impossibly, remembering what water means.
