# The Lamplighter's Plateau

The city stretches across the high tableland like a sleeping thing that forgot to wake. You can walk for hours through its streets and never see the sky change—there is no sky to change, only the permanent dark that presses down from above, soft and absolute as velvet laid over the world. No stars. No moon. No sun remembered or expected. The darkness here is not an absence but a presence, a medium through which everything else must negotiate its existence.

What light there is comes from the lamps.

They stand at intersections and along the broad arterial avenues, iron posts rising twelve or fifteen feet, each crowned with a glass housing that contains a slow flame burning something that isn't oil, isn't gas, isn't anything that flows or pools. The fuel is a pale mineral paste, pressed into bricks the size of a child's fist, quarried from seams in the plateau's edge cliffs and carted in by the ton. It burns for years. Some of these lamps have been burning since before anyone now living was born. The light they give is the color of old bone, of tooth enamel, of the inside of shells from creatures that never existed here because there was never any sea.

The buildings are blocky, utilitarian, made from the same pale stone that makes up the plateau itself. They rise three and four stories in the old districts near the center, lower toward the edges where the city frays into warehouse rows and the great silent factories. Everything is the same color: the warm gray-cream of the native rock, unstained by rain because there is no rain, unweathered by wind because the air here barely moves. Dust settles. Dust has been settling for longer than the city has stood. When you walk, your footsteps leave prints that will persist for decades unless someone sweeps them away.

The streets are wide—too wide, you think at first, until you understand that the city was built for a population that never quite arrived, or that arrived and then diminished, leaving behind infrastructure scaled for multitudes now occupied by the sparse and the scattered. You can stand at a major crossing and see the lamplight pooling in both directions, can watch a figure emerge from the darkness a quarter mile away and track their slow approach for five full minutes before they pass you. There is no hurry here. There has never been hurry. The plateau has been here for so long that the mountains that once surrounded it have worn down to suggestions, to rumpled horizons barely visible in the lamp-glow at the city's edge.

---

The factories still run.

You can hear them if you press your ear to certain walls: a deep thrumming, felt more than heard, the vibration of great engines turning over in rooms you cannot access. Chimneys rise from the industrial districts, squat cylinders of mortared stone, and from their mouths comes—nothing visible. No smoke. No steam. Whatever the factories produce, whatever they consume, the process leaves no atmospheric trace. The chimneys are warm to the touch. That is all.

Some say the factories make the lamp-fuel, processing the raw mineral into its burnable form. Others say they make nothing at all anymore, that they are simply running, that they have been running so long that no one remembers how to stop them and no one sees the need to try. The machinery inside is tended by workers who emerge at shift-change, blinking in the bone-light, their clothes and skin filmed with a fine pale powder that they brush away as they walk home through the too-wide streets. They do not speak of what they do. Perhaps they do not know. Perhaps knowing is not the point.

---

The plateau's edge is a day's walk from the city center, and when you reach it, the land simply stops.

Cliffs fall away into darkness so complete that dropping a stone produces no sound of impact—not because there is no bottom, but because the bottom is so far down and the air so still that the sound cannot climb back. The quarry-works cling to the cliff faces, scaffolding and winch-houses and narrow stairs cut into the rock, where the lamp-fuel mineral is extracted in slow careful operations that have continued without significant change for generations. The quarry workers are a separate caste, almost, living in barracks at the plateau's rim, eating food hauled out from the city on carts pulled by—

By nothing, actually. The carts move on their own. Not quickly. A walking pace. They follow tracks laid into the stone, and something in their undercarriages turns, some engine no larger than a bread-loaf that runs on principles no one has bothered to document because no one has needed to repair one in living memory. They simply work.

---

There is life here, though you must look for it.

In the gaps between paving stones, in the cracks of old walls, things grow that are not plants. Filaments, mostly. Threadlike structures that emerge from the stone itself, pale and fine as spider silk, reaching a few inches into the air before curling back on themselves. They do not photosynthesize—there is no light worth capturing—but they pull something from the rock, some slow mineral sustenance that lets them grow a fraction of an inch per year. The oldest filament colonies, in corners of the city that have gone unswept for centuries, form dense mats that crunch underfoot like frost.

And there are the listeners.

Small things. Six-legged. Carapaced in the same pale color as everything else, as if they were made from the plateau rather than merely living on it. They move rarely, and when they do, they move slowly, crossing from shadow to shadow over the course of hours. Their name comes from the way they orient toward sound—any sound—turning their whole bodies to face whatever disturbance ripples through the stillness. Footsteps. The creak of a door. The distant hum of the factories. They listen. They do not seem to eat. They do not seem to reproduce. The same listeners have occupied the same territories for as long as anyone can remember, patient as the stone they resemble, waiting for sounds that may never come.

---

The people of the plateau live quietly.

Not unhappily—there is commerce, there are gathering-halls where food is shared, there are the small intimacies of neighbors who have known each other for decades—but quietly. Voices carry too well in air this still. Conversations tend toward the murmured, the close. Children learn early not to shout. The festivals, such as they are, involve processions through the wide streets, figures carrying secondary lamps that supplement the permanent ones, creating for a few hours a brightness that approximates what other worlds might call dusk.

No one can say how long the city has stood. The buildings do not decay; the stone does not weather; the records, kept in archives near the city center, go back further than anyone has bothered to count. The population rises and falls in slow waves across the centuries, more born in some generations, fewer in others, the total hovering around some equilibrium that the city itself seems to prefer. There is no expansion, no contraction. The factories run. The lamps burn. The listeners listen.

Time here is not a river but a lake: still, deep, without current. Events do not so much happen as persist. The darkness above is the same darkness that pressed down when the first lamp was lit, when the first quarry-worker descended the cliff face, when the first listener turned its whole body toward the sound of footsteps on stone.

Nothing is coming. Nothing is leaving. The plateau simply continues, warm and worn and waiting, its bone-colored light pooling in the wide streets, its engines humming in walls too thick to see through, its edges falling away into depths where no light has ever reached and no light is expected.

You are here now. Perhaps you have always been here. Perhaps you will leave, and the city will note your departure the way stone notes the passage of dust: not at all, and forever.
