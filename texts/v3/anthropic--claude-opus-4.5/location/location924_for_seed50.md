# The Throat of the Mountain

The water comes from above, always from above, falling through the hollow core of the dead volcano in a column so vast and so continuous that it has become something other than rain. It is a river turned vertical. It is the sky emptying itself through a wound in the stone. The sound of it—the *sound*—is not something you hear so much as something you become. It rewrites the rhythm of your blood. It makes your teeth hum in their sockets.

You are inside the mountain, in a pocket of relative stillness behind a shelf of basalt that juts from the inner wall like a black tongue. The space is perhaps twelve feet deep, six feet high at its tallest point before the ceiling slopes down to meet the wet stone floor. Water streams across every surface in sheets and rivulets, but here, in this small hollow, you can almost breathe without drowning.

The shaft above you rises nearly two thousand feet to the broken rim where clouds pour in like milk into a drain. The clouds themselves are being pulled down, sucked into the throat by pressure differentials you don't understand, and as they descend they compress and release their moisture all at once. The falling water catches the light that filters through the cloud-gaps—normal daylight, somehow, pale and grey-white—and it fragments into billions of silver threads that twist and braid and separate as they plummet past your hiding place.

Below, the shaft continues down another several hundred feet before disappearing into spray and chaos. You cannot see the bottom. You're not certain there *is* a bottom, not anymore. The water is carving it deeper even as you watch, even as you listen to the bass thunder that shakes loose pebbles from the ceiling of your shelter. The mountain is being unmade from within.

---

The basalt here is young. Young in geological terms, which means it remembers being liquid. The rock face behind you still shows the frozen ripples of its cooling, still bears the smooth bulges where gas bubbles rose and stopped, trapped forever in their almost-escape. In places the stone is sharp where it fractured along crystal planes; in others it's rounded like melted wax. The formation is incomplete. Embryonic. The mountain was still building itself when something changed—when the water found the weakness at its crown and began this endless excavation.

There are lichens. Just a few, in the deeper crevices of your shelter where the spray doesn't reach with full force. They are pale grey-green, almost white, and they grow in patches no larger than coins. They are the only vegetation you've seen in hours of climbing through the mountain's wet interior passages to reach this place. Beside one lichen colony, tucked into a crack in the stone, you found a shell. Not a sea shell—something else. Something that spiraled in ways that didn't quite make sense, with an opening that faced *inward*. You left it where it was.

The cool air should be a relief after the climb, but it's the kind of cool that gets into your joints, your bones. The water falling past is snowmelt from peaks higher still, from glaciers that feed the clouds that feed this infinite cataract. The temperature hovers just above freezing. Your hands shake as you press them against the stone, trying to absorb whatever residual warmth the rock might hold from its ancient birth.

---

Someone was here before.

The marks are easy to miss in the chaos of water and light, but once you see them, you cannot unsee them. On the ceiling of your shelter, where the stone curves down to meet the back wall, there are scratches. They form patterns—not writing exactly, but something deliberate. Circles within circles. Lines that might be tallies or might be something else entirely. They were made with a hard point, perhaps a stone tool, perhaps a metal spike crude enough to leave rough edges.

Near the scratches, set into a natural depression in the floor, you find the remains of a fire. Just a dark stain now, carbon worked into the basalt's pores. Beside it, a fragment of bone. Animal, you think. You hope. It's been split lengthwise, the marrow scraped out long ago by someone who understood hunger.

How did they make fire here, in this place where everything is wet and the air itself seems to tremble with moisture? You don't know. Perhaps conditions were different then. Perhaps the water-column wavers, breathes, allows moments of relative dryness. You haven't been here long enough to know its rhythms.

---

The light shifts constantly because the clouds shift constantly, because the water-column itself shifts, bowing and straightening in the wind that howls through the shaft. Sometimes the light comes down in single concentrated beams that strike the falling water and shatter into rainbows that last only seconds before the next cloud-shadow swallows them. Sometimes the entire shaft fills with diffuse grey glow and you can see the opposite wall—dark stone, streaming wet, dotted with other shelves and hollows that might hold other hiding places, other fire-stains, other scratched mysteries.

And sometimes, in the strobing confusion of light and shadow and endless falling silver, you see movement.

Not the water. Something *in* the water. Something using the water.

They are translucent, or nearly so. They are shaped like—no. They are shaped like nothing you have words for. They are tubes. They are ribbons. They are briefly spheres before becoming tubes again. They tumble in the column as if falling, but then they move *across* the fall, then *up* through it, and you understand that they are swimming in the vertical river, that this is their ocean, this impossible plunging cylinder of sky-water trapped in the body of a dying mountain.

You see them rarely. Minutes pass, sometimes an hour, before another one drifts or darts across the field of falling silver. They might be fish. They might be something else entirely. Once, you think you see one turn toward you, though it has nothing you could call a face. It holds position against the fall for perhaps three heartbeats before letting the water take it down and out of sight.

---

The turbulence is constant but not uniform. The water-column breathes—you've decided that's the right word—expanding and contracting as the clouds above surge and thin. When it expands, the spray reaches deeper into your shelter, coats your skin, fills your mouth with cold clean nothing-taste. When it contracts, the roar somehow becomes *louder*, as if the same volume of water is being forced through a tighter space, as if the mountain's throat is swallowing.

And the mountain itself moves.

Not much. Not often. But sometimes—during the loudest moments, when the sound becomes pressure becomes pain—you feel the basalt shelf shudder beneath you. Pebbles clatter. Dust that somehow exists despite all this water sifts down from cracks you can't see. The young rock hasn't finished settling. The water is carving new channels, undermining old supports, and the whole interior of the mountain is adjusting, constantly, in micro-earthquakes that you feel more than hear beneath the overwhelming voice of the fall.

You wonder if the shelf will hold. You wonder if the whole inner wall will hold. You've seen places, climbing up through the lower passages, where great slabs have calved away and plummeted into the depths, leaving fresh bright scars of stone that haven't yet grown their first lichen.

---

The world has rules here, but they are strange rules, still forming. Water falls forever and never fills the bottom. Creatures swim in the falling rather than in the pooled. Stone remembers heat from before the water came. Marks last on ceilings where hands once reached in steadier times. Light enters from above but seems to originate from everywhere, from the water itself, as if each droplet carries a fragment of sky that it releases only in the falling.

You could leave. The passages you climbed are still there, still passable, winding down through the mountain's wet intestines to the outer slopes where things grow, where things live in ways you understand. But you stay. You stay because of the scratched circles on the ceiling, because you want to know what they mean. You stay because of the bone-fragment and the fire-stain, because someone thought this place was worth surviving in. You stay because of the swimming things, because you want to see one clearly, just once, well enough to carry its shape back to the world below.

You stay because the sound, after long enough, stops being deafening and starts being a kind of silence. Because in the gaps between the thunder-rhythm you can almost hear something else. A voice, maybe. Or the mountain breathing. Or the creatures singing to each other as they fall and rise and fall again through their endless vertical sea.

The light shifts. Another rainbow, brief and brilliant, arcs across the water-column before drowning in cloud-shadow.

You press your back against the warm-remembering stone, and you wait.
