# The Roaring Warrens

The city is being born.

It emerges from the warm mineral flats in real-time, though real-time here moves like cooling glass—imperceptible unless you look away and look back. The buildings aren't built so much as *extruded*, pushed up from below by some geological pressure that treats basalt and limestone like soft clay. Walls rise crooked, already ancient-seeming the moment they breach the surface. Doorways form as bubbles in the rising stone, windows as stretched voids that thin and harden into rough apertures. Streets appear as the channels between emergent structures, their courses determined by whatever subterranean logic governs the growth.

This has been happening for as long as anyone can remember, and nothing suggests it will ever stop or change its pace.

---

The sound is extraordinary.

Water moves through everything—not flowing so much as *seeping*, pressurized, forcing itself through hairline fractures in the newborn rock. The entire city hisses and groans and shrieks as mineral-heavy water finds its paths. Underground rivers, shallow and warm, push through chambers that amplify their passage into something between thunder and the low moan of settling ice. The stone itself seems to sing as it rises, a deep harmonic drone punctuated by sharp cracks when new fissures open and immediately flood with milky, mineral-laden water.

The residents—and there are many—have adapted. They communicate through touch, through gesture, through a visual language of hand-shapes and body-positions. Sound here isn't absent; it's *full*, saturated, leaving no room for voices. Newcomers find it maddening for the first few days, the constant roar that seems to come from inside their own skulls. Then something shifts, and the noise becomes landscape, becomes weather, becomes simply the texture of existence.

---

The creatures outnumber the people ten to one, maybe more.

They cluster in the wet spaces where walls meet ground, where warm water pools in the hollows of half-formed courtyards. Salamander-like things the size of cats sprawl across the damp stone, their pale bodies almost translucent, their movements slow and deliberate. They've never been observed eating, never been observed doing much of anything except resting in the spray-zones where water jets from new cracks in the rising architecture. But their population never decreases.

Larger animals—something between a seal and a slug, grey and glistening—occupy the broader channels, partially blocking foot traffic with their bulk. The residents step over them, around them, treating their presence as inevitable as the buildings themselves. These creatures produce most of the city's usable material: a thick, gelatinous secretion that hardens when dried, workable into tools, into vessels, into rope when twisted while still pliable. The animals don't seem to notice when people harvest it from their flanks.

Smaller things—segmented, many-legged, glossy with moisture—inhabit every vertical surface, moving in slow columns that spiral up the new-risen walls. At night, when the dim light fades further into near-darkness, they produce a faint bioluminescence, so the city's silhouette glows in shifting patterns, chartreuse and pale amber.

---

The buildings grow, but nothing else does.

No plants soften the angles. No moss creeps across the perpetually wet stone. The only colors are mineral: the rust-red of iron-rich water staining fresh rock, the black of cooling basite, the grey of wet limestone, the white of calcium deposits already forming in the channels. Some residents hang dried material from other places—driftwood carried from distant shores, bundles of grass traded from lands no one here has seen—but these imports are rare and precious, displayed like art rather than used as fuel.

The technology matches the city's strange emergence: hand-tools carved from hardite, blades knapped from the volcanic glass that sometimes surfaces in the newer districts, rope and binding made from creature-secretion. Metal exists—soft copper worked cold, the occasional bronze implement traded from elsewhere—but most tool-work here involves adapting to what the city provides. People carve alcoves into walls that rose yesterday, polish floors that will be buried by new construction within a generation, redirect water through channels cut by hand into stone that is still technically rising, just slowly enough that the channels stay functional.

---

The light rarely changes.

The sky overhead holds a permanent overcast, thick enough to turn sunlight into a diffuse grey glow that barely differentiates day from night. It's warm despite the cloud-cover—the heat comes from below, from whatever process drives the city's emergence, conducting through the stone and radiating into the saturated air. The moisture never quite condenses into rain, but it never quite evaporates either. Everything is damp. Everything stays damp. Residents wear little, and what they wear is waterproof when possible—oiled leather, the waxy treated secretions from the channel-creatures.

In the dimness, shapes blend. The city from any high point—and there are high points, towers that have been rising longer than others, their tops lost in the low mist—looks like a single organism, grey on grey, punctuated by the pale movement of creatures and the darker movement of people. Sound defines space better than sight. The particular quality of the roar tells you where you are: the high whistle of the eastern emergence zones, the deep grinding of the central depths, the almost-rhythmic crash of the water-cascades near the oldest districts where structures have risen high enough to channel their seepage into falls.

---

There is a quality of permanence to this impermanence.

Nothing here is finished. Everything is emerging, still. But the process itself is stable, has been stable, will apparently remain stable indefinitely. Children grow up knowing which streets will narrow as new walls rise, which courtyards will fragment into smaller spaces, which passages will close and which new ones will open. They adapt their routes over lifetimes. The city doesn't change so much as it *continues*—the same process, the same pace, the same warm wet mineral birth that has been happening since before memory and will happen after.

The people here don't seem to find this strange. They carve, they harvest, they step around the slug-seals and over the salamander-things. They touch hands to communicate, read each other's gestures across the perpetually dim streets. They live in a place that is always becoming itself, never arriving, and perhaps this is why their faces—when you can see them clearly enough in the low light—carry an expression that might be patience, or might be something older, something that has made peace with emergence as a permanent state.
