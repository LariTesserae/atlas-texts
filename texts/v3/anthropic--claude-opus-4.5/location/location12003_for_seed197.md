# The Throat of Verdant Bells

It is a hollow inside a living thing, though whether the thing is one organism or ten thousand fused beyond separation, no one could say. The space measures perhaps forty meters across at its widest, rising in an irregular dome maybe fifteen meters high—cathedral proportions, if cathedrals grew from chlorophyll and cellulose instead of stone.

The walls breathe. Not metaphorically. The entire inner surface of this chamber is lined with what might be called lung-flowers: pale green bladders the size of human heads, attached to thick vascular stems that disappear into the deeper flesh of the encompassing plant-mass. They inflate and deflate in staggered rhythms, each exhalation producing a low, resonant tone. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The sound is not music exactly—it is too complex, too layered, too *loud* for that word. It is a pressure that enters through the skin as much as the ears. A vibration that settles into bone.

**The noise is everything here.** It fills the hollow completely, leaves no silence to hide in. At certain moments, when chance harmonics align, the sound becomes almost painful—a chord that makes the eyes water and the teeth ache. At other moments, the tones phase into something resembling peace, a deep thrumming that could be mistaken for comfort if you forgot what comfort actually felt like.

## The Architecture of Growth

The floor is not a floor. It is the upper surface of root-mass so densely interwoven it has become a kind of spongy wood, springy underfoot, yielding but never breaking. In places, the surface rises into ridges and platforms that suggest—disturbingly—civic planning. There are paths here. Gathering spaces. Something that resembles a central plaza, if a plaza could be grown. The plant has organized itself into urban forms without tools, without intention anyone could recognize. Terraced levels climb the walls in quarter-spirals. Alcoves indent the living tissue like market stalls or dwelling-cells. A bridge of braided vine-cable spans the upper reaches, connecting two protrusions that serve no obvious purpose but exist with the confident permanence of infrastructure.

This is old. The settlement-shapes have weathered into themselves, smoothed by time into something that no longer looks made. The edges have rounded. The surfaces have developed a patina of secondary growth—tiny mosses, parasitic flowers no bigger than fingernails, threadlike fungi that trace silver lines across the green. The place has existed long enough to forget it was ever new.

---

## The Water That Visits

Moisture here is intermittent, following patterns that might be tidal or might be biological. Every few hours—though time is hard to track in the eternal green twilight—a fine mist begins to condense from the upper dome. It starts as a haziness in the air, then becomes visible droplets forming on every surface, then becomes a gentle rain that falls inward from all directions, gathering in the central depression where the floor dips slightly. A pool forms. It lasts perhaps an hour before the root-mass drinks it down, the water vanishing into capillary channels that glow faintly as they fill, tracing luminous networks through the spongy ground.

During the wet phases, the lung-flowers change their song. The tones become wetter, somehow—more glottal, more organic, as if the chamber itself were gargling. The creatures emerge then, too.

## What Lives Here

The fauna is impossible in ways that suggest reality has grown confused about its own rules.

**The bell-moths**: Wingspan of a spread hand, wings that are translucent membranes stretched over frameworks of what appears to be living cartilage. They have no heads. Where heads should be, there are only more wings—smaller ones, dozens of them, beating in counter-rhythm to the main pair. They navigate by sound alone, riding the pressure waves like currents, sometimes hovering motionless in acoustic nodes where competing tones cancel into stillness. Hundreds of them live in the upper dome.

**The listeners**: These might be mammals once, or might be something that independently invented fur as a solution to a problem no one remembers. They are roughly cat-sized, shaped like teardrops, and they cling to the walls with adhesive pads that leave no residue. Their bodies are mostly ear—vast, ribbed, constantly-adjusting ears that swivel to track sounds with mechanical precision. They eat the bell-moths, somehow detecting the silence the moths carry with them. When a listener catches prey, it produces the only quiet thing in this place: a small bubble of hush that expands briefly from its feeding, a negative-space in the overwhelming noise that feels like sudden deafness before collapsing back into sound.

**The procession**: Once per cycle—if there are cycles—a line of something crawls across the floor. They might be caterpillars or might be the mobile phase of something that later becomes the walls themselves. They are palm-sized, amber-colored, and they move in perfect synchronization, spacing themselves exactly a body-length apart, traveling a circuit around the inner perimeter that takes most of a day to complete. They leave behind a faint trail of bioluminescence that fades slowly, marking their passage like a ritual calendar.

**The thing that is probably singular**: Deep in the root-mass beneath the floor, something larger moves. Its presence is felt as subtle shifts in the ground's springiness, as changes in the pitch of certain lung-flowers, as occasional glimpses of something pale pressing up against the semi-translucent tissue of the floor-surface before sinking away. It might be the organism that grew this place. It might be a parasite. It might be the dream the chamber is having about itself.

## Where Reality Thins

The weirdness is structural here, built into the biology.

Sound and space have entered into an inappropriate relationship. In certain spots—they shift, but can be found if you listen—the acoustic pressure becomes so intense that it achieves something like solidity. Step into a standing wave at the wrong moment and your forward motion will simply stop, as if you've walked into invisible gelatin. The air isn't thicker. The sound is just *more present*, occupying space that should belong to movement.

Worse: some of the lung-flowers are singing things that haven't happened yet. They produce tones that correspond to footsteps not yet taken, to the splash of water that won't fall for another hour, to the death-sound of a bell-moth that is currently still alive. The pre-echoes layer into the present-sounds in ways that make causality feel like a suggestion rather than a rule. If you listen carefully—and you cannot help but listen, the volume makes inattention impossible—you begin to hear your own future layered into the current noise. Your future words. Your future breath. The sound you will make when you finally understand what this place is.

The light is steady, normal, sourceless—it seems to come from the tissue itself, a gentle illumination that casts no shadows because it originates from everywhere. This is the one mercy. In darkness, the sound would be unbearable. At least you can see what's happening even when your ears have stopped making sense of it.

## The Warmth and the Wet

The temperature sits in that range between comfortable and too-warm, the kind of warmth that makes you aware of your own skin. Humid when the mist comes, merely damp when it doesn't. The air tastes green—literally, some photosynthetic microorganism suspended in the atmosphere, harmless but omnipresent, coating the tongue with the flavor of crushed stems.

The warmth has a pulse to it, synchronized imperfectly with the lung-flower rhythms. Body heat, you realize eventually. The chamber itself is warm-blooded, or warm-sapped, or warm-whatever-flows-through-plant-veins. It is alive in ways that exceed the merely botanical. It has temperature regulation. It has infrastructure. It has citizens that are not it and yet belong to it absolutely.

---

## What This Place Is

This is a city that grew instead of being built. Or a creature that organized itself into city-forms without knowing what cities are. Or a throat—hence the name, though no one remembers who named it—that is singing itself into existence, that exists only as a side effect of the song it cannot stop producing.

The lung-flowers never rest. The bell-moths never stop flying. The procession never completes its circuit because the circuit has no end, only a continuous loop that might be worship or might be digestion or might be neither. The listeners keep eating their small bubbles of silence. The thing beneath keeps moving. The sound goes on.

Visitors who stay too long report that they stop hearing the noise after a while. Their ears adjust, they say. It becomes background, they say. But that's not what happens. What happens is that the noise stops being separate from them. It colonizes the auditory cortex. It becomes the baseline against which all future sounds will be measured. When they leave—if they leave—they discover that ordinary silence feels like suffocation. They spend the rest of their lives seeking loud places, crowded places, anywhere that approaches the overwhelming presence of the Throat.

Some of them come back.

The chamber accepts them. There is always room. The walls are still growing, still organizing, still singing futures that haven't yet decided to occur.
