# The Caldera Nursery

The pocket is no larger than a cupped palm, if palms were made of obsidian and cupped around fever-dreams.

You find it in the crack between two fresh volcanic stones, where the mountain's most recent exhalation has not yet cooled. Steam rises from everywhere—from the black glass, from the fractured basalt, from the wet heat that pools in every crevice like something breathing. The rock here is sharp, ungently new, edges that have never known wind or patience. Everything glistens. Water condenses on every surface, runs in threads too thin to name, collects in hollows smaller than teardrops.

But within this crack—this gap perhaps three inches across and five inches deep—something has gone wrong with the world.

Look closer. The water here does not simply condense. It *congregates*. Droplets move laterally, sideways, gathering into patterns that should not hold on vertical stone. They form shapes: spirals, branching networks, geometric progressions that shift when you blink. The heat here is intense, almost burning, and yet the water does not evaporate. It sweats. It *waits*.

And within this wet darkness, so close together they seem to overlap, so layered they blur into each other: the fauna.

They are not quite insects. Not quite arachnids. Not quite anything that has earned a name. Translucent bodies the size of rice grains, some smaller—bodies that seem to have too many legs, or legs that exist only when observed, or legs that are actually tongues, or tongues that are actually smaller creatures riding the larger ones. They move in absolute silence, their thousands compacted into this fissure-sized space, crawling over and through and *into* each other in ways that suggest their boundaries are negotiable.

Some of them appear to be eating the water. Some appear to be *becoming* the water. One—larger than the others, perhaps the size of a lentil—has a body that flickers between states: now solid chitin-shine, now liquid pooling, now something gaseous that your eyes cannot track. It transitions without warning, without apparent cause, and the creatures around it seem to orient toward it regardless of which form it wears.

The stone itself participates. Fresh obsidian, still radiating the mountain's fury, should be inert—but here, in this pocket, flakes of it lift away from the surface and hover. They don't rise far. A millimeter, perhaps two. But they hover, turning slowly, and the creatures cluster beneath them like congregants beneath inexplicable stars. The flakes cast shadows that move independently of any light source you can identify.

There are layers to this space that should not fit. Look at it directly, and it is a crack in rock, a few cubic inches at most. Look at it peripherally, and depth opens—chambers behind chambers, territories the creatures migrate between, whole geographies compressed into the space between two stones. The heat shimmers, and in the shimmer you glimpse architectures: not built things, not made things, but arrangements of bodies and water and floating stone that function as walls, as roads, as something that might be nurseries if the word could stretch far enough.

The silence is nearly total. Occasionally, a hiss of steam from somewhere above. Occasionally, a sound like a single grain of sand shifting. But the creatures themselves make no noise—or rather, they make noise at frequencies the air here refuses to carry. You feel it in your teeth. A density of communication packed into this fever-wet pocket, thousands of beings negotiating space and food and whatever passes for territory when territory folds in on itself.

Something is being born here. Or something is being practiced. The mountain is young, this extrusion younger still, and already this crack in its fresh-formed skin hosts a complexity that shouldn't have had time to develop. The creatures move too purposefully for accident. The water gathers too deliberately. The floating obsidian flakes arrange themselves in patterns that repeat, shift, repeat again—learning something, or teaching something, or remembering something that hasn't happened yet.

You could seal this crack with a single stone. Press your thumb over it and create, perhaps, the only apocalypse these beings will ever know.

You do not.

The heat pulses. A droplet of water crawls upward, against gravity, toward a flake of hovering black glass. Several creatures follow it, their bodies flickering between states as they climb the impossible gradient. They are building something. Or becoming something. Or unbuilding whatever rules say that pockets in hot stone should be empty and simple and dead.

The mountain rumbles, elsewhere, above. New stone being born. New cracks forming.

New nurseries, waiting.
