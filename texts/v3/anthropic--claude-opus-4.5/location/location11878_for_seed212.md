# The City That Grows Itself

The sound hits you first—a roar like a thousand rivers tearing through stone, except there is no stone here, hasn't been for longer than anyone could measure. The noise is *growth itself*, the wet shriek of cellulose fibers splitting and reforming, of bark plates grinding past each other as they race upward, of roots thick as temple columns drilling through older roots with a sound like bones breaking underwater. The air shudders with it. Your chest vibrates.

You stand—if standing is possible in a place where the ground breathes and buckles—at the edge of what might once have been called a valley, though the concept strains. The depression spans perhaps three miles across, and every inch of it writhes with vegetable life so dense, so urgent, so *architecturally insistent* that the distinction between forest and city has become meaningless.

There are streets here. Avenues. Plazas that hold their shape for hours before being consumed by the next surge. The plants build them—not randomly, not as humans would recognize intention, but with a pattern-hunger that mimics civilization without understanding it. Massive buttress roots weave themselves into colonnades. Vines braid into suspension bridges that connect canopy-towers before unbraiding and rebraiding elsewhere. Fruiting bodies the size of houses swell from intersections, their surfaces pocked with window-like apertures that serve no purpose except that *windows are a shape that happens here*.

The warmth is vegetal, intimate, the humid breath of a greenhouse scaled to swallow nations. Moisture beads on every surface—not rain exactly, but the exhalation of ten billion leaves transpiring in unison, a perpetual dew that runs in rivulets down trunk-walls and pools in the cup-shaped structures that form and reform at ground level. The water never stays still. It's drunk almost as soon as it gathers, pulled into root-networks that pulse visibly beneath translucent bark-skin.

Nothing here is old. That's what makes your mind slip—the freshness of it all, the rawness. Bark gleams wet and new. Broken surfaces show pale green heartwood that hasn't had time to darken. You can watch a structure emerge: first the pioneer vines racing up from the mulch-floor, then the woody stems thickening behind them, then the secondary growth filling gaps until suddenly there's a wall, a roof, a doorway framing nothing. The edges are sharp. The joins are clean. This is a city being born every moment, and also a city being eaten every moment, because what grows must grow *through* what grew before, and the sound of that consumption—that wet splintering, that vegetable digestion—never stops.

The light is strange only because of what it passes through. The sun exists, somewhere above the canopy-ceiling, and it falls in cathedral shafts through gaps that open and close as the upper architecture shifts. When a shaft finds you, the heat is sudden and thick. When it moves on, the green twilight returns, and you notice that some of the plants luminesce faintly, a backup system, a bioluminescent insurance policy against the chaos of their own shadows.

There are no animals. You feel their absence like a missing tooth. The niches exist—the fruit hangs unpecked, the hollow-tubes wind through trunk-masses like intestines waiting for something to digest, the sticky surfaces of certain pad-shaped leaves glisten with unused adhesive. The ecology screams for pollinators, for seed-dispersers, for predators to prune the weak. Instead, the plants do it themselves, badly, excessively, in ways that shouldn't work but do through sheer overwhelming fecundity.

And the wrongness. The *wrongness*.

It starts at the edges of your vision. A tendril that bends the wrong direction relative to itself. A leaf whose surface is somehow *behind* its stem. You turn to look directly and the effect slides away, but you've seen enough. The growth here has become so dense, so frantically iterative, that it's begun to overlap in ways that violate the basic courtesies of matter. Two vines occupy the same space and neither yields—they simply *both are*, superimposed, until one outgrows the paradox and the other snaps back into singular existence with a sound like a thunderclap.

In the distance, toward what might be the center of this impossible settlement, you can see a structure that's been growing long enough to achieve genuine mass—a spiraling ziggurat of wound trunks and fused canopy, easily two hundred feet tall, its silhouette constantly fidgeting as new growth adds and subtracts from its outline. It appears to be *listing* slightly to the left, except when you look again it's listing right, except neither direction exists in quite the way directions should. The light bends around it. Or it bends around the light.

The roar intensifies. Somewhere nearby, a plaza is being born.
