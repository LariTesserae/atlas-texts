# The Incubation Vent

The climbing harness cuts into your thighs as you wedge yourself deeper into the fissure. Three thousand meters below, the glacier catches what little light reaches this altitude. Three hundred meters above, the crack in the mountain opens to a sky you haven't seen in hours. You are suspended in the space between—a vertical wound in new rock, where the earth is still deciding what shape it wants to become.

The walls are warm. Not hot, not volcanic-hot, but blood-warm, the temperature of something alive or pretending to be. The stone itself is barely stone—more likeite ore that hasn't finished crystallizing, mineral-rich and sweating. Condensation beads on every surface, trickling down in threads that catch in the moss.

The moss. It shouldn't exist at this elevation. Nothing should. But the vent creates its own climate, a humid pocket where extremophile bryophytes have gone delirious with possibility. They carpet every horizontal surface in layers—silver-green, rust-red, a black variety that smells faintly of copper. Where water collects in thumb-sized pools, the moss grows upward in spiraling towers no taller than your finger.

You are not alone in here.

---

The first thing you learn: in the vent, sound has weight. It presses against your eardrums like something trying to get in.

The source is the insects. Or what might be insects—their taxonomy feels unfinished, like the rock itself. They emerge from pores in the warm stone, from eggs laid in the moss, from crevices you can't see because they're filled with *more of them*. Thumbnail-sized crawlers with too many legs. Flying things that move in coordinated pulses, their wings creating a collective thrum that resonates against the narrow walls until it becomes physical, until you can feel it in your molars.

They're not interested in you. That's the uncanny part. They flow around you like you're a rock in a stream—over your hands, across your helmet, through the gaps in your harness—but they never bite, never investigate. They're going somewhere, all of them, always going somewhere, and you're simply in the way.

The vent is perhaps two meters wide at its broadest point, narrowing to shoulder-width passages where you have to turn sideways. Every centimeter of that space is occupied. Moths the color of wet slate cluster in crevices. Millipedes form living chains across gaps in the rock. Something that might be a beetle, if beetles could be translucent and filled with visible fluid systems, crawls past your face close enough that you could count its antennae segments if the light were better.

---

The light comes from three sources, and you're responsible for two of them.

Your headlamp cuts a cone of clinical white through the organic murk. Your backup light, clipped to your harness, adds a secondary glow when you angle wrong and lose the primary beam to moss or wing or wet rock.

The third light belongs to something else.

Thirty meters up, visible when you crane your neck and squint past the seething biomass, there's a structure. Metal. Industrial. It looks like part of a gantry—support beams bolted directly into the stone, a platform that might once have held equipment. The metal is old, patinaed green and brown, but someone wired it with lights that still flicker. Battery-powered, maybe. Solar, somehow, through some crack above you can't perceive. The lights are the color of aged paper, and they illuminate what must have been a monitoring station: brackets for instruments, a junction box with its cover hanging open, cables that disappear into the rock like planted roots.

They were studying this place. Someone was. The bolts are new enough, geologically speaking—the vent is still forming around them, mineral deposits beginning to skin over the metal, the mountain trying to digest what was installed. A laminated tag hangs from one bracket, text in a language that uses more consonants than you're used to, partially obscured by a living mat of something between fungus and algae.

You clip in at a new anchor and begin the next pitch upward, toward the gantry, because the fissure narrows above it and you'll need the platform to plan your route. Every handhold brings you closer to that concentration of life and light and noise.

---

The soundscape shifts as you climb. Lower down, the dominant frequency was the insect-thrum—a chord made of ten thousand small wings and clicking mandibles. But there are larger things here.

You hear them before you see them: shuffling sounds, wet sounds, the particular noise of something with mass moving through something without much clearance. In your headlamp beam, you catch motion. A shape like a knotted rope unwinds itself from a crack in the rock, reveals itself as a limbless amphibian—salamander-relative, maybe, something that took a different evolutionary turn. It's longer than your arm, pale as the rock's unfinished interior, and it moves through the insect masses like a whale through krill, mouth open, collecting.

There are birds, too, if birds can be this small and this specialized. Hummingbird-sized but shaped more like swifts, built for maneuverability in tight spaces. They don't sing—they click, a rapid-fire echolocation that adds another layer to the vent's acoustic texture. They catch insects on the wing, which sounds violent when you're close enough: the snap of tiny beaks, the crunch of chitin, repeated hundreds of times per minute by dozens of hunters you can barely track.

Something larger moves in your peripheral vision. You freeze, let your headlamp sweep slowly toward it.

The cave spider—it must be a spider, though its proportions are wrong for anything you've studied—has built a web across a section of the vent you were about to climb through. The web is three-dimensional, a maze of silk strands at different tensions, each one vibrating at its own frequency in the insect-stirred air. The spider itself is the size of your spread hand, and it's watching you with a cluster of eyes that catch your light and throw it back multiplied.

The web is too dense to climb through. You'll have to find another route.

---

At the gantry, you pull yourself onto the platform and discover you're not the first visitor in recent years. Someone left a notebook in a waterproof bag, wedged behind the junction box. The moisture got in anyway—the pages are warped, the ink blurred—but you can make out diagrams. Cross-sections of the vent. Population counts, maybe, columns of numbers with tiny sketched icons beside them. Temperature readings that spike at certain depths.

The gantry groans beneath you, or the mountain groans around it. New rock is not stable rock. You feel the platform shift a millimeter, watch a fresh crack appear in the stone where one of the anchor bolts meets formation. The vent is still growing, still changing. In a decade, this platform might be fully encased. In a century, crushed.

Above you, the fissure continues toward the surface, but the route is choked with life. Moths cling to the walls so thickly that the rock beneath them is only a hypothesis. The moss has given way to something more complex—a growth like pale coral, branching structures that seem to feed on the mineral seep. Creatures nest in its branches: things too small to identify, things that move in unison, things that are perhaps a single organism distributed across a hundred bodies.

The noise here is physical. It enters through your skin. The walls of the vent have created a resonance chamber, and every sound amplifies and returns amplified further. You have to think in words, deliberately, to maintain the thread of your own cognition against the intrusion.

You switch off your headlamp to rest your eyes, and in the darkness, the bioluminescence reveals itself. Faint glows in the coral-structure—chemical signals, mating displays, warning colors rendered in light. The insects carry their own tiny lanterns, blue-green and amber. The vent becomes a river of moving stars, flowing upward toward air you cannot see.

---

The fissure breathes. Not metaphorically—the temperature differential between the warm interior and the frozen peak above creates airflow, a gentle but constant exhale that carries moisture and heat and the spores of impossible moss toward the glaciers. Everything here rides that breath. The insects migrate up and down with its rhythm. The cord-salamanders follow the insects. The clicking birds follow everything.

You are inside a system. A pocket ecosystem balanced on the edge of emergence, feeding on geothermal chemistry and mineral seep, building itself in real-time in a crack in young stone at the roof of the world. It is deafeningly, overwhelmingly alive, and it does not need you, and it will not mourn you when you climb out and return to the quiet of open sky.

But you stay a while longer in the gantry's dying light. Watching the web-spider repair a strand. Listening to the vent's continuous roar resolve, moment by moment, into its component voices. Feeling the rock slowly, imperceptibly, grow.
