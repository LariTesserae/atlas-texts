# The Suspended Cisterns of Vael-Ascending

The city is still becoming.

It rises through warm, heavy water—an architecture of enormous clay vessels and woven fiber chambers, each one anchored to the others by cables of braided kelp and the slow-hardening secretions of creatures no one has seen in generations. The cisterns hang at different depths like a constellation rotated ninety degrees, and between them, in the dim green-gold light that filters down from somewhere far above, the people of Vael move along rope bridges and swim through corridors of trained vegetation.

You are perhaps two hundred meters below the surface, though "surface" here means something different than it would elsewhere. The water above you is not ocean—it is the body of a vast freshwater upwelling, a thermal column that has been rising from fissures in the seabed for only a few centuries. The heat comes from below, and it carries minerals that cloud the water with pale sediment, giving everything a soft, particulate haze. Light enters this column from the sides more than from above—refracted through the curved boundary where hot fresh water meets the colder salt sea surrounding it. The effect is a perpetual twilight, sourceless and gentle, that makes distance difficult to judge.

The cisterns themselves are the oldest structures, some of them predating the current generation's great-grandparents. They began as simple fired-clay vessels, enormous amphorae meant to store breathable air for the divers who first explored this thermal anomaly. But they grew. Each generation added chambers, extended walls, sealed cracks with mixite paste made from crusite shells and volcaniciteiteiteite glass ground fine as flour. The clay has taken on the character of living coral—pocked, layered, marked by repair upon repair until the original form is barely visible beneath its own history.

---

Inside the largest cistern, called the Breath-Hold, the air is thick and warm and tastes of iron. Forty or fifty people can gather here at once, though they rarely do—the Vael prefer smaller spaces, rooms that can be sealed quickly if the walls should crack. The interior surfaces are covered in a luminescent growth, something between moss and slime, that provides just enough light to see by. It pulses with a rhythm that doesn't match any heartbeat, slow and arrhythmic, like breathing during deep sleep.

The furniture is minimal: woven sleeping platforms suspended from ceiling hooks, clay vessels within vessels for storing food and tools, and everywhere the careful infrastructure of survival. Air-traps. Pressure valves made from fish bladders and carved bone. Speaking-tubes that run between chambers, carrying whispered conversations through the water. The technology is primitive by any measure—there is no metal here, or almost none, just the occasional bronze knife passed down through families like sacred text—but it is also profoundly adapted. These people have been learning this one problem for eight generations: how to breathe in a world that is mostly breath-less.

The lush growth outside the cisterns is a vertical forest, though "forest" implies ground, and there is no ground here. Kelp and water-vines climb the thermal column like flames climbing air, their fronds spreading wide to catch whatever light penetrates the haze. Among them, stranger things grow: bulbous structures that may be plants or animals or something else entirely, their surfaces covered in cilia that wave in currents too subtle to feel. Fruit-like nodules hang in clusters, some of them edible, most of them not, a few of them actively dangerous in ways the Vael have catalogued in songs passed from parent to child. The vegetation is dense enough that swimming through it requires parting curtains of growth, pushing through resistance, navigating by memory and the feel of familiar textures against your skin.

---

Fauna is rare, but not absent. Something large moves through the outer reaches of the thermal column—the Vael call it the Patron, though they've never seen it clearly. Just the pressure wave of its passing, the way the vegetation bends, the occasional glimpse of something pale and finned disappearing into the sediment haze. It has never attacked the cisterns. It has never approached them. The Vael believe it is waiting for the city to finish becoming, though they disagree about what will happen then.

Smaller creatures exist in greater numbers but remain elusive: translucent eels that live inside the kelp stems, crustaceans that have adapted to the mineral-rich water by growing shells of calcium so thick they sink like stones unless they keep moving. Birds, of course, are impossible here. The Vael have stories about them—flying things that live in the dry world above the water's edge—but they have the quality of myth, creatures too strange to be real.

The sound is what strikes visitors most, if visitors ever came. The silence. Water conducts sound beautifully, but there is so little sound to conduct. The occasional creak of a rope bridge taking weight. The muffled thump of a clay vessel being moved. The soft, constant, almost subliminal hum of the thermal current itself, felt more than heard, a vibration that enters through the bones of the skull. The Vael speak in whispers and hand-signs. Their music, when they make it, is percussive—tapping on hollow chambers, creating rhythms that travel through the city's structure like messages through a nervous system.

---

The strangeness here is not sudden or dramatic. It accumulates. It is the way the light shifts without any visible source. The way the vegetation sometimes moves against the current, as though reaching for something. The way certain cisterns, the oldest ones, seem to have developed features that no one remembers building—small chambers within chambers, passages that lead to dead ends, walls that are warmer than they should be. The Vael do not worship their city, exactly, but they treat it as something more than an object. They speak to it. They apologize when they must cut into its walls for repairs. They believe, though they would not put it this way, that the city is also becoming something, and that their presence within it is part of that process.

The elevation is high in the sense that matters here: they are far above the sea floor, suspended in the thermal column's warmth, looking down into depths where the water turns cold and dark and pressurized beyond what their bodies could survive. Looking up toward a surface they cannot reach without days of decompression, slow ascent through temperature gradients that would kill them if rushed. They are mountain-dwellers in a world without mountains, perched on warmth and breath and the patient accumulation of clay and fiber, waiting for their city to finish becoming whatever it is becoming.

Below, the fissures continue to release their heat and minerals.

Above, the surface waits, impossibly distant, a boundary no one here has ever crossed.

And the Patron circles, slow and patient, in the haze at the edge of vision.
