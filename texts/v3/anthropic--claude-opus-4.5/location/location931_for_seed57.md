# The Pocket Behind the Waterfall

There is a space behind the falling water that should not be as large as it is.

From outside, the waterfall appears ordinary—a modest cascade spilling over a limestone lip into a pool no wider than a blanket spread for a meal. The water is cold, fed by snowmelt from somewhere higher, somewhere that doesn't quite connect to any mountain the surrounding geography would suggest. But step through the curtain of falling water, press yourself against what should be solid rock, and you find yourself inside.

---

The chamber breathes.

Not metaphorically. The walls contract and expand in cycles so slow you might mistake the movement for your own pulse, your own uncertain perception in the near-total darkness. But stay long enough—and time moves strangely here, pooling and eddying like the water that fills everything—and you will see the moss compress, release, compress again. The rhythm takes perhaps an hour. Perhaps a day. The distinction has grown soft.

Water saturates every surface. It does not drip so much as *exist* in continuous slow migration, sheeting down walls that shouldn't curve the way they do, collecting in channels that spiral rather than descend. The chamber is small—you could cross it in twelve steps if you walked straight, which you cannot, because straight has lost its meaning here. The geometry folds. You walk twelve steps and arrive somewhere adjacent to where you began, but not the same. The moss has different patterns. The water tastes older.

---

The vegetation is impossible and lush.

Ferns grow sideways from the wet stone, their fronds a deep blue-green that borders on black in the minimal light. They do not photosynthesize—there is no sun here, only the faint bioluminescence of organisms you cannot quite see, a glow that exists more as a memory of light than light itself. The ferns feed on something else. Perhaps the geometry. Perhaps the slow breathing of the walls. Their roots have woven through the limestone so thoroughly that it's unclear which came first—the stone or the growing things.

Liverworts carpet every horizontal surface in overlapping layers, each stratum representing what might be centuries of patient accumulation. They are cold to the touch, almost frozen, and yet they grow. Slowly. In the time it takes you to blink, a single cell divides somewhere in the chamber. This is the pace of life here.

Mushrooms cluster in the corners—if corners is the right word for spaces where walls meet walls that shouldn't exist. They are pale, translucent, and when you look at them directly, they seem to occupy less space than when viewed from the edge of your vision. Some of them have begun growing through things. Not around. Through. You find a mushroom emerging from the center of a stone, and when you touch it, your finger passes into something that feels like cold water but isn't wet.

---

The water is everything.

It fills the air as humidity so complete that breathing feels like drinking. It pools in depressions that are deeper than the floor should allow—you lower your hand into a puddle that shouldn't reach past your wrist and feel cold water close over your elbow, your shoulder, and you pull back sharply, and your arm is fine, merely damp, but you do not try again. The water falls continuously from somewhere above, through an opening that you cannot locate no matter how you crane your neck. It falls in sheets and threads and single drops that take too long to descend, hanging in the dark air, catching the faint glow of the liverworts and holding it.

There is a stream, of sorts. It winds through the chamber floor in a channel it has carved through centuries of patient flow, and the limestone here is sharp, young, the marks of erosion still fresh and angular. This is a new place, despite everything. Or perhaps it is a place that remains perpetually new, the rock reforming as quickly as the water wears it away, caught in a loop of creation that never settles into age.

---

Something built a marker here, once.

In the deepest part of the chamber—or what feels deepest, though distance has become unreliable—there is a cairn of stacked stones. They are not limestone. They are dark, smooth, river-worn, and they did not come from this place. Someone carried them here. Someone who understood that this space existed, who found the way through the waterfall, who stayed long enough to gather stones and stack them in a shape that might once have meant something.

The cairn is half-swallowed now. Moss and liverwort have claimed three of the stones entirely, and the water has begun to deposit minerals on the others, coating them in a thin white crust that will eventually seal them into the floor. In another few centuries—or whatever passes for centuries here—the marker will be indistinguishable from the cave itself. Someone's intention, becoming geology.

There are no other signs of whoever built it. No tools, no bones, no scratched messages on the walls. Just the cairn, and the slow patience of water and moss erasing it.

---

The sound is the sound of water, but muffled.

Outside, the waterfall roars—a constant white noise that fills the forest pool. Inside, that sound becomes something else. Dampened. Diffused. The falling water still falls, but it falls *quietly*, as though the rules that govern acoustics have grown uncertain. You hear the dripping, the sheeting, the distant rush, but it all comes from too many directions, overlapping and canceling, until what remains is a hush that feels almost deliberate. Almost listening.

Occasionally, something moves.

You never see it. There is a ripple in a pool that shouldn't ripple, a shadow that shifts where shadows have no source, a sound like a very small body sliding over wet stone. Whatever lives here is rare, careful, adapted to the darkness and the strangeness. Perhaps it is blind. Perhaps it sees in ways you cannot imagine. You feel it watching you, but that might be the chamber itself, the slow breathing walls, the geometry that folds and refolds around your presence.

---

The weirdness is structural.

It is not that strange things happen here—though they do, constantly, in the way water falls too slowly and stones occupy spaces they shouldn't and the air tastes of time. It is that the rules themselves have grown soft. Cause and effect still operate, but loosely, with exceptions, with margins of error that allow for the impossible.

If you walk the full circuit of the chamber—all twelve steps, however many times it takes—you will eventually arrive at a place where two versions of the same fern grow from the same spot, each one real, each one casting the same shadow. If you stay long enough, you will find that your memories of entering have begun to shift, rearranging themselves into sequences that didn't happen, that couldn't have happened, that feel nevertheless true. The pocket behind the waterfall does not obey. It suggests. It offers alternatives.

---

You could stay.

The cold is not painful, merely present. The darkness is not absolute—there is always enough light to see the next step, though never more than that. The water would sustain you, and the mushrooms, you suspect, might be edible, though you do not know what eating them would mean for the parts of you that still expect the world to behave. The chamber breathes, and if you match your breathing to its rhythm, something shifts in your chest. Something opens.

But the waterfall is still falling, out there, in the world that measures time in ordinary ways. The pool is still cold and clear and behaving itself. And the gap between inside and outside—the threshold you crossed—will not stay open forever. Already you can feel the walls beginning to notice you, the geometry starting to fold you in.

You leave while you still know which direction leaving means.

---

Outside, the forest is ordinary. Birds call. The water roars. The sun has moved, though you cannot say how much.

Behind you, through the curtain of falling water, the pocket breathes.
