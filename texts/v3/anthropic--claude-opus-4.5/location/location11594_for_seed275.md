# The Rootworks of Chelm-Below

The city grows downward.

Not built—*grown*. The distinction matters here, in the vast cavity that opens beneath the salt plateau, where no water has flowed for six hundred thousand years. The Rootworks of Chelm-Below exist in that paradox: a metropolis of living architecture in a place where nothing should live at all.

---

You descend through the Entry Bore, a shaft carved—no, *chewed*—through three hundred meters of compressed halite. The walls still bear the spiral grooves of the founding taproots, long since calcified into something between wood and stone. Light filters down from the sodium-arc lamps bolted to those walls, their cables running alongside the massive freight elevators that carry ore and workers between surface and substrate.

The first thing you notice is the quiet. A city this size should roar, but the Rootworks absorbs sound the way the plants absorb everything else. What reaches you is muffled: the distant clank of extraction machinery, the hum of generators, the occasional voice echoing up from the residential tangles below. It sounds like being inside something's chest cavity, listening to its slow mechanical heartbeat.

The plants here don't need water. They were engineered—or evolved, or *decided*, depending on which Rootworks faction you believe—to metabolize the mineral salts directly. Their root systems penetrate the crystal matrix, drawing up sodium and potassium and trace metals, extruding them as structural polymers that form the city's bones. Every building, every bridge, every spiral staircase descending into the lower districts is made of growth-matter: fibrous, pale, faintly warm to the touch.

---

The cavity itself extends perhaps twelve kilometers at its widest point, though the boundaries are imprecise. The Rootworks is still *becoming*. You can see it at the edges, where young tendrils probe into virgin salt, where new chambers are slowly opening as the foundational plants dissolve mineral and replace it with themselves. The process takes decades. Entire neighborhoods exist as blueprints encoded in the root-tips of parent structures, waiting to unfold.

The ceiling hangs forty meters overhead in the Central Hollow, supported by pillar-plants whose trunks are wider than factory smokestacks. Bioluminescent organs cluster at their crowns, casting a perpetual blue-green twilight that the residents supplement with electrical lighting—the industrial apparatus grafted onto the living framework like prosthetics on a body that doesn't quite need them but finds them useful.

The ground—if you can call it ground—is a dense mat of interwoven root tissue, springy underfoot, perforated by access shafts and drainage channels (for the industrial runoff; the plants themselves produce no liquid waste). Streets are clearings where the growth has been trained away from walking paths. Buildings rise as denser accumulations, their walls formed from braided fibers, their interiors hollowed by controlled die-back, their windows simply gaps where growth was never permitted.

---

The industry here is extraction. The Rootworks exists because what lies beneath the salt—deeper still, in the primordial basement rock—is valuable: rare-earth deposits, crystalline formations with applications in processes you'd need a specialized education to understand. The plants do the primary excavation, their root-tips producing acids that dissolve pathways through the substrate. Human workers follow with their machines, harvesting what the plants expose, running conveyor systems and processing mills that growl and clatter in the lower industrial districts.

The fauna have adapted, or been brought, or both. Pale creatures navigate the root-corridors: mining beetles the size of dogs, bred to clear debris from the extraction faces. Soft-bodied things that live in the growth-matter itself, visible as moving bulges beneath the fibrous surface—maintenance organisms, breaking down dead tissue, redistributing nutrients. And the aerial life: moth-analogues with wings like paper, clustering around the light sources, their larvae feeding on the pollen-equivalents that the flowering structures release in slow, seasonal waves.

You see them everywhere once you start looking. The city is not *occupied* by life; the city *is* life, and other life lives in it, and the humans are simply the largest and most intentional of the symbiotes.

---

The residential districts spiral around the Central Hollow in loose, interrupted rings. Sparse—that's what strikes you. The population density would horrify a surface urbanist. People live in clusters, family groups and work-crews occupying the same architectural organism, but vast stretches of growth-matter lie between settlements. Dark tangles where the bioluminescence doesn't reach. Regions still too young to support habitation. Zones where the plants have grown in unexpected directions and the surveyors haven't yet mapped safe paths through.

The temperature holds steady at something cool—the salt acts as thermal mass, the plants generate minimal heat, and the depths have their own climate, indifferent to the surface world's seasons. People wear layered clothing, practical fabrics. The architecture doesn't accommodate central heating; you bring your warmth with you, or you generate it through work.

And there is always work. The extraction mills run in shifts, their rhythms setting the pace of life in the lower districts. The agricultural sectors—where certain plants are trained to produce edible fruits, where fungal analogues are cultivated in controlled die-back zones—operate on longer cycles, measured in weeks rather than hours. The growth-monitors walk their endless circuits through the expanding frontier, checking root-health, measuring extension rates, occasionally making the delicate interventions that guide new construction toward the blueprints the city planners have encoded.

---

There are places in the Rootworks where you can stand at the edge of a new excavation and watch the city build itself. The root-tips glow faintly as they work, a chemical byproduct of the dissolution process. They move too slowly to see directly, but if you wait—an hour, two hours—you can perceive the change. The boundary of mineral and life shifts. The future grows into the space the past once occupied.

The residents say you can hear it, if you press your ear to the growth-matter in those frontier zones. A sound below sound. The grinding of crystal into nutrient. The city digesting the world and becoming more of itself.

They say the founders came here because they were looking for somewhere that had never known water, never known the constraints of surface ecology. They wanted to prove something could be built from nothing organic, using organisms that had been taught to need nothing organic. They wanted a city that was its own ecosystem, closed and complete, independent of the wet world above.

The Rootworks is almost there. Almost complete. Almost ready to stop expanding and simply *be*.

But the root-tips keep pushing outward, and the blueprints in the parent structures keep unfolding, and somewhere in the deep code of the founding organisms, the imperative remains: *grow*. *Become*. *Continue*.

The city doesn't know how to stop.

No one is certain it should.
