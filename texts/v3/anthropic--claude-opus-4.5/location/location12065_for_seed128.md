# The Crying Wall

You find it behind a service panel in the station's lower ring—a maintenance alcove no larger than a sleeping berth, humid and dim, thrumming with sound.

The walls are not walls but layered membrane, semi-translucent and sweating. Engineered tissue stretched over a skeleton of channels and reservoirs, the whole alcove functioning as some kind of atmospheric processor or perhaps a biological filter for the station's water reclamation. The technology is old but not obsolete—grown rather than built, its original designers working in paradigms that current engineering has moved past but never fully understood. The membranes pulse faintly, rhythmically, and water beads on every surface, collecting in the shallow grooves of the floor before being drawn back into the system through capillary action.

The sound is tremendous. Not painful exactly, but *present*—a layered drone of pumps and biological processes, the deep thrum of fluid moving through channels, the higher frequencies of membrane vibration, the clicking and chirring of the alcove's inhabitants. It fills your chest. It replaces thought with sensation.

Because the space is *occupied*. Densely, intimately occupied. Small things move across every surface—maintenance fauna, bred or modified for this environment. Thumbnail-sized cleaners with iridescent shells traverse the membrane walls, their movements leaving brief dry trails that quickly bead over again. Something longer and pale, like a ribbon with too many legs, winds through the drainage grooves. In the corners where moisture collects thickest, clusters of filter-feeders open and close in slow rhythm, their translucent bodies revealing the dark threads of whatever they're processing.

The warmth is bodily. The whole alcove breathes.
