# The Terraced City of Unmoving Mist

The city rises in steps carved from the shoulder of a mountain that has never known a landslide. Each terrace is perhaps forty feet above the one below, connected by stone stairways worn smooth not by time but by the particular quality of feet that have walked them in exactly the same patterns for as long as anyone can remember. The stone is pale gray, almost blue in certain lights, and it holds the cold of altitude in a way that makes touching it feel like pressing your palm against deep water.

Mist inhabits the spaces between terraces. Not fog that rolls in and out, but mist that simply *is*—occupying the middle air in soft curtains that never thicken into rain, never thin into clarity. Moisture beads on every surface: on the carved lintels of doorways, on the woolen cloaks of inhabitants, on the pale leaves of the plants that grow in careful beds along each terrace's edge. The condensation neither drips nor dries. It persists.

---

The vegetation is strange in its abundance. Moss carpets every horizontal surface not actively walked upon, a deep green that verges on blue, soft enough to sleep on. Ferns uncurl from cracks in the terrace walls, their fronds heavy with droplets that catch what light filters through the permanent overcast. In the planting beds, residents cultivate a kind of broad-leafed vegetable with stems like celery and leaves that taste of mineral water. The plants grow lushly but slowly—or perhaps they simply maintain, neither bolting to seed nor withering back, holding their green perpetually.

There are trees, though not many. They grow in a grove on the highest terrace, twisted low by a wind that no longer blows. Their bark is silver-gray, their leaves small and waxy, and they bear a fruit like a pale plum that is always exactly ripe, never falling, waiting to be picked but patient about it. Birds nest in these trees—small things with wet-looking feathers and calls like water drops striking stone—and they raise their young in nests that never need rebuilding, because nothing here decays enough to require it.

---

The city itself is extensive, spreading across perhaps two dozen major terraces and countless smaller ones, built into the mountain's face over generations that no one counts anymore. The architecture is stone and timber and woven fiber, roofs shingled with overlapping plates of slate, walls thick against a cold that is always present but never deepens into winter. There are no metal hinges; doors pivot on stone pins socketed into stone frames. Tools are bone and shaped flint and fire-hardened wood, though fire itself is rare here—the damp makes it difficult, and the inhabitants have learned to eat their food prepared in other ways, fermented and cured and softened in the mineral-rich water that seeps from the mountain's heart.

The streets, if they can be called that, are the terraces themselves and the narrow passages that run behind buildings where the mountain's raw face shows through. People move through them at a walking pace that suggests neither urgency nor leisure. There are marketplaces, though the word implies more exchange than actually occurs—goods are distributed, yes, but according to patterns so long-established that they function more like circulation than commerce.

---

What populates this place: the small wet-feathered birds, yes, and also something larger that moves in the mist between terraces. Not threatening—nothing here threatens—but present. A kind of mountain ungulate with hooves adapted to wet stone, with eyes that gleam pale in the diffuse light. They move in small herds along the edges of the city, grazing on moss, and the inhabitants neither hunt them nor herd them. The animals are simply there, as the mist is there, as the stone is there.

Insects exist in careful populations. A kind of moth with wings like damp paper. Beetles that live in the moss and make a sound like tiny bells when they move. Flying things that are not quite flies, not quite anything else, that hover in the mist and seem to feed on the moisture itself.

And in the water that pools in cisterns carved into each terrace—fed by condensation that flows through carved channels, never overflowing, never running dry—there are small pale creatures with too many legs that the inhabitants consider neither food nor pet but something else, something that belongs to the water the way the birds belong to the trees.

---

Sound here is a rare thing, a small thing. The mist absorbs it. Conversations happen at close range, voices soft not from secrecy but from the acoustic reality that louder speech simply dissipates into the wet air. The beetles make their bell-sounds. Water drips, but always in the same places, at the same intervals, a rhythm so consistent it becomes indistinguishable from silence. Occasionally a bird calls out—a single clear note that seems to hang in the air longer than physics should allow—and then the quiet reasserts itself.

Footsteps on wet stone. The soft sound of cloth against cloth. A child's laugh, muted by distance and moisture. These are the sounds of the city, and they are always the same sounds, always at the same intervals, because nothing here changes.

---

The light filters through cloud cover that has been exactly this gray for longer than memory. Not dark—you can see clearly enough to work, to walk, to read the simple pictographic script the inhabitants carve into wayfinding stones. But directionless, without shadow, a light that seems to emanate from the air itself rather than any particular source. Day and night must differ, but the difference is subtle—a deepening of the gray, a slight cooling of the already cool air, and then a lightening again, and no one remarks on it because it has been this way always and will be this way always.

The inhabitants speak of this constancy not as curse or blessing but as fact, as they might speak of stone being hard or water being wet. They have songs, old ones, passed down in patterns that never vary, and the songs do not speak of change because there is no change to speak of. History here is not a timeline but a texture—the way things are, the way things have been, the way things will continue to be.

---

There is something unusual about the geometry of the terraces, if you look closely. The angles don't quite add up. A stairway that should take you up two terraces somehow deposits you on the same level you started from. A building that seems to face east from the front faces east from the back as well. The mountain itself, if you try to trace its shape against the sky, refuses to resolve into a coherent silhouette. This is not spoken of. It is perhaps not even consciously noticed, so accustomed are the inhabitants to navigating by landmark and habit rather than by logic.

Water flows in directions that seem to change depending on where you're standing. The mist is thicker in places that are, by any reasonable measure, exposed to open air, and thinner in sheltered courtyards. A fruit picked from the silver-barked trees weighs nothing in your hand until you bite into it, and then it weighs exactly what a fruit should weigh, as if the act of attention grants it substance.

But these are small things, almost impossible to catch directly, noticed only in peripheral vision or in the strange dreams the altitude and the damp and the sameness sometimes bring. The city is a city. Its people are people. The mountain is a mountain, and the mist is only mist, and if the edges of things sometimes seem softer than they should be, well. This is a soft place. It has always been a soft place. It will always be a soft place.

Nothing here is wrong. Nothing here will ever be wrong.

The mist holds everything in place, and the everything lets itself be held.
