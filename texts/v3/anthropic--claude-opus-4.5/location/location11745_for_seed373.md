# The Screaming Garden

The first thing you understand is that the plants are the sound.

Not rustling, not wind-through-leaves, but *voice*—a roar of botanical intent so loud it presses against your chest like a hand. The chamber is perhaps a hundred meters across, domed by a ceiling of pale synthetic nacre that sweats faint condensation despite the cool air, and every surface that isn't walkway or pillar is covered in vegetation that will not stop moving, growing, dying, growing again.

They call this a garden. The word feels inadequate.

---

The flora here evolved—or was engineered, or dreamed itself into being—on a world with no liquid water. Their chemistry runs on something else: a pale gas that seeps from vents in the floor, visible only as a faint pearlescent shimmer where it pools in hollows. The plants drink it through leaves that are more membrane than matter, tissues so thin you can see the machinery of their cells when the light catches right—and there is machinery there, structures too regular for pure biology, helical scaffolding at scales that shouldn't be visible to the naked eye but somehow are.

They grow fast. Terrifyingly fast.

A vine as thick as your arm erupts from a crack in the flooring, climbs three meters in the time it takes you to register motion, flowers in a burst of geometric petals the color of old copper, and then *screams*—a sound like tearing metal, like a choir holding a note past the point of breath, like something vast and satisfied. The flower's center spirals open and releases a cloud of spores that catch the light like static discharge, and within seconds the vine is already browning, curling, collapsing back into the substrate even as three more surge up beside it.

The sound never stops. It layers. Hundreds of plants cycling through their compressed lives, each one vocalizing its growth and its death, and the acoustics of the domed chamber turn it into a wall of noise that seems to have texture, temperature, *intent*.

---

The fauna moves through this chaos like fish through water.

They are abundant because the garden feeds them endlessly—fallen matter, spore-clouds, the bodies of their own dead, all of it cycling so fast that predation becomes almost cooperative, a dance rather than a hunt. You see creatures like translucent salamanders with too many joints, their bodies flexing in ways that suggest their skeletons are suggestions rather than structures. You see something that might be a bird if birds were made of folded paper and hunger, diving through a thicket of reed-like growths that whistle in frequencies that make your teeth ache. You see beetles the size of your palm, their carapaces shifting through colors that don't quite correspond to the visible spectrum, and they move in patterns that seem almost linguistic—not random foraging, but *communication*, a constant chatter of position and resource that adds its own clicking rhythm to the din.

Nothing here holds still. The garden won't let it.

---

The built structures are few and strange.

A single spire rises from the garden's center, perhaps thirty meters tall, made of something that looks like glass but isn't—it flexes slightly in response to the plant-roar, as if it's listening, as if it was designed to resonate. There are panels on its surface that glow with symbols in a script you don't recognize, though some of the curves remind you of the helical structures in the plant-cells, the same mathematical signature repeating across scales.

Scattered around the edges: platforms of the same material, some intact, some cracked, some entirely overgrown. The plants seem to avoid the intact ones—or perhaps the intact ones are the ones the plants have chosen to avoid, for reasons of their own. On one platform you can make out what might have been instruments, or terminals, or altars, now fused with a century's worth of growth that has died and been reborn so many times the layers have fossilized into something like amber.

Someone built this. Someone brought these plants here, gave them this domed chamber and its gas-vents and its nacreous sky, and then—

What? Left? Became part of it? Ascended to some state where the distinction stopped mattering?

---

The temperature stays cool, almost refrigerated, and you realize the chill must be deliberate—a brake on the system, a throttle on the growth. Imagine this place warm. Imagine the vines growing faster, the flowers screaming louder, the spore-clouds so thick you couldn't see your own hands. The cold is mercy. The cold is what keeps the garden from eating itself.

A creature that might be a mammal if mammals could be made of interlocking geometric plates shuffles past your feet, pauses, tilts what passes for its head toward the central spire. The symbols on the spire pulse in a pattern you almost recognize, and the creature moves on, satisfied or summoned, you can't tell which.

The sound presses in. The plants keep growing. The garden has been here longer than you can fathom, balanced on the knife-edge of its own turbulence, and it will be here long after you leave—if the door you came through is still there, if the path hasn't already grown over, if the screaming hasn't already taught you how to become something that doesn't need to leave.

You step onto one of the avoidance-platforms. The sound doesn't quiet, but it changes pitch, becomes almost harmonious for a moment, as if the garden has noticed you and decided—for now—to let you stay.
