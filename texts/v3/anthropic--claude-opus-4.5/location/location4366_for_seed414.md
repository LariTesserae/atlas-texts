# The Breathing Cleft

You find it by accident—a crack in the high plateau, no wider than your shoulders, where the basalt has pulled apart like lips parting to speak. The opening exhales warmth. You descend.

The cleft drops fifteen meters through raw rock that hasn't yet decided what it wants to become. The walls are soft in places,iteiteiteite likeite not quite crystallized, not quiteite settled—as if the stone remembers being molten and hasn't fully committed to solidity. Your fingers leave faint impressions that might last centuries or might smooth themselves away by morning. This is young geology, still tender, still forming its opinions.

At the bottom: a chamber the size of a modest chapel, lit by bioluminescence that pulses in slow tidal rhythms. The light comes from colonies of something between lichen and something else—flat organisms the color of weak tea that cling to the damp ceiling and emit a glow that strengthens and fades over minutes-long cycles. In their dim yellow illumination, you can see the walls sweat. Moisture beads and runs in thin rivulets, collecting in a series of shallow basins worn into the floor—not by water alone but by the countless bodies that have come here to drink.

---

The sound reaches you before you understand its source: a layered chorus of clicking, whistling, the papery rustle of membranes, the wet percussion of throats inflating and deflating. The chamber amplifies everything. Sound here is a presence, a pressure against your eardrums.

They emerge from crevices you hadn't noticed—small creatures, mostly, though *small* covers considerable range. Salamanders the length of your thumb with skin so thin you can see their organs pulsing. Beetles with shells that haven't hardened yet, translucent amber over pale bodies. A snake as thick as your wrist and entirely eyeless, navigating by heat and vibration, sliding past your ankle with complete disinterest. Moths with wingspans no bigger than coins, but hundreds of them, their collective flutter creating a sound like shuffling cards.

And the frogs. The frogs are why the place is loud.

They're the size of your palm, spotted gray and rust, with throat sacs that inflate to twice their body volume. When they call, the sound bounces off the raw rock and multiplies. Three frogs calling becomes thirty. Thirty becomes three hundred. The chamber throbs with it. Your ribs vibrate sympathetically.

---

There are marks here, if you look. Handholds worn into the descent—subtle, almost geological, but too regular. A flat stone near the largest pool, smoothed by sitting. A small pile of rocks in one corner, stacked with what might be intention, though the intention has long since departed. Whoever came here came without tools, without fire, without anything that would outlast their presence by much. They drank from the pools. They listened to the frogs. Perhaps they spoke to the soft walls, left handprints that were absorbed back into the stone.

The warmth comes from below—deep heat rising through fissures, the plateau's volcanic bones still cooling from some ancient eruption. It keeps the chamber at a temperature that feels like held breath, like the moment before summer breaks. The few plants that grow here are pale things, thin-stemmed, reaching toward the bioluminescent ceiling with leaves like paper. They grow slowly. Everything here grows slowly, changes slowly, the frogs' generations measured in decades, the lichen's expansion measured in centuries.

But the sound—the sound is *now*, insistent, alive, a constant reminder that patience and stillness are not the same as silence.

You could stay an hour. You could stay a week. The cleft would hold you, feed you moisture, keep you warm. The creatures would grow accustomed to your breathing, your heartbeat, would incorporate you into the rhythm of the place.

Eventually you would start to hear the patterns in the chorus—which frog leads, which follows, how the beetles' clicking syncs with the light's slow pulse.

Eventually you might understand what the soft walls are becoming.
