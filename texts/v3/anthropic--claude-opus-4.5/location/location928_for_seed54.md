# The Nursery Vaults of Keth-Somnar

The city rises in darkness, and the darkness is wet.

You stand on a transit ridge—a spine of engineered basite that runs between two vast depressions, each filled with structures that descend rather than climb. The cold presses against your thermal layer like a hand, patient and heavy. There is no sun here. There has never been a sun here. The world of Keth-Somnar orbits nothing; it drifts in the interstellar medium, a rogue planet that captured its inhabitants rather than growing them.

The city exists in the perpetual twilight of bioluminescent fauna—millions upon millions of creatures whose bodies produce the only light this world has ever needed.

---

Below you, to the left, the Gestation Wells sink into the planetary crust. Each well is perhaps three hundred meters across, ringed with platforms and walkways of that same dark basite, connected by suspension bridges that sway almost imperceptibly in the convection currents rising from below. The wells are not empty. They are *full*—densely, impossibly full—of larvae.

The larvae of Keth-Somnar's dominant species resemble, at first glance, enormous translucent worms, each perhaps two meters long at this stage of development. But they are not worms. Their bodies contain structures that fold through more dimensions than you can perceive directly; you see only the three-dimensional cross-section of something larger, something that pupates partly *elsewhere*. They hang suspended in a mucoid medium that weeps constantly from the well walls—the planetary crust here is porous with thermal aquifers, and the city's infrastructure channels this water into the wells through systems you cannot fully see.

The larvae glow. Each one carries bioluminescent organs that pulse in slow, complex patterns—blues and greens and a color you don't have a name for, something that sits between ultraviolet and visible spectrum and makes your eyes water. There are thousands of them in each well, packed close enough that their light bleeds together, creating the shifting illumination that lets you see anything at all.

They are loud.

The sound is not screaming, exactly. It is more like singing, if singing could happen in frequencies that bypass your ears and settle directly into your chest cavity. The larvae communicate constantly—with each other, with their tenders, with the city itself—through vibrations that travel through the mucoid medium, through the basite infrastructure, through your bones. The chorus is massive. It layers and interfolds. It doesn't stop.

---

To your right, the other depression holds the Molt-Cradles: structures like inverted domes, built from a material that seems to absorb what little light reaches it. These are where the larvae go when they're ready to transform. You can see tenders moving between them—adult members of the species, though "adult" is a simplification of a lifecycle that includes seventeen recognized stages. They walk on limbs that shouldn't support their mass; they carry tools that seem to work by convincing matter to rearrange itself. Their bodies are matte black, absorbing the larval light, but their eyes glow with captured luminescence.

The tenders are everywhere. Hundreds of them, visible from where you stand. They move in patterns that seem random until you watch long enough to recognize the underlying structure—a slow, patient choreography of care. They check the chemical composition of the mucoid medium. They adjust the temperature differentials between wells. They remove larvae that have died and carry them elsewhere, to processing facilities you can't see from here.

The dead ones still glow for a while.

---

The city is young. You can tell because some of the structures are still growing.

Not being built—*growing*. The basite extrudes from seed-nodes that the founders placed when they first arrived here, countless generations ago. The material crystallizes according to programmed patterns, responding to population pressures and resource flows, extending new platforms and bridges and well-rings as the colony expands. The process is slow—you could watch for hours and see no visible change—but if you look closely at the edges of the newest structures, you can see where the surface is still rough, still porous, still drinking in minerals from the thermal water that seeps through everything.

The city is building itself around the larvae because the larvae are the point. Everything else—the transit ridges, the tender quarters, the chemical processing plants that glow faintly in the distance with their own captive organisms—exists to support this single purpose: the raising of young.

---

The cold deepens. A weather front is moving through—not wind, exactly, but a pressure change that carries denser, colder gas down from the upper atmosphere. The larvae respond by glowing brighter, generating metabolic heat, and their song shifts into a lower register. The tenders move faster, checking thermal regulators, adjusting the composition of the mucoid medium to prevent crystallization.

You hear, beneath the larval chorus, the infrastructure-sounds of the city: the hum of systems you don't understand, the click and shudder of matter being convinced to move against its preferences, the constant wet sound of water seeping through porous stone and being channeled into the wells.

Something moves in the darkness beyond the city's edge. Something large. The larvae in the nearest well all turn—*turn*, in unison, their eyeless faces orienting toward that distant motion—and for a moment their song becomes a single sustained note.

Then it passes, and the chorus fragments back into its usual complexity, and the city continues its slow work of bringing new life into being.

The darkness presses close. The water seeps. The larvae glow and sing and grow toward whatever they will become.
