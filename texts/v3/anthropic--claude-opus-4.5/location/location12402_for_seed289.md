# The Singing Cyst

Beneath the cracked foundation of an abandoned water treatment facility, where a concrete inspection shaft dead-ends into bedrock, there is a cavity the size of a child's coffin. The walls glow.

The light comes from everywhere at once—a dense, biological luminescence packed so tightly that shadows have no room to form. Bioluminescent organisms coat every available surface in overlapping colonies: pale blue fungi no larger than pinheads, amber bacterial films, white mycelia that pulse with their own cold fire. They have filled this space completely, leaving no stone visible, no dark corner, no gap between one glowing thing and the next. The brightness is surgical, relentless, the kind of light that has no source because it *is* the source, emanating from a thousand thousand tiny lives pressed body to body in the dark.

The cavity drips. Condensation forms on the concrete cap above—moisture drawn down through hairline fractures in the foundation, collecting and falling in irregular drops that the organisms drink before they can pool. Each drop strikes a different surface, a different colony, and each colony *sings back*.

This is what makes the space unbearable to remain near for long: the sound. Not loud in volume but loud in *presence*—a dense, layered drone of biological frequencies that fill the small chamber completely. The fungi emit ultrasonic clicks that drop just barely into human hearing range. The bacterial films vibrate at frequencies that resonate in tooth enamel. Somewhere in the packed mass, something makes a sound like a sustained exhalation, a note that never wavers, never shifts, never ends. The acoustic density matches the visual density matches the biological density: saturation, completeness, no room for silence.

Crawling through this compressed ecosystem—over it, under it, between the hair-thin gaps in the luminescent carpet—are creatures that should not work. Translucent worms with too many segments, their bodies visible only as shadows moving across the glow. Pale arthropods the size of rice grains, their carapaces prismatic, walking inverted on the ceiling. Something that might be a flatworm or might be a colonial organism moves in peristaltic waves across a fungal mat, feeding or being fed upon or engaged in some exchange that has no name in surface biology. They are abundant in the way that organisms in a petri dish are abundant—packed, thriving, occupying every possible niche in a space that offers almost nothing but light and occasional water and each other.

The chamber is young. The bedrock here fractured only recently—geologically speaking, perhaps a century, perhaps less—when the water treatment facility's settling cracked something deep. The cavity is still forming, still finding its final shape. The walls are raw, the stone surfaces not yet smoothed by the ages of microbial activity that will eventually round every edge. The colonies arrived first as spores, then as pioneers, then as a civilization compressed into a space smaller than a bread box. They are building something. The chamber itself is the construction site, the organisms both workers and material, their bodies and their waste products slowly, slowly altering the chemistry of the stone.

But nothing changes.

This is the strangeness at the heart of the Singing Cyst: it has reached equilibrium. The system is complete. The populations have stabilized at exactly the density the space can support. The condensation falls at exactly the rate the colonies require. The organisms feed on each other in perfect closed loops, nothing wasted, nothing accumulated, nothing lost. The sound does not modulate because the conditions that produce it do not shift. The light does not flicker because the colonies do not fluctuate. Time moves through this space without leaving marks.

The concrete inspection shaft above still bears the faded stenciled numbers of municipal workers—**Section 7-C, Do Not Enter**—but the hatch has rusted shut. The facility above was decommissioned decades ago. No one comes here. No one knows this chamber exists, this impossible terrarium suspended in perfect stasis beneath the skin of the earth.

If you press your ear to the ground in the right place—in the overgrown lot where the treatment facility used to stand, now just concrete pads and chain-link—you can hear it. A hum. A drone. The sound of a thousand small things singing in unison in a space too bright and too full and too strange to belong to the world above.

It has always sounded exactly like this.

It will always sound exactly like this.
