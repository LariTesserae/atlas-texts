# The Scoured Plateau of the Final Altitude

The plateau extends beyond the reach of sight, a tabletop of stone so ancient it has forgotten what mountains once rose here. This is the roof of a continent—perhaps the roof of the world—a flat immensity lifted three miles into a sky the color of pale brass. The air is thin and cool, carrying almost no moisture, and the stone beneath your feet has been polished by epochs of wind into something like frozen water, grey-brown and faintly translucent at the edges where it catches light.

There is no water here. Not in any crevice, not in any hollow, not in the atmosphere. The dryness is absolute, a condition rather than an absence. The stone has achieved a kind of terminal smoothness, scoured by time and wind until it resembles the surface of a worn tooth. In places, the erosion has exposed layers of different mineral density, creating vast striations that run for dozens of miles—pale cream against iron grey, rust-orange threading through charcoal—like the growth rings of a planet laid bare.

But the plateau is not still.

---

Above you, the sky churns. Atmospheric rivers of different densities collide and twist around each other, visible as faint differentiations in the brass-colored light. The plateau is so vast and so elevated that it generates its own weather system, but this weather has nothing to do with rain or clouds as you understand them. Instead, you witness thermal cells the size of cities, invisible except where they bend light at their boundaries, rising and falling in patterns that shift every few minutes. The air moves in silence—or near-silence—a whisper of pressure change against your ears, a subsonic thrum you feel more than hear.

The turbulence is constant. Not violent in the way of storms, but restless, recursive, a system that has been seeking equilibrium for so long it has forgotten what equilibrium means. Dust devils made of nothing but temperature gradients spin up and dissipate within seconds. Waves of air pressure ripple across the plateau's surface, and you can see them pass through the stone's translucency—shadows moving through the mineral layers below, as though something is swimming through the rock itself. It is only light, refracted and bent, but the effect is unsettling.

---

The structures appear at intervals that suggest intention without revealing logic.

They are small—rarely taller than a person, often half-buried in the polished stone as though the plateau has slowly absorbed them over uncountable years. Their material is difficult to identify. Some appear to be metal of a color you cannot name, somewhere between copper and the inside of a shell. Others seem to be made of compressed air, or of frozen mathematics: geometries that remain stable despite having no apparent physical structure, edges that exist without surfaces to connect.

When you approach one, you notice it responds to the atmospheric turbulence in ways the plateau does not. The air-pressure waves that pass harmlessly over the stone seem to *interact* with the structure—feeding into it, perhaps, or emerging from it. You cannot tell which. The structure makes no sound, but you feel a faint warmth near it, or a faint cold, and the sensation changes depending on which direction the wind is not blowing from.

These are not ruins. They are not active, either. They exist in some third state—quiescent technology, perhaps, or technology that operates on timescales so long that a human lifetime would not be enough to observe a single cycle. Whatever purpose they serve, they have been serving it since before your species existed, and they will continue long after.

---

Life here is rare but not absent.

You see them sometimes at the edge of vision: creatures like folded paper, like origami made of chitin or cartilage, drifting on the thermal gradients. They do not fly so much as *navigate*—adjusting their angles to ride the pressure differentials, moving in three dimensions the way a fish moves through water. They are silent. They seem to eat nothing. Perhaps they feed on the turbulence itself, on the energy differentials, on the information contained in the air's endless reorganization.

One passes close enough for you to see its surface: faceted, reflective, covered in tiny structures that might be sensors or might be mouths. It does not acknowledge you. It is occupied with the wind.

---

The light is strange but not wrong. The brass-colored sky admits illumination evenly, without obvious source, and the polished stone catches and holds it. Shadows are soft-edged and directionless. Nothing glitters; nothing is dark. The plateau exists in a state of permanent gentle visibility, every detail equally available to the eye.

You could walk for days and see no change. You could walk for weeks and find only more plateau, more buried structures, more folded creatures riding the invisible rivers. The turbulence would continue—the air reorganizing itself, the pressure waves rippling, the thermal cells rising and falling in their meaningless ballet.

This is the oldest place. This is where geography comes to rest. And yet it will not be still—cannot be still—because the physics of this altitude and this dryness and this vastness have conspired to create perpetual motion from nothing. The plateau has been scoured down to its final form, and its final form is chaos.
