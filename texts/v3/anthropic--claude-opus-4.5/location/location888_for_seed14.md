# The Abyssal Migration

The darkness here is old. Not the darkness of an unlit room, which waits for light to arrive, but the darkness of a place that finished with light geological ages ago—a darkness that has settled into itself like silt into the deepest trenches.

You are below. Far below. The weight of water above you is incomprehensible, a column of cold black ocean pressing down with the patience of mountains. There is no surface here in any meaningful sense—the surface is a rumor, a theoretical boundary so distant it belongs to another world entirely. What exists is depth, and depth, and more depth still, and you are suspended somewhere in the lower reaches of it, where the water has been water for so long it has forgotten how to be anything else.

But the water moves.

This is the turbulence: not the frothy chaos of storms, but something vaster and slower and more terrible. Currents older than species twist through the abyss, rivers within the ocean that flow sideways and down and in directions that seem to have nothing to do with the shape of the world above. They carry cold with them—the cold of polar depths, of waters that sank centuries ago near ice sheets and have been traveling ever since through the planetary dark. Where these currents meet, they do not mix peacefully. They shear against each other, create rolling walls of pressure differential, spinning vortices that extend for miles and persist for decades.

The density of movement here is overwhelming. Every cubic meter of water is doing something—sliding, rotating, compressing, exchanging heat with its neighbors in transactions too slow for human perception but relentless across time. Parcels of water from different ages and different oceans pass through each other like ghosts, each carrying its own cargo of dissolved minerals, its own temperature signature, its own ancient chemical memory. The currents layer and braid and fold back on themselves. There is no stillness anywhere.

And through all of this, pressed close by the weight of everything above: life. Sparse, yes—but sparse the way stars are sparse, which means scattered across immensity but still numbering in the billions. The creatures here are rare in any given location but the location extends in all directions farther than seeing could ever reach, and so rareness becomes a kind of population.

What moves through the migrating currents are things shaped by pressure and darkness into forms that barely remember the architectural plans of surface life. Pale worms as long as arms, segmented and eyeless, riding the thermal boundaries where cold meets colder. Jellied masses that pulse with bioluminescent organs so dim they register more as suggestions than light—brief blue-green glimmers that appear and fade in your peripheral vision, never where you look directly. Something larger passing at distance, its outline visible only as a moving absence, a shape that occludes even the faint bacterial glow of the water itself.

The floor is down there somewhere—a vast plain of sediment that has been accumulating since this ocean was young. Marine snow falls constantly from above, the dead and dying matter of all the upper waters drifting down through miles of darkness to settle into ooze. The ooze is ancient beyond reckoning, compacted in its lower layers into something approaching stone, the fossil record of a hundred million years of death pressed flat. Nothing grows from it. Occasionally the currents scour patches bare, revealing pale clay, gray-green and slick, before the sediment resettles.

The sound is the sound of pressure: a deep, subsonic hum that is less heard than felt, the voice of the water column singing to itself. Occasionally something else—a distant groan that might be geological, might be biological, might be the cry of current shearing against current somewhere miles away. These sounds travel strangely, arrive from unexpected directions, fade before they can be identified.

No one has built anything here. No one has touched this place with tools more sophisticated than the crude mineral structures some of the worms secrete around themselves—tubes of calcium carbonate, barely more than crystallized reflex. The primitive technology of survival: a shell, a mucus coating, the ability to generate enough faint light to find a mate in the endless dark.

The currents are shifting now—you can feel it, a change in the pressure against whatever you imagine yourself to be in this place. One of the great deep rivers is turning, responding to temperature changes that began decades ago in waters you will never see. The turbulence intensifies. Somewhere in the distance, the migration continues: billions of small pale things riding the ancient conveyor of the abyss, moving through darkness toward darkness, their journey measured in years and thousands of miles, all of it happening in profound quiet, in profound cold, in the packed and restless stillness of the deep.
