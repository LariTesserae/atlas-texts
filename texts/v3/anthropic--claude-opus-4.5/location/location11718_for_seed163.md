# The Roaring Nursery

The plateau is still being born.

You stand at the edge of a highland basin—perhaps three valleys wide, ringed by young peaks that haven't yet learned their final shapes. The rock beneath everything is fresh, pale, recently thrust up from deeper earth, and it weeps. Water seeps from every exposed stone face, runs in sheets down raw cliff walls, pools in depressions that haven't yet decided if they're lakes or meadows. The whole landscape is slick, gleaming, catching the fierce high-altitude light and throwing it back in ten thousand wet mirrors.

And covering everything—claiming everything—the moss.

Not moss as groundcover. Moss as *world*. Moss as the fundamental material from which this place is constructing itself. It grows in carpets three feet thick, in hanging curtains from rock overhangs, in great quilted expanses that undulate across the basin floor like frozen green waves. Where water runs, the moss drinks it and swells into saturated cushions you could sink an arm into. Where rock juts up, the moss climbs it, softens it, begins the patient work of making it into something else. The colors run from silver-green to deep emerald to a yellow-gold that blazes under the bright sky, and the whole living mass breathes moisture back into the air, feeds the perpetual cycle of condensation and seepage that keeps everything wet.

The sound is extraordinary.

From somewhere beneath the moss-floor comes a deep industrial grinding—the voice of the earth still settling, still finding its shape. It resonates through the wet rock, through the spongy plant-mass, emerges as a constant bass thrum you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. Layered over this: the roar of water, dozens of temporary streams rushing through channels that barely exist yet, cutting new paths, abandoning old ones, the whole drainage pattern of the plateau still in flux. And then the wind—the high cold wind that comes tearing across the basin, finding no trees tall enough to slow it, howling through gaps in the young mountains, shaking water from every moss-frond in glittering curtains.

You have to shout to be heard here. Even your thoughts feel quieter than the landscape.

Scattered across the basin, you can see the pylons.

They're industrial-age work—iron or steel, painted red once but now rust-streaked and moss-claimed, their bases sunk deep into the wet ground. Power lines stretch between them, though some have sagged or snapped, hanging in loose curves that the moss is already beginning to explore. A pumping station sits at the basin's eastern edge, half-swallowed by growth, its engine still running—that grinding beneath your feet comes partly from here, from machines that someone built to drain this place, or harness it, or measure it. They failed, or succeeded, or were simply absorbed into the larger process of the plateau becoming itself.

A small hut nearby, corrugated metal, door standing open. Inside: dials, levers, a chair with moss growing up through its seat cushion. Logbooks swollen with moisture, pages fused together. Someone worked here once, monitoring something. The machinery continues without them.

The light is almost violent. At this elevation, the air is thin enough that the sun comes through sharp and unfiltered, illuminating every water droplet, every moss-filament, every exposed mineral vein in the young rock. No shadows here are truly dark. Even the hollows glow with reflected green.

You scan for animals and find almost nothing. A single bird, small and grey, riding a thermal near the eastern peaks. Something that might be a salamander in one of the deeper pools—a flicker of movement, gone before you can focus. The moss is too dominant, the water too cold, the noise too overwhelming. Life here means plant life, means the slow patient colonization of raw stone, means waiting out centuries while the mountains finish rising and the drainage patterns stabilize and the grinding underneath finally falls silent.

But that silence is generations away. For now, the plateau roars its becoming, pours water from every surface, builds itself in moss and mineral and the patient accumulation of organic matter that will someday—long after the pylons rust to nothing, long after the pumping station finally stops—become soil.

You are witnessing something very young, and very loud, and very alive in its vegetable patience.

The light doesn't let you look away.
