# The Woven Deeps

The ocean here is not water but thread—countless billions of fibers drawn through the darkness by hands that finished their work so long ago that the work itself has become the world. They called it weaving, once. Now it simply is.

You descend through layers of fabric that have no surface, no beginning. The uppermost reaches are loose gauze, threads spaced wide enough that small currents move between them, carrying the faint cold that permeates everything. Light does not reach here from any sun; what little glow exists comes from phosphorescent dyes worked into certain strands generations past, now fading to the dimmest blue-green suggestion of illumination. Enough to know that darkness surrounds you. Enough to see your own hands, barely.

The threads thicken as you go deeper. Gauze becomes muslin becomes canvas becomes something denser still, fibers packed so close they hold their own pressure, their own weight of textile above pressing down. The cold intensifies—not freezing, but a deep permanent chill that has settled into the weave itself, stored in the spaces between threads like memory stored in bone.

This is the nature of the place: everything constructed, nothing natural, and yet so old that the distinction has worn away. The looms that made this world were primitive—wooden frames, hand-thrown shuttles, warp and weft drawn tight by human effort alone. No machine touch. Every inch of this submerged immensity passed through fingers, was counted, was intentional. The fiber itself was grown, somewhere, somewhen—but that place is legend now. What remains is the made thing. The making finished. The made thing becoming everything.

---

Vegetation grows here, impossibly. Lush even in darkness, even in cold, even in a medium that is not water but the memory of water translated into cotton and linen and hemp. The plants have adapted across eons to this woven substrate. Their roots are themselves threadlike, fine as silk, working between the fibers of the world-fabric, drawing whatever moisture collects in the perpetual damp chill. Their stems are pale, nearly translucent, bioluminescent only at the edges where they photosynthesize the dim glow of ancient dyes. Leaves spread flat against the weave like pressed flowers, silver-green, collecting what light exists across maximum surface area.

In the denser middle depths, whole forests of this growth mat together—layers of overlapping fronds creating a secondary architecture within the primary one. You push through curtains of cold wet leaves, each one the size of your palm, smooth as waxed paper, leaving traces of luminescence on your skin. The vegetation is silent. No rustling, because there is no wind, only the slow pressure-shifts of textile current. No birdsong, no insect hum. The absence of fauna is total and has been for so long that the plants themselves have forgotten what pollination meant. They reproduce by fragmentation now, pieces of themselves breaking free to drift downward and root elsewhere in the endless weave.

---

The scale is incomprehensible. You have been descending for what feels like days, and you have not reached bottom, because there is no bottom—or rather, the bottom is so far below that it exists only in theory, in the faith that the original weavers must have begun somewhere. The fabric extends in all directions beyond the reach of any sense. Whole weather systems form in the gaps between thread-densities: thermal gradients where the chill deepens create slow circulations, currents of cold carrying seeds and spores and fragments of frayed fiber through darkness that goes on forever. You could travel for years and not reach an edge. Perhaps there is no edge. Perhaps the weavers worked in a sphere, or worked until they died and their children continued and their children after that until the work enclosed the world it was woven upon.

The erosion here is not geological but temporal. The oldest threads are soft as cobweb, fibers separated and frayed by the sheer weight of time passing through them. In places the weave has worn through entirely, creating voids—pockets of true darkness where nothing is, where you float in absence, the fabric-walls around you the only proof that anything exists at all. These voids are sacred, or feared, or both. The distinction has eroded too.

Sound moves strangely through thread. Mostly there is silence—the damp muffling of fiber packed close, absorbing vibration before it can travel far. But in places where the weave runs parallel and tight, sound channels unexpectedly, and you might hear a distant thrumming: the great slow settling of the fabric-mass above, compressing imperceptibly under its own weight, threads grinding against threads in frequencies below human hearing, felt in the chest as a kind of permanent unease. The sound of patience. The sound of time passing through material that will outlast it.

---

Here and there you find the tools. Shuttles carved from bone, smooth with handling. Heddles of shaped wood, the grain long since gone dark. Drop spindles trailing remnant fiber. They are embedded in the weave itself—not lost, but left. When the work was done, the workers set down their tools and became part of what they had made. There are no bodies. The bodies became thread eventually, one way or another. But the tools remain, primitive and perfect, the only hard objects in a world gone soft with age.

You understand, after a while, that the weave itself is still settling. Still finding its final form. The ancient erosion continues: threads sliding against threads, densities shifting downward, the whole vast textile sinking into itself across timescales that make human life a flicker. In another million years the gauze above will be canvas. The canvas will be felt. The felt will be something denser still, something that has no name yet, something the original weavers could not have imagined but worked toward anyway, patient as the cold, patient as the silence, patient as the lush pale plants that spread their leaves toward dying light and wait for nothing at all.
