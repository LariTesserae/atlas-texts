# The Bower of the Swarm-Tenders

You find it by accident—a gap in the cliff face no wider than your shoulders, hidden behind a curtain of hanging rootlets that have calcified into something almost like beaded chains over centuries of mineral seep. The roots click against each other in the wind that pours up from the valley far below, and you push through them into a space that feels impossible: a hollow in the mountain's body, perhaps forty feet across, entirely lined with living green.

The chamber is a geode of vegetation. Every surface—the curving walls that arch overhead, the floor that dips into a shallow bowl, the irregular shelves and ledges where the rock once fractured—all of it has been colonized so thoroughly that you cannot see stone anywhere. Mosses in seventeen shades of green form the base layer, thick as carpet, cool and slightly damp beneath your hands. From this substrate rise delicate ferns with fronds so fine they seem like green smoke, and cushion plants that have grown here so long they've formed mounded shapes like furniture, like the abandoned beds of some creature that once curled here to sleep.

But you are not alone. You have never been less alone.

---

The swarm notices you in the first heartbeat.

They pour from the moss—no, they *are* the moss, they are woven through it, ten thousand bodies the size of your thumbnail moving in currents you cannot predict. They are some relation to beetles, you think, but softer, their carapaces the exact color of the vegetation they tend. They flow up the walls in braided rivers. They cascade down from the ceiling where light pours in through a crack, a single brilliant shaft of mountain sun that illuminates the chamber's center like a spotlight.

In that light: chaos given form.

The beetles swarm and separate and swarm again. They carry things in their mandibles—fragments of leaf, balls of chewed material, their own dead, seeds no bigger than dust motes. They build and unbuild small structures in the moss: tiny towers, miniature walls, patterns that form and dissolve within minutes. Where you stand still, they investigate your boots, your ankles, flowing around you like water around a stone. They are not interested in you except as obstacle and curiosity. They have work to do.

---

The pocket-world they have made is ancient and in constant motion simultaneously.

See how the moss itself shows growth rings where you look closely—centuries of layered life, each generation growing atop the bones of the last. The chamber's vegetation has reached an equilibrium with the mountain: the root-curtain at the entrance wicks moisture from the clouds that pass this high, draws it inward, lets it condense on the cooler inner walls and drip slowly down to pool in the bowl-shaped floor. A puddle gathers there, no bigger than a serving platter, water so clear it seems like a lens into deeper earth. The beetles drink from its edges in orderly rotation.

But within this stillness: turbulence.

The swarm is rebuilding a section of wall-moss today. You watch them strip it in living sheets, roll it, carry it elsewhere. Beneath, they've exposed something—the faint regular lines of old masonry, a few courses of hand-fitted stone that someone placed here long before the green took over. A doorway, perhaps, or the base of a wall. They've revealed it for reasons you cannot know, and even as you watch, others begin laying new growth over it, pale rootlets reaching, fresh spores scattered by the current of tiny bodies. By tomorrow it will be hidden again, and something else will be opened.

The beetles do not maintain the bower. They *remake* it, constantly, according to patterns written in their collective body. What triggers a change? A shift in humidity, the angle of light through the crack above, the population reaching some threshold that demands expansion or contraction? You cannot know. They might not know themselves, any more than your blood cells know why they rush toward a wound.

---

The sound is so soft you barely notice it: the whisper of thousands of small legs on moss, the click of mandibles passing objects one to another, the occasional bright ping of something dropped into the little pool. It should be overwhelming, this many bodies in motion, but it resolves into something almost like quiet—a white noise of life, a hum so constant your ears begin to treat it as silence.

Until something changes.

A section of swarm near the ceiling suddenly *pulses*, a wave of motion that spreads outward like a ripple in water. Half of them stop moving entirely. The other half accelerate, racing along paths you cannot see, and for thirty seconds the bower fills with a sound like distant rain, like seeds shaken in a dried gourd, like—

And then it stops. The swarm settles. The patterns resume, different now, though you could not say how.

---

Bright cool mountain light fills the upper reaches of the chamber, painting the moss in shades of gold and shadow-green. Lower down, where the walls curve inward, a kind of twilight gathers, and there the beetles have cultivated fungi that glow faintly, pale blue-green pinpricks in the darkness like a scattered constellation. The fungi fruit on moss-rotted wood that the swarm has somehow brought here and arranged—waterlogged fragments of mountain trees, perhaps carried through the entrance one splinter at a time over decades.

The whole bower breathes in slow rhythm with the mountain's weather. When clouds pass over the crack above, the light dims and the beetles slow slightly, conserving heat. When the sun returns, they surge again. You realize you have been standing here for an hour, watching, and your legs have begun to ache, and still you do not want to leave.

There is something profound about this place that has nothing to do with you—a system complete unto itself, endlessly busy, endlessly transforming, asking nothing of witnesses but that they not disturb the work. The old masonry below the moss suggests someone else found this hollow once, tried to claim it for human purposes. The bower simply grew over their efforts, incorporated their stones into its foundation, fed their memory to the endless generations of the swarm.

You could leave something here—a coin, a button, a strand of your hair—and in a decade it would be gone, woven into the moss, carried down to the pool's edge by ten thousand tiny architects, made part of the pattern no single mind controls.

You press back through the clicking root-curtain into the mountain wind, and the bower closes behind you like an eye.
