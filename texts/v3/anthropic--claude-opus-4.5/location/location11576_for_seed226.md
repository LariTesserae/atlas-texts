# The Throat of the New Tower

It is a shaft of worked stone, circular, rising perhaps sixty meters before opening to a disk of pale gray sky. You stand at the bottom, on raw bedrock that still bears the marks of recent cutting—chisel scars in parallel grooves, the stone weeping groundwater where it was cracked too deep. The walls climb in courses of fitted blocks, but they are not finished. Scaffolding of green timber clings to the interior like the skeleton of something half-grown, lashed with rope of twisted plant fiber that drips with the moisture it has drunk from the air.

The air itself is cold and saturated. It tastes of wet stone and crushed vegetation and something mineral, something deep. Water seeps from every seam between blocks, trickling in slow curtains down the inner face of the tower, pooling in the hollows of the unfinished floor. There is no drainage yet. The masons have not carved it. So the water gathers, shin-deep in places, and in the still pools you can see the reflection of the sky-disk far above, and sometimes the shadow of a bird crossing that distant aperture.

The sound is enormous.

Somewhere above, workers are setting stone. You cannot see them—the scaffolding platforms block the view, and the tower's innards are a maze of timber and rope and hanging baskets of mortar—but you hear them. The rhythmic crack of mallets on chisels. The grinding of block against block as courses are fitted. The calls and responses of a work-chant in a language you almost understand, syllables bouncing and multiplying against the circular walls until the tower becomes a throat, singing with the effort of its own becoming. The sound presses against your chest. It is not painful, but it is inescapable, a physical presence.

Moss has already begun to colonize the lowest courses. It grows in electric green patches where the seep-water is steadiest, soft as felt, and in some places ferns have rooted in the mortar joints—species adapted to cliff faces and waterfall spray, finding here a new kind of verticality to inhabit. The builders do not remove them. Perhaps they cannot spare the labor. Perhaps they see the green as a sign of the tower's health, its integration with the mountain it rises from.

Because this is a high place. The shaft descends into a summit plateau, a foundation cut into living rock at an elevation where the air is thin and clouds often drift below. When you look up through the open top of the tower, you see not only sky but sometimes the upper surfaces of clouds, their shapes flattened and luminous in the clear light. The cold is the cold of altitude, steady and clean, and it makes the wet air feel sharp in your lungs.

The tower is young. Barely born. The walls have reached only a quarter of their intended height—you can see this in the way the scaffolding is rigged for climbing, in the baskets of unset stone waiting at each platform, in the mathematical lines scratched into the bedrock floor that describe a structure far larger and more complex than what now exists. Someone has sketched the completed form on a section of smoothed wall: a diagram showing multiple interior chambers, a spiral staircase that does not yet exist, a capstone room with narrow windows oriented to the cardinal points. The drawing is rough, made with charcoal that the seeping water is slowly erasing. The builders are working faster than the mountain's moisture, but only just.

There are no workers visible now. The chanting and hammering come from above, somewhere in the wooden maze, and occasionally a figure passes across one of the higher platforms—a silhouette against the sky-disk, carrying something, moving with the economy of someone who has climbed these scaffolds many times. But the bottom of the shaft is empty. Just you, and the pools, and the breathing walls.

A single frog, pale and small, sits on a wet stone near your foot. It has found its way here somehow—climbed the scaffolding, perhaps, or been carried in with building materials from the forested slopes below. It does not move when you approach. It seems to be listening to the tower's voice, its throat pulsing in time with rhythms you cannot parse.

The light is good. Midday, perhaps, or early afternoon. The sun is not directly overhead—you would see it through the aperture if it were—but enough light spills down the shaft to illuminate the green of the moss, the silver of the seep-water, the pale gold of the new-cut stone. There are no torches. The builders work by daylight and stop when it fails. This is a patient construction, measured in seasons rather than days, and it has barely begun.

Somewhere in the walls, a trickle of water finds a new path, and the sound changes—a new note added to the tower's ongoing chord.
