# The Hollow of Settled Dust

The darkness here is nearly complete, but not absolute. Some faint luminescence persists—perhaps phosphorescent minerals in the rock walls, perhaps the slow decay of something older—casting just enough gray-blue glow to separate shapes from void. Your eyes adjust slowly, finding edges.

You stand in a depression, a natural bowl carved into stone by processes that finished their work long ago. The hollow measures perhaps forty paces across, its walls rising twice the height of a person before curving inward toward a rough ceiling that nearly closes overhead, leaving only a narrow aperture to whatever sky exists above. Through that gap, no light falls. The world outside must be equally dark, or darker still.

The floor is dust. Not sand—finer than that, softer, the accumulated powder of stone slowly releasing itself grain by grain over spans of time too long to name. It lies perhaps ankle-deep, undisturbed. No wind reaches this place. No water has ever pooled here. The dust has found its angle of repose and holds it, perhaps has held it for centuries. Your footsteps leave perfect impressions that will remain until some unimaginable future event erases them.

The walls show the record of old weathering: rounded edges, smoothed protrusions, the patient work of thermal expansion and contraction in some era when temperature varied more than it does now. But that era has passed. The air in the hollow holds steady, warm and dry, carrying no scent except the faint mineral breath of stone itself. The warmth seems to rise from below, from deeper rock that remembers older heat.

Sparse growth clings to crevices in the walls—pale structures that might be lichen, might be something else entirely. They grow in scattered patches, each the size of a spread hand or smaller, their surfaces textured like dried coral. They produce no moisture, seem to require none. Perhaps they feed on the rock itself, or on the faint radiation that provides the only light. They are the same temperature as the stone, the same temperature as the air. They have been here as long as the dust has been settling, growing so slowly that growth and stillness become indistinguishable.

The silence is profound but not oppressive. Occasionally—rarely—a grain of dust detaches from somewhere high on the wall and falls, making a sound so small it barely registers: a whisper of contact, then nothing. These events occur perhaps once in an hour, perhaps less often. Between them, the quiet is simply a fact of the place, as much a feature as the walls themselves.

Near the center of the hollow, a formation rises from the dust: a low mound of stone, waist-high, its surface worn smooth by the same ancient processes that shaped everything else here. It resembles nothing in particular. It is simply the part of the floor that resisted wearing down, that held its shape while everything around it became powder. The dust has drifted against its base on one side, forming a gentle slope.

There are no bones here, no shells, no trace of animal presence past or present. The hollow has never been inhabited. Whatever creatures might exist elsewhere in this world, they have not found this place, or have not chosen it. The pale growths on the walls are the only life, if life is what they represent. They do not move. They do not respond to presence.

The temperature remains constant. The darkness remains constant. The dust lies as it has lain, accepting whatever new grains settle upon it, preserving the memory of every small disturbance in its perfect surface.

Time passes here, but passes so evenly, with so little variation, that its passage becomes theoretical. The hollow simply exists in its state of long equilibrium, neither waiting nor resting, neither ancient nor patient. It has found its form. It keeps it.
