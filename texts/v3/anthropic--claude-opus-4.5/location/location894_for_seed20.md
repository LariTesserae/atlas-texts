# The Drowning Bell

Beneath the warm dark water, someone built a cathedral of air.

It rises from the silted plateau like a held breath—a dome of trapped atmosphere perhaps forty meters across, its boundary not glass or stone but *intention*. The membrane between water and air shimmers with a surface tension that shouldn't exist at this depth, this pressure. Where the dome meets the seafloor, it simply... decides. Here, water. There, not-water. The edge trembles constantly, uncertain of itself.

You are inside. You came in through—it doesn't matter how you came in. You are here now, standing on mineral deposits that have built up in strange terraces over time, your feet on ground that is sometimes chalite, sometimes calcium formations, sometimes something that crunches like old paper and reforms behind you.

The sound is *impossible*.

---

The dome amplifies and translates and *invents* noise from the turbulent waters beyond. You hear the deep calling to the deep—currents that speak in bass frequencies so low they become physical pressure against your chest. You hear what might be the grinding of tectonic plates, except it comes from everywhere, from above, from the direction sound shouldn't travel. The membrane itself hums at a frequency that makes your teeth ache, and within that hum there are voices, or suggestions of voices, or the memory of voices that haven't been spoken yet.

Sometimes the water *screams*.

Not metaphorically. The ocean outside has moods, and its current mood is something between fury and birth-terror. Columns of superheated water erupt from vents you cannot see, and when they hit colder currents, the temperature differential creates shockwaves that translate through the dome's membrane as sounds like tearing metal, like whale-song played backward through a broken speaker, like your own name spoken by someone who learned it phonetically and doesn't understand what names are for.

You cannot hear yourself think. You can only think *between* the sounds, in the brief guttering silences when the chaos outside pauses to draw breath.

---

The dome's interior is nearly empty. This is what makes it bearable—the sparse architecture of what might be sanctuary.

Three structures rise from the terraced floor. They are not buildings so much as *accretions*: mineral deposits that have organized themselves into shapes too regular for nature, too organic for intention. One resembles a spiral staircase that leads nowhere, its steps made of layered deposits in colors the dim light barely reveals—ochre, verdigris, the black of old blood. Another is a low wall that curves in on itself, creating a space just large enough to shelter one body from the sound, if you press yourself against it and cover your ears and still hear everything anyway. The third is a pillar, cracked down its center, from which pale threads emerge—the only vegetation here.

The threads are not kelp. They are not any marine plant you know. They drift in the trapped air like smoke, like hair underwater, except there is no water here, except there is, except the rules keep *changing*. They seem to photosynthesize the dim bioluminescence that filters through the dome's membrane—light from creatures outside that pulse and flare as the currents throw them against the boundary like moths against a lantern.

---

The water beyond is warm, bathwater-warm, heated by vents and upwellings and perhaps by its own agitation. The dome's air is warmer still, humid to the point of condensation, so that moisture collects on your skin and in your lungs and you are never entirely sure whether you are breathing or beginning to drown very slowly.

The plateau this dome rests upon is young—fresh volcanic material, edges still sharp where the seafloor has torn itself open to make room for new geology. You can see it through the membrane in flashes: black basalt formations like frozen lightning, cooling pillars that haven't yet learned to be smooth. Sediment clouds billow past in the current, and sometimes the clouds part and you see *other* domes in the distance, smaller, their membranes flickering with instability. Some of them have collapsed. Some are in the process of collapsing, water rushing in to fill the space where air used to pretend it belonged.

Some of them are growing.

---

The turbulence is the dome's heartbeat. The membrane breathes with it—expanding slightly when the currents pull, contracting when pressure waves from distant eruptions push. You learn to read its rhythm like weather, though the rhythm lies. It will seem stable for minutes at a time, the roar outside settling into something almost like white noise, and then—

—a current shifts, or a vent erupts, or something large moves past (you catch glimpses, shapes too fast to resolve, creatures that shouldn't exist at this depth or at any depth, things with too many fins or not enough body or geometries that make you look away)—

—and the dome *shudders*, and a portion of the membrane thins, and for a terrible moment you see the water bulging inward, remembering that it should be here, that air doesn't belong this deep, that you are an accident in a place made of accidents—

—and then it holds. The boundary remembers itself. The water recedes to its proper side of the question.

Until next time.

---

You have found, in your explorations of the terraced floor, small artifacts. Shells that shouldn't exist—they are made of materials that don't occur in oceans, copper and something like glass and something like compressed sound. A single tooth, massive, from a creature whose jaw would have been larger than the dome itself. Scratchings in the mineral deposits that might be writing or might be the marks of something dragging itself toward the spiral staircase, or away from it.

Nothing here built this place. Nothing here *meant* this place. But something uses it, or used it, or will use it—the tenses are difficult here, in the pressure and the noise and the dim light that seems to come from memories of light rather than any present source.

You have seen one creature inside the dome. Once. It emerged from the membrane as if the boundary didn't apply to it—a translucent thing like a jellyfish made of mathematics, trailing tendrils that wrote equations in the humid air before dissolving. It drifted toward the cracked pillar, touched one of the pale threads, and was absorbed. Or transformed. Or translated into a frequency you couldn't perceive.

The dome screamed for an hour after that, or the water did, or you did. It was hard to tell.

---

You cannot stay here. The sound will break you eventually—not your ears, which have already surrendered, but something deeper, some internal architecture that relies on silence to know its own shape. Already you have lost time. Already you have found yourself standing in different parts of the dome with no memory of walking. Already you have heard your name in the chaos outside and had to remind yourself that the ocean doesn't know your name, that names don't travel at these pressures, that you are not being called, you are not being *summoned*—

But you cannot leave either. The membrane that lets you breathe is also the membrane that keeps you. You have pressed your hands against it, felt its warmth, felt the water on the other side pressing back with patient hunger. There is no door. There is no exit you can perceive.

There is only the dome, breathing.
There is only the sound, endless.
There is only the warm dark water, waiting for the air to forget itself, for the boundary to thin one final time, for the held breath to finally, finally release.

---

Outside, something vast moves past. You cannot see it. You hear its passage as a temporary silence—the chaos of the water parting around a presence so large it creates its own stillness.

For one moment, you can hear yourself think.

And then it's gone, and the drowning bell fills again with the voice of the deep, and you sit on the terraces of mineral deposit, and you watch the pale threads drift in air that shouldn't exist, and you wait.

For what, you don't know.
But the water does.
The water remembers everything that has ever drowned.
