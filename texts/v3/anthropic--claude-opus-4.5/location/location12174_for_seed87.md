# The Drowned Nursery

Below the flood, beneath the newer waters that came and kept coming, there is a city that was never finished being built. It rests in a pocket—a single room, really, though room is the wrong word. Call it a hollow. A void shaped like an intention. The size of a chapel, or a heart, or the space between two hands cupped together in the dark.

The city was meant to be vast. You can tell by the foundations, which crowd against each other in the murk: walls that rise to nothing, archways that frame only more water, streets that spiral inward like the chambers of a nautilus shell. Everything is dense with the effort of becoming. Stone presses against stone. Pillars lean toward each other like they're trying to share warmth. The buildings—if you can call them buildings—are sketches of themselves, half-emerged from the silt floor like teeth coming in wrong.

It's cold here. The kind of cold that has weight to it, that settles into the spaces between things and refuses to leave. The water is the color of iron and old glass, barely lit by something far above—a surface that may or may not still exist. The light comes down in threads, gray and uncertain, like memory of light rather than the thing itself.

---

And the creatures. God, the creatures.

They fill every available inch. They've made the unfinished city their own in ways its builders—whoever, whatever they were—never imagined. Pale things cluster in the hollow window-frames: something like anemones but with too many feeding arms, each arm tipped with what might be an eye or might be a small, perfect mouth. They don't move fast, but they're always moving, a slow constant reaching that makes the walls seem to breathe.

In the unfinished streets, eels the length of your arm wind between the foundations. They're translucent—you can see their organs pulsing, their slow simple hearts. Schools of something smaller than minnows drift through archways in formations that seem almost architectural, building shapes out of their own bodies: temporary spires, momentary domes. Then dispersing. Then building again.

There are crabs. There are always crabs. But these are wrong somehow—their shells are softer than they should be, yielding, like they haven't decided yet whether to be crabs at all. Some of them carry fragments of the city on their backs: chips of carved stone, pieces of what might have been mosaic. They move with terrible purpose, arranging and rearranging the rubble into patterns you can almost understand.

---

The silence is what catches you. Water this full of life should have *sound*—clicks and burbles and the wet percussion of feeding. But here there's only the faintest whisper: the brush of fin against stone, the settling of sediment, the subcutaneous hum of something geothermal far below. It's the silence of a held breath. The silence of something waiting to be born.

Because that's the thing about this place. It's not dead. It's not even dormant. It's *nascent*. The city is still being built—not by hands anymore, but by accretion, by the patient work of calcium and time. The creatures are laying down their dead in the unfinished places. Shells and bones become mortar. The walls grow a millimeter a century. Coral—or something like coral—creeps along the abandoned streets, filling in the gaps, making new rooms where the old blueprints left empty space.

You can see it if you stay long enough in the dim gray light: the architecture changing. A doorway that wasn't there an hour ago. A column that's grown another hand's width of spiraling growth. The city is building itself now, cell by cell, death by death, and the creatures are both the builders and the building material, and they don't seem to mind. They go about their slow business—feeding, breeding, dying in useful places—with something that might be purpose or might just be the inevitable patience of things that have never known hurry.

---

Somewhere in the center of the hollow, there's a structure more complete than the others. A dome, or the memory of a dome, its surface crusted with something that glows faintly bioluminescent in shifting patches of blue-green. Inside, barely visible through gaps in the growth, there are shapes that might be statues or might be the mineralized remains of the builders themselves, caught mid-gesture, still reaching toward a ceiling that finally, finally, after all this time, is beginning to close above them.

The water here tastes of salt and stone and something else—something metabolic, something alive. It tastes like patience. Like the slow dream of completion.

The city is not finished. The city may never be finished. But it is being built, still, and the creatures tend it like gardeners, like mourners, like children who don't know they're making something sacred.
