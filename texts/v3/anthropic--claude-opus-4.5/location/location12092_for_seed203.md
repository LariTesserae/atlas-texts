# The Luminous Hollow

Beneath the world's skin lies a space that should not exist—a cavity so vast it holds its own weather, its own horizons, its own slow catastrophes. You stand at the edge of a root-shelf, looking out across a void perhaps three hundred kilometers wide, and the light that fills it comes from everywhere at once, sourceless and cold and bright as winter noon.

This is the Hollow: a planet-wound still fresh in geological time, carved by some event no one remembers. The walls curve away and up and eventually become a ceiling lost in luminous haze, and every surface—every cliff face, every hanging shelf, every pillar of stone that rises from depths you cannot see—is sheathed in growth. Not decorated. *Sheathed*. The rock itself is merely armature. The plants are the architecture now.

---

The vegetation here has evolved for brightness without sun. Vast pale mosses spread in continents across the walls, their tissues laden with bioluminescent bacteria that burn perpetually cold. Lichen crusts glow in soft violets and greens, overlapping in patterns that seem almost deliberatesome
kind of territorial logic you can't quite parse. But these are just the substrate, the base layer. Above them rise the true structures.

Shelf-fungi the size of cathedrals jut from the walls at improbable angles, their surfaces rippled and whorled, growing in stacked formations that create terraces, walkways, entire ecosystems unto themselves. Some have grown for perhaps a century; their edges are still sharp, still young in fungal terms, still expanding outward at rates visible over months. Others have begun to merge, their tissues fusing into bridges that span chasms.

And everywhere, falling from above, climbing from below, spiraling around the great stone pillars: the root-systems of something massive. You never see the plants they belong to. Perhaps they exist somewhere in the ceiling-haze, in soil-pockets hundreds of kilometers overhead. Perhaps they've died long ago and these are their ghosts, their root-networks become organisms in their own right, seeking water that pools in fissures and collects as condensation on cold stone.

The roots are pale as bone and thick as ancient trees. They web the space like frozen lightning. Some hang free, swaying in currents you can barely feel. Others have found purchase and grown fat with moisture, splitting into fractal networks that colonize entire cliff faces. Where they cross, they graft together, forming lattices, hammocks, accidental platforms. The Hollow's emptiness is structured by them—vast volumes of air interrupted by these organic cables, creating a three-dimensional maze that extends in every direction.

---

The cold comes from below. The Hollow opens onto something deeper, some geologic absence that breathes refrigerated air upward in slow cycles. The temperature hovers just above freezing, cold enough that your breath fogs, cold enough that the moisture in the air—and there is moisture, there is always moisture—condenses on every surface in thin films that never quite dry.

This is a damp cold. Not wet, not dripping, but *present*. The root-flesh gleams with it. The fungi-shelves grow slick. Water beads on the underside of stone ledges and hangs there, patient, growing incrementally larger until some vibration shakes it loose and it falls—falls for a long time, disappearing into the bright depths below.

Sometimes you hear rain that isn't rain: the collective release of a million condensation droplets from a root-lattice somewhere in the middle distance, a brief hissing static that fades as the water disperses into the void.

---

The light reveals everything and hides nothing, but there is so little to see. The Hollow is empty in the way that open ocean is empty—the space itself is the thing. You can walk for days along the shelf-fungi, climbing root-bridges, following terraces that spiral down or up, and encounter almost nothing that moves.

Almost.

Something lives in the mid-reaches—pale arthropods the length of your arm, segmented bodies that grip the roots with too many legs, mandibles working slowly at the plant-flesh. You see them rarely, and always at a distance, frozen in their feeding. They seem to sense vibrations and go still when you approach. Once, you watched one for an hour, waiting for it to move, and it never did. They are patient in a way that suggests they have no predators, or that their only predator is time.

Occasionally: a flutter of something membrane-winged, far off, catching the light as it spirals between root-cables. Gone before you can focus. The ecology here is sparse but present, distributed thinly across such vastness that populations must be counted in density-per-cubic-kilometer.

---

The built things are not abundant, but they exist, and they complicate the story you might tell yourself about this place.

Platforms have been lashed to root-intersections using cordage made from plant-fiber—simple construction, the knot-work suggesting hands rather than tools. Some platforms are bare. Others hold the remains of shelters: bent-branch frames, woven moss panels now collapsed or half-rotted, stone arrangements that might have been hearths if fire were possible here. (Is fire possible here? The air is cold and damp, but perhaps in the driest seasons, if dry seasons exist—)

You find a cache of tools wrapped in fungal leather: stone blades, handles of carved root-wood, a scraping implement whose purpose you can't determine. The edges are still sharp. The wrapping has kept the moisture at bay. This was left here deliberately, carefully, by someone who intended to return.

In another place, a rope-bridge spans a sixty-meter gap between two shelf-fungi, swaying gently in the constant thermal currents. The ropes are newer than the platforms. Someone maintains them. Someone still moves through this space, though you never see them, never hear them, find only the evidence of their passage: a fruit-rind drying on a ledge, a cleared patch where moss has been harvested, scuff-marks on a root where feet have gripped.

---

The turbulence happens without warning.

You feel it first as a pressure change, a thickening of the air, and then the thermals that usually flow gently upward *reverse*—cold air from above plunging down in a column, meeting warm air rising from some heat-source you can't identify, and the collision happens somewhere in the middle distance. Fog blooms instantly. The moisture in the air precipitates into visibility, and suddenly you can see the currents: vast spiraling streams of vapor, twisting around each other, carrying debris—torn moss, detached lichen crusts, the membrane-winged things tumbling helpless.

The roots begin to sway. The sound is the first real sound you've heard in days: a creaking, groaning chorus as thousands of organic cables flex under wind-load. The lattices strain. Somewhere, something snaps—a root or a fungal shelf, something structural—and you hear it give way and fall, and you don't hear it land.

These storms can last hours. The Hollow holds them like a bowl, lets them spiral and exhaust themselves. The fog rolls through in waves, sometimes so thick you can see only a few meters, other times thinning to let the light back in. Water condenses on everything in quantities that finally deserve the name *rain*—not falling from above, but forming directly on every surface, sheeting down root-cables, pooling in fungal crevices, dripping from every edge.

And then: stillness. The currents stabilize. The fog dissipates into general haze. The water begins its slow return to vapor.

But things have changed. Root-lattices have reconfigured. Shelf-fungi have broken and new surfaces are exposed. Somewhere, a section of the wall has become accessible that wasn't before. Somewhere else, a path has been severed.

The Hollow is reshaping itself constantly. It is young, this wound. It is still settling into itself. The plants grow fast and fall fast and grow again. The architecture is always temporary.

---

Sound comes late and strange.

When the air is still, there is almost nothing: your own breath, the slight creak of plant-fiber under your weight, the occasional drip. But sound travels far in this space, and sometimes you hear things from kilometers away, delayed and distorted. A crack as a root breaks. A hissing wash of distant condensation-rain. Once, unmistakably, a voice—someone calling out, the words unintelligible, echoing through the void for long seconds before fading.

You called back. No response. You tried again an hour later, and heard nothing, and you haven't heard voices since.

---

At the edges of the Hollow, where the walls curve closest, the growth is densest. Root-systems pile on root-systems, creating tangles so thick they become nearly solid—a kind of organic fill that accumulates in crevices and niches. These are the newest zones, the places where the Hollow is still actively expanding, where rock fractures and plant-life rushes to colonize before the surface is even fully exposed.

Here the stone is sharp-edged, fresh-fractured, sometimes still warm from the energies that broke it. The geometry is chaotic—no erosion yet to smooth the angles, no time yet for the plants to fully integrate the new territory. You can see strata exposed: layers of different rock-types, fossil-traces that hint at what existed before the Hollow opened, veins of mineral that glitter in the sourceless light.

The expansion is slow but continuous. You imagine you can feel it, sometimes—a sub-audible vibration, a sense that the walls are breathing outward millimeter by millimeter. In a thousand years, this space will be larger. In ten thousand, larger still. What happens when it reaches the surface? When the ceiling finally thins and breaks?

Perhaps it already has, somewhere. Perhaps the Hollow is just the deepest chamber in a network of cavities that riddle the entire crust. Perhaps the world above is beginning to collapse into these growing voids.

---

You have been here for some indeterminate time. Days blur into each other without cycles of darkness. You've learned to navigate by the root-lattices, to find sleeping-places in the larger fungal shelves, to harvest water from condensation-points and food from the growths you've identified as non-toxic.

You've found more platforms. More caches. More evidence of whoever else moves through this space. You've started to leave your own marks: scratches on root-bark, arrangements of stones, signals you hope are legible.

The Hollow teaches patience. Teaches scale. When you climb to a high shelf and look out across the kilometers of emptiness—the bright haze, the root-lattices webbing the void, the distant walls curved like the inside of an immense egg—you feel something that is neither despair nor wonder but some third thing. A recognition, maybe. The understanding that you are inside something alive, something still becoming itself, and that your presence here is incidental and brief and also, somehow, exactly what this place has been waiting for.

A witness. A way for the Hollow to know it exists.
