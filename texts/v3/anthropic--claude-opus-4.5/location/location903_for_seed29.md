# The Furnace Pocket

Inside the mountain's throat, where magma once pushed but never broke through, there is a chamber no larger than a closet. The walls are fresh—geologicaly speaking, they finished forming perhaps a decade ago, the last of the molten rock cooling into a shell of black glass still warm to the touch. The air inside sits at temperatures that would blister skin within minutes, held there by the mountain's slow internal heat radiating up from depths where the mantle still dreams of eruption.

No water has ever touched this space. The rock sealed around nothing but mineral vapor and the dry exhalations of cooling stone. The walls are slick not with moisture but with a thin film of precipitated sulfur, yellow-gray deposits that catch no light because there is no light to catch. The darkness here is absolute—not the darkness of night, which knows stars, but the darkness of being swallowed, of existing inside something that has never opened to the sky.

And yet the chamber is full.

They came up through microscopic fissures in the rock, following gradients of heat and chemical traces that human senses could never detect. Thermophilic beetles, each one the size of a grain of rice, their shells the color of dried blood. They pack the walls in overlapping layers, tens of thousands of them in a space that could barely hold a human curled fetal. Their metabolisms run on sulfur compounds and the trace metals leaching from the fresh volcanic glass. They do not see—they have no eyes—but they feel heat with such precision that they navigate the chamber's topology by temperature alone, clustering where the rock runs hottest, their bodies pressed so tight against each other that the chamber holds no empty air.

The sound they make is enormous for something so small. A grinding, clicking chorus of mandibles working against stone, of legs shifting against shells, of spiracles opening and closing in synchronized waves that create a throbbing hum. In a space this size, the noise compounds against itself, echoing off the glass walls until it becomes a single sustained tone somewhere between a purr and a scream. The beetles are eating the chamber itself, scraping away the sulfur deposits, slowly enlarging their pocket world by fractions of millimeters per year.

No structures here—nothing built, nothing intended. Just the beetles and the rock and the heat and the dark. But the beetles move in patterns that suggest something almost mechanical. They rotate positions in shifts, the outermost layer cycling inward while the inner layer cycles out, a living engine of consumption and redistribution. Their waste products—tiny pellets of processed mineral—accumulate in the chamber's lowest point, gradually filling the floor with a sediment that will, over centuries, become a new kind of stone.

Sometimes a beetle dies. Its neighbors consume it within hours, recycling every molecule of usable matter. Nothing leaves this space. Nothing enters except the slow seep of heat from below and the trace chemistry that sustains them. The chamber exists in a state of near-perfect metabolic closure, a pocket ecosystem running on geological time, patient as the mountain that contains it.

The mountain itself is young, still rising imperceptibly, still settling into its final shape. This chamber may persist for a thousand years or collapse tomorrow as new pressures find new paths through the rock. The beetles have no knowledge of this. They know only the heat, the sulfur, the press of bodies against bodies, the endless slow grinding that is both their labor and their song.
