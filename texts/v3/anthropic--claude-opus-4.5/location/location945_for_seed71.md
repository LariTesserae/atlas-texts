# The Plateau of Settled Geometries

The plateau extends beyond the reach of horizon—a cold, high table of stone that curves with the planet itself, vast enough that weather is born here, lives out its quiet span, and dies without ever touching lower ground. Stand at any point and you stand at the center of something immeasurably old, something that finished the work of becoming itself so long ago that the stone has forgotten it was ever otherwise.

The bedrock is exposed in sheets the color of cooling forge-ash, worn to a polish that holds no memory of its original form. Whatever mountains once rose here have been ground down through eons beyond counting, their substance redistributed into this endless, gently undulating surface. The erosion is so ancient, so complete, that the plateau has achieved a kind of terminal smoothness—not flat, but *resolved*, like a mathematical proof worked out in stone. Shallow depressions curve across its face in arcs that span dozens of miles, the ghosts of impact craters or collapsed magma chambers, now mere suggestions in topography.

Yet something has begun to happen to the bedrock. In places, the stone has started to *unfold*.

---

It begins subtly—a hairline crack that runs for a hundred yards, perfectly straight where no straight line should exist in nature. Then the crack widens, and what lies beneath is not more stone but a kind of negative space, a darkness that light touches but does not illuminate. The locals call these features *seams*, though that word fails to capture how they seem to exist at an angle to ordinary matter, how they remain exactly the same depth whether viewed from a foot away or a mile distant.

The seams propagate slowly, following patterns that suggest tremendous underlying structure. Seen from sufficient height—and there are those who have climbed the ancient signal-cairns to look—the seams trace out shapes that might be geometric, might be organic, might be something else entirely. They grow at the pace of continental drift, inches per decade, inexorable and patient. The oldest seams have widened enough that a child could fall into them, and those children do not fall the way physics suggests they should. They fall *slowly*, silently, their features growing calm as they sink into that dimensionless dark, and whether they reach a bottom no one knows.

The plateau's inhabitants have learned to map the seams, to predict their propagation, to build where the stone is still whole. But the whole stones grow fewer each generation.

---

Water comes in cycles measured not in seasons but in decades. The plateau is too high and too vast for consistent weather patterns—it makes its own. Great condensation fronts sweep in from the edges where the land falls away into distant lowlands, carrying moisture that pools in the shallow depressions, freezes into temporary lakes of black ice, then slowly sublimates back into the atmosphere over years. The air holds a persistent chill that never quite reaches brutal cold, hovering in that range where water exists in all three states simultaneously: frost riming the stone at dawn, melt-streams trickling through the afternoon, fog rising at dusk to begin the cycle again.

The condensation gathers differently near the seams. The dark within them neither absorbs nor reflects moisture—instead, droplets that drift too close seem to forget which direction to fall. They hang suspended, orbiting nothing, until eventually they drift close enough to touch the seam's edge and vanish inward. The locals have developed ceramic collection vessels with carefully angled lips, positioned to catch water that rolls away from seams rather than into them.

In the temporary lakes, things grow.

---

They are not plants, exactly. The locals call them *winterweed*, though they share no taxonomy with anything that grew in sunlight before the seams began. They emerge from spores that seem to exist perpetually suspended in the plateau's air, settling into any water that pools long enough to freeze. Beneath the black ice, they unfurl—pale fronds of something that might be mineral, might be fungal, might be crystalline, arranged in patterns that echo the seam-geometry with unsettling fidelity.

The winterweed grows upward toward the ice, pressing against it without breaking through, spreading beneath the frozen surface in gardens of impossible intricacy. When the ice finally sublimates, the winterweed remains, standing in the open air in defiance of its former medium, skeletal and white and faintly luminescent. A depression that held water for seven years might support a winterweed forest half a mile across, the fronds reaching heights of twelve or fifteen feet, their branches interlocking in structures that visitors from below mistake for architecture.

They are not architecture. But they are not not-architecture either.

The winterweed is the plateau's only vegetation, if vegetation it is. It neither grows nor dies in any conventional sense—it *persists*, maintaining its form until something disturbs it. Touch a frond and it crumbles to powder that smells of ozone and deep cold. Leave that powder for a decade and it will drift into the next depression, waiting for the next condensation cycle. The winterweed has been doing this since before the seams opened, perhaps since before the plateau was a plateau at all.

It makes no sound when it crumbles. Nothing here makes sound.

---

The settlements cluster on the high ground—not that any ground is truly higher than any other on this worn-flat expanse, but certain areas have remained seam-free for centuries, and these are where the people build. They are not cities in any lowland sense. They are careful geometries of worked stone, laid in patterns that the inhabitants say ward against seam-propagation, though whether this is engineering or ritual or some combination no one can prove. The buildings are low, thick-walled, their proportions governed by rules passed down through generations of builder-priests who claim to understand the angles at which reality sits most stably.

The architecture favors curves interrupted by precise corners—doorways that are round until suddenly they aren't, walls that follow gentle arcs before breaking into hard vertices. The inhabitants say these shapes confuse the seam-logic, make the stone difficult to unfold. The seams say nothing, but they do seem to propagate more slowly near the settlements, curving around them like streams around boulders.

Perhaps thirty of these settlements exist across the plateau's continental expanse, each housing a few hundred souls, each visible from the others only as faint smudges in the impossibly clear air. The people travel between them on foot, following routes marked by cairns that serve as both navigation and signal-towers, their journeys measured in weeks. They carry sleds of carved winterweed-bone (the densest parts can be worked, briefly, before they remember what they are and crumble), loaded with the tools of their civilization: hand-polished lenses for studying the seams, ceramic vessels for water collection, carved instruments for making the precise sounds that their prayers require.

The sounds are quiet. Even the prayers are quiet. The plateau punishes noise.

---

Nothing with blood lives here. No insects, no birds, no burrowing creatures in the stone. The people themselves are the only warm-blooded things for thousands of miles, and they are acutely aware of this absence. They speak of their ancestors' journey from below, of the animals that sickened and died during the climb, of the way the silence pressed against their ears until they learned to lower their voices, then to whisper, then to barely speak at all.

The plateau doesn't forbid fauna, exactly. But something about its nature—the cold that isn't quite cold enough, the air that carries moisture but not scent, the geometry of the seams pressing against the edges of animal cognition—makes warm blood a burden. The people survive through discipline, through ritual, through buildings designed to hold heat in ways that don't draw seam-attention. Their children are raised in near-silence, taught to make themselves quiet not just in voice but in presence, to take up as little space in reality as possible.

They are thin, these people. Their faces are angular. They move with a conservation of motion that reads as either grace or grief.

---

The light here is silver-gray, filtered through the high thin atmosphere in ways that flatten shadow and stretch distance. The sun rises and sets at angles that feel wrong to lowland eyes, describing an arc that seems too tight, as though the sky were closer than it should be. No clouds form directly over the plateau—they gather at the edges, where the condensation fronts build, but they never cross into the interior. The stars at night are thick and strange, constellations that don't quite match lowland charts, as though the plateau were looking up at the sky from a slightly different angle than the rest of the world.

The seams glow faintly after dark. Not with light exactly, but with an absence of darkness—a way of being visible that has nothing to do with illumination. In the settlements, people cover their windows at night not to keep out cold but to keep out the sight of the spreading seams catching starlight that never quite reaches them, marking their slow expansion in traceries of not-quite-light that look, from the right angle, like writing in a script that predates language.

---

The plateau is dying. Everyone knows this. The seams are growing, decade by decade, and the whole ground is shrinking. The settlements that existed a thousand years ago are mapped in the oral histories, their locations now just spaces where the stone has unfolded entirely, leaving patches of that dimensionless dark open to the sky like wounds in the world's fabric. The people speak of this with equanimity. They were raised in silence; they understand that endings are quiet.

But the plateau is also active in ways that contradict its dying. The condensation fronts still build, still sweep inward. The winterweed still persists. The seams, in their slow growth, create new geometries that the builder-priests study and incorporate into their protective architectures. There is a dynamism to the decay—not a battle but a dance, reality and un-reality partnering across eons, neither leading, both moving toward some resolution that the people of the settlements will not live to see.

They build anyway. They map the seams, predict their spread, lay their stones in their careful angles. They whisper their prayers in sounds so soft they barely disturb the air. They raise their children, send their sleds across the vast worn surface, tend to the winterweed gardens that sometimes grow in shapes that answer questions no one asked.

And beneath them, patient and ancient, the plateau continues the work of becoming something else.
