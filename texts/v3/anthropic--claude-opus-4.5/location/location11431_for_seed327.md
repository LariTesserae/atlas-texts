# The Crying Ledge

The plateau rises like a blunt tooth from the mountain's jaw, a thrust of dark basalt capped with centuries of lichen in colors that have no names—grays that edge toward green, whites that remember yellow. The ledge is perhaps forty paces across at its widest, shaped roughly like a cupped hand, and it sits impossibly high, wreathed in cloud that moves through rather than around it.

Water is everywhere here, though there is no visible source. It weeps from the rock face that rises behind the ledge, seeping from cracks and running in thin sheets over stone worn smooth by its passing. It beads on every surface, collects in shallow depressions, pools in the centers of lichened boulders. The air itself is thick with it—a perpetual mist that never quite becomes rain, that soaks through wool and leather and settles cold on the skin. Somewhere deep in the mountain, aquifers press upward through fractured stone, and the plateau sits at their emergence point, eternally damp, eternally dripping.

The sound is tremendous.

Not from the water—the seeping and pooling is quiet work—but from the birds. They have colonized every viable surface. Swifts nest in the cracks of the back wall, hundreds of them, their bodies small and dark as flung stones, their voices sharp and constant. Larger birds—some hawk-like, some crow-like, one species with a red throat-patch and a call like grinding ice—wheel in the updrafts that rise from the valleys below. They scream and chatter and argue over territories measured in inches. At dawn and dusk, when they launch in masses to hunt the insect clouds that rise from the forests far below, the sound becomes a roar, a physical pressure against the ears.

And there are the goats.

Wild, sure-footed, with coats so thick they seem twice their actual size. A small herd, perhaps eight or ten, moves across the plateau with casual indifference to the sheer drops on three sides. They graze on the lichens and the tough small plants that grow in the rock cracks—sedges, a flowering thing with purple heads no larger than a fingertip, a kind of wiry grass that clings to any soil-filled crevice. The goats bleat and jostle and clatter their hooves against stone. Their kids play at the edges of deadly falls. The adults watch the birds with horizontal-pupiled eyes that hold neither fear nor curiosity—only the calm assessment of creatures that have calculated all local threats and found them wanting.

Among the lichened boulders, almost invisible until you learn to look for them, stone cairns rise in modest stacks. The oldest have begun to lean, their stones furred with the same growth that covers everything here. The newest—and even those are old, perhaps a generation or more—stand straight enough, built from rocks gathered from the ledge itself. There are perhaps a dozen of them. Grave markers or territorial claims or way-markers for some route that leads here and then ends. Someone stacked them by hand. Someone climbed all the way up here, again and again, and left these small monuments to purposes now impossible to know.

Near the center of the ledge, where the plateau dips slightly and water collects in a pool no deeper than a hand's breadth, there is a structure. It can barely be called that—a low wall of stacked stone forming three sides of an incomplete rectangle, high enough to sit behind and shelter from wind but not high enough to stand within. A few flat stones have been laid as flooring inside, worn smooth by use. A fire-pit marks one corner, blackened stones around a depression now filled with rainwater. Bone fragments in the ash—small bones, bird bones, perhaps fish brought up from some stream far below. Whoever built this place came here to watch something, or to wait for something, or simply to be in this high wet screaming place where the world falls away on every side and the clouds pass through like breath.

The cold is not bitter but it is insistent. It works into joints, settles in the lungs. The kind of cold that makes a body want to move, to work, to maintain heat through action. The birds seem immune to it. The goats seem immune to everything. Only the lichen and the small plants seem to acknowledge the temperature, growing slowly, incrementally, in the patient way of things that have adapted to marginal conditions.

Sometimes the clouds part. When they do, the view is vertiginous—valleys so far below they appear painted rather than real, their forests reduced to texture, their rivers to silver threads. Other peaks rise in the distance, some higher, some crowned with snow. The plateau is not the highest point, but it is high enough that the world below seems like a separate realm, governed by different rules, populated by different creatures. Up here, there is only the stone and the water and the birds and the goats and the lichen, and whatever comes climbing to stack rocks and shelter behind low walls.

At night, the birds roost. The plateau grows quieter but never silent—there are the settling sounds, the occasional dispute, the persistent dripping. The goats huddle together for warmth. The mist thickens, glows faintly if there is a moon, becomes absolute darkness if there is not. The incomplete shelter offers some protection from wind but none from the wet. Anyone spending the night here would wake damp and cold and unrested, and perhaps they would look at the cairns in the first gray light and understand something about endurance, or stubbornness, or whatever quality drives a creature to return again and again to a place that offers discomfort and exposure and the constant screaming of birds—and yet, somehow, holds them there, on that crying ledge where the mountain weeps and the world drops away and something unnamed waits in the space between stone and cloud.
