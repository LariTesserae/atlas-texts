# The Abandoned Smelting District

The heat comes up through your boots before you notice anything else. This whole district sits on what the old engineers called thermal veins—cracks in the bedrock where something deep and hot bleeds warmth into the foundations. The ground stays wet here, always, condensation weeping from every iron surface, pooling in the ruts between cobblestones, running in thin sheets down the brick faces of empty factories.

Nothing grows. The soil gave that up generations ago, poisoned by decades of slag and chemical runoff. The stones themselves carry a mineral sheen, deposits of copper oxide and iron rust painting the streets in streaks of verdigris and orange. Where walls have crumbled, you can see the layered strata of industrial waste—compressed ash, vitrified sand, the dense black residue of burned coal packed into something almost geological.

The smelting district stretches for perhaps two miles along the river's edge, though the river itself has shrunk to a series of connected pools, its water thick and warm and perpetually steaming. The buildings stand in various states of collapse—some merely hollow, their roofs fallen in and their windows empty, others reduced to single walls propped against nothing. Chimneys rise everywhere like a forest of brick fingers, most of them cracked, a few still standing straight against the dim sky. The light here filters through a permanent haze, sulfurous and amber, that turns noon into perpetual dusk.

And yet.

Move quietly. Step wrong on these wet cobblestones and you'll know why.

The creatures came after the workers left. Nobody planned for them, nobody invited them, but when the furnaces cooled just enough and the worst of the fumes dispersed, something found this place hospitable. They roost in the chimneys—you can hear them shifting, occasionally, a scrape of claw on brick, a leathery rustle—and they hunt in the steam that rises from the river. Salamander-things, the locals call them, though they're warm-blooded and furred, adapted to heat that would kill most mammals. They range from the size of a cat to the size of a large dog, and they move in family groups through the ruined factory halls, picking through the carcasses of smaller prey.

The smaller prey is everywhere. Beetles the size of your thumb cluster on any surface warm enough, feeding on the mineral crust, on each other, on the droppings of larger things. Pale crabs work the river pools, their shells stained the same copper-green as the walls. Something like a rat but longer, thinner, hairless, threads through the collapsed rooflines in numbers you'd rather not count. At night—and it's always almost night here—you can see their eyes reflecting what little light remains, hundreds of small gleams in the dark factory interiors.

The salamander-things keep the population in check. Barely.

Walk the main thoroughfare, the one they called Foundry Road, and you pass the great hulks of machinery too heavy to salvage. Casting frames stand open like metal ribcages. A crane, half-toppled, reaches across the street with its hook still dangling. The stamping mill's drive wheel lies where it fell, ten feet across, slowly being buried by the accumulated grime of years. Water drips from everything—condensing on cold iron, running down in rust-red streams, collecting in the depressions worn into the street by decades of heavy carts.

The sound here is strange. Muffled. The haze and the heat seem to swallow noise, so that even the constant dripping fades into a kind of white silence. Occasionally a salamander-thing calls to its family—a low churring sound, almost mechanical—and occasionally something crashes in the distance, another roof giving way, another wall surrendering to time. But mostly the district holds its breath. The creatures have learned quiet. So has everything else.

The district was built to last. Stone foundations, brick walls three feet thick, iron frames meant to bear the weight of industry for centuries. It has lasted, after a fashion. The bones remain even as the flesh rots away. And in that skeleton, in the warmth and the wet and the dim amber light, a different kind of life has taken hold—not human, not planned, but thriving in its way.

At the district's edge, where the haze thins slightly, you can see the city proper on the hills above. Lights there, and movement, and the ordinary business of living. Down here, in the lowland where the heat gathers and the water never quite drains, the factories dream their slow industrial dreams while the salamander-things pad through the ruins, and the beetles feed, and the steam rises endlessly from the poisoned river.
