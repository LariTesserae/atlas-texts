# The Cistern

Forty feet down, in water that has forgotten the sun, a chamber persists.

It is not large—perhaps the footprint of a modest house, though the ceiling rises higher than you'd expect, vaulted in a way that suggests purpose without revealing it. The water fills everything. There is no air pocket, no surface to break. You are simply *in* it, suspended in a medium that holds you with patient indifference.

The walls are stone, but not hewn stone—more like stone that has been *resolved*, settled into its final configuration over decades of saturation. Mineral deposits trace pale veins across the surfaces, calcium and lime drawn out by the slow patience of groundwater. In places the stone has developed a texture like frozen cloth, rippled and soft-looking though it would rasp against skin. The floor is silt over flagstone, the silt undisturbed, the flagstones visible only at the edges where the walls meet the ground.

Light comes from above—not daylight, but something filtered through so many feet of water that it arrives blue-grey and directionless, illuminating without casting shadows. It is enough to see by. It is not enough to feel warm.

The temperature hovers at the edge of discomfort. Not cold enough to shock, but cool enough that you become aware of your own heat, the way it bleeds outward into water that will never warm.

Three pipes emerge from the eastern wall. They are iron, thick-bodied, corroded to a brown that has stabilized into something almost geological. They do not rust further; they do not flow. Whatever industrial purpose brought them here—drainage, perhaps, or the movement of something heavier than water—has long since ceased. But the pipes remain, their mouths dark and perfectly round, aimed at nothing. They have become features of the space rather than intrusions into it.

No fish. No weed. No bacterial film clouds the water. The cistern is clean in a way that suggests either extreme purity or extreme hostility to life—and you cannot tell which. A few pale threads of vegetation cling to the base of the pipes, something between moss and root, but they do not move, do not grow, simply *are*. They may have been here for years. They may be here for years more.

The silence is the silence of water: not empty, but *full*, full of the sound of your own pulse, the creak of pressure in your inner ear, the faint high tone that might be the pipes resonating at some frequency below hearing. If you held still long enough, you might stop noticing it. But you haven't held still that long yet.

Nothing changes here. The light does not shift with passing clouds. The silt does not drift. The water does not circulate. The cistern has reached its equilibrium and will maintain it, indifferent to witness, until something from outside disturbs the balance.

You are that disturbance, for now. When you leave, the silt will settle. The water will still. And the cistern will continue, forty feet down, in its own quiet dark.
