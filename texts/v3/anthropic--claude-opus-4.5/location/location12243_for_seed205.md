# The Singing Plateau

The plateau rises from the world like a thought the earth couldn't quite finish—a tilted expanse of living matter that stretches so far in every direction that weather forms in its hollows, gathers its own momentum, and dies before reaching the edges. You could walk for seasons and never find the rim. The locals, those few who exist here, say the plateau has no edge at all, that it curves back on itself like a tongue curling toward a throat.

Everything here is plant. Not plants growing on something, but plant *as* the something—the ground itself a compressed mat of rootweave so ancient and dense it has forgotten how to be individual organisms. Walk across it and you're walking on centuries of fused vegetable matter, spongy in some places, hard as fired clay in others. The surface undulates in long slow waves frozen mid-crest, as though the whole mass tried once to crawl somewhere and gave up. Canyons cut through where eons of seasonal moisture have dissolved channels, their walls revealing strata of different growth eras—pale fungal ages, dark chlorophyll millennia, bands of purple where some forgotten radiation event altered the chemistry of everything that lived.

It's hot. The kind of hot that has weight, that presses on the skin like wet wool. The plant-matter below radiates stored warmth long after the pale sun drops behind the permanent haze that hangs at the plateau's edges. The air smells of fermentation, of slow vegetable rot becoming slow vegetable growth, a cycle so ancient it has achieved equilibrium. Condensation beads on every surface at dawn, runs down the grooved channels of the vine-towers, collects in natural cisterns where the rootweave cups upward. By midday it's gone, drunk by the substrate or evaporated into the shimmering air. Rain comes in bursts—not storms but *weeping*, as though clouds form so quickly over particularly active growth zones that they can't hold together, releasing their moisture in localized torrents that last minutes and leave behind mud that hardens within the hour.

The vine-towers dominate the middle distances. They rise at irregular intervals across the plateau—some separated by miles of low scrubland, others clustered in groves so dense they lean against each other for support. Each tower is a single organism technically, or perhaps a thousand organisms that have lost the capacity for separation. They spiral upward in thick braids, trunk-width cables of living tissue wrapped around themselves in helical patterns that seem almost intentional, almost constructed. Some reach heights where their upper canopies disappear into the haze layer, their leaves drinking whatever moisture lives up there. Between these towers: space. Breathing room. The plateau's density is vertical, not horizontal, leaving vast stretches of low groundcover between the organic monoliths.

And everywhere, everywhere, the fauna.

They are the sound before they are anything else. The plateau *screams*. It whistles and clicks and drums and roars and produces frequencies that exist below hearing as pressure and above it as the sensation of needles in the eardrums. Close your eyes anywhere on this plateau and you cannot hear yourself think—the biological noise enters through your skull bones, through your chest cavity, through the fluids of your inner ear until you become a resonating chamber for their collective voice.

The canopy-swingers move through the upper reaches of the vine-towers in troops of hundreds, their calls coordinating movements so precise that whole populations shift position simultaneously, a murmuration of furred bodies that blocks out what light filters through the leaves. They have too many limbs—seven, or nine, the count differs depending on life stage—and faces that rotate through expressions too quickly for any mammalian brain to read. They're not hostile. They're not *anything* toward humans. They simply occupy so much of the sonic and physical space that hostility would be redundant.

The groundskeepers are worse, in their way. They travel in columns that can take days to pass a single point, their segmented bodies pressing against the rootweave, their mandibles processing dead vegetation into paste they carry in abdominal pouches back to mound-structures that rise like boils from the plateau's surface. The sound they make is chemical—a pheromone communication system so intense it registers as smell but also as sound, a hum in the sinuses, a pressure behind the eyes. Step too close to a column and they don't attack; they simply incorporate you into their flow, pressing past on all sides until you're pulled along with them for miles before you can work your way to the edge of the living river.

In the tower-roots live the drummers. No one sees them fully—only their echo-organs, structures like stretched membranes mounted on towers of cartilage that project from holes in the plant-mass. They drum with something, limbs or tails or specialized appendages, producing rhythms that seem random until you track them across weeks and realize they're communicating *something*, warnings or courtship or territorial claims that span distances measured in days of walking. The drumming vibrates up through the rootweave so thoroughly that the plateau itself becomes the instrument, carrying messages to drummers on the other side of horizons.

The air belongs to the drift-colonies. Not individuals but composite organisms, membranous bags filled with lighter-than-air gases that trail tentacles down into the canopy to feed. They sing—a mournful chorus of resonance as the wind passes through the tubes of their bodies, each colony tuned by its size and structure to a different pitch. Where they gather in numbers, the sky becomes an organ, playing itself.

And through all this: the humans.

They live in the hollow places, where ancient growth has died back and left cavities in the substrate. Their shelters are frames of dried vine-material, bound with fiber rope, covered in layers of processed leaf-matter that resists the moisture and the heat. Small structures—never more than a few families together, spread across distances that ensure each settlement can survive independently. They have fire, carefully contained in stone-lined pits, because everything here would burn if given encouragement. They have knives of knapped glass-stone, not native to the plateau but traded up from somewhere below, somewhere far. They have baskets and nets and wrapped bundles of things they need, all made from plant-matter because everything here is plant-matter eventually.

Their marks on the land are faint. Paths worn smoother than the surrounding surface. Cisterns improved with clay-lined rims. Ropes strung between towers marking routes through the acoustic chaos, because in the overwhelming sound, visual navigation becomes essential. They paint symbols on the bark of certain vine-towers—counting marks, direction markers, warnings about dangerous fauna or unstable ground. Generations of feet have worn certain routes into slight depressions that collect morning moisture, creating ribbons of greener growth that serve as trails even when the original walkers are centuries gone.

They carry plugs of wax in their ears, adjusted throughout the day—more during the evening cacophony of the canopy-swingers, less during the relative quiet of deep night when only the drummers maintain their vigil. They speak in hand-signs as often as words, a visual language evolved alongside their spoken one, because sometimes the plateau is simply too loud for mouths to matter. Their children learn to read the sound-scape before they learn to walk, to distinguish the safe chorus from the dangerous discord, to know which frequencies mean *column approaching* and which mean *drift-colony descending*.

The strange things happen in the deep places, where the rootweave is thickest and oldest. There are pools in those regions where the plant-matter has decayed into something almost liquid, almost alive in its own right—not water but not solid, a medium that flows upward as readily as down, that carries things that should not float. Things grow there that are neither plant nor animal but something the plateau has invented for itself, organisms that process sound into nutrition, that eat the vibrations of the drumming and the screaming and the singing and produce in turn a silence so profound it can be felt as pressure. The humans avoid these places. They say the silence there has weight, has hunger, that it wants to spread.

The plateau dreams, they say. At night, when the activity settles into its nocturnal patterns, the vast mat of living matter enters something like sleep—a metabolic slowdown that causes the ground to settle, to contract slightly, to exhale gases that have accumulated during the day's growth. In those hours, walking the surface feels like walking on something breathing. Some of the older humans claim they've felt the plateau move deliberately, shifting them gently away from dangers they couldn't perceive, guiding their sleepwalking feet around unstable sections. Others say this is fantasy, that the plateau doesn't think, can't think, is simply too large and too old for intention.

But something coordinates the vine-towers' spiral directions. Something ensures the cisterns fill evenly across impossible distances. Something has arranged this overwhelming, screaming, singing, drumming life into a pattern that sustains itself across scales that dwarf human comprehension.

The plateau holds it all. The heat, the moisture, the sparse density of presences scattered across its vastness. The small human shelters, the primitive tools, the scattered marks of intelligent presence absorbed into something so much larger that intelligence seems beside the point. The established erosion channels, the balanced weathering of eons, the long existence that has found its own state and rests there, ancient and active and utterly, deafeningly alive.
