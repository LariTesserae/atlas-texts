# The Drowning Gardens

The water here is not water as you know it. It is cold and dense and utterly dark, and it has been here longer than the stone it covers. You descend through it as through black oil, and the pressure builds not painfully but insistently, reminding you with each fathom that you are entering a place that operates by its own physical grammar.

There is no surface that you can see. Perhaps there was once—perhaps there still is, somewhere impossibly far above—but in the Drowning Gardens, that question belongs to theology rather than geography. What matters is here: the vast submerged plateau where the kelp forests grow in absolute darkness, where the cold seeps into everything but never quite freezes, where structures that someone built a very long time ago still stand among the root-like holdfasts of plants that should not exist.

---

The kelp—if kelp is the right word—rises from the seafloor in great scattered groves, each trunk as thick as a man's torso and tall enough to vanish into the darkness above. They are not green. In the rare moments when bioluminescent blooms drift through (we will speak of those), they reveal themselves as deep bruise-purple, almost black, with fronds that spread laterally like the arms of drowned dancers. The plants grow slowly, imperceptibly, but they grow. You can see it in the way they have embraced the old structures, wrapped around cornerstones, pushed through windows, lifted roof-slabs on patient vegetable shoulders.

The vegetation is lush but sparse—a seeming contradiction that the Gardens resolve through scale. Each grove is dense within itself, fronds overlapping, holdfasts tangled, a thicket of slow life. But between the groves lies open seafloor, wide expanses of silt and stone where nothing grows except thin bacterial mats that glow faintly amber when disturbed. The groves are islands in a dark continent. You could swim for what feels like hours between them, seeing nothing but the occasional standing stone, the foundation of something forgotten, the suggestion of a path worn into bedrock by feet or currents or both.

---

The structures are primitive but deliberate. Walls of fitted stone without mortar. Doorways sized for something roughly human-shaped but perhaps not human-minded. Small shrines or markers at regular intervals along what might have been roads, each one carved with spiraling patterns that seem to move in your peripheral vision but hold perfectly still when observed directly. The buildings have no roofs—perhaps they never did, or perhaps the roofs were made of something that dissolved long ago, organic material returning to the water that birthed it.

In one grove, you find what might have been a meeting hall. The kelp has grown up through its center, a single massive specimen that has split the floor and now holds the walls apart like hands preventing a wound from closing. Around its base, objects lie half-buried in silt: hand-shaped tools of green-corroded copper, pottery shards with geometric patterns, something that might be a musical instrument—a frame of bone or antler strung with fibers that still hold tension, that still thrum faintly when the currents shift.

No one has been here in a very long time. But the tools suggest they might return, or that they left expecting to.

---

The sound is what you notice first and forget last. The Drowning Gardens are loud with water-noise—not the simple rush of a river or the crash of surf, but the deep industrial groan of a liquid world in motion. Currents collide somewhere below, generating infrasonic pulses that you feel in your chest more than hear. The kelp fronds rasp against each other in slow rhythms, a papery whisper amplified by the density of the medium. And there is a clicking—irregular, sourceless, mechanical in its precision but organic in its variation—that rises and falls throughout the day-cycle (there is a day-cycle here, though it has nothing to do with light; the currents shift, the pressure changes, the Gardens know morning and evening by metrics you cannot perceive).

The clicking comes from the plants. Not from animals—there are no animals, not a single fish or worm or swimming thing, and this absence is perhaps the strangest element of the strangeness. The kelp itself produces sound. The mechanism is unclear. Perhaps gas bladders in the fronds, contracting and expanding with pressure changes. Perhaps something in the cellular structure, some piezoelectric property of the alien cellulose. The plants click and tap and occasionally emit low tones that might be communicative or might be nothing more than the sound of growth, of cells dividing, of a forest conducting its slow business in the dark.

---

The cold is constant but not killing. It sits at the exact threshold where ice would form if the salinity were lower, where hypothermia would be inevitable if you were truly biological in the way you assume. The water is dense enough, mineral-laden enough, that it behaves more like a heavy atmosphere than a true liquid. You move through it as through cold honey. The kelp fronds are rigid with chill, their tissues adapted to near-freezing in ways that make them almost crystalline at the edges—touch them wrong and they shatter like sugar-glass, though they will regrow from the break within a season.

The temperature never varies. Something deep below—geothermal vents, perhaps, or some slow nuclear process in the planet's core—maintains the Gardens at their precise frozen equilibrium. Warmer water rises from below, meets the ancient cold descending from above, and the mixing zone is here, in this stratum of dark and growth and ruin.

---

Light comes rarely and sideways. There is no sun. There may never have been a sun, or the sun may be so far above the water-column that its light disperses into pure abstraction long before it reaches these depths. What illumination exists is chemical, biological, geological—bioluminescent blooms that drift through like slow-motion snowstorms, each mote a single-celled organism pulsing blue or green or that specific amber that reads as warmth even when it isn't. Mineral veins in certain stones that hold light somehow, releasing it over hours or days in faint seepage. The corroded copper of ancient tools, which sometimes sparks with galvanic reaction when currents bring fresh minerals past.

In these moments of partial illumination, the scale of the Gardens becomes clear. The plateau extends in all directions, buckled and folded by geological forces but essentially flat, a high shelf in a deeper abyss. You can see grove after grove receding into murky distance. You can see the ruins between them, the suggestion of a planned settlement, a civilization that chose to build here in the permanent night of an underwater plateau and then chose to leave, or was taken, or transformed into something that no longer needed walls.

---

The processes never stop. Currents shift the silt in slow dunes that migrate across the seafloor over centuries. The bacterial mats grow and die and grow again in amber patches. The kelp forests expand and contract according to cycles too long for any single observation to capture—some groves are clearly young, their holdfasts fresh against the stone, while others have been here so long that they have become part of the geology, their ancient trunks mineralized into columns that no longer grow but still stand, monuments to themselves.

And the structures are changing too. Not decaying—the water here preserves more than it destroys—but being incorporated. The kelp wraps around them. The silt buries their foundations. The minerals in the water deposit on their surfaces in thin layers that will, in another thousand years, make them indistinguishable from natural outcroppings. The Drowning Gardens are swallowing their own history, not violently but inevitably, with the patience of a world that has nothing but time.

---

You float in the darkness between two groves, listening to the click-language of the kelp, feeling the cold press against you like a second skin. Something is different about this place—not wrong, exactly, but operating on principles you don't fully understand. The absence of animal life should mean silence, stillness, a dead zone. Instead, the Gardens are loud and active, full of vegetable commerce, mineral transactions, the slow negotiations of water and stone. Life found a way here, but it found a different way. The plants are the animals. The currents are the weather. The darkness is not absence but medium, the substance through which everything moves.

Somewhere below, the clicking intensifies briefly—a cascade of sound rolling through the kelp-trunks like a wave, like a message, like the Gardens dreaming of something they cannot name.
