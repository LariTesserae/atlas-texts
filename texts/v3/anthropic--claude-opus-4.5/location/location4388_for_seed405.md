# The Engine That Drowned Itself

Beneath the thermocline, where the cold Atlantic presses like a hand against your chest, there is a factory that was never built.

It occupies a pocket of space no larger than a cathedral's nave, though the word "occupies" suggests a relationship with space that doesn't quite apply here. The factory *insists* itself into being. You find it in the hollow between two underwater ridges, young volcanic formations still sharp-edged and black, their surfaces not yet softened by the slow patience of current and time. The ridges almost touch, leaving a gap perhaps sixty meters wide, and within that gap—

The machinery hangs suspended in the water column like an industrial jellyfish, like the fever dream of a drowned engineer. Pistons cycle without connecting to anything. Gears mesh with gears that aren't there until the teeth begin to interlock. Conveyor belts carry water to water, somehow distinguishing between the cargo and the medium. The whole apparatus pulses, contracts, processes *something*—you can hear it, a grinding and churning that shouldn't travel this clearly through seawater but does, a sound like factories heard from childhood bedrooms, that distant adult thunder of production.

There are no walls. No floors. The factory exists as pure function, machinery that remembered what it was supposed to do but forgot it needed a building to do it in.

---

The fauna have adapted, or perhaps the fauna were always here, or perhaps the factory is adapting to *them*—causality doesn't flow correctly in this place. The fish are dense, schooling between the mechanisms in ribbons of silver and grey. Small pelagic things mostly, their scales catching the strange even light that has no source. They flow *through* the gears sometimes, their bodies flickering, momentarily becoming teeth or cogs or drive-shafts before emerging on the other side as fish again. None of them seem harmed by this. None of them seem to notice.

Crabs colonize the conveyor belts, riding them in endless loops, filter-feeding on whatever the belts are transporting. Their shells have taken on a metallic sheen that might be adaptation or might be transformation—hold still long enough here and you start to wonder which category *you'd* fall into.

Larger shapes move at the periphery. Sharks, maybe. Something with the hydrodynamics of a shark. They patrol the boundary where the factory's influence frays into ordinary ocean, and they never cross into it, and they never leave. Waiting.

---

The water is cool—not the killing cold of the true deep, but the patient chill of spaces that never see sun. Twelve degrees, perhaps. Eight. The temperature fluctuates in ways that track with the machinery's rhythms rather than with any current. When the great central piston completes its compression cycle, the water warms by half a degree. When the nameless engines idle—and they do idle, they rest, they breathe in their mechanical way—everything cools again.

Sparse vegetation clings to the volcanic ridges that bracket this wound in physics. Tube worms, mostly, and bacterial mats in rust-orange and sulfur-yellow. Not much can grow here; the water chemistry shifts too often, becomes too industrial, carries too much of whatever the factory excretes. But at the edges, in the calmer zones, you find small gardens of the hardy and the strange. Anemones that pulse in time with the piston-rhythm. Kelp that grows in perfect right angles.

---

The light is the hardest part to accept.

It's normal light. *Surface* light. The kind of illumination you'd expect on an overcast afternoon, diffuse and grey-white and directionless. But you are—you *know* you are—hundreds of meters down, in a place where light should not exist, where pressure should have killed you, where your lungs should be full of ocean. The light doesn't come from the machinery. It doesn't come from bioluminescence. It simply *is*, as if this pocket of space has confused itself about its depth, has remembered being shallow and can't let go of the memory.

Sound, too, breaks its own rules. The grinding and churning is loud—oppressively loud at the factory's heart—but the acoustics are wrong. Sound should blur underwater, should lose its edges. Here it stays sharp. You can hear individual mechanisms. You can hear the click of crab-legs on metal that shouldn't exist. You can hear, if you listen carefully, something that might be a heartbeat or might be a pump cycling, slow and vast and patient.

---

The factory is processing *seawater*. That much becomes clear if you watch long enough. The conveyor belts carry it. The gears grind it. The pistons compress it. But what comes out—what drips from the far end of the apparatus in thick, luminous ribbons—is not seawater anymore. It is something heavier. Something that sinks through the ocean below the ocean, falling toward a floor that this place might not have.

The fish follow these ribbons down sometimes. They don't come back up.

New fish arrive to replace them. They always do. The school maintains its density as if replenishing itself from some infinite reserve, as if the factory is producing them too, somewhere in its maze of purposeful machines.

---

You could leave. The boundary is visible—a shimmer where reality remembers its own rules, where pressure exists again and darkness waits and sharks patrol their endless circuits. You could swim toward it and probably make it and probably emerge into an ocean that makes sense.

But the factory hums. The gears turn. The conveyor carries water to water, and somewhere in the cycling, something is being *made*. Something the ocean needs, or something that needs the ocean, or something that will replace both when it's finally finished.

The process is young. You can tell by the sharpness of it, the way the machinery hasn't yet worn smooth. Whatever this is, it started recently. Yesterday. A million years ago. Time, like everything else here, doesn't work right.

The crabs ride their endless conveyor.

The sharks wait.

The drowned factory churns on.
