# The Pocket Sea

It exists inside a seed.

Not metaphorically—literally inside a seed the size of a walnut, suspended in the amber sap of a tree so ancient its trunk has become indistinguishable from the ridge of basalt it grew from. The seed never fell, never germinated. Instead, it filled with cold water across centuries, its interior spaces expanding through some patient vegetable logic until what should have held an embryonic tree became an ocean.

---

The aperture is barely visible—a crack in the seed's hull no wider than a hair, where water seeps in from the perpetual mist that hangs in this sheltered ravine. Through means that shouldn't work but do, the interior space exceeds the exterior by a factor of perhaps ten thousand. You could swim for minutes in any direction without touching a wall, though the walls are always there, curving gently, made of the seed's own tissue grown strange and luminescent.

The water is cold. Not freezing, but cold enough that your movements become deliberate, your thoughts slow to match the place. It tastes of nothing—no salt, no mineral, no organic residue. Pure in a way that surface water never achieves.

---

They are everywhere: the plants.

Not rooted in soil because there is no soil. They drift in slow suspension, forests of kelp-that-isn't-kelp rising and falling on currents that seem to originate from nowhere. Their fronds are translucent, pale green and paler blue, lit from within by some bioluminescence that shouldn't exist in vegetation but does here, in this pocket where the rules arrive already bent.

The plants are the architecture. They have grown into structures—spirals and lattices, hollow spheres you could swim through, curtains of tissue-thin leaf that partition the space into rooms without walls. Nothing designed this. Nothing intended it. The plants simply grew, and what they grew into happens to resemble intention. A chamber here, a corridor there. Alcoves where the fronds thicken into something almost solid.

The vegetation fills perhaps a third of the available space, which means two-thirds remains open water—swimming lanes of emptiness between the drifting structures. Sparse for an ocean so dominated by plant life, but the sparseness is what makes movement possible. What makes observation possible. You can see far in any direction, watch the slow tumbling of a leaf-sphere in the distance, track the gradual collapse and rebuilding of a spiral as its component fronds age and are replaced.

---

The sound is the first thing you notice. The last thing you forget.

It comes from the plants themselves—or from the water moving between them, or from the seed's hull resonating with frequencies that originate in the tree's slow metabolism, or from all of these together. A droning, thrumming chord that fills the entire space at a volume that feels like pressure against the ears. Not harsh. Not grating. But *present* in a way that silence never could be. The sound is the medium. You swim through it as much as through the water.

It shifts over hours—not in melody, but in density. Sometimes the overtones cluster together and the drone becomes almost melodic. Sometimes they spread apart and it becomes texture, a roughness in the water itself. The plants seem to respond, or perhaps to cause the changes. Their fronds angle differently depending on the pitch.

---

There is no animal life. None. Not a microbe, not a larva, not a drifting spore-creature hoping to find purchase. The water is sterile in that one specific way while being full of vegetable presence. This should feel lonely. Instead, it feels correct—as if animals were never meant for this space, as if their absence is not absence but simply the natural state of an ecosystem that evolved along a different branch.

You are the only moving thing that chooses its direction.

---

The technology—if that's the word—is in the seed itself.

Its interior surface is covered in structures too small to see unaided but visible in their effects: the way light bends as it passes certain regions, the way temperature remains constant despite no apparent heat source, the way the water pressure stays survivable despite the depth implied by the space. These structures grew, the way the plants grew. Biological engineering at a scale that implies either unimaginable age or unimaginable sophistication or both.

Occasionally, a pulse of something moves through the hull—visible as a faint ripple in the bioluminescence, audible as a momentary shift in the drone's frequency. The seed is not dead. It is not dormant. It is *doing something*, has been doing something for so long that the doing has become indistinguishable from being.

---

Change comes slowly here.

A frond might take a week to unfurl. A spiral might spend a month collapsing before beginning its reconstruction. The water itself seems to move in geological time—currents so gradual they're imperceptible except as variations in where you find yourself after long stillness.

You learn to match it. You learn to hover in the cold water and watch a single leaf-edge brighten by the smallest measurable increment over the course of an afternoon. You learn that patience here isn't virtue but simply the only possible relationship with time.

---

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The plants glow. The hull glows. The water itself seems to carry luminescence as if brightness were dissolved in it. The effect is an even, pearlescent illumination that casts no shadows—or casts shadows in every direction at once, which amounts to the same thing.

It's beautiful. It's deeply, fundamentally strange. It shouldn't exist and yet it has existed for longer than most surface civilizations, this pocket sea inside a seed inside a tree inside a ravine that no one visits because the mist there never lifts and the rocks are too sharp for climbing.

You found it anyway. Or it found you—let you find it, let you through the aperture that shouldn't admit anything larger than a water droplet.

You swim between the drifting architectures. The drone fills your skull. The cold fills your body. And somewhere above you, separated by a membrane that is also a universe, a tree continues its slow growth toward a light it will never reach.
