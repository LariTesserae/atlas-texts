# The Cistern of Small Inheritances

Beneath the ruined city, in a chamber the old engineers hollowed from living limestone, the water table breathes.

The cistern was built to serve ten thousand households. Now it serves other purposes. The ceiling vaults twelve feet overhead, its plaster long since sweated away to reveal the pale rock beneath, and that rock weeps. Not dramatically—nothing here is dramatic anymore—but persistently, the way grief becomes routine. Moisture beads on every surface, collects in the carved channels that once directed flow, pools in the basin that spans most of the floor. The water is warm. Blood-warm. It has traveled through so much heated stone to arrive here that it carries the earth's own fever.

The light comes from above, through three shaft-holes where bronze grates rusted to nothing centuries ago. It enters as columns of dim ochre—the sun filtered through the dust of the dead city overhead—and spreads thin across the water's surface, catching the slight movements there.

They came for the warmth first, then stayed for the wet, then forgot there was anywhere else.

---

The frogs are the most numerous. Thousands of them, though you might not realize it immediately. They are small—the largest no bigger than a child's thumbnail—and they have evolved coloration that matches the mineral-stained stone: cream, rust, the grey-green of old copper. They cling to every vertical surface in clusters that look like lichen until you see the breathing. Their calls are not the booming chorus of marsh frogs but something smaller, higher, a sound like water dripping that isn't water dripping, a continuous soft static of communication that your ear learns to subtract from silence.

The salamanders live in the water itself, their bodies pale and elongated, grown strange in the perpetual dimness. They hunt the larvae of things. They are hunted in turn.

The hunters are harder to name.

There are eels, or what function as eels—limbless, sinuous, with skin that produces a faint phosphorescence when disturbed, so that their movements through the shallow water leave trails of blue-green light that fade over seconds. They are not aggressive. They eat what floats down from above: dead insects, organic debris, the occasional drowned rat from the city's remnants. But they are *present* in a way that makes the water feel occupied, owned.

And then there are the crabs.

---

The crabs should not exist. This is not crab habitat. There is no salt, no tide, no connection to any sea. And yet here they are: pale, soft-shelled, the size of coins, moving sideways along the basin's edge in their thousands. They click their small claws against the stone in patterns that almost suggest meaning. They have learned, across whatever impossible generations brought them here, to climb. You find them on the walls, on the ceiling, hanging inverted above the water like a living constellation, their eyes—dark beads on pale stalks—tracking movement with unnerving attention.

They do not seem to eat the frogs. The frogs do not seem to fear them. Some accommodation has been reached, in the long slow time this place has had to find its balance.

---

The vegetation is minimal but specific. A moss grows at the waterline, forming a ring of muted green around the entire basin. It is dense and fine and slightly iridescent in the light-columns. Nothing else photosynthesizes here—there is not enough light—but something else grows in the deeper shadows near the walls.

A fungus, pale and branching, emerges from cracks in the stonework. It spreads in formations that suggest coral, or perhaps brain matter, its surfaces glistening with the same moisture that covers everything. Small beetles with copper-colored shells navigate its branches. The fungus may be what the crabs eat. The fungus may be what everything eats, at some remove.

---

The city above has been dead for four hundred years. Not destroyed—abandoned. The water table rose, the wells turned mineral and strange, and the people left for rivers that behaved as rivers should. They left behind their cistern, their channels, their carved basins with decorative borders of geometric patterns that the crabs now use as highways.

In the silence—the *almost*-silence, the soft static of frog-calls and the occasional plop of something entering or leaving the water—you can hear the cistern continuing its work. It collects. It holds. It maintains its temperature through some principle of thermal mass, its moisture through the pressure of all that stone above. It will be here, doing this, being this, long after the city above has weathered to its foundations.

The crabs watch you. The frogs pretend not to notice. Something luminous moves beneath the water's dark surface, and you understand that you are a guest in a house that was never built for you, that has become, in the long absence of its builders, the property of small inheritors with strange patience and unknowable purposes.
