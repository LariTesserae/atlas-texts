# The Wept Terraces

The light is almost unbearable at first—a pale, sourceless brightness that seems to emanate from the wet surfaces themselves, reflected and re-reflected until the entire valley glows with a kind of cold luminescence. This is a world of condensation, of mineral milk, of water that has no source and no apparent destination.

The terraces rise in concentric tiers, hundreds upon hundreds of them, each lip no taller than a hand-span but stretching the width of the valley. They were built—you can see that immediately—but built by hands that understood patience in a way that defies comprehension. The stone is a pale calcium carbite, hand-shaped and hand-smoothed, fitted without mortar in joints so fine that water weeps through them in sheets rather than streams. Each terrace holds a shallow pool, each pool feeds the one below through channels carved with bone tools, and the whole structure descends like a frozen waterfall from the high col above to the basin below, where the water simply... stops. Gathers. Goes no further.

There is no life here. Not as you would recognize it. No moss clings to the wet stone. No algae colors the pools. The water is mineral-rich and sterile, supersaturated with dissolved calcium that leaves white crusts on every edge, that builds up in slow accretions on every surface not actively worn down by maintenance. And there is maintenance—you can see the evidence everywhere. Scrape marks where the crust has been chipped away. Channels kept clear by regular attention. Pools dredged of sediment.

But no one is here now.

---

The terraces are packed together so tightly that there is no stone visible between them—only the constructed lips, the held water, the carved channels. Every surface serves a purpose. Nothing was left unconstructed. Even the valley walls bear the marks of shaping: catchment basins carved high up to gather fog-drip, stone gutters running in switchbacks down to feed the uppermost pools. The air is cool and saturated, beading on every surface including your skin, and the brightness makes it hard to judge distance. What looks like a neighboring terrace might be a hundred paces away, the intervening pools invisible, all merged into a single sheet of reflected sky.

The fog comes and goes. Not dramatically—there is nothing dramatic here—but in slow pulses that might take hours to notice. The valley breathes, in its way. Moisture gathers on the high stones, trickles down, fills what was emptying. The terraces never overflow. The water level in each pool holds steady within a finger's-width, controlled by the geometry itself, by the precision of those ancient overflows.

---

Sound carries strangely. Your footsteps on the wet stone produce a muffled slap that seems to die immediately, absorbed by the moisture in the air. Occasionally there is a *plink*—a single droplet falling into a pool with a sound that seems too loud, too clear, before the silence closes over it again. The distant terraces produce a continuous near-silence that might be the sum of ten thousand such droplets, too soft and too constant to resolve into individual sounds. It is not quite quiet. It is full of almost-nothing.

The terraces are young, geologically speaking. Their edges are still sharp where they have not been worn by the water itself. The calcium carbite shows no weathering beyond what the mineral-laden water has deposited. Whoever shaped this place did so within memory—within the memory of stone, at least, which measures time differently than flesh. Perhaps a thousand years. Perhaps ten thousand. The builders understood what they were making: a machine for moving water from the clouds to the basin, slowly, in sheets, without haste.

---

There are structures among the terraces. Not buildings exactly—platforms of fitted stone, some raised slightly above the water level, others submerged in the shallow pools. They seem to serve as workstations, places where someone might kneel to chip away the calcium crusts, to clear a clogged channel, to perform the endless small repairs that keep the system flowing. Stone tools lie on some of these platforms: chisels shaped from harder mineral, scrapers of obsidian traded from somewhere far away, polishing stones worn smooth by use. They are laid out carefully, not abandoned—waiting.

In the basin at the valley's base, the water has built something of its own. A formation of pure white calcium, rising in rounded mounds and frozen cascades, grown centimeter by centimeter over decades or centuries as the supersaturated water finally releases its mineral burden. It looks almost like a frozen fountain, or like the ruins of a city made entirely of candle wax. This was allowed. Perhaps encouraged. The terraces above are maintained with obsessive care, but here, at the terminus, the water is permitted to build what it will.

The brightness flares slightly as the fog thins overhead—still no visible sun, just a diffuse intensification—and for a moment you can see the full extent of the terraces, tier after tier rising toward the col, each one holding its portion of the slow water, each one constructed by hands now absent. The whole valley hums with patient purpose. Whatever was meant to happen here is still happening, maintained by the geometry itself, by the careful construction that turns fog into flow.

The builders will return, or they won't. The water will keep weeping through the stone either way, filling and spilling, building its white monuments in the basin below. You are standing in a machine that has outlived its operators, or that waits for their return, or that never needed them at all except for those first shaping years. The terraces do not care which. They hold their water. They glow in the sourceless light. They wait.
