# The Plateau of Gathered Waters

The high tableland stretches for miles in every direction, its edges lost in the soft haze of perpetual humidity. This is young rock, thrust up from below within living memory of the stone itself—the basalt still holds sharp edges where it fractured during the uplift, and the jointing patterns remain crisp, geometric, not yet softened by the patient work of weather. But water has already found this place, has already begun its slow claiming.

The plateau's surface is not flat but gently undulant, rising and falling in long waves that create shallow basins and subtle ridges. Every depression holds water. Not deep water—rarely more than knee-height—but persistent, fed from below by springs that seep through the porous young rock, and from above by a sky that never quite decides whether to rain or merely breathe moisture. The air itself is thick with it, warm and close, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone.

The basins connect to one another through overflow channels, water trickling and spreading across the dark rock in thin sheets that catch the light. The entire plateau is webbed with these water-paths, a slow circulatory system without urgency. Where the rock dips low enough, true marshes have formed—patches of tall sedge and flowering rush, their roots submerged in water the color of weak tea, stained by tannins leaching from the vegetation itself. These green patches are scattered across the plateau like islands, separated by expanses of wet bare rock and the shallow reflecting pools.

---

The settlement called Three Springs sits near the plateau's center, where underground aquifers breach the surface through three distinct vents, each large enough to walk into if one were willing to wade chest-deep. The town spreads loosely around these sources, its buildings widely spaced—not clustered for protection or warmth, but arranged with breathing room, as though the inhabitants have internalized the plateau's own spacious logic.

The structures are made from the plateau itself. Cut basalt blocks, still sharp-cornered and precise, stacked without mortar in walls that follow the natural jointing of the stone. The roofs are something else—woven reed mats laid over wooden frameworks, the reeds harvested from the marsh-islands and renewed each year when they begin to leak. Every building has channels cut into the stone threshold, directing the inevitable seepage away from living spaces and back toward the nearest basin.

Perhaps eight hundred people live here, though the town seems built for fewer. Many structures stand empty or half-finished, as though Three Springs is waiting for something—more settlers, or simply holding space for possibility. The streets are not streets but causeways, slightly raised above the surrounding rock to stay dry during the heavy misting that passes for storms here. Stone steps descend from these causeways into the water-basins, worn smooth at their edges by generations of feet carrying jars and vessels to fill.

---

The people of the plateau have built their life around the water's rhythm, though the rhythm is so steady it barely qualifies as one. The springs flow at the same rate they flowed a century ago. The basins fill and overflow along the same paths. The marshes neither grow nor shrink. There is no flood season, no dry season—only the constant soft abundance of water that never quite becomes too much.

They work with copper tools, greenish and worn, and with stone implements that they shape from the plateau's own basalt. The copper comes from somewhere else, traded up from the lowlands in quantities too small to waste. A copper knife is a thing passed from parent to child. The basalt chisels and scrapers, though—those are everyday objects, knapped fresh when needed, left behind without sentiment when they break. The sound of stone shaping stone is one of the few sounds that punctuates the plateau's quiet: a rhythmic tapping that carries across the wet rock, answered sometimes by an echo from the empty distances.

They weave baskets so tightly they hold water. They dry reeds into rope. They fish, somehow, in the shallow basins—small silver things no longer than a finger, the only visible animal life, and even these seem barely present, glimpsed as flickers in the tea-dark water and then gone. Or perhaps not fish at all but something else, some plateau creature that exists in the space between categories. The people do not speak of them often.

---

The light here is soft and even. The moisture in the air scatters the sun, creates a luminous diffusion that casts few shadows and seems to come from everywhere at once. At midday the plateau glows. At evening it dims gradually, without drama, the sky shifting through shades of pearl and silver before settling into a darkness that is never quite complete—the wet rock still catching and holding whatever light remains.

Sound behaves strangely on the plateau. The moisture dampens everything. Voices carry but arrive muffled, their edges worn away. The water makes no real noise—it is too slow, too shallow, too patient. What remains is near-silence: the soft breath of wind across the basins, the occasional call of a marsh bird that might or might not exist, the distant tap of someone working stone.

The silence has a quality. It is not the silence of absence but of presence held still. The plateau is not empty—it is full of water, of stone, of the scattered green life of the marshes, of the patient people in their patient town—but everything here has found its equilibrium. Nothing strains against its boundaries. The water flows where it has always flowed. The reeds grow where they have always grown. The people draw water from the springs their grandparents used, carry it home along the same causeways, sleep in houses built from the same stone their ancestors quarried from the same exposed faces.

---

There are shrines at the three springs—not buildings, just arrangements of standing stones placed in the water, rising from the surface like fingers. The stones are carved, but simply: spiral patterns that might represent the springs' upwelling, or the movement of water across rock, or something else entirely. The people leave offerings here—woven reed-bundles, small copper beads, vessels of water poured back into the water it came from. The rituals have no priests, no elaboration. They are done quietly, individually, whenever someone feels the need.

At the plateau's edges, the land drops away sharply. The uplift that created this place left cliffs on most sides—sheer faces of young basalt falling hundreds of feet to forested lowlands that seem impossibly distant, impossibly green, impossibly alive with movement. From Three Springs, on clear days, you can see the forests as a dark blur at the horizon, beyond the haze. Few people go to the edges. The plateau is its own world, sufficient unto itself.

The young rock continues to seep. Water beads on every surface in the morning, collects in every hollow, finds its way through every crack. The plateau is saturated, has been saturated since it rose, will remain saturated until erosion finally carves it into something else—valleys, canyons, the skeleton of what it once was. But that is futures away. For now, the rock holds its shape, the water holds its paths, and the people of Three Springs hold their place in the soft bright silence, waiting for nothing, wanting nothing, the world around them as still and certain as stone.
