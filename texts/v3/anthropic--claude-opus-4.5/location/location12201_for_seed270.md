# The Nursery Vault

Beneath the salt pan, through a fissure no wider than a man's shoulders, you descend into the nursery.

The chamber is perhaps thirty meters across, no more—a bubble in the ancient mineral strata, its ceiling low enough that you could touch it if you reached. The walls are salt-glass, compressed over epochs into something between crystal and ceramic, veined with darker minerals that spiral inward like the growth rings of some planetary tree. No water has touched this place in geological ages. The air is cool and perfectly dry, preserving everything, eroding nothing further. This is a space that finished becoming itself before the first fish crawled onto land.

The floor is invisible.

Every surface—every plane, every crevice, every angle of this compressed space—writhes with life.

---

They came here when the surface died. The salt-crawlers, the stone-eaters, the chitin-bearers, the soft translucent things that have no names in any language. They found this pocket beneath the dead world and they *filled* it. Generations beyond counting have been born and died and been consumed in this chamber. The ecology is so dense that individual creatures cannot be distinguished without sustained attention—it reads first as texture, as living wallpaper, as a single pulsing organ.

The sound is constant. A wet industrial roar.

Mandibles clicking against mineral surfaces in overlapping rhythms. The scrape of countless small bodies dragging across salt-glass. A low thrumming from somewhere—vibrating through the floor, through your bones—that might be communication or digestion or reproduction or all three at once. Chittering that rises and falls in waves, as populations on one side of the chamber trigger responses on the other. The sticky-wet sound of creatures climbing over creatures climbing over creatures.

---

In the chamber's center stands the machine.

It is perhaps two meters tall—a cylinder of corroded iron, its surface rough with mineral deposits and the slow accretion of shed chitin. Pipes emerge from it at irregular angles, some broken, some still intact, disappearing into holes bored into the walls. A panel on its face, glass cracked but intact, displays nothing. A crank-wheel protrudes from one side, frozen in position.

This was an atmospheric pump. Industrial era, perhaps earlier. Someone lowered it down here to push breathable air into a mine, or to extract gas from some deeper pocket. It failed—or the operation was abandoned—and the machine was left behind.

The creatures use it as a hub.

Lines of flat-bodied things, like centipedes pressed through a mangle, flow up and down its surface in continuous columns. They carry fragments of mineral, shed exoskeletons, the bodies of their dead. Something in the machine still radiates warmth—residual chemical processes, or simply the heat of so many bodies concentrated around it. A clutch of pale eggs, each the size of a lentil, clusters in the crank-wheel's shadow.

---

The life here is not vegetation. There is no photosynthesis, no chlorophyll analogue. Instead: chemotrophy, lithotrophy, necrophagy. Creatures that extract energy from mineral bonds, from sulfur compounds leaching through the salt, from each other. The entire chamber is an engine of consumption and transformation, where nothing is wasted because nothing *can* be wasted in a space this small, this crowded, this old.

You identify—with effort—perhaps a dozen distinct forms.

The floor-carpet species: flat, segmented, overlapping like scales, colored in salts—white, pink, yellow, the orange of iron oxide. They are the foundation layer. Everything else walks on them.

The vertical climbers: those centipede-mangled things, moving in columns, their purpose unclear but their dedication absolute.

The ceiling-hangers: soft-bodied, translucent, pendant like living stalactites, occasionally dropping to the floor-carpet below with a wet *thap*, only to be consumed within seconds by whatever reaches them first.

The sphere-rollers: pill-shaped creatures that form defensive balls and are pushed—herded—by smaller attendants toward the machine or away from it based on signals you cannot perceive.

The singers: you cannot see them, but something is producing that low thrumming, that bass note beneath the chaos.

---

The chamber has rules. You learn them by watching.

Nothing stops moving. Motion is safety. The creatures that pause—injured, exhausted, dying—are consumed before they finish being still.

The machine is neutral ground. All species approach it, none attack near it. Perhaps the warmth is sacred. Perhaps there is some chemical boundary you cannot detect.

The ceiling drips. Not water—a mineral suspension, white and viscous, seeping from hairline cracks in the salt-glass. The floor-carpet species orient toward these drips, clustering beneath them, absorbing whatever nutrients leach from the dead world above.

Sound is language. The clicking, the thrumming, the scraping—these are not noise. Populations respond to acoustic signals you cannot parse. Waves of behavior ripple across the chamber's surface, triggered by sounds too low or too patterned for you to consciously hear.

---

And here is the strangeness, the thing that makes this chamber *unusual* rather than merely alien:

The creatures know you are here.

They do not flee. They do not attack. But since you descended through the fissure, the patterns have *changed*. The columns on the machine now move counterclockwise instead of clockwise. The floor-carpet has thickened near your feet, soft bodies climbing over each other to form a cushion you did not request. The ceiling-hangers drop more frequently, as if in display.

Something is being communicated. Something is being offered, or demonstrated, or tested.

The nursery vault has been here for epochs. It has perfected itself. It has no need of you.

But it is *interested*.
