# The Condensation Hall

Beneath the street-grid of a city that no longer remembers its builders, there is a single room the size of a cathedral. The ceiling arches forty meters overhead, ribbed with structural members that gleam faintly where condensation catches what little light exists. The walls curve inward at their base, meeting a floor that slopes gently toward a central drain—though the drain has long since ceased to function as intended, and now serves as the origin point of a shallow pool that expands and contracts with the room's breathing.

The room breathes. This is the first thing you notice, even before your eyes adjust: the sound of it. A vast rhythmic exhalation, mechanical but organic in its cadence, like the bellows-lungs of something architectural. Every eleven seconds, vents hidden in the darkness above release pressurized air that moves through the space in a warm wave, carrying with it the accumulated moisture of the city's water table, drawn up through capillary channels too fine to see. The air hits the cooler upper surfaces and surrenders its burden. Water beads. Water drips. The dripping is constant, arrhythmic, and everywhere—a rain that falls from ceiling to floor in ten thousand tiny threads, never heavy enough to soak you but persistent enough that you will never be dry.

Between these exhalations, there is suction. A lower register, almost subsonic, as the system draws breath back in through grates along the curved walls. The grates themselves are marvels—self-cleaning, self-maintaining, their mesh reconfiguring on scales your eye cannot track to prevent clogging. This is engineering that exceeds what you know how to build. The materials are recognizable in the way that bronze is recognizable: clearly metal, clearly worked, but the working speaks of knowledge that has moved past yours and kept walking.

The room is almost empty.

This is its function, you realize slowly. This is a space designed for emptiness. For the movement of air and water through vacancy. There are no furnishings, no machinery you can identify as separate from the room itself. The walls, where the condensation has not claimed them, show the ghosts of mounting brackets—thousands of them, in precise grids, sized for something that has been removed. Whatever equipment once filled this space has been taken, and what remains is the support system for its absence: climate control for nothing, drainage for nothing, structural capacity bearing no load.

Except.

In the far corner, where the curve of the wall creates a pocket of relative stillness, something has moved in.

It is not large—perhaps the size of a dog, though shaped nothing like one. It clings to the wall at an angle that suggests either suction or magnetism or something else entirely. Its surface is matte black and appears to absorb the scarce light rather than reflect it, making it difficult to determine where it ends and the shadow begins. It is the only fauna you have seen in the hours since you descended, and it has not moved since you noticed it. Whether it is sleeping, dead, or simply patient beyond your understanding, you cannot say.

The warmth here is blood-temperature. Not uncomfortable, but insistent. The air feels thick with it, substantial, like you could cup it in your hands. Combined with the moisture, it creates an environment that seems to want things to grow—but nothing does. The walls are clean of biological accumulation. The pool in the center holds no algae, no microbial film, nothing but water so pure it looks like liquid glass. Whatever sterilization processes run here, they run still, silent and perfect.

Along the eastern wall—though direction is a courtesy you grant yourself, having lost true orientation long ago—there are words. Cut into the surface, deep enough that condensation pools in their channels and runs down in legible streaks. The language is not yours but the meaning comes through in the way that warnings come through: bright, repetitive, emphatic. The words say CAPACITY and THRESHOLD and something that might be PATIENCE or might be PRESSURE or might be a concept for which you have no equivalent. They say these things over and over, in spiraling patterns, like liturgy or like instructions for a machine that reads through repetition.

The sound never stops. The breathing, the dripping, the subsonic pull. Occasionally there is something else—a metallic settling, a vibration that travels through the floor and up into your bones, as if somewhere deeper than this the city is shifting its weight. These sounds come every few minutes, unpredictably, and each time you flinch. Each time the creature in the corner does not.

You have been here for hours now. The entrance you came through—a maintenance shaft that spiraled down through geological strata clearly artificial, bedrock that was poured rather than formed—remains open behind you. You could leave. The city above is empty too, but it is a different emptiness, one with sky and wind and the memory of inhabitants. This emptiness is functional. This emptiness is the point.

The ceiling drips. The room exhales.

The creature in the corner opens an eye—or a sensor, or a aperture—and closes it again.

You realize you have not been breathing in time with the room, and you wonder if that is why it noticed you.
