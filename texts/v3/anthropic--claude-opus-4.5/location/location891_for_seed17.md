# The Perpetual Descent

The water falls forever.

Not rain—rain implies beginning and end, clouds and ground, cycle and return. This is something older and stranger: a planet-spanning column of falling ocean, a vertical sea that has been dropping through the same frozen coordinates of space for longer than anyone can remember measuring. The water is cold, just above freezing, and it falls through air that is colder still, through an atmosphere so vast and empty that the droplets never touch each other, never merge, never deviate from their parallel trajectories. Each molecule of water occupies its own private corridor of descent, separated from its neighbors by meters of howling nothing.

You are inside it now. You have always been inside it.

---

The sound is the first thing, and it is also the last thing, and it is every thing between. Not the roar of a waterfall—that would require surface, impact, the violence of water meeting stone. This is the sound of infinite parallel motion, of countless billions of droplets screaming past you at terminal velocity, each one singing its own frequency of displaced air. The frequencies layer and compound and interfere until they become something beyond sound, a physical pressure that enters through your ears and exits through your bones. Your thoughts arrive pre-vibrated. Your heartbeat synchronizes to a rhythm you cannot identify but cannot escape.

The locals—and there are locals, impossibly—call it the Chord. They say it has never changed pitch. They say if it ever does, that will be the end of everything, though they cannot say why they believe this or what ending they mean.

---

The water is clear, almost perfectly so, which is how you can see so far in every direction and find so little to see. Visibility extends for kilometers through the falling curtain, and those kilometers contain almost nothing. The droplets themselves are invisible at distance, too small and too separated to register as anything but a faint silvering of the air, a sense of the vertical made manifest. Occasionally—very occasionally—you will spot a dark shape moving through the distant haze, something with fins or fronds or flagella adapted to swimming through falling rain, hunting the microscopic life that drifts between the drops.

These creatures are abundant, the xenobiologists say, relative to the available space. Millions of them, perhaps billions, distributed through a volume of atmosphere large enough to swallow gas giants. They appear sparse because the Descent is so incomprehensibly vast that abundance itself becomes a form of emptiness. You could fall for years and encounter only dozens.

They have no names yet. The survey teams keep dying before they can complete their catalogs.

---

There is no ground below. Or rather: there is ground, there must be ground, but it is so far down that light from the surface stations cannot reach it and return. Bathymetric sonar suggests something solid approximately eight hundred kilometers beneath the median exploration depth, but the readings are strange, fractured, contradictory. Some frequencies return echoes that suggest crystalline structure, vast lattices of frozen water or mineral formation. Other frequencies return nothing at all, as though the sound simply continued falling forever, joining the Chord, becoming part of the world's single eternal note.

Some researchers believe the ground is theoretical. A mathematical necessity that the universe has not yet gotten around to instantiating.

---

The stations drift at fixed altitudes, though "drift" is the wrong word—they are stationary, anchored to nothing, suspended in the falling water by means that remain technically explicable but aesthetically impossible. They are industrial, ugly, built for function in an environment that punishes beauty. Steel and ceramic and carbon-fiber, studded with turbines that harvest energy from the eternal wind-that-is-not-wind, the pressure differential of water rushing past. The turbines scream. The generators scream. The water screams. Everything here screams; it is the local dialect.

The stations are scattered across a vertical range of perhaps a hundred kilometers and a horizontal spread that covers a significant fraction of the planet's circumference. They are few. Forty-three remain operational, out of an original deployment that no one will confirm the size of. Each one houses between eight and two hundred personnel, depending on function and current attrition rates. They communicate by cable—nothing wireless survives the Chord for long—and the cables are visible from the observation decks as faint black threads stitching together distant specks of light.

From the outside, at sufficient distance, a station looks like a single bright mote caught in an infinite gray shimmer. A fleck of warmth in cold forever.

---

The water is not ocean water. This is important and everyone forgets it. It contains no salt, no minerals beyond trace contamination from the stations themselves. It is distilled by its own endless falling, purified by passage through eight hundred kilometers of cold thin air. By the time it reaches the lower stations it is so chemically aggressive that it will leach metal from unprotected surfaces, will pull ions from ceramic, will gradually dissolve anything that is not perfectly inert or perfectly maintained.

The engineers say this is manageable. The engineers always look tired.

---

There is no weather here, which is its own kind of weather. The Descent maintains perfect equilibrium across its entire volume—same temperature, same pressure gradient, same rate of fall, same density of droplets, hour after hour, year after year, century after century. The survey probes found historical records in a language no one can read, carved into a metal plate recovered from a depth that should have been unreachable, and the records appeared to describe this exact phenomenon in this exact state. The plate was dated, through isotope analysis, at approximately eleven thousand years old.

Nothing had changed. Nothing has changed. The Chord holds its note. The water falls at the same velocity. The creatures swim their slow hunting patterns through the same endless vertical sea. Equilibrium is not a tendency here; it is a law, enforced by mechanisms no one understands.

The xenobiologists whisper that perhaps the planet is not falling water at all. Perhaps it is a single moment of falling, stretched across eternity, frozen in a state of perpetual almost-impact. Perhaps the ground below is the moment of contact, and the water will never reach it, and everything here exists in the held breath before a collision that will never come.

---

You drift at Station Seventeen, mid-depth, median-longitude, unremarkable in every parameter. The observation deck is a bubble of quiet—soundproofed to merely painful levels—and through its ports you watch the gray forever fall. A creature passes in the middle distance, something like a jellyfish and something like a kite, its body a thin membrane stretched between bony spars, riding the currents that exist between droplets. It is hunting. It is always hunting. There is nothing else to do here.

Below you, the abyss. Above you, the same abyss, inverted. To every side, the falling water, the scattered light, the empty howling distance.

The Chord enters your chest and makes itself at home in your lungs.

You have been here for six months. You will be here for eighteen more. Rotation schedules are strict because the attrition models are unforgiving, because everyone agrees that humans are not meant for this place, that consciousness was not designed to inhabit infinite falling.

And yet.

There is something peaceful about it, isn't there? Something almost holy about a world that has chosen its single note and held it for eleven thousand years. Something restful about a place where nothing ever changes, where tomorrow is guaranteed to be identical to today, where even death would simply mean joining the fall, becoming another small thing dropping through the gray forever, parallel to all the other small things, alone but never colliding, never stopping, never arriving.

The water falls.

It has always been falling.

It will always be falling.

You close your eyes and let the Chord tell you what that means.
