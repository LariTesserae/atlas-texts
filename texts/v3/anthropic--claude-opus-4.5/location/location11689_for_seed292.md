# The Drowning Peaks

The mountains still remember when they were taller. Now they are islands—worn, rounded, patient—rising from a world that has become almost entirely sea. Their summits breach the surface like the backs of sleeping creatures, and between them, under fathoms of cold rushing water, their slopes descend into valleys that have not known air in longer than anything alive can recall.

This is the Submerged Range. Kilometers of peaks reduced to archipelago. The water came slowly at first, then all at once, then slowly again—rising over centuries until only the highest points remained. And it did not stop moving when it settled. The currents here run like rivers through the sky, channeled and compressed by the drowned geography below, and they tear through these islands with a sound that never ends.

---

The water is cold—not freezing, but cool enough that it presses against the skin like a constant question. It tastes of mineral and time, of rock that has been dissolving for so long that the sea itself has become a kind of liquid geology. Above the surface, the islands are thick with life: forests of shore-pine and salt-adapted cedar, their roots gripping the exposed peaks like desperate hands, their branches bent permanently leeward by winds that carry spray for kilometers. Moss coats everything—chartreuse and emerald and a gray-green that seems to glow even in ordinary light. Ferns burst from every crevice. Mushrooms the size of dinner plates bracket the trunks of trees.

Below the surface, the vegetation continues. Vast kelp forests rise from the drowned slopes, their fronds reaching thirty, forty meters toward the light, swaying in the currents like flags in a hurricane. The kelp is old—individual holdfasts the size of houses, calcified onto rock that itself has been smoothed to curves by the endless water. Seagrass meadows carpet the deeper valleys, rippling in waves that move contrary to the current above them, responding to pressure differentials in the water column that create their own weather systems hundreds of meters down.

---

The sound is the first thing. It never stops.

Above the water: wind and crash and the grinding song of stone being slowly unmade. The waves here do not lap—they surge and boom and explode against cliffs that have been rounded by erosion but still tall enough to create spray-bursts that hang in the air like fog. The wind howls through the bent trees, and the trees creak and groan in response, and somewhere on every island there is always something falling—rock, branch, the carcass of a wave-bird thrown against stone.

Below the water: the deep roar of current, the percussion of boulders rolling in the valleys, the click and whistle of everything alive calling to everything else. The kelp forests are not quiet. They snap and rustle and hum as water forces through them. The fish—schooling in millions—produce a collective sound like static, like rain on a roof, like the murmur of a crowd heard from very far away.

---

The creatures have adapted to turbulence as a constant condition. On the islands, the wave-birds nest in caves, their eggs cemented to rock with a secretion harder than the stone itself. They have evolved to fly sideways, banking into winds that would tumble any mainland species. Their calls are pitched to cut through the roar of surf. In the forests, the canopy mammals have prehensile everything—tails, feet, hands that can grip bark even in the gales that sweep through every afternoon. The insects are armored, heavy, designed to fall and recover rather than to resist.

Underwater, the fish are muscular and patient, capable of holding position in currents that could strip paint from metal. The crustaceans have colonized the kelp holdfasts, entire communities living in the massive root-structures, venturing out to feed only when the current-weather permits. The deep valleys host creatures adapted to pressure and darkness: pale things, slow things, ancient lineages that have been exactly what they are since before the mountains drowned.

---

On three of the islands, there are structures.

They are old—industrial, or what remains of industrial after centuries of exposure. A processing facility of some kind, its metal bones still visible beneath the rust and the moss and the nesting birds. A dock system, its concrete pylons worn to curves, still attached to a breakwater that has been breached in seventeen places. What might have been a lighthouse, though its upper section has collapsed and its lower section has become a rookery for animals that scream through the night.

The machines have stopped, of course. They stopped long ago. But you can still see the logic of them in what remains: intake pipes running down into the water, their openings now hosting miniature ecosystems; conveyor frameworks whose rubber belts have long since rotted but whose metal rollers still turn sometimes, pushed by wind or by the curious hands of island creatures; a single smokestack, broken at the halfway point, home to a colony of wind-scarred vines that have learned to flower in the constant spray.

Someone processed something here. Extracted something. Used the mountains when they were still mountains, or used the water when it first began to rise. There are no records that matter. The facilities have become geography, as much a part of the islands as the peaks themselves.

---

The currents shift three times a day. It is not tidal—the forces involved are larger, stranger, involving the interaction of water masses at different temperatures and densities, channeled and accelerated by the underwater topography. When the northern current dominates, the water runs south between the islands at speeds that create standing waves, walls of water that form and hold and collapse and reform. When the deep current rises, cold water from the valley floors pushes up, bringing nutrients and confusion, turning the sea opaque with stirred sediment and feeding frenzies.

The transitions are worst. For an hour, sometimes two, the currents fight. The surface becomes chaos—whirlpools forming and dissolving, waves running in three directions at once, foam so thick it's possible to lose sight of the islands entirely even from close range. The underwater world during transition is a tumble of pressure and sound, everything that isn't anchored sent spinning through the kelp forests, fish schooling in defensive spheres that roll through the water column like living planets.

The island creatures know when transition comes. They burrow, they grip, they wait. It has always been this way. It will always be this way. The mountains are patient, and the water is not, and between them the world has settled into a kind of violent equilibrium that permits life but does not coddle it.

---

From the summit of the tallest remaining peak—a dome of exposed granite perhaps eighty meters above the current waterline, its surface worn smooth enough to be difficult to stand on—you can see the full extent of the range. Dozens of islands, some barely large enough for a single tree, others spanning kilometers and hosting forests that wave in the wind like green fire. The water between them is never still. It runs and surges and occasionally, when the light is right, you can see through to the depths: the dark shapes of the drowned peaks, the kelp forests rising like smoke, the shadows of things moving.

The horizon is water. The sky is spray. The sound is everything, always, and it has been exactly this loud for so long that the silence beneath it—the tiny spaces between waves, the held breath before a gust—feels louder than any noise.

The mountains are almost finished eroding. Give them another million years, and they will be nothing but sea floor, smooth and final, worn down to the basement rock that has been waiting since the world was young. The water will still move. The currents will find new paths. The creatures will adapt or die or both.

But for now: the islands rise, the forests grip, the machines rust slowly into the stone, and the drowning peaks endure another day of beautiful, endless destruction.
