# The Weeping Grotto

The water comes from everywhere and goes nowhere.

It seeps from the pale limestone walls in sheets so thin they barely qualify as liquid—more a constant brightening of the stone, a perpetual almost-falling that thickens the air and furs every surface with moisture. The grotto is perhaps forty paces across at its widest, ceiling low enough that you could touch it if you stretched, though you wouldn't want to. Everything drips. Everything has always dripped. The sound is less a dripping than a presence, a soft static hiss of ten thousand tiny waters finding their way down.

The floor is a maze of shallow pools separated by ridges of calcium deposit, each pool no deeper than your ankle, each ridge worn smooth as old soap. The water is warm—blood-warm, bath-warm—and perfectly still despite its constant replenishment. It must go somewhere, sink into some deeper limestone vein, but the drainage and the seepage have reached an agreement so ancient that the water level never changes. You could come back in a hundred years and step into the same pools at the same depth.

No plants. The warmth and wet should birth something green, but the stone offers nothing that roots could grip, and whatever minerals saturate the water have proven inhospitable to even the most desperate algae. Instead the grotto is painted in mineral colors—rust-orange ferric stains bleeding down from hidden iron seams, pale yellow sulfur halos around certain seeps, the grey-white of the limestone itself where nothing has claimed it. The ridges between pools show banding like tree rings, centuries of deposition laid down in layers you could count if you had patience and good light.

You don't have good light. The entrance—a slumped passage to the south where the ceiling drops nearly to the water—admits a grey glow that diffuses through the humid air and loses itself before reaching the far walls. Everything exists in soft focus, shapes suggesting themselves rather than declaring. The water catches what light there is and holds it, each pool a dim mirror reflecting a ceiling it can barely see.

The built things are old.

Three stone basins sit at the grotto's heart, carved from limestone blocks that must have been carried in—the native rock here doesn't form such clean shapes. They're simple work, hand-chiseled, the tool marks still visible where the mineral deposits haven't covered them. Channels connect them to particular seeps in the wall, guiding flows that someone, long ago, found worth separating. The ferric water goes to one basin. The sulfur-tinged flow to another. The third catches what seems to be pure limestone runoff, the clearest of the waters, though even it carries a faint mineral taste.

Why someone did this, what they used these separated waters for, the grotto doesn't say. The basins have no inscriptions, no decoration, nothing but function worn soft by time. Near the largest pool, a flat stone lies half-submerged—perhaps a working surface, perhaps a seat, perhaps just a stone that happened to be flat. A corroded copper cup rests on one of the ridges, green with age, its handle broken. That's all. Whoever built the basins, whoever drank from that cup, they left nothing else behind.

Sometimes a pale salamander moves through the deeper pools, slow and blind, hunting things too small to see. You might wait an hour between sightings.

The warmth is constant. The seeping is constant. The soft hiss of water finding stone is constant. The grotto has been doing exactly this for longer than the copper cup has been corroding, and it will continue doing exactly this long after the cup has gone to green dust. Nothing here is waiting for anything. Nothing here is becoming something else. The water weeps, the stone receives it, the pools hold their perfect level, and the dim light makes everything gentle and indefinite, a place that exists slightly out of focus, slightly warm, slightly wet, and absolutely, permanently, itself.
