# The Terrace Nests of Emmelwound

The sound reaches you before the place resolves itself—a layered cacophony that seems to have weight, to press against the skin. Frogs, yes, thousands of them, but also the drilling of insects, the territorial shrieks of small birds, the wet slap of things moving through water, the constant drip and trickle and pour of moisture finding its way down through levels of living architecture. And underneath it all, a hum, almost too low to hear, that you realize is the vibration of the wooden platforms and rope bridges as bodies move across them ceaselessly.

Emmelwound occupies a natural amphitheater in the hills—perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest, cupped by slopes thick with fern trees and dripping moss. The bowl itself has no ground, not anymore. Where soil might once have been visible, there is now only plant matter in various stages of life and decay, a spongy substrate that breathes water when stepped on. Pale roots web across the surface like veins on an ancient hand.

The people here—and there are many, far more than the space should reasonably hold—have built upward and outward into the vegetation itself. Platforms of woven reed and split bamboo cling to the trunks of massive tree ferns, connected by rope bridges that sway constantly with foot traffic. Some platforms hold single families; others are common spaces where figures crouch around smoking fires or tend to racks of drying fish and foraged tubers. The structures rise perhaps four or five levels in places, disappearing into the canopy where light filters green and gold through overlapping fronds.

Everything is wet. Moisture beads on every surface—the bark, the leaves, the rope railings, the skin of the people who move through this space. A light rain fell recently, or perhaps the rain never fully stops here; the air itself seems saturated, and you can see mist rising from the warmer lower levels where bodies and cooking fires generate heat. Water pools in the cups of massive bromeliad-like plants that have been encouraged to grow at the platform edges, serving as cisterns, washing basins, sometimes homes to kept fish or the fat amber larvae of water beetles.

The fauna makes navigation treacherous. Frogs the size of your palm sit on every available surface, their calls rising and falling in waves that seem almost coordinated. Iridescent beetles trundle across the bridges, ignored by hurrying feet. Small mammals—something like lemurs with prehensile tails and wet black eyes—swing through the middle canopy, occasionally dropping onto platforms to steal food and shrieking when driven off. Birds are everywhere: tiny jeweled ones that hover at flowers, larger ones with curved beaks that hunt the frogs, and a species of drab brown bird that seems to have domesticated itself, nesting in the eaves of human structures and feeding on scraps.

And there are the eels. The whole bowl, you realize, sits above a flooded depression—what might have been a pond once and is now a warm, murky breeding ground that extends beneath the root layer. Eels move through this water, occasionally surfacing at gaps in the platform structure, their pale bodies catching light. The people here tend them, or harvest them, or perhaps simply coexist; you see children dangling lines through holes in the lower platforms, and old women gutting long silver bodies on stone slabs worn smooth with use.

The settlement feels old but not ancient. The main support trees are mature, their trunks scarred where platforms have been attached and reattached over generations. Rope bridges have been replaced many times, the anchor points showing layers of wear. But the shape of things has stabilized—the paths are known, the gathering places established, the rhythm of the place set into its bones. It endures by continuous maintenance, by the labor of hands reweaving and retying and replacing what the wet and the creatures wear away.

The turbulence here is not destruction but activity. Bodies in constant motion: children chasing the lemurs, adults carrying bundles across swaying bridges, elders shouting conversations across gaps in the canopy. A dispute breaks out on a platform to your left—something about fishing rights in a particular section of the eel-water—and voices rise to match the volume of the frogs. On another platform, a group works together to reinforce a sagging section of floor, their coordinated grunts adding to the choir. Someone is always arriving or leaving by the paths that wind up into the ferny hills. Someone is always being born or dying in the small private spaces screened by hanging moss.

The light comes diffuse through the canopy, bright enough for working but soft, the color of new leaves. In places where the canopy parts, columns of brighter light fall through like blessings, and plants crowd toward these openings, their leaves overlapping in competition. The people have learned to grow things in these light-wells: climbing vines that produce small bitter fruits, shelf fungi cultivated on dead wood, trailing herbs whose leaves they crush for medicine or flavoring.

The air smells of green growth and black water, of smoke and bodies, of the particular musk of the frogs and something sweeter beneath it—the fermented smell of rotting vegetation being slowly processed by the substrate into new soil. It is not unpleasant, but it is thick, almost chewy. Breathing here feels like drinking.

You realize, after a time, that the boundaries of Emmelwound are both clear and porous. The settlement ends where the platforms end, where the paths become game trails, where the canopy closes fully and the cultivated gives way to wild. But the wild is not truly separate—the same frogs, the same birds, the same eels probably swim out through underground channels to wherever the water goes. The people here have not conquered this bowl in the hills; they have woven themselves into it, become another species in its ecology, loud and busy and fecund as everything else.

The hum continues beneath it all. The ropes sway. The frogs call. The rain, you suspect, will begin again soon.
