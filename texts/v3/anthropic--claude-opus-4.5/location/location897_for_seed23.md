# The Breathing Terraces

The city is built into a shoulder of mountain where the rock itself exhales. Every surface weeps—not rain, not meltwater, but something that rises from deep below, forced upward through a million capillary fractures in stone that has been cracking and resealing itself for longer than anyone can reckon. The water emerges cold, colder than the frigid air, and it gathers in films across every tilted slab, every carved step, every rope-lashed platform. Where the sun strikes it—and the sun strikes everywhere here, fierce and white at this altitude—the water throws back light so bright the whole settlement seems to flicker.

You can fit the whole place in a single long gaze. Perhaps three hundred structures, if you count the hanging baskets as structures, and the woven blinds stretched between the leaning stone pillars that someone carved a thousand years ago or that grew this way or both. The terraces step up the mountainside in irregular shelves, some natural ledges widened by generations of careful chipping, others built up with fitted stones so perfectly matched they might have fused together. The paths between them are worn to bowls and troughs, the stone remembering every foot that ever climbed this way.

Nothing stays still.

The fauna have learned to move with the water. Salamanders the size of a child's forearm, their skin pearl-white and faintly luminescent even in the blinding daylight, flow across the wet rock in continuous ribbons—never resting, always migrating between temperature gradients, following some logic of heat and cold that the people here have charted but never fully understood. Where three or four streams of salamanders converge, they pile and tumble over one another in knots that look almost intentional, almost linguistic, before unspooling again. The birds are stranger: short-winged and dense-bodied, they spend more time climbing the wet stone than flying, their feet spreading into something like suction cups that let them walk inverted along overhangs. Hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, moving in waves across the vertical surfaces of the terraces, picking at the thin orange lichen that grows wherever salamander-slime has dried.

The lichen is almost the only vegetation. That, and the cultivated moss-gardens the people keep in their deepest shelters, away from the light that would burn them brown. Some homes have a single treasured fern, wrapped in wet cloth, tended like a child.

The people themselves are part of the motion. They have built their lives around the fact that the mountain breathes, that the water flows are never the same from hour to hour, that the salamander-roads shift and the bird-waves follow and the lichen blooms and dies back in patches that seem random but carry information for those who know to read it. When a new seep opens in the rock—and they open constantly, the mountain's exhalation finding new paths—someone will be there within minutes, rerouting a channel, laying a new catch-basin of woven gut stretched over bent-wood frames. When a seep closes, the terraces downstream must adapt. Whole households relocate in a morning, carrying their possessions in nets slung between poles, finding new niches.

Their tools are bone and stone and sinew. Metal is a rumor from the lowlands. They chip obsidian into blades so thin the light passes through pink, and they use these to score channels in the rock, guiding water where they need it. They have rope-work so complex it functions almost as architecture—suspension bridges no wider than a hand, load-bearing nets, cradles for their young that hang from the stone pillars and sway in patterns calculated to soothe. Everything is lashed, knotted, woven. Nothing is permanent because nothing can be.

The sound is the sound of water doing everything except making noise. It moves in sheets too thin to drip, in films too slow to trickle. What you hear instead is feet on wet stone—the particular squeak and pad of skin on film-water—and the soft percussion of salamanders pouring over one another, and occasionally a bird-call so brief and high it seems more like a malfunction of the inner ear than a voice. The people speak in murmurs and hand-signs. Shouting would be grotesque here, somehow, a violation of the hush that the brightness enforces.

Because the light is religious. There is no other word. At this altitude, with the thin air and the wet stone throwing back every photon, the sun becomes something you don't just see but feel as pressure, as presence. The people squint and shade their eyes with carved bone visors, peer out from under canopies of stretched hide, but they also turn their faces up to it, letting it burn, as though the burning were necessary. Their skin is weathered to a texture like the rock itself—cracked and smoothed and cracked again.

What is strange here, beyond the constant motion, beyond the cold wet brightness, is the way time seems to be moving at different speeds in different places. On one terrace, a woman has been building the same stone wall for what looks like years—each stone fitted and refitted, tested against the water-flow, removed and replaced. On the terrace above her, a group of children dismantle and rebuild a rope-bridge three times in an hour, adapting it to a seep that has moved a hand's-width since morning. The salamanders cycle through their routes faster than the eye can track; the lichen grows across generations. The birds are somewhere in between, their wave-motions hypnotic, their purpose unclear but obviously urgent.

The mountain breathes out. The city breathes with it. Nothing rests, nothing settles, and yet everything here is ancient, everything worn into its perfect form by time and motion and the cold bright water that never stops rising through the cracked and living stone.
