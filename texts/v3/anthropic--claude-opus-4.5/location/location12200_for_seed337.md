# The Undercity of Growing Walls

You descend through the old well shaft, past the water table, into a settlement that is still in the process of becoming. The Undercity spreads through a network of natural caverns, but the caverns themselves are young—limestone depositing in real time, walls thickening perceptibly over generations, stalactites descending like slow rain frozen mid-fall. The ceiling drips. Not water, or not only water—mineral solution, calcium-rich, leaving pale residue on everything it touches.

The light comes from oil lamps set in niches that workers must re-carve every few years as the stone grows over them. Normal light, yellow and smoky, reflecting off wet surfaces and the pale accumulating mineral skin that coats every structure. The air is cool and damp, carrying the faint chalk-smell of active deite forming.

---

They have built a city in a place that is building itself, and the two processes compete.

The streets are packed tight—not from planning but from necessity. Every inch of floor space represents ongoing negotiation with the growing stone. Buildings crowd against each other, walls shared to conserve precious horizontal ground. The architecture is primitive but ingenious: hand-carved stone blocks, wooden beams treated with animal fat to resist the mineral creep, rope bridges spanning where the floor has grown too uneven to walk. Clay tiles on roofs, though here "roof" means the underside of the next level's floor, supported by columns that must be regularly checked for encrustation.

The city rises in terraces following the cavern's natural shelves. Three major levels, connected by carved stairs that curve around the growing formations rather than cutting through them—the people here learned early that severed stalactites bleed mineral-water for years, coating everything downstream in unwanted stone.

---

Population density pushes at every boundary. Homes are narrow and deep, carved back into walls rather than spreading outward. Markets operate in shifts because the central cavern cannot hold everyone at once. You hear the quiet shuffle of thousands of feet on stone, the low murmur of voices kept soft by convention—sound carries strangely here, echoes building on echoes, so the culture has evolved toward near-silence. Conversation happens close, faces inches apart, words barely above breath.

The moisture is intermittent, following patterns the tunnel-keepers track obsessively. Dry periods—sometimes lasting weeks—allow for major construction. Wet periods drive everyone indoors and coat the streets in slick mineral film. Small pools collect in low points, carefully channeled to cisterns or allowed to drain through carved channels to the deeper caves below. Some of these pools host pale, blind fish—the only fauna that thrives here, protected and occasionally harvested for oil and protein.

Vegetation exists in cultivated pockets: fungus gardens in humid chambers, pale root vegetables grown in composted waste and mineral-enriched soil hauled from the surface. The farms are sparse but essential, tended by specialists who understand the precise moisture and temperature requirements. Lichen grows wild on any surface left untreated, adding organic orange and grey-green to the dominant whites and yellows of the mineral formations.

---

What makes this place strange—not weird, just deeply unusual—is the constant, visible activity of geological time compressed into human scale. You can watch the walls grow. Not moment to moment, but week to week, month to month. A doorframe left untreated will narrow perceptibly over a season. The stone remembers everything in its layers: a generation's worth of soot, a year when the mineral content changed and left a darker band, the outline of a ladder that stood too long against a wall.

The people here think in accretion. Their calendar marks wet-seasons and dry-seasons and the great slow pulse of the deposit cycle. Their craft tradition centers on working with the growth—encouraging stone to form around objects, embedding tools and treasures in living walls, creating surfaces that the mineral solution will coat and preserve. Their dead are placed in alcoves and allowed to become part of the architecture.

The city is perhaps two hundred years old. The caverns it occupies will not exist, in their current form, in another five hundred. The Undercity is a temporary arrangement between human need and geological emergence, each reshaping the other in the cool damp dark, the quiet broken only by dripping water and the soft sounds of people living very close together in a world that is still learning what shape it wants to be.
