# The Grown Plateau

The first thing you understand is that nothing here was placed. Everything was planted.

The plateau rises from lowland mists like a thought half-formed—its edges still soft, still deciding where they want to be. But the softness is deceptive. What looks like stone is bark compressed beyond reason, centuries of growth folded into itself until it achieved the density of granite. What looks like soil is root-matter so ancient and interwoven it has forgotten it was ever separate plants. The entire landmass is one vast organism, or perhaps a consortium of organisms that long ago agreed to become architecture.

You stand on a surface that breathes. Not metaphorically—the ground beneath you rises and falls in slow tidal rhythms, the plateau's metabolism working at geological pace. The movement is so gradual you only notice it when you've been still for an hour and realize the horizon has shifted by a hand's width. This is the only change. The Grown Plateau reached equilibrium centuries ago and has no intention of disturbing it.

---

The geography extends in every direction with the logic of a planned city, though no mind planned it. Boulevards of moss-covered mega-stems radiate from central nodes where trunk-matter has spiraled into cathedral spaces. The vegetation doesn't fill these spaces so much as *constitute* them—walls are woven from living wood, floors are packed leaf-litter fused into something like tile, ceilings are canopy so dense it functions as thatch. There are rooms here. Corridors. Plazas open to grey-white sky where the canopy thins to allow rain through designated channels.

The rain falls often. You can taste it before it arrives—the air thickening, the moisture already present in everything intensifying until drops simply manifest from oversaturation. Water runs through the plateau along routes that were grown for it: gutters of curved bark, cisterns that are hollow gourds the size of houses, aqueducts formed from vine-networks trained over centuries into perfect gradients. Nothing pools. Nothing floods. The plateau drinks what it needs and channels the rest to weeping walls at its edges, where waterfalls feed the mists below.

The sounds of water are constant. Dripping. Trickling. The hollow percussion of rain on drum-tight leaf-membrane. Occasionally a distant roar as some major channel releases accumulated flow. And beneath it all, something stranger: a hum. Low and resonant, it rises from the plateau itself—the vibration of sap moving through vessels wide as tunnels, the subsonic song of a living mass so large it generates its own acoustics. You feel it in your teeth more than hear it.

---

Walk the moss-boulevards and you find them empty. Not abandoned—the concept doesn't apply. The spaces between structures stretch wide and silent, scattered with only the occasional feature: a bench-like growth, a fountain where water wells up through porous wood, a spiral staircase of fungal shelves climbing toward an observation platform. The plateau built all of this for no one in particular. Or perhaps for inhabitants who never arrived. Or perhaps for inhabitants who were always the plateau itself, and these apparent structures are organs rather than architecture.

Rare movements catch your eye. Something like a bird—but feathered in leaves, hollow-boned with plant fiber—drifts between canopy layers. It makes no sound. In a cistern, something long and pale undulates just below the surface: a worm or fish or root-tendril granted motility. These creatures are not visitors. They are the plateau's fauna, grown from the same substrate as everything else, performing functions you cannot parse. Pollination, perhaps. Pest control. Or simply decoration—the plateau's notion of wildlife.

---

The light here is strange only in its consistency. The canopy filters everything to a warm amber-green, shadowless and even, as though the plateau has optimized for a particular quality of illumination and achieved it permanently. Morning and evening look the same. You suspect noon looks the same as well. The sky above the canopy shows clouds perpetually, but their movement brings no variation to what reaches below.

You climb one of the observation platforms—a structure that might be a watchtower if anyone were watching. From here, the plateau's extent becomes clear: valley-sized, ridge-backed, its boundaries marked by the transition from solid growth to the unformed regions where new plateau is slowly accreting. You can see the edges in mid-emergence—raw and wet, bark not yet hardened, structures half-realized, the blueprint of rooms visible in the grain of young wood. The plateau is still growing. Just imperceptibly. Just along its margins.

At the center, where you are, nothing has changed in longer than memory. The equilibrium is total. The architecture breathes and drinks and hums, and waits for nothing, and completes itself in every moment exactly as it completed itself in every moment before.

You could live here, you realize. The plateau has made spaces for living. It simply hasn't specified what kind of living, or for whom.
