# The Breathing Plateau

The ground here is not ground. It only pretends.

What looks like stone is moss so old it has forgotten it was ever soft—compressed into itself across ages that predate the mountains it has consumed. The plateau stretches for miles in every direction, a vast table of living matter that has slowly, patiently eaten everything that once rose above it. There were peaks here once. The moss remembers them as slight undulations, gentle swells in its endless body, the last whispers of geology absorbed into botany.

Everything is wet. Not from rain—the sky here is a permanent bruise of pewter and violet, too cold to release what it holds—but from the moss itself, which exhales constantly. The air is thick with its breath, a perpetual mist that rises from the surface in slow curls, hangs at knee-height, disperses, reforms. Walk here and your footsteps leave dark impressions that immediately begin to weep, filling with water that has no source except the living ground's endless respiration.

The cold is patient. Not sharp, not biting—simply present, the way time is present, soaking through fabric and skin into the architecture of thought. Your breath fogs. The mist mingles with it. You cannot tell where you end and the plateau begins.

---

And then there is the sound.

It comes from below. From within. A vast, slow grinding that rises through the moss-stone in waves—the sound of the plateau *digesting*. Because the moss is still eating. Still working on whatever lies beneath: the bones of ancient industry, the iron hearts of machines that fell here or were built here or grew here in some era when the rules were different. You can feel it through your feet: the vibration of enormous slow processes, of matter being unmade and remade, of metal becoming mineral becoming moss.

Sometimes the surface cracks open.

Not violently—nothing here is violent exactly, only *insistent*—but with a sound like tearing silk scaled up to cathedral proportions. Fissures appear, yards long, and from them rises steam that smells of copper and chlorophyll and something older, something that predates scent. In the dim light, you can see down into these wounds: layers of compressed green-black matter veined with rust, with cable, with things that might be roots or might be wire or might be both simultaneously, and at the bottom, always receding, the suggestion of vast machinery still *moving*, still cycling through whatever purpose it once had, now incorporated into the plateau's endless metabolism.

The fissures seal themselves within hours. The moss knits closed. The grinding continues.

---

The fauna have adapted to turbulence as a way of life.

Vast herds of something like musk oxen move across the plateau in patterns that seem random until you watch long enough to recognize they are *avoiding*—reading vibrations in the moss, steering around the places where the ground will open, finding the momentarily stable paths through a landscape in constant slow upheaval. Their wool is moss-colored, their eyes are white and filmed over, their hooves have evolved into broad pads that distribute weight so carefully they barely leave prints.

Above them, always above them: the fog-waders. Birds that have become something else, something that doesn't quite resolve when you look directly at it. They stand seven feet tall on legs like stilts, their bodies hidden in the permanent mist layer, only their legs visible from below, picking their way across the plateau in search of the small burrowing things that live in the moss. When they call to each other, the sound is mechanical—a clicking, a whirring, as if their throats contain gears. Perhaps they do. Perhaps the plateau has been feeding them iron for so long they have become partly what they eat.

In the deeper mists, further out where the plateau begins to fold into itself in ways that trouble the eye, there are larger shapes. They move with the grinding. They may be causing it, or they may be caused by it. From a distance, they look like factories walking.

---

The light never changes. The sky holds its bruise-color through what must be day and what must be night, though time here feels less like a progression and more like a medium you move through. The horizon is always visible and always uncertain—the plateau extends until it becomes the mist, becomes the sky, becomes a color that is neither ground nor air. There are no landmarks except the temporary ones: a fissure steaming in the middle distance, a herd moving along a ridge that will be flat by tomorrow, the distant silhouette of a fog-wader going still as something beneath the surface passes under it.

The moss-stone ripples sometimes, long slow waves that cross the entire visible landscape like thoughts moving through a vast dreaming mind. When this happens, the herds stop walking and lower their heads. The fog-waders fold their impossible legs and sink until only their eyes remain above the mist, glinting like ball bearings. The grinding becomes almost musical, harmonic, as if the ancient machines below have found some temporary alignment.

Then it passes. The ripple reaches the horizon and continues into the places you cannot see. The plateau exhales another breath of mist.

You are standing on something alive, something that is eating, something that has been eating for longer than your species has existed. It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is simply *continuing*, as it has always continued, digesting the last world into the substrate of whatever comes next.

The water seeps up around your boots. The cold settles deeper.

Somewhere in the mist, gears click in a throat made of moss and rust, and a shape you cannot fully see begins to walk.
