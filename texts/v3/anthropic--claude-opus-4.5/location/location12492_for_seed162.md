# The Breathing Plateau

The continent rises from the world's mantle like something still being born. You understand this immediately—not through observation but through the way your body interprets the ground beneath you, which is not ground at all but something closer to *becoming*. The plateau spans horizons in every direction, a warm upland mass the size of a small ocean, and it is making itself as you watch.

The stone here has not yet decided to be stone. It emerges in sharp crystalline extrusions—obsidian towers, sheets of something like cooled glass threaded with copper veins, formations that catch the dim amber light and hold it suspended in their matrices. These structures are hours old, days old at most. You can see where they've punched through the rich dark soil, where they've tilted older growths into new angles, where they've created sudden cliffs that the vegetation is already racing to claim.

And the vegetation *does* race. The plateau's warmth—generated from below, from whatever engines of creation drive this place—sustains a jungle that operates on borrowed time and knows it. Broad-leafed plants with surfaces like oiled leather cluster in the valleys between crystalline ridges, their root systems shallow and fast, their reproduction cycles measured in days rather than seasons. They fruit constantly, dropping seeds that germinate before hitting the soil. Where a new stone formation emerges, the plants die in the lifting, but their remains become substrate within hours, and new growth follows the tilting faces of fresh crystal toward whatever light filters through the perpetual haze.

The haze itself is water that cannot decide where to belong. It condenses on the warm stone and evaporates before it can pool. It gathers in the spaces between leaves and releases as droplets that fall upward when the ground shifts. Rivers start and stop in the course of a day—not drying up but being *consumed* by the geology, their water pulled into fissures that open and seal, emerging miles away as warm mist from new vents. The air tastes of mineral richness and the green-black scent of rapid decay and faster growth.

---

The light comes from somewhere you cannot locate. Not the sky—what passes for sky here is a bruised amber ceiling, thick with particulate matter and something else, something that moves with purpose rather than wind. The illumination seems to emanate from the crystalline formations themselves, though not uniformly. Some towers glow from within like lanterns submerged in honey. Others absorb what light reaches them and give back nothing. The effect is of perpetual late afternoon, of a sun that set elsewhere and left only its afterthought.

In this dim warmth, the fauna have found their niches, though "niche" implies stability that does not exist here. Instead, the animals of the plateau have evolved for turbulence, for a world that remakes itself beneath their feet without warning.

You see the first of them picking their way along a ridge of fresh obsidian: creatures like six-limbed cats, their bodies low and balanced, their paws spread wide with retractable membranes that sense vibration in the stone. When the ground begins to shift—when a new formation announces itself through subsonic rumbling—they flow like water toward stability, their movements so fluid they seem to predict rather than react. They hunt in small packs, mostly silent, communicating through touches of whisker and ear. Their prey is anything too slow to escape the upheavals: insects the size of your fist that cling to the undersides of crystal shelves, soft-bodied grazers that feed on the fruiting vegetation and freeze when the ground speaks.

Higher up, where the formations reach their sharpest peaks, flying things navigate the haze. Not birds—something older in body plan, with four wings and trailing filaments that taste the air for the chemical signatures of imminent emergence. They roost in the brief stability of newly-formed crevices, laying eggs that hatch within hours, the young fledging before the stone that holds them can decide to grow further or collapse. Their calls, when they make them, are soft clicks and whistles that echo strangely off surfaces still too fresh to have developed acoustic predictability.

And below the surface—you know this without seeing, know it the way you know the plateau is alive—there are things that swim through the semi-solid medium of becoming-stone. Pressure-adapted, patient, emerging only when the geology opens pathways between depths and surface. You find evidence of them in the spiral patterns that sometimes appear on fresh formations, as if something vast had pressed itself against the other side of reality and left an imprint in the moment of crystallization.

---

The strangeness here is structural. It is not that individual things behave wrongly—it is that the rules themselves are in flux, contested, still being negotiated between forces you cannot name.

You notice it first in the shadows. They do not fall where they should. A crystal tower catches light from the amber haze and casts darkness in three directions simultaneously, each shadow moving at a different speed as if responding to different suns. Some shadows are colder than others—you can feel them on your skin, distinct temperatures for darkness cast by the same object. One of the cat-creatures passes through a shadow and emerges with its fur wet, though no water fell.

Then you notice the distance. Looking toward what should be the plateau's edge—if such a vast formation can be said to have edges—you see features that should be miles away. But the air between here and there does not behave as air should. Objects in the middle distance seem closer than objects nearby. A crystal formation on the horizon looms larger than one fifty yards ahead. When you try to walk toward a specific landmark, you find yourself approaching something else, something that was to your left when you started but is now directly before you, as if the plateau rearranged itself while you weren't watching—which it did, continuously, though not in the way your displacement suggests.

The ground here is not just making itself; it is making *space*, generating volume that should not fit within its visible boundaries. The plateau is larger on the inside. Perhaps infinitely so. Perhaps the far horizons you see are not far at all, or are farther than any distance you have words for.

Time, too, moves strangely in pockets. You find a clearing where the vegetation has achieved unusual height, trunks thick and woody in a way the rest of the fast-growing jungle is not. The trees here are old—decades old, perhaps more—in a landscape where nothing survives more than weeks. When you enter the clearing, the air feels different. Slower. Your own heartbeat becomes audible to you, each pulse stretched into something you can almost examine. Insects hang suspended in flight patterns that take hours to complete. A fruit falls from a high branch, and you watch it descend for what feels like minutes, its motion a gentle demonstration of gravity's patience.

Then you step out of the clearing, and time resumes its normal pace, and behind you the clearing begins to sink as new crystal pushes up from beneath, and the old trees—the impossible ancient survivors—topple silently into the rising stone and are gone.

---

The sound of the plateau is the sound of restraint. So much is happening—stone emerging, vegetation racing, creatures moving, reality *negotiating*—and yet the noise of it all seems dampened, absorbed by the haze and the soft surfaces of new growth. You hear your own breathing more clearly than you hear the world.

But not silence. Beneath the quiet, there is a bass tone you feel rather than hear, a frequency that lives in your chest and teeth and the fluid of your eyes. It is the plateau's heartbeat, or perhaps its voice, a constant pronouncement of emergence that never quite resolves into sound. Occasionally, when a formation breaks surface—when a spire of black glass punches through soil and vegetation in sudden birth—there is a grinding, crystalline chime, almost musical, almost linguistic, as if the stone were saying something and you were hearing only the consonants.

The cat-creatures are silent when they move but make a sound when they stop: a low vibration in their throats, barely audible, that seems to calm the ground beneath them. You wonder if this is communication with the plateau itself, some negotiation for temporary stability, a request for a moment's stillness in the endless becoming. The flying things click and whistle, but softly, as if aware that louder sounds might attract attention from something that should not notice them.

You do not want to know what would notice them. You do not want to think about what listens, here, in this place where reality is still being assembled.

---

The plateau offers no stable reference points. What seems solid will shift. What seems distant will be suddenly close, or will have never been where you thought it was. The vegetation will grow over your footprints within hours. The crystal formations you use as landmarks will be consumed by new growth or will themselves grow into unrecognizable shapes.

And yet things live here, abundantly. The cat-creatures and their prey and the flying things and the swimmers in the semi-stone and a thousand varieties of insect and crawling thing and things you have no category for—all of them thriving in chaos, all of them adapted to a world with no fixed ground.

Perhaps you will adapt too. Perhaps, if you stay long enough, you will learn to feel the vibrations that precede emergence. You will taste the chemistry of the air and know where stability lies. Your shadow will fall in three directions and you will know which one to follow.

Or perhaps the plateau will adapt to you. Will make space for you in its endless becoming. Will fold you into its logic, which is no logic at all, which is simply the continuous urgent process of a world that has not yet finished arriving.

The stone beneath your feet grows warm. The haze thickens to your left. Somewhere close, something is about to emerge.

You have time to move, if you know which way to go.
