# The Condensery

A natural chimney opens in the high frozen plateau, dropping forty meters into a chamber that shouldn't exist at this altitude. The walls are volcanic glass—obsidian smoothed by ten thousand years of thermal cycling, cracked in places by the cold, mended in others by mineral seep. Light pours down through the chimney like a searchlight, so bright against the surrounding darkness that the air itself seems to have texture, and you can see individual ice crystals suspended and slowly falling.

The chamber is perhaps thirty meters across at its widest, narrowing toward both ends into passages too tight for a human body. Every surface that isn't glass is covered in frost formations—not the simple rime you'd expect, but elaborate crystalline architectures. Some grow in flat hexagonal plates stacked like pages of a book. Others spiral upward in hollow tubes, or branch into feathered structures that tremble visibly when air moves through the chimney above.

There is no life here. Nothing grows, nothing feeds, nothing reproduces in the biological sense. But the frost is *active*. You can watch it happening if you're patient—the slow advance of crystal edges, the occasional soft collapse of an overgrown formation, the way moisture migrates from warmer surfaces toward colder ones in patterns that almost seem purposeful. The chamber breathes with a cycle tied to the sun's passage overhead. When light floods in, the temperature rises just enough to sublimate the thinnest crystals. When shadow returns, they rebuild.

At the chamber's center, someone has stacked stones.

The cairn is old—older than the current frost formations, which have begun to colonize its surfaces. The stones aren't volcanic glass but granite, carried here from somewhere else, from *far* below, probably. They're stacked with care, each chosen to balance on the one beneath it, the whole structure rising to chest height in a rough spiral. Near the top, someone has wedged a shaped piece of bone—from what animal, in this lifeless height, is unclear. The bone has been carved with simple marks. Not writing, perhaps. Or writing in a system now forgotten. The marks have been partially obscured by mineral deposits, white blooms of calcium or silica that make the bone look diseased.

Other signs of passage: a shallow depression in the glass floor where someone once made fire, the surface bubbled and rehardened into a rough patch. A series of handholds chipped into the wall near one of the narrow passages, leading up toward a ledge that no longer exists—the glass has calved away at some point, taking whatever the handholds once led to. Near the chimney's base, wedged into a crack, a stone blade with a broken tip. The edge is still sharp after however many centuries. Whoever left it either couldn't retrieve it or chose not to.

The sounds here are small and precise. The tick of contracting glass as temperatures shift. The whisper of frost crystals growing, a sound almost below hearing, like paper being written on very slowly. Occasionally, wind in the chimney above creates a low harmonic tone—the whole chamber is shaped, perhaps accidentally, to resonate at certain frequencies. When this happens, the suspended ice crystals dance in visible patterns, congregating in nodes and antinodes before settling back into random drift.

Moisture enters primarily through the chimney—fog and cloud-vapor pulled down by some thermal trick, condensing on every surface before slowly migrating toward the coldest point, which is currently a shadowed alcove on the chamber's southern side. Here the frost has built a structure nearly a meter deep, a frozen waterfall that will never flow, its surface etched with interference patterns from repeated cycles of sublimation and regrowth. In another thousand years this accretion may block one of the narrow passages entirely, changing the chamber's airflow, beginning some new phase in its slow transformation.

The light moves. It is doing so now. The sun's angle through the chimney shifts the bright column across the floor by perhaps a meter each hour, and wherever it touches, the frost begins its brief dying—sending up faint streamers of vapor that catch the light themselves, creating a haze that shifts and reforms. In the illuminated zone, the obsidian floor reveals its depth: layers of flow and cooling, trapped bubbles, internal fractures that scatter light into faint rainbows. When the sun is directly overhead at midday, the entire chamber fills with reflected brilliance, every crystal surface throwing light at every other until you cannot tell where illumination ends and solid matter begins.

Then shadow returns. The chimney holds light only at certain hours. The rest of the time, the chamber exists in a deeper darkness than seems possible—the obsidian absorbs almost everything, and only the frost formations show as faint gray shapes against pure black. In this darkness, the sounds become more prominent. The growing frost. The settling stone. The deep, nearly subsonic tones when wind finds the chimney's edge.

Something about the cairn suggests memorial rather than marker. The bone placement, the care with which the stones were chosen. Someone climbed to this place, carried granite from far below, built this structure in a chamber of glass and eternal cold, carved marks into bone, and then—left, presumably. Returned to wherever humans or human-like beings lived in the lowlands, in warmer and softer places. Left this behind as the frost began its patient colonization.

The marks on the bone might be a name. Or a date. Or a warning. The frost, indifferent, continues to grow over them.
