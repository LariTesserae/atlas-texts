# The Rootworks

The sound comes first—a vast, grinding respiration that fills the entire basin, the slow hydraulic wheeze of ten thousand roots drinking in unison. It is not loud in the way of sudden things. It is loud in the way of oceans, of wind through mountain passes, of processes so large they become atmosphere. The sound *is* the air here, pressing against eardrums with the patient weight of growth itself.

You stand in what was once a quarry, perhaps, or a meteor scar—some wound in the earth that measured three miles across before the forest claimed it. The walls slope down in rough terraces, and everywhere the roots have come. Not trees as such, not anymore. The vegetation here has passed beyond individual organisms into something more like infrastructure, a single interconnected mass of woody tissue that fills the depression like frozen water, like a solid lake of pale growth.

The roots are enormous—arterial trunks as wide as rail cars, branching into capillary networks that web across every surface. They are not brown but white, the bleached ivory of things that have never seen sun, their skin smooth and slightly damp with condensation that beads and runs in slow tributaries down their flanks. Where they touch, they have fused, creating arches and chambers, hollow spaces where the growth has wrapped around itself and rotted from within, leaving cathedral vaults of living wood.

The cold is botanical. It radiates from the root-mass itself, which breathes out refrigerated air as it processes whatever minerals it draws from the broken stone below. Your breath fogs. Frost rimes the older roots where they meet the basin walls, delicate white fur on white flesh.

---

The light filters down from somewhere far above—the basin's lips are thick with conventional forest, dark conifers that lean inward and block most of the sky. What reaches the floor is gray and diffuse, sourceless, barely enough to distinguish shape from shadow. The roots glow faintly in this dimness, bioluminescent bacteria living in symbiosis with the growth, producing a sick greenish radiance that makes the whole space feel like the inside of a body.

And scattered through this organic labyrinth: the machines.

They are old but not ancient. Forty years, perhaps fifty—industrial equipment from an extraction operation that tried to mine the basin before the roots came or perhaps caused them to come. Excavators list at strange angles where roots have lifted them, their tracks suspended in air, their cabs crushed inward by slow wooden pressure. A processing plant clings to the northern wall, half-swallowed, its corrugated metal roof buckling upward where a root has pushed through from below. Conveyor systems run through the root-mass like embedded arteries, some still connected to power, their belts occasionally jerking forward a few inches with a shriek of seized bearings before stopping again.

The pumps are what you hear. Somewhere beneath the visible root-mass, the old drainage pumps still run, fighting an unwinnable war against the moisture the roots themselves produce. They cycle on and off in staggered rotation, diesel generators coughing to life in buried bunkers, and each cycle sends a shudder through the basin floor that the roots amplify, transform, translate into that great vegetable groan that fills every cubic foot of air.

---

Movement is rare here. The roots grow, but slowly—you would have to watch for hours to see a tendril advance another inch across stone. Occasionally water drips from a condensation point, falls twenty feet, strikes a lower root with a sound swallowed instantly by the general roar. The machines creak as they settle further into their organic tombs.

Once, you see something alive that is not a root: a salamander, white and eyeless, pulling itself along a root-trunk with the slow determination of the very cold. It stops when it senses your vibration, waits, continues. There must be others. The ecosystem here is simple—bacteria, fungi, small things that feed on rot, smaller things that feed on them. But they are sparse, scattered, barely present against the overwhelming fact of the growth itself.

The roots want only to expand, to fill every space, to convert stone and metal and air itself into more of themselves. They are not malevolent. They are not anything. They are simply a process that has found its ideal conditions in this cold wet hollow, and they will continue until they reach the surface, until they cover the surrounding forest, until something stops them or nothing does.

You feel your footsteps absorbed by root-flesh. The sound closes around you like a fist made of patience. Somewhere, a pump cycles on.
