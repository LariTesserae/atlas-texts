# The Flats of Constant Rain

The rain has fallen here for longer than geology should allow. Not storms—never storms—just this ceaseless vertical descent, fat drops falling from a ceiling of cloud so old and settled it might as well be stone. The sound is everything. It fills the basin from edge to eroded edge, a white roar so total that it becomes a kind of pressure, a weight against the eardrums that never lifts. After enough time here, you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as a condition of existence, like gravity or the passage of time.

The land beneath this eternal downpour has worn itself to nearly nothing. What were once mountains are now suggestions, low undulating ridges that barely interrupt the horizon. The basin stretches maybe fifteen miles across, though distances deceive when the air is this thick with falling water. Everything is the color of wet slate—gray stone worn smooth as skin, gray pools spreading and connecting and separating, gray sky pressing down. The water finds no purchase to drain away properly; the bedrock has been scoured to near-impermeability over impossible ages, so it simply pools and creeps and overflows from one shallow depression to another, a landscape of shifting mirrors.

Nothing grows. The constant bombardment of water has leached away anything that might feed roots, stripped the stone of minerals century by century until only the hardest bones of the earth remain. No lichen crusts these rocks, no algae films the pools. This is a place that has been cleaned down to its mathematical essence—just water, stone, and the endless arithmetic of erosion.

---

And yet the pools *move* with life.

They came from somewhere else, these creatures—must have, because nothing could have evolved in this mineral emptiness. Perhaps they crawled up from deeper aquifers, or arrived on winds that blew different directions in some earlier age. But they have made the Flats their own, and they are *everywhere*. In the dim light—what filters through that ancient overcast is the gray-white of a dying flashlight—you see their pale shapes sliding beneath the water's surface, breaching to gulp air that is more water than gas, clustering in the shallows where some temperature gradient or mineral trace creates conditions they prefer.

They are amphibious, mostly. Soft-bodied things like salamanders without the salamander's grace, their skin translucent enough to show the slow pulse of organs beneath. They range from finger-length to the size of small dogs, and they move in ways that seem almost random until you watch long enough to see the patterns—the way they aggregate around certain stones, disperse when the rain intensifies, pile atop one another in writhing mounds when some signal you cannot detect passes through their numbers. Their calls—clicks and whistles and low grinding croaks—somehow cut through the rain's roar, or perhaps operate on frequencies the rain doesn't touch. You hear them constantly, a biological counterpoint to the water's white noise.

There are swimmers too, in the deeper pools. Eel-shapes with too many fins, things like flattened crabs that scuttle along the bottom sediment. And in the air above the water, insects that aren't quite insects—six-winged, iridescent even in this dim light, dancing between the raindrops with impossible precision. The whole basin hums with feeding and breeding and dying, an ecosystem that has somehow assembled itself from water and stone and nothing else, or from resources so subtle you cannot perceive them.

---

The structures appear every mile or so, regular enough to suggest intention. Low cairns of stacked stone, none more than waist-high, many collapsed into mere suggestions of their original form. Some are circled by shallow channels that might be natural erosion or might be carved drainage. A few still hold objects in their hollow centers—clay vessels long since dissolved to lumps, metal implements rusted to orange stains, what might be bone tools worn to abstract shapes.

Someone built here. Someone *lived* here, unthinkably. Left these markers across the basin in a grid that implies surveying, measurement, a systematic relationship to this punishing place. There's no way to date them—the constant water makes a mockery of normal decay, everything aging at once or not at all. They might be centuries old. They might be as ancient as the worn-down mountains.

The creatures avoid the cairns. Not with fear, exactly—more like disinterest so complete it suggests instinct. You can stand beside one and watch the pale bodies flow around you at a distance of several feet, a bubble of emptiness in the teeming wet.

---

The temperature hovers just above true cold—cool enough that your skin prickles and your breath might steam if you could see it through the rain, but not cold enough to freeze. The water falls at whatever temperature the eternal clouds maintain, and the pools match it, and the creatures seem to regulate themselves to exactly this threshold. Some of them run hot to the touch, you discover when one brushes against your ankle—hot enough to startle, their metabolisms burning fast to maintain whatever internal state they require.

The rain changes rhythm in ways that predict nothing. Sometimes it drums harder for an hour, then eases to something almost gentle. The creatures respond—scattering when the intensity rises, emerging in greater numbers during the lulls. The pools swell and shrink, new channels opening across the worn stone, old ones silting closed with the fine powder of dissolved rock. Nothing is quite still. The whole basin breathes with a slow tidal pulse that owes nothing to moons or seasons, only to the endless negotiation between falling water and patient stone.

At the basin's edges, the land rises imperceptibly toward those eroded ridges. The rain falls the same there, but the water runs off faster, leaving stretches of bare wet stone that gleam like dark mirrors. Fewer creatures venture to these margins. The cairns stop well before the slopes begin. Whatever was mapped here, whatever was claimed, it held only to the basin floor where the water pools and life congregates and the sound fills every hollow space until there is no room left for silence.
