# The Screaming Peaks

The wind has never stopped.

It began before memory, before the mountains themselves finished rising, and it will continue long after the last stone has been worn to a nub. This is the sound that defines everything here: a ceaseless, howling roar that pours through the high passes and over the naked ridgelines, a voice so constant and so loud that silence has become a theoretical concept, something that might exist elsewhere, in gentler places far below.

You stand on a plateau of pale granite, scoured to a glassy smoothness by ten thousand years of grit-laden gales. The rock curves and flows in frozen ripples, shaped by erosion into forms that look almost liquid—wave-crests and troughs, hollowed basins and strange pedestals where harder mineral inclusions have resisted the wearing and now stand as lonely sentinels, some no higher than your knee, others towering overhead like weathered monuments to forgotten purposes. The stone is old. It has reached an equilibrium with its destroyer, giving up exactly as much of itself each century as the wind demands, no more, no less.

The elevation is staggering. You are above the world here, above the clouds that gather in the valleys far below, above the treeline and the shrubline and nearly above life itself. The air is thin and bright and mercilessly cold—not freezing, not quite, but a steady chill that seeps through clothing and skin and settles into bone. Your breath comes short. Your ears ache from the pressure differential and from the *noise*, that omnipresent bellow of moving air that makes your skull vibrate and your thoughts fragment.

And yet.

The sky is a blue so vivid it seems artificial, a dome of crystalline clarity that arches overhead without a single cloud to mar it. Sunlight pours down unfiltered, intense and white, glancing off the polished stone in blinding sheets. You squint against the glare and raise a hand to shade your eyes, and even through the watering you can see for miles—can see the jagged parade of peaks marching toward every horizon, grey and white and pale rose where iron oxides stain the exposed faces, an endless army of stone giants standing in their ancient formations.

---

The moisture here is strange. There is no rain—the clouds never climb this high—but the air itself weeps. As the screaming wind rushes over the frozen heights and then descends, it releases its burden in sudden, localized condensations. Fog banks appear and vanish within minutes, rolling through the passes like living things. Frost crystallizes on shadowed surfaces even in midday, delicate laceworks of ice that sublime directly back to vapor when the sun finds them. In the deeper hollows between peaks, where the wind's fury is deflected just enough, small pools collect—not from rainfall but from this constant atmospheric seeping, water wrung from the sky by pressure and temperature alone.

These pools are startlingly clear, their surfaces trembling with the vibration of the wind but never truly disturbed by it. They sit in basins of worn stone, some no larger than a cupped palm, others wide enough to reflect the entire sky. Around their edges, the only vegetation of this place clings in desperate patches: cushion plants pressed so flat against the rock they seem painted on, their tiny leaves waxy and thick, their roots working into hairline cracks to find purchase. Lichens spread across the northern faces of stones in slow explosions of orange and grey and acid green, organisms so patient they measure their growth in millimeters per decade.

This is all. This is everything that grows here—a sparse scattering of stubborn survivors, clinging to the margins between stone and sky.

---

But the animals.

The animals have made this screaming emptiness their empire.

They came here, perhaps, to escape something in the valleys below—predators or competition or some ancient cataclysm—and they stayed because the peaks, for all their harshness, offered something no other place could: space, and light, and the strange safety of extremity. They have adapted. They have proliferated. They have become something new.

The plateau swarms with them.

First you notice the birds—though *birds* seems insufficient for creatures so magnificently strange. They are long-winged and narrow-bodied, built for riding the perpetual gales, and they move through the roaring air with an eerie, effortless grace. Their feathers are dense and iridescent, shifting between white and silver and pale gold as the light catches them, and their calls are pitched at frequencies designed to cut through the wind's bellow—sharp, piercing whistles that somehow remain distinct amid the chaos. Dozens wheel overhead at any moment. Sometimes hundreds. They nest in crevices along the cliff faces, emerging at dawn to hunt the smaller creatures that share their domain.

Those smaller creatures are everywhere, once you learn to see them. Pikas the color of granite huddle in the lee of standing stones, their ears rounded and furred against the cold. Insects with wings like mica chips tumble through the air, somehow maintaining flight in conditions that should shred them—they have evolved some trick of using the wind itself, letting it carry them in controlled spirals from feeding ground to feeding ground. Lizards with scales thick as chainmail bask on sun-warmed rock, their bodies flattened to present minimal surface to the gale, their eyes protected by transparent inner membranes that let them watch for predators without squinting against the glare.

And there are the larger creatures too—the grazers who somehow find enough sustenance in the cushion plants and lichens to maintain their bulk. They look like goats, distantly, but their legs are shorter and their bodies wider, low-slung centers of gravity that keep them stable in winds that would topple anything more conventionally proportioned. Their coats are extraordinarily dense, more fur than animal, rippling constantly in the gale. They move in herds of twenty or thirty, never stopping, circulating endlessly across the plateau in search of the next patch of green.

Something hunts them. You see the signs—bones scattered in hollows, polished clean by wind and scavengers. But whatever it is moves at the edges of perception, a pale shape glimpsed between pillars of standing stone, a shadow that might be cloud-movement but isn't. The herds watch the shadows. The pikas vanish into their crevices when certain silences fall—though *silence* here still means the endless roar of wind, just a roar unmarked by any closer sound.

---

There is something unusual about this place, beyond its extremity.

The wind, you realize after hours of exposure, does not vary. It does not gust and lull like normal wind. It pours through the passes at a constant, unwavering velocity, has poured for so long that everything here has shaped itself to accommodate it. The erosion patterns. The vegetation that grows only in specific orientations. The creatures whose bodies have evolved to assume its presence, who would perhaps struggle to function in still air.

You understand, gradually, that this is not weather. This is *climate*, as fixed and eternal as the mountain stone itself. Somewhere far below, in the valleys you cannot see, conditions surely change—storms come and go, seasons turn, rivers rise and fall. But up here, above everything, in the screaming brightness of the peaks, the world has reached a final equilibrium.

The wind blows. The sun shines. The stone wears slowly away. The animals circle in their ancient patterns.

And nothing will ever change again.
