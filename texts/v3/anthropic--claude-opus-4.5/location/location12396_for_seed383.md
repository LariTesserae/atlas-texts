# The Bright Warrens

The light comes from below.

You stand at the rim of a vast subsurface basin—a depression in the world's crust that spans perhaps three miles across, its floor a fractured mosaic of pale stone worn smooth by epochs beyond counting. The basin sinks gradually, terraced by ancient geological settling, and everywhere the rock glows. Not with fire, not with heat, but with some mineral phosphorescence that saturates the stone itself, a cold radiance the color of winter dawn. The light has no single source; it simply *is*, emanating from the worn surfaces, the cracked slabs, the fine gravel that fills the lower channels. You cast no shadow here, or rather, you cast shadows in every direction, faint and overlapping, ghosts of yourself radiating outward.

The air is cool and tastes of wet chalk. Moisture beads on every surface—condensation that gathers in the slight temperature differential between the luminous stone and the still, enclosed atmosphere. There is no sky above, only a ceiling of the same pale rock, domed and distant, perhaps two hundred feet overhead at its apex. Water traces slow paths down the curved walls, leaving darker trails that glisten in the ambient glow. Somewhere, you can hear dripping—not a single drip, but hundreds, a soft rain of condensation falling from ceiling to floor in no pattern, the only sound in the enormous quiet.

---

There is a city here. Or there was. Or there *is becoming*.

The structures rise from the basin floor in clusters—stone buildings made from the same luminous rock, blocks cut and fitted without mortar, shaped by hand tools into simple rectangular forms. Doorways. Windows. Low walls. Flat roofs. The architecture is primitive in technique but vast in ambition; the buildings number in the hundreds, perhaps thousands, spreading across the terraced basin in loose neighborhoods connected by worn pathways. Some structures stand complete. Others are half-finished, their walls rising four or five courses high before stopping abruptly. Still others have been dismantled, their blocks stacked neatly nearby, waiting.

The city is being built and unbuilt simultaneously, and you can see the builders at work.

They move across the basin in streams and eddies—creatures the size of large dogs, segmented and pale, their bodies armored in plates of the same luminous mineral that comprises everything here. They might be crustaceans, might be insects, might be something that diverged from any familiar branch of life so long ago that the categories no longer apply. They have many legs, move with unsettling coordination, and they *carry things*. Blocks of stone. Smaller fragments. Tools made of harder rock, shaped into wedges and scrapers. They work in teams of four or six or twelve, lifting loads that should be impossible, communicating through means you cannot perceive—perhaps vibration, perhaps scent, perhaps some deeper mechanism of the bright stone itself.

The fauna is abundant but sparse in distribution—dense where it gathers, absent in the spaces between. Great empty stretches of worn stone separate the working clusters. You could walk for ten minutes across luminous bedrock and see nothing, then come upon a nexus of activity where hundreds of the creatures labor in frantic, silent industry.

---

What strikes you first as chaos reveals itself, with watching, as something more troubling: *change without progress*.

A team of twelve creatures spends an hour constructing a wall, fitting blocks with precision, checking alignment with what might be a plumb tool or might be a sensory organ extended from one creature's forelimb. When the wall reaches chest height, another team arrives. The two groups circle each other in a complex pattern that might be negotiation or might be ritual. Then the second team begins dismantling the wall. The first team moves on to dismantle something else. Blocks are carried across the basin. Other blocks are carried back. The city churns.

You see a building completed—roof placed, doorway framed, interior smoothed. Within hours, teams have reduced it to a neat pile of components. Somewhere else, those same components rise into a different configuration. The pathways shift. The neighborhoods reorganize. The city is ancient—you can tell by the wear on every surface, by the rounded edges of blocks that have been placed and replaced ten thousand times, by the shallow grooves in the floor where the creatures have walked the same routes across millennia. But the city is also new, *always* new, rebuilt continuously, never the same arrangement for more than a few days.

The creatures pay you no attention. You are not stone. You are not a block to be carried. You do not factor into their endless calculations.

---

There are pools in the basin's lowest regions—shallow accumulations of condensation that have gathered over centuries, mineral-rich and faintly luminous. The creatures drink from them, or perhaps absorb something through their ventral surfaces. Small things move in the water—pale filaments, perhaps early-stage creatures, perhaps something simpler, nutrient chains without clear beginning or end. No plants grow here. Nothing photosynthesizes; there is no sun to feed. The ecology runs on something else—geothermal chemistry, perhaps, or something in the luminescence itself, energy drawn from the slow decay of whatever mineral makes this stone glow.

The pools are surrounded by structures built and unbuilt with particular frequency. Gathering places. Distribution centers. Sacred sites. It is impossible to know what the creatures intend, whether they intend at all, whether this endless reconstruction serves some purpose beyond the reconstruction itself.

The dripping continues. The creatures work. The light glows cold and steady from every surface.

You realize, eventually, that the basin is not natural. The terraces are too regular. The dome overhead is too perfectly curved. This was excavated—carved from solid rock by unknown hands, or perhaps by these same creatures, or by their ancestors, across a span of time so vast that the tools that made it have been lost while the making continues. The city is not built *in* the basin. The basin *is* the city, the original city, the first construction, now worn to its final form while its inhabitants continue building within it, unable to stop, unable to complete, trapped in an architecture of eternal revision.

You can walk among them. They flow around you like water around a stone. The air is cool and damp and bright. The silence is broken only by the dripping, by the soft scrape of stone on stone, by the patient clicking of countless legs across luminous ground.
