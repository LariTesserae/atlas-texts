# The Hanging Hollows of Verthane

The first thing you notice is the sound. A vast, resonant humming that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere—the low drone of wind moving through billions of hollow stems, each one a different diameter, a different length, producing its own note in an endless vegetal orchestra. The sound never stops. It builds and falls, shifts register as air currents move through the emptiness, but it is always there, always loud, a thrumming presence that becomes the substrate of thought itself.

You are standing—though that word feels inadequate—on a platform of woven root-matter, somewhere in the upper reaches of what can only be called a sky-forest. But forest implies ground, implies soil, implies the comforting solidity of earth beneath. Here there is no ground. There is only the Hollow: a vertical emptiness that extends both up and down beyond sight, beyond comprehension, a cylindrical void perhaps three hundred kilometers across, its walls made entirely of living plant tissue.

The Verthane system is young. The great volcanic caldera that forms the Hollow erupted less than a geological heartbeat ago, leaving walls of fresh basalt that rise—or fall, depending on your orientation—for dozens of kilometers in every direction. The rock is still sharp-edged, still showing the frozen flow-patterns of its molten birth. But the plants came quickly. The spore-carrying winds found the warm stone within decades, and now the entire interior surface of the caldera is sheathed in growth so thick that the rock has become merely an armature, a skeleton for the true body of this place.

---

The vegetation here is architecture. It is infrastructure. It is the world itself.

From your position on the root-platform, you can see the great Pipe-Stems extending outward into the void—tubular growths, some as wide as rivers, others thin as human fingers, all of them hollow, all of them catching the wind and singing. They grow in radiating fans from the caldera walls, reaching toward the center of the Hollow but never quite meeting. The longest extend perhaps a kilometer into empty air before their own weight becomes too much and they curve downward, still growing, still humming. When they finally break—and they do break, regularly, filling the air with tremendous cracking reports like thunder—they fall for long minutes before vanishing into the darkness below.

Between the Pipe-Stems, and growing from them, and wound through them, are the Skeins: fibrous rope-plants that stretch across the void in complex cat's-cradle patterns, creating a three-dimensional web that extends as far as you can see in any direction. The Skeins are the highways of this place. Things move along them—you can see distant shapes traveling the strands, some the size of dogs, some larger than houses, all of them dark silhouettes against the slightly-less-dark void.

And everywhere, covering every surface, filling every gap between the larger structures, are the Mats: colonial organisms that form sheets and curtains and drapes of vegetable matter, swaying in the constant wind, collecting moisture from the air, photosynthesizing in the faint light that filters down from somewhere impossibly far above. The Mats are cold and damp to the touch. Everything here is cold and damp. The altitude steals warmth; the constant mist—condensation from somewhere, temperature differentials in the air, moisture squeezed from clouds that wander lost through the Hollow—keeps every surface slick.

---

You are high. You know this without needing to measure. The air is thin enough that breathing requires conscious effort, thin enough that the cold cuts deeper than it should. The caldera walls rise another five or six kilometers above your position, their upper reaches lost in a darkness that might be night sky or might simply be distance. Below, the Hollow descends into absolute black. No light reaches the bottom. No light has ever reached the bottom—the Pipe-Stems and Mats grow so thick in the upper reaches that they form a living ceiling, a photosynthetic canopy that captures what little illumination penetrates from outside.

That light is faint. A dim gray glow permeates everything, sourceless, arriving from no particular direction. It might be reflected starlight. It might be bioluminescence from organisms too small to see. It might be something else entirely—the caldera walls retain volcanic heat in places, and where they do, strange glowing mosses grow in patches of amber and pale green. But the overall impression is of darkness. Of perpetual dusk. Of a world that has never known noon.

---

They live here, the Hollow-dwellers. You have not seen them yet, but you have seen their work.

The rope bridges. The platforms. The great woven baskets, big as barns, suspended from Skein-junctions and filled with cultivated Mat-gardens. The stone tools, obsidian-sharp, left embedded in the walls of harvested Pipe-Stems. The fire-pits—rare, precious, fed with dried plant matter and tended carefully against the damp—whose smoke rises in thin columns until the wind takes it.

They are scattered, the settlements. Days of travel apart along the Skeins, sometimes weeks. The Hollow is vast enough to have weather patterns of its own: storms that form in the lower reaches and rise violently upward, bringing rain that falls sideways, driven by winds that shift without warning. Seasons, too, of a sort—periods when the moisture content of the air increases, when the Pipe-Stems swell with growth and the humming reaches a fever pitch. Periods when things dry out, when the Mat-curtains curl and brown at their edges, when fire becomes a genuine danger.

The people move with the seasons. They follow the moisture. They harvest Pipe-Stem interiors for food—the hollow centers contain a starchy pith, nutritious if bland—and they hunt the creatures that travel the Skeins.

---

The fauna. Yes.

You hear them before you see them: clicks and whistles overlaid on the great humming, calls that carry across kilometers of open air. The Skein-runners are quadrupeds with gripping feet, their bodies elongated and flexible, built for traversing rope-plants at speed. They travel in herds that flow along the strands like water, hundreds of bodies moving in perfect coordination, the vibration of their passage adding new harmonics to the Hollow's song.

The Pipe-climbers are different: spiral-bodied things with no clear head or tail, encircling the great stems and inching upward over months, feeding on the outer tissue, their progress so slow they seem almost botanical themselves. When they pupate, they become something else—winged and vast, with membranous sails that catch the updrafts from the volcanic deeps. You have seen their shed cases, brittle and translucent, clinging to the Pipe-Stems like abandoned architecture.

There are predators. There must be predators. The Hollow is too active, too full of motion and growth and noise, to support only prey. At night—if night means anything here, if the shift from gray to black constitutes a meaningful change—you have heard sounds that are not humming, not wind, not the calls of Skein-runners. Low sounds. Wet sounds. The creak of strands under tremendous weight.

You do not know what hunts in the darkness below.

---

The Hollow breathes. This is not metaphor. The temperature differentials between the warm volcanic depths and the cold upper reaches create air currents of tremendous power, rivers of wind that flow upward during certain hours and reverse during others. The Pipe-Stems act as channels, focusing these currents, amplifying them. When the direction shifts, the entire sonic landscape transforms—the humming drops an octave, rises again, develops new overtones. Experienced travelers can navigate by sound alone, reading the wind's direction from the chord of the Hollow.

Mist flows through the void in great slow curtains. It collects on the Skeins, drips from the Mats, pools in the cupped leaves of plants that have evolved specifically to harvest it. The people collect it in woven vessels, in hollowed Pipe-Stem segments, in anything that will hold water. There are no rivers here. No lakes. Only moisture in transit, condensation and evaporation in endless cycle.

Sometimes, rarely, ice forms. The coldest nights bring frost to the upper reaches, a glittering crust on every surface that vanishes within hours but transforms the Hollow, briefly, into something crystalline and alien. The people tell stories about these nights. The ice remembers, they say. The ice holds the shape of what it touches.

---

From where you stand, looking outward into the void, you cannot see the far wall of the caldera. It is simply too distant. The middle air is too thick with life, too cluttered with Pipe-Stems and Skeins and drifting Mat-fragments, too dark. But you know it is there. You know that the Hollow is finite, that somewhere beyond the visible range, another wall rises, another community clings to the singing stems, another group of people tends their fires and listens to the same vast humming that fills your own ears.

You wonder if they can hear your voice, carried on the wind. You wonder if anyone is listening.

The Hollow answers with its own sound, as it always has, as it always will: the chord of a million hollow tubes, the breath of a world that is nothing but air and growth and the absence of solid ground. It is loud. It is constant. It is, somehow, not frightening—not after you have lived with it a while. It becomes the texture of existence. It becomes home.
