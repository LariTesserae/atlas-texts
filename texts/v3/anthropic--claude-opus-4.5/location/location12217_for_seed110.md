# The Weeping Alcove

The darkness here is total and warm as blood. You find this place by feel—a cleft in basalt where the mountain is still deciding what it wants to be. The opening is narrow enough that you turn sideways to enter, and then the rock gives way to a chamber no larger than a sleeping body curled tight.

Water is everywhere but there is no flow. It seeps. It beads. It hangs. The walls of this pocket are slicked with condensation that never quite falls, droplets swelling to trembling fullness and holding there, surface tension winning against gravity in some patient standoff that has lasted longer than you can imagine. The air itself feels wet enough to drink if you breathed slowly and carefully. The temperature is bathhouse-warm, blood-warm, the warmth of something's interior. The moisture carries that warmth into your lungs, your pores, every fold of skin.

The stone itself is new. Not new to you—new to existence. This is geology mid-thought, basalt that hasn't finished cooling from some deep volcanic whisper that pushed it up through older rock. The surface is rough in places where gas bubbles almost formed but didn't, smooth in others where mineral-rich water has begun laying down the first molecular suggestions of flowstone. In ten thousand years there might be stalactites here. For now there are only wet patches that feel somehow thicker, denser, more *intentional* than the surrounding rock.

The chamber is crowded despite its emptiness. This is the paradox you feel immediately: nothing moves, almost nothing lives here, and yet the space is saturated with presence. Every surface is doing something. Mineral deposits cluster in colonies of crystal too small to see in this darkness, but you can feel them beneath your fingertips—roughness giving way to smoothness giving way to roughness in patterns that repeat like breath. Bacterial films coat the wetter patches, invisible and nearly inert, metabolizing at rates too slow to measure. They've been here since water first found this crack, and they will be here when the mountain finally decides its shape.

In one corner—if a space this small can be said to have corners—someone has wedged three flat stones into a rough shelf. The arrangement is deliberate. On the shelf sits a clay vessel, unfired, still soft enough that your thumb would dent it. It holds nothing but darkness. Beside it, a bundle of dried stems tied with plant fiber, placed with evident care and then abandoned or completed. How long ago? The humidity here arrests decay. These objects could be ten years old or ten generations old. They feel less like tools than like offerings, or perhaps like the beginning of an argument with the mountain about what this space is for.

The only sound is the almost-sound of water not quite falling. A subsonic suggestion of drip that never arrives. If you hold very still and stop breathing, you might hear the faint ticking of your own pulse echoed back by the close walls. The chamber is too small for proper acoustics—sound doesn't reverberate so much as sink into the wet stone and disappear.

Something lives in the deepest crack where two walls fail to meet. You won't see it. You might feel it, if you reach your hand into that gap: a faint displacement of air that suggests breath, or possibly just the thermal gradient where deeper warmth meets the chamber's merely warm. Whatever it is, it doesn't move while you're present. It may not move at all. It may be less an animal than an organized patience, something between fungus and intention, waiting out the centuries while the mountain makes up its mind.

The alcove wants nothing. It changes nothing. It has achieved a perfect equilibrium between the water that seeps in and the water that slowly, imperceptibly evaporates back through the entrance crack. The temperature holds steady because the mountain holds it steady. The darkness is absolute because this is a place that has never known light and has no use for it. You are the most dynamic thing to enter this space in—how long? The objects on the stone shelf suggest someone came before. But perhaps they too held still. Perhaps they understood that this is not a place for doing, but for becoming, at whatever rate the stone allows.

If you stay long enough, moisture will bead on your skin. The warm wet dark will start to feel less like absence and more like presence, less like a void than like the interior of something alive and thinking very, very slowly. The chamber is so small and so full of waiting-to-be that you become part of its density, another presence in the saturated dark, another thing deciding what it wants to become.
