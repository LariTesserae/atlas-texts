# The Breathing Plateau

At first you think it is a machine. Then you think it is a mountain range. Then you understand it is both, and neither, and that the distinction stopped mattering here before you were born.

The plateau extends beyond the curve of the world in every direction—a continent of black stone rising three kilometers above the frozen marshlands that ring its base, its edges sheer and ruled-straight as if cut by instruments. The stone is young, geologically speaking: sharp-cornered, unweathered, its fracture planes still glinting like broken glass. In places you can see where it emerged—pushed up through older crust in sheets and columns, leaving behind geometric canyons that drop away into permanent shadow.

But the stone is not empty.

Across every surface, sunk into every face and ledge and vertical cliff, the infrastructure spreads. Not built *on* the plateau—grown *into* it, or perhaps the plateau grew around it, or perhaps the two emerged together in some process that predates the categories of natural and artificial. Pipes thick as rivers run along the canyon walls, sweating condensation that freezes into beard-ice before melting again in the next thermal cycle. Vents the size of stadium doors exhale warm wet air in steady rhythm—the breath of the plateau, someone might have called it once, before there was no one left to call it anything.

The sound is constant. Not a roar but a chord: the bass thrum of pumps moving fluid through channels you cannot see, the higher whistle of wind through the vent-mouths, the arrhythmic *clank-clank-clank* of mechanisms whose purpose has been lost but whose function continues. It echoes off the canyon walls and returns changed, harmonizing with itself, building into something that registers less as noise than as pressure against the bones of your skull.

---

Moisture beads on every surface. The plateau generates its own weather—warm air rising from the deep vents meets the permanent cold of this altitude and condenses into fog that never quite becomes rain, that drifts through the canyons in slow rivers of grey, that clings to the infrastructure and drips steadily down through drainage channels older than any living city. The stone is always wet. Water runs in sheets down the cliff faces, following grooves that might be natural erosion or might be designed flow-paths, pooling in basins where green-black moss grows in carpets so thick and soft they seem to breathe.

This is the only life here. The moss, and something like lichen that crusts the older pipes in patterns of rust-orange and verdigris, and in the deeper channels a kind of pale grass that grows toward the heat-vents like prayer. No insects. No birds riding the thermals that rise from the warm canyons. No small mammals denning in the endless crevices of the infrastructure. Just the plants, patient and slow, colonizing every surface where warmth and water meet.

The cities—if they are cities—occupy the wider canyon floors and the occasional flat expanse where the plateau forgot to rise. From above they would look like circuit boards: regular grids of structure interrupted by larger nodes, everything connected by the same pipe-and-channel network that webs the entire continent. But from within they are stranger than that. The buildings are not buildings but *housings*—shells around machinery, or machinery that happens to be hollow enough to shelter other machinery, or perhaps living space that was later converted or was always meant to serve both purposes in ways the designers never distinguished between.

Nothing moves in them. Nothing has moved in them for a very long time. The streets—if they are streets—are slick with moss and pooled water, lit by the ambient grey of the fog and, at irregular intervals, by lights that still function: cold blue-white industrial illumination spilling from fixtures that no one maintains but that draw power from the plateau's deep processes regardless. The silence of the cities is louder than the machinery-sound that fills the canyons. It is the silence of absence made physical, of spaces designed for population that were never populated, or that were populated once and have been empty now for longer than they were full.

---

Here is what you come to understand, standing at the edge of a canyon that drops a kilometer straight down, watching the fog rise from below in curtains:

The plateau is not working toward anything. It processes water—draws it up from the saturated stone below, heats it, circulates it, vents it, collects it, processes it again. The pipes carry fluid in loops that connect back to themselves. The mechanisms that clank and thrum in the deep housings do not build anything or produce anything or prepare for anything. They run. They have always run. They will, by every indication, always run, until the stone itself erodes away around them—and the stone is young, barely touched by time, as if it emerged already cradling its infrastructure only a few thousand years ago.

The cities were never finished because there was nothing to finish. They are part of the system—nodes in the circulation network, perhaps, or heat sinks, or simply spaces the process required in order to be the shape it is. That they look like places where people might live is incidental, or perhaps intentional in a way that has nothing to do with people, or perhaps intentional in a way that *had* to do with people once and doesn't anymore.

The sound never stops. The fog never clears. The moss grows a millimeter per century, and the water runs, and somewhere in the deep canyons a vent exhales and a pump engages and a mechanism whose name is lost performs its function perfectly, as it always has, as it always will, while the plateau breathes and sweats and waits for nothing at the top of the world.
