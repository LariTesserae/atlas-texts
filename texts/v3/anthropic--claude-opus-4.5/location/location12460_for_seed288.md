# The Sunken Rookeries

The city fills a depression in the earth, a vast bowl-shaped hollow perhaps three miles across, sunk beneath the level of the surrounding plains. From the crater's rim, you can see how the settlement sprawls across the basin floor and climbs the interior slopes—structures of pale stone and dried earth packed tight in some districts, scattered loosely in others, all of it softened by the relentless green growth that has claimed every surface.

There is no water here. The basin was carved by something else—some ancient geological settling, a slow collapse completed so long ago that the broken edges have weathered into gentle slopes. The city adapted to its absence. Its people, whoever or whatever they were, built with materials that needed no mixing, no mortar wet from wells. Mud brick dried by patient air. Fitted stone. Woven structures of living vine trained over armatures of bone.

The vegetation is what strikes you first, after the sound. Every building supports growth. Thick-stemmed succulents with waxy blue-green leaves colonize rooftops and ledge-lines. Climbing plants with finger-thin tendrils have worked their way into every crack, splitting walls slowly, binding them with root-grip even as they break them down. The plants draw moisture from the air itself, from the fogs that settle into this low place on cool nights and linger through the dim days. The light that reaches the basin floor is always filtered—by the bowl's own rim, by atmospheric haze, by the canopy that has grown over certain districts where trees found purchase in accumulated soil and rose until their crowns tangled together.

And everywhere: the birds.

---

The sound is constant and enormous. It rolls across the basin in waves, builds in certain quarters and fades in others, but never truly stops. Thousands upon thousands of them—hundreds of thousands, perhaps—roosting in the ruins. Nesting in window-holes. Filling the hollow towers that may once have been granaries or temples or simply homes. The calls layer and compete: harsh croaking chorus from the dark-feathered flocks that claim the eastern slopes, a higher piping from the smaller species that swarm through the understory vegetation, occasional piercing shrieks from the larger raptors that ride the thermals above the bowl's rim.

The air itself vibrates. Your chest hums with it.

They are not birds in any familiar sense, though they occupy that niche. Six-limbed things that fold their forward pair into wings while the middle set serves some social or manipulative purpose. Scaled rather than feathered, their scales fine enough to appear soft from a distance. Colors ranging from rust-brown to pale grey to iridescent black. The smallest are the size of sparrows. The largest, glimpsed rarely on upper ledges, spread wings wider than a person is tall.

They have made this place theirs for so long that their presence has shaped it. White guano streaks every surface, piled thick in some courtyards, crusted and ancient on walls where generations have perched. The plants that thrive here are those that tolerate or require the nitrogen-rich deposits. The architecture has been modified by accumulated nesting—gaps in stonework packed with dried vegetation, rooftop structures entirely buried under layers of stick and fiber.

---

You descend into the basin on a path worn by something—not human feet, and long ago regardless. The sound intensifies as you go lower, bouncing off the bowl's slopes, concentrating. The air grows cooler still, trapped in the depression, mixing with the slight ammonia tang of the rookeries. The light dims further. By the time you reach the basin floor, it feels like perpetual dusk.

The streets are wide in some places, narrow in others, following no grid you can discern. The buildings seem to grow from the ground rather than sitting upon it—their foundations have been buried by centuries of accumulated debris, their ground floors become basements filled with soil and growth. What were once second stories are now street level.

The birds ignore you, mostly. They wheel overhead, perch on eaves, argue in clusters over territory or food or nothing at all. Some watch with sideways eyes that hold no recognition. They have been here so long, and nothing has threatened them in so many generations, that fear has faded from their behavior. You are simply another element of the landscape, slow-moving and irrelevant.

In the deeper districts, where the canopy closes overhead and the light falls to near-darkness, the sound changes character. Less a roar and more a constant muttering, rustling, the small sounds of countless lives in close proximity. Wings adjusting. Scales scraping stone. The wet tearing of something being consumed. The high anxious calls of chicks in their thousands, demanding and receiving.

The city extends for hours of walking. It was built for inhabitants that no longer come, maintained now only by the slow work of roots and the patient architecture of nesting. Some structures have collapsed entirely, their stones scattered and overgrown. Others stand surprisingly intact, their walls supporting themselves through geometry that has proven stronger than time. In certain squares, you can see the faded remains of what might have been paint or carved decoration, worn nearly smooth by weather and the constant traffic of small clawed feet.

At the basin's center, a structure rises taller than the rest—a tower or spire of darker stone, spiraling slightly as it climbs, its upper reaches invisible in the perpetual haze. The birds avoid it. A rare silence hangs around its base, the sound washing around it like water around a stone. Whether this is instinct or learned behavior, something sacred or something feared, you cannot tell. The plants grow thickest here, as if reaching for the quiet.

---

The cycles of this place are long and slow. The temperature varies little between day and night in this sheltered hollow. The fogs come and go, but the underlying dimness remains. The birds breed in seasons you cannot quite discern—perhaps responding to some subtle signal invisible to you, a change in light quality or air pressure or the growth patterns of the succulent fruits they feed upon. The population ebbs and swells over years and decades rather than months.

The ruins will continue settling. The plants will continue growing and dying and feeding the soil. The birds will continue their vast communal existence, their chorus rising and falling, their generations replacing each other in the same nesting hollows their ancestors claimed centuries ago.

You are a brief visitor in a place measured by different time. The sound washes over you, through you, until it becomes almost ignorable—a baseline against which all other perception occurs. You find yourself watching the slow unfurling of a succulent's new leaf, a process that will take days, and it feels appropriate to the rhythm of things.

Somewhere in the upper reaches of the bowl, a raptor screams. The smaller birds briefly scatter and settle. The sound continues.
