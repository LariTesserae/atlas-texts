# The Condensery

The building sits in a depression, half-swallowed by decades of settling into wet gravel. It was a pumping station once, or something like one—a single structure of riveted iron plates and sweating glass, maybe forty feet long, with pipes emerging from its walls at odd angles before plunging into the saturated ground. The roof has partially collapsed inward, creating a bowl that collects the perpetual drizzle, and water runs down the interior walls in sheets that catch what little light filters through the grimy windows.

Everything here is wet. The gravel yard surrounding the building has become more pool than ground, with water standing an inch deep across most of it, fed by seepage from below and mist from above. The air itself feels thick enough to drink—not fog exactly, but a kind of permanent condensation, as if the atmosphere has forgotten how to hold its moisture. Metal surfaces bloom with complex rust in colors that shift from orange to black to an unsettling green. The iron plates of the building have become geological features in their own right, their corrosion patterns as intricate as lichen.

There is no plant life. Not a blade of grass pushing through the gravel, not moss on the stonework, not algae in the standing water. Whatever conditions killed the vegetation here—or prevented it from ever establishing—remain in effect. The water stays clear in a way that feels wrong, the gravel stays bare, the rust spreads but nothing grows on it.

---

What moves instead are the eels.

They are everywhere. Thin ones like copper wire, thick ones like forearms, pale ones nearly translucent and dark ones that absorb what little light reaches the water. They slide through the flooded gravel yard in numbers that beggar belief, their bodies weaving between the stones, occasionally breaking the surface with a muscular ripple before submerging again. They enter and exit the building through the submerged portions of its walls, through cracks in the foundation, through the pipes that were meant for pumping but now serve as highways for sinuous traffic.

Inside the structure, they are denser still. The collapsed roof has created a kind of reservoir, and in the dim interior light—what comes through the filthy glass, what reflects off the water—you can see them layered. Swimming at different depths, some resting coiled in the angles where rusted machinery meets standing water, others moving with what seems like purpose along routes they've established through repetition. The water in here is warmer than outside, heated by something below, and steam rises to meet the drizzle coming down through the broken roof.

The machinery itself is hard to interpret. Cylindrical tanks, valves frozen with corrosion, a control panel with dials whose needles rusted in place decades ago. Pipes run everywhere—horizontal, vertical, spiraling—and the eels use them all. Some pipes are wide enough that you can see their passage through portholes meant for human inspection: bodies sliding past in the gloom, dozens per minute, engaged in circulations whose logic belongs to them alone.

---

The sounds they make are soft but constant. The slip of skin against metal, the small splash of a surface break, the deeper gurgle of movement through flooded pipes. Occasionally a resonant hum comes from the larger tanks, as if the eels' massed presence sets the metal vibrating. The building itself contributes: the tick of cooling iron in the cool air, the drip of condensation, the groan of settling plates as the structure continues its slow subsidence into the wet ground.

Some of the eels are strange. You notice this gradually. Most follow the familiar body plan—elongated, finned, smooth-skinned—but others have features that don't quite fit. One coiled in a corner has what appear to be gill structures that extend too far from its body, drifting like ribbons in the slight current. Another moving through the shallows shows markings that pulse faintly, bioluminescent in a pattern too regular to be accidental. In the deeper water of the interior reservoir, shapes move that are larger than they should be, or configured differently, their outlines wavering in the poor light.

The system here—building, water, eels—has been running for a long time. Long enough that the architecture of the structure has become partially incorporated into whatever the eels are doing. They've worn grooves into certain surfaces, deposited mineral residue on others. The pipes they travel most frequently have been smoothed inside by the passage of countless bodies. The eels have also, somehow, maintained the building: water drains through channels that seem to have been kept clear, and certain valves—though rusted beyond human operation—show signs of being manipulated by other means, pushed open or wedged closed by persistent pressure.

---

At the far end of the building, past where the roof is most collapsed and the water deepest, there is a vertical pipe three feet in diameter that descends into the ground. The eels spiral down into it continuously, rising back up on the other side, and the water in that section carries a faint warmth and a mineral taste you can smell from feet away. Something below. An aquifer, a thermal vent, a flooded mine—the origin of all this wet, the reason the pumping station was built here in the first place, now given over entirely to its current inhabitants.

The light is failing, or perhaps was never strong. The drizzle continues. Somewhere in the gravel yard, a larger eel surfaces briefly—thick as a leg, mottled grey—watches you with an eye that holds neither fear nor interest, and submerges again. The water ripples outward, meets other ripples, settles. The building breathes its steam. The pipes carry their traffic.

This is not your place. But it permits you to stand at its edge, ankle-deep in clear water, and witness what it has become.
