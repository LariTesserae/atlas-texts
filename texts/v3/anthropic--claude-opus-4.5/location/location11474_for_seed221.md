# The Condensery

It is a room the size of a closet, bolted to the outside of a atmospheric processing station at seventeen thousand feet. The walls are copper and brass gone green with eternal moisture, every surface beaded, every seam weeping. Pipes enter from below and above and from angles that suggest the station's architecture follows no human logic of up or down. The pipes are sweating too. Everything here sweats. The cold is the kind that stops being temperature and becomes texture—a film on every surface, a pressure against exposed skin, a taste of metal and altitude at the back of the throat.

You cannot move without touching something wet.

The room was built to house a single industrial dehumidifier, a machine the size of a suitcase that hums at a frequency felt more than heard. It has been running at the same speed, pulling the same moisture from the same thin air, for longer than anyone has serviced this station. The moisture goes somewhere. Into the pipes. The pipes go somewhere. No one checks anymore. The gauges are frozen at readings that made sense once, their needles calcified in position by deposits of whatever minerals the air carries at this height. The machine will run until the station falls. The station will not fall. It was built by people who understood that some things must simply continue.

But the dehumidifier is not alone.

---

They came in through the drainage gaps—spaces barely wide enough for condensation to trickle out, now packed with bodies. Glacier moths, though that name belongs to a different species that lives lower down and dies in winter. These do not die. These are the size of thumbnails, blue-white and grey-white and the particular yellow-white of old ivory, and their wings are like wet paper, and there are hundreds of them, and they have been here so long they've become architecture.

They cling to every surface in overlapping layers. They carpet the pipes. They hang from the low ceiling in clusters that look like mineral formations until you see the legs, the antennae, the compound eyes catching what little light leaks through the moisture-fogged porthole. They fill the corners in drifts. They pack the space beneath the dehumidifier so densely that the machine appears to float on a living mat of wings.

And they are screaming.

---

Not screaming. Singing. Stridulating. Rubbing specialized structures on their legs and wings together in patterns that individually would be inaudible but collectively become a wall of sound—a sound like metal stress, like ice calving, like radio static given malicious intent. The frequency shifts in waves as different clusters synchronize and desynchronize. Sometimes it almost resolves into a chord. Then it collapses back into chaos.

They have been doing this at exactly this volume, in exactly these patterns, since before anyone living has checked this room. Maintenance crews documented the moths in the station's first decade. They documented the sound. They documented that it never changed—not with season, not with external temperature, not when the porthole was opened, not when specimens were removed (they returned within hours, somehow, from outside, seventeen thousand feet up, in air too thin and cold for anything to fly far). The crews stopped coming. The documentation stopped. The moths remained.

Some of them are dead. You can tell by the ones that don't move when others crawl over them. But the dead ones are integrated into the mass—their bodies serving as platforms, as insulation, as anchor points for the living. The colony does not distinguish. The sound does not diminish. When one dies, another crawls to take its position in the choir, and the chord continues.

---

The cold preserves everything. The moisture baptizes everything. The dehumidifier pulls water from the air and the air pulls water from somewhere else and the balance holds, has held, will hold. The moths drink condensation from the walls and from each other's wings and from the bodies of their dead. The walls oxidize so slowly that the green patina is still spreading, still finding new copper to convert, a process that will take centuries more to complete.

Nothing here is finished becoming what it is.

The station groans in the wind outside—a different sound, lower, felt through the bolts that anchor this room to the greater structure. The moths do not react. The dehumidifier does not react. The moisture continues to bead and weep and trickle toward drains that are clogged with wings.

If you pressed your ear to the porthole glass, you would hear the wind. If you pressed your ear to the wall, you would hear the choir. If you stayed long enough, you would stop being able to tell which sound was outside you.

The room has space enough for one person to stand. Not comfortably. Not without pressing against the moths, feeling them compress like damp velvet, feeling the ones at the edges begin to crawl toward your warmth. They would not harm you. They would simply incorporate you, temporarily, into the architecture of their continuing.

Outside, the sky is the color of wet slate. The mountains below are invisible in cloud. The air is too thin to breathe easily, and the cold is a thing that enters through the eyes.

Inside, the moths sing, and the machine hums, and the water runs, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and nothing changes.
