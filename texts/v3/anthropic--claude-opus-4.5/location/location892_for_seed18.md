# The Hanging Nursery of Engine Spire Nine

The chamber rises like a throat of iron and glass, a vertical shaft perhaps forty meters tall and no more than fifteen across at its widest. Every surface is made—riveted plates of weathered bronze, conduits wrapped in decaying rubber insulation, grated walkways that spiral up the walls in a helical embrace. The metal has aged into its own beauty: patinated green in the crevices, worn to a buttery gold where hands and bodies have touched it across decades or centuries of use.

But the metal is almost invisible now.

Moss climbs everything. Ferns erupt from seams between plates. Flowering vines with trumpet-shaped blossoms the color of rust and cream have threaded themselves through the spiral walkways, hanging in curtains that sway in the thermal updrafts. The vegetation is impossibly lush for such a contained space—every horizontal surface hosts miniature forests, every vertical one streams with epiphytes and climbing things that have found their way into bolt-holes and ventilation slits. The growth is old, established, as much a part of the structure now as the steel itself. No one has trimmed it back in generations. No one needs to.

---

The sound is the first thing. The last thing. The only thing, until you learn to hear beneath it.

Somewhere below—far below, through layers of grating and down shafts that descend beyond sight—an engine runs. Not smoothly. It labors. A massive piston rhythm, a mechanical heartbeat so loud it exists less as sound than as a constant pressure against the chest. **CHUNK-hisssss. CHUNK-hisssss.** Every three seconds, perfectly regular, the compression stroke. The hiss is steam, escaping through pressure valves, and it rises up through the shaft bringing warmth and moisture with it.

This is how the nursery breathes.

The steam condenses on the upper walls, on the glass panels set into the domed ceiling where gray light filters down. It gathers into droplets that grow fat and fall—an intermittent indoor rain that patters on the broad leaves of the canopy plants, that drips down through layers of growth, that feeds the whole improbable ecosystem. The air is warm and close, humid enough that surfaces gleam wet, but not oppressively so. The temperature holds steady. Has held steady for longer than anyone has been counting.

---

The animals have learned to live with the noise, or perhaps they were bred for it—it is difficult to say anymore what is wild here and what was introduced by hands long absent.

Moths the size of your palm drift through the mist, their wings patterned in industrial warnings: chevrons, stripes, symbols that might once have meant something on factory floors. They seem unbothered by the perpetual thunder. Small birds—or things shaped like birds, with too-flexible necks and eyes that gleam copper in certain light—nest in the vine curtains and raise their young in the vibration. You can see them if you watch: darting between the fern fronds, catching insects that spiral up on the thermal columns.

There are other things. Pale salamanders on the wet pipes, translucent enough to show the shadows of their organs. Something larger that moves behind the vegetation on the far wall—you only ever see the leaves shaking in its wake. A colony of creatures like wet paper folded into the shape of wasps, building their geometric nests in the shelter of a defunct control panel, its dials all frozen at values that no longer matter.

The animals are abundant but the space is hollow—vast volumes of empty air between the presences, between the hanging gardens and the spiral walks. The center of the shaft is nothing but updraft and mist and the endless mechanical pulse. You could fall for a long time before you hit anything.

---

The engine below does not run the nursery. The nursery runs the engine.

This much becomes clear if you study it: the vines descending into the lower shafts, the root systems that have infiltrated the machinery itself, the way the plants pulse—almost imperceptibly—in counter-rhythm to the piston stroke. Something about the process, the steam, the mineral-rich condensation, feeds them. And something about them, their chemical exhalations, their slow vegetable labor, feeds the engine back. A symbiosis that established itself long ago, that has found its own balance, that will continue long after you leave.

The light through the glass ceiling never changes. Overcast. Gray. Normal. The day outside might be any day. The engine speaks its two-syllable name into the humid air. **CHUNK-hisssss.** The moths drift. The rain falls. The slow cycle turns.
