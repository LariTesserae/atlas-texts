# The Nursery Basin

The basin is perhaps two hundred meters across, a shallow depression worn into stone so old it has forgotten what kind of rock it used to be. The edges rise gently, smoothed by epochs into soft lips that could be granite, could be sandstone, could be something between—the mineral boundaries themselves have blurred over time, elements migrating through crystal lattices until the geology became a single continuous gradient from here to there. Water seeps from everywhere. Not springs exactly, but a general weeping, as if the stone itself perspires in the warm air.

The water collects in a braided network of channels no deeper than your ankle, wandering across the basin floor in paths that shift hourly. The stone between them stays damp, filmed with moisture that catches the bright overhead light and throws it back in pale sheets. There is no visible sun—the sky is a luminous white-gold membrane, sourceless and even, the kind of light that casts no shadows or casts them everywhere equally, depending on how you choose to see it.

---

They come here to be born.

The fauna arrives from elsewhere—you can see them at the basin's edges, descending the worn slopes in ones and twos. A creature like a wet leather kite furls itself down the southern rim, membrane wings folding against a body segmented like bamboo. Something eyeless and heavy pulls itself over the eastern lip, leaving a track of disturbed water behind it. In the northwest quadrant, a swarm of things too small to resolve individually moves as a single rippling sheet across the stone.

They come to the nursery, and the nursery provides.

The structures are few and simple: three pylons arranged in a loose triangle near the basin's center, each one a column of material that looks like frozen smoke, grey and slightly translucent, rising to just above head height. They hum at a frequency below hearing, felt in the teeth and the soft places behind the eyes. The technology inside them operates on principles that would require new mathematics to describe—something about information density in matter, about the negotiable boundary between template and instance. The pylons have been here longer than any of the species that use them. Perhaps longer than the species that built them.

An animal approaches the nearest pylon—six-legged, furred in something iridescent that might be hair or might be fiber optic filament, its head a blunt wedge with no visible features. It stands before the column and waits. The pylon's hum shifts, individuates, becomes almost a voice. The animal shudders once, twice, and then—

A second animal steps out of the first. Not born in any biological sense. Copied. Instantiated. The template and the instance stand together for a moment, identical and separate, and then they move apart without ceremony, wandering in different directions across the wet stone.

---

The nursery basin is full of these duplications. Everywhere you look, creatures stand in contemplation before the pylons or drift away in twos and threes and larger multiples. A single serpentine thing has become seven of itself, the copies coiling around each other in what might be recognition or might be simple confusion. The leather-kite creature spreads its wings near the western pylon, and two more unfurl behind it, still damp with the residue of becoming.

The vegetation is limited but persistent: a lush carpet of something moss-like grows in patches where the water pools longer, deep green and thick as velvet. It seems immune to being trampled—the countless feet and bodies and stranger appendages pass over it without damage, and it springs back within seconds. Small fern-like fronds cluster at the basin's edges, their leaves perpetually dripping, adding their small contributions to the general wetness. Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. The plants here are witnesses, not participants.

The air is warm and close, humid without being oppressive. It smells of wet stone and something faintly electrical, ozone or ionized water or the particular scent of matter being convinced to remember itself twice. The light never wavers. There is no wind.

---

Mostly, it is quiet.

This is the strange thing. With so many creatures present—you can count dozens at any moment, from the vast to the minute—the basin should be loud with calls and movement and biological noise. But the animals are silent. They process through the space with a solemnity that might be reverence or might simply be the particular stillness of creatures focused entirely on a single purpose. The only sounds are soft ones: the shuffle of feet on wet stone, the occasional splash as something crosses a deeper channel, the distant susurrus of water seeping endlessly from the rock.

The pylons hum. The copies emerge. The multitudes disperse.

You realize, watching, that the basin must have been deeper once—much deeper. The erosion here is so profound, so ancient, that the original topography has been essentially erased. This shallow depression is what remains of mountains, perhaps. Certainly of something much larger. The pylons themselves show no wear; they are the fixed points around which everything else has slowly, slowly ground down.

At the far edge of the basin, a creature you cannot quite focus on—something that seems to exist at a slight angle to your perception—stands before the third pylon. It has been there since you arrived. It may have been there for years. Waiting. Considering. When it finally steps forward and lets the pylon read it, the copy that emerges is somehow *older*, worn smooth by time that hasn't happened yet, a duplicate from a future erosion.

It walks toward you, this impossible twin, and then past you, and away.

The water continues to seep. The nursery continues its work. The basin holds them all, patient and ancient, wearing itself away one century at a time.
