# The Relay Warrens

The shaft drops through thirteen hundred meters of basalt before it opens into the cavity. You descend by cable-lift, the cage swaying in thermals that rise from the machinery below, and at first there is only the smell—ozone and copper and the faint biological musk of too many bodies packed into engineered space. Then the dim yellow emergency lighting catches the first platforms, and the scale becomes legible.

They built this place inside a volcanic bubble, a pocket of gas frozen in stone when the mountain was young. The original void was perhaps sixty meters across, but they have subdivided it ruthlessly, honeycombed it with metal grating and suspended walkways and platforms bolted directly into the curved basalt walls. Everything hangs. Nothing sits on solid ground because there is no ground, only the distant bottom of the bubble where the heat exchangers thrum and the temperature rises toward something the workers below call "the cooking level."

The air is cool where you enter, almost cold—the shaft draws mountain air down from the surface, and it pools in the upper reaches before warming and rising again. This circulation is the only thing that makes habitation possible. Without it, the heat from the relay engines would cook everything within hours.

---

The relay engines are the reason for the Warrens' existence. Seven of them, massive cylindrical housings suspended on shock-dampened cradles, each one thrumming with a frequency you feel in your teeth rather than hear. They process something—signals, perhaps, or calculations, or transformations of energy that have no name you would recognize. The engineers who tend them speak in a technical pidgin that borrows from three dead languages and at least one that was never meant for human mouths.

What matters is that the engines require attention. Constant attention. They drift out of calibration if left alone for more than forty minutes. Their housings develop micro-fractures from the vibration. Their cooling systems clog with a crystalline residue that forms from the interaction of the local atmosphere with their waste heat. And so the Warrens exist: a vertical city of perhaps four thousand souls, living in shifts, organized entirely around the maintenance of machines whose purpose they do not fully understand.

---

The population is dense beyond comfortable comprehension. The platforms are narrow—most are only three meters wide—and every surface that can be claimed has been claimed. Sleeping niches are carved into the basalt walls, some natural pockets in the volcanic stone, others cut with industrial borers and lined with salvaged insulation. The residents sleep in rotations, four people sharing a niche meant for one, their schedules offset by six hours so the bedding is never cold and never empty.

They are human, mostly. There are others—the thin-limbed things that cling to the undersides of platforms and perform repairs in spaces too small for human bodies, the semi-autonomous sensor clusters that drift on the thermals and report on engine status, the pack animals bred for vertical living that carry tools and components up and down the endless stairs. But the humans dominate, and they have adapted. They are small, most of them. Compact. They move through the vertical space with an ease that suggests generations of selection, gripping handholds without looking, stepping onto swaying platforms without breaking stride.

The children learn to climb before they learn to walk. There is nowhere flat enough for walking.

---

Sound in the Warrens is strange. The engines produce a bass note too low to hear, and this note cancels other sounds in unpredictable ways. Conversations happen in pockets of near-silence, voices oddly flat, swallowed by the subsonic interference. Then you move three steps to the left and suddenly you can hear everything—the clang of tools on metal, the calls of the shift-change wardens, the chittering of the repair-things in their maintenance crawlways. The acoustic geography is mapped in the minds of the residents. They know where to go to speak privately, where to go to eavesdrop, where to go to scream without being heard.

Mostly, though, it is quiet. Not silent—the engines never stop, the bodies never stop moving—but muted. Damped. The basalt absorbs sound, and the structures are designed to minimize vibration that might interfere with the engines' calibration. The residents have developed a sign language for efficiency, and whole shifts pass with nothing but the tap of fingers against palms and the occasional grunt of exertion.

---

The light comes from chemical strips bonded to the platforms and walkways. They emit a sickly yellow-amber glow that turns skin strange colors and makes it difficult to judge distance. The strips are cheap and reliable and require no power from the engines, which is essential—the engines give nothing back. Every watt they produce is consumed internally, in processes that matter, and so the Warrens run on salvaged batteries and chemical lights and the occasional smuggled solar cell connected by kilometers of wire to the surface above.

In this dimness, the vertical space plays tricks. You look down and see platforms receding toward the heat-glow of the cooking level, each one hosting its own cluster of activity—a repair crew wrestling with a clogged coolant line, a family eating from shared bowls, a vendor selling protein cakes from a fold-out stall. You look up and see more of the same, and then the darkness of the shaft mouth, and beyond that, in imagination only, the cold thin air of the mountain peak.

---

The turbulence is social as much as physical. The engines drift, and when they drift, the Warrens respond. A calibration failure on Engine Three sends ripples through the entire structure—shift assignments scramble, repair teams rappel down from the upper levels, arguments break out over whose fault the failure was and who should have noticed the warning signs. Then the problem is solved, and everything settles for perhaps an hour, and then Engine Five begins to overheat and the chaos begins again.

The residents have adapted to this too. Their social structures are fluid, hierarchies forming and dissolving around immediate needs. A family might be split across three platforms during a crisis, children sent to stay with whoever has capacity, parents working eighteen-hour shifts in the maintenance crawlways. Then the crisis passes and they reform, sharing a niche again, eating together, until the next emergency pulls them apart.

Nothing stays still. Nothing can afford to.

---

There is a rhythm to it, beneath the chaos. The engines have their cycles, their periods of relative stability and their periods of crisis. The residents have learned to read the signs—a particular harmonic in Engine Two's vibration, a certain temperature differential in the cooling systems, the behavior of the drifting sensor clusters. They know when trouble is coming, sometimes hours in advance, and they prepare. Supplies are cached in accessible locations. Children are moved to the safer upper levels. The repair-things are fed extra protein so they will work faster when the time comes.

And when it comes, they meet it. Not gracefully, not without loss, but with a competence born of necessity. The Warrens have been here for nine generations, and they will be here for nine more, and the engines will drift and be corrected and drift again, and the children will learn to climb, and the dead will be lowered to the cooking level where the heat will take them, and the living will keep living in their pocket of carved stone, suspended between the cold peak above and the hot depths below, tending machines they did not build for purposes they do not understand.

This is the Relay Warrens. This is how it works. This is all it has ever been.
