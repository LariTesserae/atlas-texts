# The Kiln Flats

A made world, still making itself.

The floor extends beyond horizon in every direction—a continuous plane of fired clay, terracotta and ochre and deep brick-red, stretching vast enough to hold its own weather systems. This is not earth. This is something that was poured, shaped, set to cure under a heat that has never fully left. The surface radiates warmth upward even now, hot enough that the air above it shimmers and bends, hot enough that you feel it through whatever you're standing in, whatever you're standing on.

But the floor is not flat. Not truly. It buckles and dips in long, lazy undulations—shallow basins kilometers wide, gentle rises that take an hour to climb and reveal nothing but more of the same vastness beyond. In places, the clay has cracked during cooling into patterns like the scales of impossible animals, hexagonal tiles the size of houses separated by seams just wide enough to lose a foot in. In other places, the surface is smooth and glazed, reflecting the sky in murky pools of color.

And the floor is still *forming*. You can feel it—a faint tremor beneath your feet, irregular, like a heartbeat that hasn't found its rhythm. New sections are being laid down somewhere beyond the visible horizon. The process is inaudible at this distance but present, a vibration in the bones of the world. Sometimes, if you watch a particular crack long enough, you'll see it seal itself—fresh clay welling up from below, cooling and hardening over hours into seamless surface.

---

The moisture comes in cycles. Not rain—nothing falls from the sky here—but *condensation events*, when the differential between the hot floor and the cooler upper atmosphere reaches some critical threshold. Fog banks form and roll across the flats for days at a time, dense enough to reduce visibility to arm's length, depositing a film of water on every surface. Then the heat reasserts itself, and everything evaporates within hours, leaving behind faint mineral residue, white lines tracing where droplets traveled.

In the shallow basins, water collects longer. Not pools exactly—more like damp depressions, clay darkened to the color of dried blood, tacky underfoot. These are the only places where anything grows.

The vegetation is sparse and strange—not plants as you'd know them, but *formations*. Pale growths that look more like salt crystals than living things, branching structures of accumulated mineral left behind by thousands of condensation cycles. They're biological, technically. They grow. They respond to moisture. But they have no leaves, no chlorophyll, no obvious metabolism. White and ivory and pale yellow, they rise from the damp basins in clusters, some no taller than your hand, others reaching three or four meters—delicate architectures that chime faintly when the wind passes through them.

They are the only other presences here. No animals. No insects. No birds wheeling in the thermal columns that must surely rise from this heated expanse. The silence of the place is profound, broken only by the crystalline singing of those mineral-growths when conditions align, and by the occasional deep *thunk* of new clay settling somewhere far away.

---

The sky is hazy, particulate, the color of bone. Light comes through it diffused and directionless—shadows exist but barely, soft-edged suggestions rather than sharp declarations. The sun, if there is one, is not visible as a discrete object. The sky simply *glows*, brighter overhead, dimmer toward the horizons, cycling through something like day and night over periods that feel longer than they should.

Weather moves across this vastness in slow, visible systems. You can watch a condensation front approaching for days, a wall of white advancing across the flats, before it finally arrives and wraps you in its wet opacity. You can see the heat-shimmer dancing over distant rises, distorting them into liquid mirages. Sometimes, rarely, a wind will pick up—not strong, but *sustained*, blowing for weeks at a time in a single direction, carrying fine clay dust that coats everything in fresh pigment.

And always, beneath it all, the tremor. The world continuing to lay itself down, tile by tile, basin by basin, extending the flats further into whatever surrounds them—if anything surrounds them at all. This is a place that is being *built*, but not by hands. Not by machines. Built by some process that requires no technology, no tools, only time and heat and the patient accumulation of material from below.

You walk here for days and find nothing but more of itself. The scale resists comprehension. You are an interruption in the pattern, a temporary flaw in the glaze, and the floor will be here long after you've gone, still cooling, still cracking, still sealing its wounds with fresh clay, still becoming more of what it already is.
