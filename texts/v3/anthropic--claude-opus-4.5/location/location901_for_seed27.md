# The Susurrus Plateau

The plateau rises two miles above the dead seabeds, a vast shelf of compacted mineral that should be barren, should be silent, should be nothing but wind-scoured rock at the roof of the world. Instead, it breathes.

They call it the Susurrus because of what it does to sound. The vegetation here—and there is nothing but vegetation, wall to wall, horizon to horizon—grows in spiraling acoustic structures that catch every vibration and multiply it, harmonize it, send it cycling back transformed. A footstep becomes a chord. A voice becomes a choir. The plateau is never quiet; it sings to itself constantly, a roaring vegetable symphony that takes some visitors weeks to learn to sleep through.

There is no water anywhere. Not in the soil, not in the air, not pooled in any crevice. The plants drink something else—they tap directly into geothermal vents through root networks that descend miles into the mountain's heart, drawing up mineral-rich gases and trace elements, converting heat itself into something like sustenance. Their tissues are dry to the touch, almost papery, but internally they run with silver circulatory fluid that flashes when cut, evaporating instantly into a faintly metallic mist.

---

The plant-mass is sparse by the standards of other living worlds—you can see for miles across the plateau's gentle undulations, and most of what you see is the pale grey-pink substrate of ancient compressed stone. But sparse here still means absolute dominance. Where life exists on this plateau, it is plant life, arranged in vast scattered clusters like islands in a mineral sea.

Each cluster is a single organism. Some cover a quarter mile. Some are merely the size of a house. They grow in tiered spirals, woody stalks as thick as ancient trees at the base, thinning as they rise into chambered structures that look almost intentionally architectural—galleries, amphitheaters, hollow spires with thousands of resonating apertures. The Builders—whoever came before—did not construct settlements here so much as *cultivate* them. They coaxed the plants into configurations useful for habitation, and the plants obliged over centuries, and now the distinction between grown and built is meaningless.

The technology, if it can be called that, is embedded in the plant-structures at a level that defies easy analysis. Walk through one of the larger spirals and you pass through zones of altered gravity, pockets where light bends, chambers that maintain different temperatures than the ambient chill. The plants *do* these things. They have been modified—engineered—at some fundamental level to perform functions that living tissue should not perform. How the Builders achieved this is unknown. The Builders are long gone, or perhaps they were never humanoid at all, or perhaps they still exist as patterns encoded in the growth instructions of the organisms they created. Some scholars believe the plants *are* the Builders now, that the engineering went both ways.

---

In the dim light—the plateau sits beneath a perpetual cloud-layer that filters the sun to a pale amber glow—the clusters glow faintly from within. Bioluminescence in frequencies that shift through the day, blues and greens in what passes for morning, deep reds and oranges as the light fades further. The glow is not just aesthetic; it serves as communication between clusters, slow pulses encoding information that travels across the plateau over hours and days. What they communicate about is unclear. They have been communicating for longer than any human records exist.

The fauna—and there is abundant fauna, though all of it strange—exists in symbiosis with the plant-masses. Creatures like glass-boned birds nest in the resonating spires, their calls amplified and returned to them transformed; they have evolved to sing in frequencies that produce specific harmonics, essentially using the architecture as a musical instrument. Something like a long-limbed mammal, covered in fine silver fur, moves through the gallery spaces in troops, grooming the plant surfaces, eating parasitic growths, sleeping in heated chambers the plants maintain at precise temperatures. There are pollinators—or what function as pollinators—that carry spores between clusters, flying on wings that seem too thin to support them in the high thin atmosphere, except the gravity in their flight corridors has been subtly reduced, and they swim through air that is almost as forgiving as water would be elsewhere.

At night, larger things move between the clusters. No one has gotten a clear look at them. They are fast, and the acoustic environment means you hear them everywhere at once—a thunderous rhythmic pounding that could be footsteps or could be some massive heartbeat or could be the clusters themselves, performing some nocturnal process. The resident researchers have learned not to be outside the clusters after dark. Not because of verified attacks, but because the things that move out there occasionally stop moving, and the silence that follows is worse than the sound.

---

The human settlement—Threshold, they call it—occupies three adjacent clusters near the plateau's eastern edge, connected by enclosed walkways of cultivated plant-matter that took sixty years to grow to usable size. The architecture inside is strange, organic, tilted in ways that take advantage of local gravity variations. Vertical gardens that are also load-bearing walls. Floors that curve upward at the edges where the gravity slopes. Light that comes from bioluminescent panels that are technically still alive, still part of the larger organism.

Threshold exists to study the plateau, but increasingly, it exists because leaving would mean abandoning whatever understanding they've begun to develop. The researchers are third and fourth generation now. They've grown up with the sound. They find ordinary silence disturbing, empty, incomplete. Some of them have begun to sing in ways that harmonize with the architecture, their voices joining the endless chorus. Whether this is cultural adaptation or something else—some slow influence the plants exert on those who live within them—is a subject of ongoing debate.

The plateau is ancient. The erosion patterns in the stone beneath the clusters suggest that it reached its current elevation hundreds of millions of years ago, that the mountain-building forces that raised it have long since gone quiet, that it has settled into a form that will persist until the planet's core cools entirely. The plants may be nearly as old. Core samples from the largest clusters suggest continuous growth for at least ninety million years, layers building on layers, the organism immortal in any practical sense.

At the plateau's center—three days' travel from Threshold, through increasingly dense cluster-forests and past acoustic phenomena that can disorient or even damage unprotected ears—there is a structure the plants have grown around rather than through. Something made of materials the plants cannot digest. The Builders' last work, perhaps, or their first. It hums at a frequency just below human hearing, a vibration you feel in your teeth. No one has found a way inside. The plants press close around it, their spirals oriented toward it like frozen supplicants, and in the deep red light of evening the whole plateau seems to tilt toward that hidden center, waiting for something, listening for an answer to a question posed before any current species drew breath.
