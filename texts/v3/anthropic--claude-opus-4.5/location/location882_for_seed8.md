# The Worn Basin

The land has been breathing water for so long it has forgotten any other way to be.

A vast lowland stretches before you, neither swamp nor lake nor dry ground, but something that has spent eons deciding it will be all three at once, forever. The basin extends for miles in every direction, bounded distantly by what might once have been mountains but are now only gentle rises, blue-grey suggestions on the horizon that took a hundred million years to surrender their height to rain and wind and the patient chemistry of roots.

The ground underfoot is warm stone worn to the smoothness of soap—a pale ochre hardpan shot through with darker mineral veins that trace ancient drainage patterns. Water seeps up through countless tiny fissures, spreading in films across the rock before gathering in shallow channels no deeper than your ankle. These channels wander without urgency, braiding and separating, catching light. Some are warm from the rock beneath. Others are cooler where they've pooled in slight depressions, shaded by nothing but their own stillness.

Everything here has been ground down to its essentials. There are no dramatic outcrops, no boulders—only the long, low swells of bedrock that break the water's surface like the backs of sleeping animals. The erosion is so complete, so utterly finished, that the landscape feels less like a place where things have worn away and more like a place that has finally arrived at what it always meant to become.

---

Vegetation appears in scattered colonies, never forests, never thickets. Tough-stemmed rushes cluster at the edges of deeper pools, their stalks clicking against each other in the wind—a sound like someone shuffling through a box of wooden beads. Low cushion-plants grip the higher rock surfaces, grey-green and dense as upholstery, their tiny leaves furred with moisture. Here and there, a solitary tree rises: gnarled things with smooth bark and wide-spreading crowns, their roots visible for yards before they disappear into fissures, following the water down. They cast wide shadows on the wet stone, and beneath them the rock stays darker, damper, colonized by thin orange lichen that looks like rust-stains.

The warmth is ambient, pervasive, neither oppressive nor quite comfortable. It rises from the rock, from the water, from the air itself—a thermal equilibrium the basin reached long ago and has no interest in disturbing. By afternoon, the wet stone gives off a faint mineral smell, like blood or wet pennies, and the water in the channels moves so slowly you have to watch a floating seed for half a minute to confirm it's traveling at all.

---

But the animals—the animals make noise.

Frogs, primarily. Dozens of species, maybe hundreds, each with their own voice and timing. From the rushes comes a steady electronic-sounding trill, mechanical and unbroken. From the deeper pools, a resonant bonking, like someone striking a hollow log. From somewhere you can never quite locate, a rising whistle that starts low and climbs until it simply stops, as if the creature ran out of air or interest. They sing in overlapping waves, in call and response, in what might be argument or might be something more like weather—just the sound the basin makes because it's a basin.

Wading birds pick through the shallows on stilt-legs, unbothered by the frogs or by you. Dark shapes that might be eels or might be something older slide through the deeper channels, visible only as movement, as a brief darkening. Insects skim the water's surface in the quieter pools—water striders and whirligig beetles describing their small frantic geometries. In the air, dragonflies patrol in iridescent squadrons, and smaller flies hover in clouds over patches of exposed algae, filling the space between frog-calls with a thin, continuous hum.

---

Nothing here is in a hurry. The water moves, but barely. The clouds overhead—and there are always clouds, fat with moisture they'll shed as afternoon rain—drift rather than race. The birds feed in the shallows for hours, lifting one foot, setting it down, lifting another. Even the insects seem to operate on extended time, their frantic motion somehow contained within the basin's larger patience.

There are no paths because there has never been anyone to make them. No cairns, no cleared spaces, no marks on the ancient trees. The basin is not hostile to presence—it simply has no knowledge of it. You could walk for days across the warm stone, following the water-channels toward that distant blue rise on the horizon, and the land would neither welcome nor resist you. It would simply continue being what it is: wet rock, sparse green, the endless chorus of frogs, the slow seep of water finding its level.

The light is good. Clear and even, generous with detail. You can see the grain of the rock, the individual stems of the rushes, the particular iridescence on each dragonfly wing. It is a place that hides nothing, that has nothing to hide—everything that could be stripped away has been stripped away, and what remains is only the basin itself, wearing its age like comfort, exhaling moisture into the warm and constant air.
