# The Wicker Warrens

Beneath the cracked clay flats, where no rain has fallen in living memory, someone long ago began weaving. They wove walls from dried sedge hauled from distant marshes. They wove floors from stripped bark and knotted fiber. They wove ceilings that became the floors of chambers above, and those ceilings became floors again, downward and downward, until the warrens reached seven layers deep into the earth, a honeycomb of basketwork rooms suspended in excavated darkness.

The entrance is easy to miss—a woven hatch no larger than a wine barrel's mouth, tucked beneath an overhang of hardened clay. But drop through, and you enter a world that breathes.

---

Every surface is alive. Not with water—there is no water here, none at all, not even condensation on the walls—but with things that have learned to live without it. The wicker walls support entire ecosystems of lichen that extract moisture from the air itself, from the exhalations of the creatures that pass through, from the slow chemical breakdown of the plant fibers they consume. These lichens glow faintly amber and rust-red, providing enough light to navigate by, though oil lamps of rendered fat hang at intersections, their wicks made from twisted hair.

The weaving is old. Generations old. You can see where repairs have been made—newer fibers standing out pale against the darkened, oil-seasoned originals. The oldest sections have taken on the hardness of wood, compressed and polymerized by decades of use, by the oils of countless hands and bodies pressing past. But the structure remains sound. The original weavers knew what they were doing. The chambers flex slightly underfoot, absorbing movement, and the whole warren creaks softly in a constant, almost subliminal voice—the sound of fiber settling against fiber, of weight redistributing, of an enormous basket slowly, imperceptibly shifting.

---

The warmth comes from below and from bodies. The lowest levels sit near enough to geothermal vents that the air rises tropical and close. It carries the smell of dry grass and animal musk and something sweeter—the fermented sap of desert succulents, stored in woven vessels and left to turn to something between vinegar and wine.

In this warmth, the fauna thrives.

Beetles the size of thumbnails traverse the walls in glittering columns, their carapaces black and bronze. They eat the lichen, and their frass falls to the floors where it's swept into collection baskets and used to feed the pale crickets kept in hanging cages. The crickets sing at unpredictable intervals—a sudden chorus that rises and falls within seconds, leaving silence that feels more profound for having been broken.

Larger things move through the corridors. Ferrets, or something like them—elongated bodies covered in fine scales rather than fur, adapted to the dry heat. They're half-domesticated, tolerated for the way they hunt the thumb-beetles when populations surge. You'll turn a corner and find one watching you from a wall-niche, its eyes catching the lichen-light, before it flows away into some gap in the weaving you hadn't noticed.

And there are the keepers themselves.

---

They are small people, the warren-dwellers. Compact. They move through spaces that would require larger folk to crawl, and they've built accordingly—some passages open into chambers of surprising height, but most are intimate, meant for two or three bodies at most. Their tools are simple: bone awls, stone knives, wooden shuttles worn smooth. They repair and extend the warrens with the same techniques their ancestors used. Each family maintains their section, knows which fibers are weakening, which joins need reinforcing. Children learn the patterns before they learn to speak.

The warrens are dense with them. You hear their presence more than see it—soft footfalls, murmured conversation through woven walls that hide nothing but sight. Someone is always moving, always tending, always feeding the crickets or harvesting lichen or weaving a new basket to replace one that's finally given way.

But they are quiet about it. The warren teaches quiet. Sound carries strangely through the fiber walls, muffled and intimate and everywhere at once. So they speak softly, step softly, live softly. The loudest sound is the occasional flare of cricket-song, and even that feels absorbed by the wicker, drunk up by the countless interlocking fibers until it becomes just another texture in the silence.

---

Light filters down through woven shafts that pierce upward to the surface, but it arrives diffused, scattered by the lattice of fibers until it becomes a sourceless amber glow. By the time it reaches the fourth level, it's barely a suggestion. By the sixth, only the lichen remains, and the small clay lamps, and the eyes of the ferrets catching what little there is.

The lowest level—the seventh—is kept closed. The hatch is bound with cord that shows no sign of recent disturbance. When asked, the keepers say only that it's where the first weavers went, when they were finished. That the warmth there is too much. That the lichen grows strange.

But sometimes, if you press your ear to the woven floor, you can hear something moving down there. Something patient. Something that has had a very long time to adapt to the dark.

---

The warrens are not natural. Every surface, every passage, every chamber exists because someone made it. But they've been made so long, and tended so carefully, and inhabited so thoroughly, that they've become their own kind of ecosystem—a world of woven grass and quiet footsteps and small scaled ferrets and the endless, gentle creaking of a structure that lives because it is lived in.

You could spend days here. Weeks. The keepers would make room. They always do.

They're very good at making room.
