# The Hive Plateau

The darkness breathes.

You stand on a shelf of volcanic glass, perhaps forty paces across, jutting from a cliff face at an elevation where the air should thin but instead thickens with heat rising from somewhere far below. The glass beneath your feet is old—weathered to a matte finish, its once-sharp edges worn to curves that cup small hollows. In those hollows, things move.

No light reaches here. The plateau exists in a permanent shadow cast by an overhang of basalt so ancient it has begun to sag, to drip frozen stone fingers toward the shelf below. What illumination there is comes from the creatures themselves—not light exactly, but a thermal glow your eyes learn to parse, the way night vision resolves into shapes that aren't quite color but aren't quite nothing.

They are everywhere.

The plateau is *packed* with them. Segmented bodies the length of your forearm flow over and under each other in continuous ribbons. They have no eyes that you can see, but they have *presence*—each one aware of its neighbors, of you, of the heat differentials in the air. Their shells are mineral, grown from the same volcanic glass they traverse, so they appear as living extensions of the stone itself, distinguished only by movement. When they pause, they vanish. When they move, the entire surface of the plateau writhes.

The heat is oppressive, radiating up through the glass from thermal vents somewhere in the mountain's guts. The creatures cluster most densely around certain points—cracks in the glass where the warmth concentrates—and in these spots they pile three, four, five bodies deep, their mineral shells clicking softly against each other in the only sound this place produces: a faint, arhythmic ticking, like cooling metal, like a clock with too many hands.

Among the glass-shelled ribbon-creatures live others. Spherical things the size of fists, rolling slowly through the traffic, extruding thin filaments that taste the air or the stone or something else entirely. Flat disc-shaped organisms that cling to the underside of the basalt overhang, their edges rippling with cilia that harvest... what? Heat? Vibration? Some chemical gradient invisible to you?

There is no water here. The air is mineral-dry, carrying a faint taste of sulfur and heated stone. The creatures do not drink. They do not eat anything you would recognize as food. Their metabolism runs on temperature differentials, on the slow chemistry of hot glass and cooler air, on processes that have been refining themselves since this plateau was a fresh wound in the mountainside.

Near the eastern edge—you know it is east only because the heat is slightly less there, suggesting distance from the volcanic source—a structure stands. A single column of stacked stones, clearly placed rather than fallen, perhaps waist-high. The ribbon-creatures flow around it but never over it. The spheres roll wide. It is old, this cairn, older than the weathering patterns on the glass around it, which means something built it before the plateau settled into its current form. The stones are not volcanic. They came from somewhere else, carried here, stacked with intention.

What intention? The cairn marks nothing obvious. No trail leads to or from this place. No other structures exist within sight. It simply stands, a single gesture of *building* in a landscape that otherwise belongs entirely to the creatures and the heat and the dark.

The activity never stops. The ribbon-creatures are always moving—not frantically, but with purpose, following gradients you cannot perceive. The spheres roll their slow patrols. The disc-things pulse overhead. Population density shifts as thermal conditions change: when a new vent of heat rises through a crack, bodies flow toward it in a living tide. When a section of glass cools slightly, the creatures drain away, leaving momentary clearings that fill again within minutes.

You are standing in the middle of a creature so large it has no single body—an ecology so dense it functions as an organism, its thousand forms the cells of something that thinks in heat and moves in mineral time. The plateau is not a place where animals live. The plateau *is* the animal, and you are inside it, and it has not yet decided what to do with you.

The ticking continues. The darkness breathes. Somewhere in the mountain below, the heat shifts, and the entire surface of the plateau ripples in response, a wave of glass-shelled bodies adjusting to a new gradient, and for a moment the cairn stands alone in a momentary clearing, the only still thing in a world made entirely of motion.
