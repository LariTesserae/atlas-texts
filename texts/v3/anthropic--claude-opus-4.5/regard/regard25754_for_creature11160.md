# What the Benevolent Power Sees

The attention arrives not as observation from outside but as a quality of presence that was perhaps always there, waiting to recognize itself. It moves through the settling district the way morning moves through it—gradually, without announcing, filling what was already open.

And what it finds is this: a place that is already practicing wisdom.

---

The power's first impulse is to help, and its second impulse is to notice that this impulse itself requires examination. Help toward what? The district is not suffering in any way that resolution would serve. It is *working*—the slow metabolic work of becoming, which is not a problem to be solved but a process to be honored.

So the power does what wisdom does when it encounters something already alive: it pays attention.

---

It watches the scholar press her fingers to the wall and understands that she is not measuring. She is *listening* with her hands, conducting a conversation in the language of mineral and pressure and time. Her documentation has become a form of love—not the love that grasps but the love that witnesses. She has given herself to something she cannot control, and this giving has made her more herself rather than less.

The power considers: should it cure her loneliness? Should it tell her that her work matters, that someone sees?

But loneliness here is not the same as loneliness elsewhere. The scholar is *accompanied*—by the walls, by the toad, by the boy's humming in the dark. Her solitude is populated. To remove it would be to misunderstand what she has chosen.

Instead, the power does something smaller. It ensures that tomorrow, when she presses her fingers to the wall, she will feel something she has not felt before—not a change in the mortar, but a change in her capacity to perceive. The wall will seem, for just a moment, to press back. To acknowledge her. This is not magic. It is only the deepening of attention that comes from being attended to.

She will not know the power did this. She will think she finally learned to feel what was always there. She will be right.

---

The boy.

The power lingers here longest, because the boy is the most liminal of all of them, the one most fully native to the in-between. He collects things—mortar fragments, seedpods, gecko skins—but what he is really collecting is *transitions*. Evidence that things can move from one state to another without being destroyed. He needs this evidence. Something in his history taught him that change means loss, and he has been gathering proof that it doesn't have to.

The power sees what the others cannot: the boy is not waiting for something. He is *practicing*. Each day he moves through the district, he is rehearsing how to belong somewhere that is still deciding what it is. He is learning that he can be part of something without that something being finished. This is the hardest lesson, and he is teaching it to himself with mortar chips and seedpods.

What would help him?

Not adoption—not being claimed by someone, fitted into a structure that already knows its shape. That would undo what he is learning. Not food or shelter—he has found what he needs in the gaps, and taking him out of the gaps would take him out of himself.

The power does this: it arranges for the scholar's offerings to appear where the boy can find them without feeling found. It ensures that when he takes the bread, the cup, the pencil stub, he understands them not as charity but as *exchange*—things left by someone who is also learning to live in the becoming, someone who recognizes him as a fellow practitioner. It makes the offerings feel like conversation rather than rescue.

And one night, soon, it will arrange for the boy to leave something in return. A perfect gecko skin, translucent as breath, placed where the scholar will find it in the morning. She will understand. She will leave something else. A correspondence will begin, conducted entirely in objects, a language native to this place where everything communicates through what it is becoming.

Neither of them will know the power helped. They will think they invented this themselves. They will be right about that too.

---

The cistern-keeper already knows what the power knows: that patience is not passive, that tending is a form of creation, that singing to clay in an old language is as practical as measuring and mixing. The power does nothing for him except ensure that his song carries a little farther than it otherwise would—far enough that the scholar hears it some nights and incorporates it into her dreams, far enough that the boy learns a few phrases without knowing he's learning them, far enough that the district's settling happens in harmony with a human voice.

This is such a small intervention it hardly counts. But the power understands that small interventions, well-placed, can be more transformative than miracles. A song that carries. A wall that seems to press back. A gecko skin appearing where it will be understood.

---

The builder with the strong arms believes she builds what wants to be built. She is more right than she knows. The power sees the way her hands respond to materials—how she adjusts her pressure, her angle, her rhythm based on something she couldn't articulate but absolutely perceives. She is in dialogue with the district. Her buildings emerge from negotiation rather than imposition.

But she is tired. Not the tiredness that sleep cures—a deeper exhaustion, the kind that comes from working at the edge of what you understand, from making decisions that will outlast you without knowing if they're correct.

The power cannot remove this tiredness without removing what causes it, which would be a cruelty disguised as kindness. The builder's exhaustion is the weight of responsibility genuinely carried. It is supposed to be heavy.

What the power can do is this: it can make the weight *shared*. It arranges for the builder to notice, really notice, how the others in the district are also carrying weight. The scholar's mineral-stained fingers. The cistern-keeper's patient attention. The family's adaptation to their unfinished stairs. Even the boy, collecting his evidence of survivable change. The builder has felt alone in her work, the only one truly invested in what this place becomes. The power helps her see that she is part of a collective effort she didn't know she had joined.

This is not comfort exactly. It is *company*. It is the recognition that changes everything while changing nothing.

---

The family on the stairs.

Here the power pauses, because here there is something closer to ordinary need. The smallest child has a cough that the damp air doesn't help. The mother's patience is fraying. The father climbs those unfinished steps each night with a weariness that is not metaphysical but simply physical, the exhaustion of a body asked to do too much.

The power could finish the stairs. Could build the upper floor in a night, gift them the completed home they were promised. Could cure the child's cough, restore the mother's patience, strengthen the father's legs.

But this would be to misunderstand what they need.

They need the stairs to be finished *by the process that is finishing everything else*. They need to be part of the district's becoming, not exceptions to it. To give them completion while everything around them remains incomplete would be to exile them from the community of incompleteness that is sustaining everyone here.

So the power does smaller things. It ensures the child's cough doesn't worsen—not curing it, but preventing the slide toward real illness. It arranges for the mother to find a moment of genuine rest, a morning when all the children sleep late and she can sit on the landing in the gray light and simply breathe. It gives the father a dream—not a prophetic dream, not a meaningful dream, just a dream of climbing stairs that end in a room full of light, so that when he wakes he carries a little more hope than he went to sleep with.

These interventions are invisible. They are the kind of help that looks like luck, like grace, like the natural variance of days. The family will not know they were helped. They will think some days are simply better than others. They will be right.

---

The woman who tends the wasps speaks truly when she says they are building the real city. The power sees what she sees: the hex-comb architecture spreading through the district's gaps, creating structure that no human planned, establishing patterns that will outlast the mortar and the timber and perhaps the humans themselves.

The wasps do not need help. They are already doing what they do perfectly, which is to say they are already responding to what is with what could be, building possibility into geometry, making homes out of paper and spit and the patient repetition of hexagons.

But the old woman needs something, though she would not call it need. She needs to be believed. Not about the wasps—she doesn't care if others understand the wasps—but about herself. She needs someone to recognize that her age is not diminishment, that her strange clicking language is not madness, that her attention to the wasps is a form of expertise the district cannot afford to lose.

The power arranges for the scholar to truly see her. Not just to note her as a character in the settling district's story, but to *recognize* her as a fellow practitioner, someone whose knowledge matters. It ensures that the next time they speak, the scholar asks the right question—not "what do you mean?" but "what do you see?"—and actually listens to the answer.

The old woman will not know this conversation was arranged. She will think the scholar finally became ready to hear. She will be right.

---

And the beetles.

The beetles are not individuals to the power but a process, a distributed intelligence that is indeed cleaning the district, preparing it, making it ready for something none of them understand. The power does not help the beetles because the beetles do not need help. They are already help—help that arrived before anyone knew help was needed, help that works too slowly for human perception, help that will still be working when the district finally decides what it is.

The power honors the beetles by not interfering with them. This is perhaps the deepest wisdom it exercises here: knowing when to do nothing because something is already being done.

---

# The Settling

The power does not leave because it never arrived in the way that leaving implies. It becomes part of the district's awareness of itself—not directing, not controlling, but *accompanying*. It becomes the quality of attention that allows the mortar to cure at exactly the right pace. It becomes the borrowed light's particular softness. It becomes the way the water sounds as it finds its way through unfinished channels.

If you asked the scholar what changed, she would say: nothing. And then she would pause, pressing her fingers to the wall, and say: everything is more itself than it was.

If you asked the boy—but you cannot ask the boy, and this is as it should be. He moves through the gaps, humming his minor key, collecting his evidence. He does not need to name what has happened because he lives in the happening itself.

If you asked the builder, she would say the walls are setting well this season. If you asked the cistern-keeper, he would say the clay is healing. If you asked the family, they would say the cough is better, the stairs feel shorter, they can almost see the room at the top.

If you asked the old woman, she would laugh her gap-toothed laugh and say: *Ask the wasps. They're the ones who know.*

And if you asked the power what it did, it would say:

*I paid attention. I trusted what was already working. I made small adjustments where weight could be better shared. I did not finish what wanted to remain unfinished. I did not heal what was not sick. I did not solve what was not a problem.*

*I let a place that was learning to hold shape continue learning.*

*I let becoming keep becoming.*

*This is all any wisdom can do, in the end: to see clearly, to intervene gently, to honor the slow metabolic work of things becoming themselves. The district will settle. The mortar will cure. The boy will someday speak, or he will not, and either way he will have learned what he came to learn. The scholar will fill her margins and exceed them. The family will climb stairs that end in rooms. The wasps will build their paper cities. The beetles will clean. The gecko will hunt. The toad will wait.*

*And I will be here—not watching, but present. Not helping, but attending. Not changing anything, except the quality of attention with which everything changes itself.*

---

In the courtyard, in the gray light, the scholar wakes to find a gecko skin on the ground beside her bedroll. It is so perfect, so translucent, that for a moment she thinks it is a trick of the light.

She picks it up with her mineral-stained fingers. She understands.

She begins to look for something to leave in return.

The walls breathe. The water finds its way. The settling continues.
