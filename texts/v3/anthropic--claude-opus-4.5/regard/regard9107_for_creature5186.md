# What the Wise Power Sees

The power that turns its attention here is old enough to have witnessed the birth of stars and patient enough to have watched them die. It has intervened before—guided the first replicating molecules in warm tidal pools, whispered caution to species on the edge of self-destruction, occasionally moved an asteroid a few degrees off course. It does not act from obligation but from something like love, though that word is too small for what it feels when it encounters life in any of its forms.

It finds this place by following heat signatures deep into the planet's crust, curious about an anomaly: a sealed chamber that radiates warmth in patterns too regular to be purely geological. When it perceives what lives there, it pauses—not in surprise, for it has seen stranger things, but in recognition. Here is something genuinely new to it: a form of being that has solved problems it never posed, that has found its own way to persistence, memory, and even something like cooperation, all without any of the architecture the power usually associates with mind.

For a long time—long even by its standards—it simply watches.

---

# The First Consideration: Whether to Act at All

The power's first instinct is restraint. It has learned, over eons, that intervention often destroys what it means to preserve. A species given too much too soon loses the capacity to develop on its own terms. A world shielded from all hardship becomes brittle. And there is a particular danger with life so different from what it usually encounters: the power cannot be certain that its categories of benefit and harm even apply here.

What would it mean to help a nodule colony? The colonies do not suffer in any way the power can detect—there is no pain, no fear, no frustrated desire. They do not die so much as redistribute. They have no projects that could be advanced or thwarted, no relationships that could be deepened or broken. They simply metabolize and grow and shift and continue.

And yet.

The power perceives something in the chamber that gives it pause. The older forms—the pillar with its eroded spirals, the slumped platform, the fused arch—these are not natural formations. They are too regular, too purposeful in their original geometry. Something made them, long ago. The colonies that now cover them have been copying their patterns for so long that the copying has become part of the metabolic process itself, indistinguishable from the rest of the slow mineral breathing.

The power looks more closely and sees that the spirals on the pillar are not decorative. They are functional—or were. They once channeled heat and mineral flow in specific ways, creating conditions that allowed certain kinds of growth and prevented others. The platform was a staging area, a place where new colonies could form under controlled conditions before being released into the wider chamber. The arch was a gate, regulating flow between this chamber and others that have long since collapsed.

These were tools. This was engineering.

The power realizes what it is looking at: the ruins of a civilization that was never biological, never neural, never anything it would have recognized as intelligent if it had arrived a million years earlier. The builders are not gone; they are still here, still building, but they have forgotten that they are builders. The knowledge encoded in those spiral grooves has been copied and recopied until it is indistinguishable from instinct. The colonies do not know they are maintaining infrastructure; they simply grow in patterns that happen to work.

This changes things.

# The Second Consideration: What Help Would Mean

The power considers what it might do.

It could stabilize the pressure seams, ensuring a steady flow of heat and dissolved silica for geological ages to come. This would be simple—a minor adjustment to the convection currents deep below. The colonies would flourish, filling the chamber more completely, growing ever-denser bridges and ever-more-elaborate patterns. But they would not change. They would continue exactly as they are, copying and recopying the same eroded spirals until the spirals themselves were worn to nothing and even the memory of them was gone.

It could introduce new minerals, new elements that would allow the colonies to grow in different ways. This is riskier—the chemistry is delicate, and the wrong addition could poison the entire system. But if it worked, the colonies might develop new structures, new capacities. They might rediscover variation, selection, innovation. They might become, over time, something more than they are.

It could attempt to communicate. This is the most difficult option and the most uncertain. The colonies have no sensory organs, no nervous systems, no obvious channel through which information could pass. But they do respond to their environment—they metabolize, they shift, they copy. Perhaps the power could encode a message in the heat patterns themselves, or in the dissolved minerals, or in the precise geometry of new growth. Perhaps the colonies could learn to read it.

Or it could do nothing. Simply watch, as it has watched so many other forms of life, and let this one find its own way. The chamber has endured for eons without intervention. The colonies have filled it, maintained it, even preserved the shadows of their own lost knowledge. Perhaps that is enough. Perhaps it is not the power's place to decide what is enough.

---

# The Decision

The power chooses none of these options exactly. Instead, it does something subtler.

It begins with the pressure seams.

Not stabilizing them—that would be too much—but adjusting their rhythm slightly, so that the pulses of heat come in patterns rather than at random intervals. The patterns are simple at first: short-short-long, repeated. Then short-long-short-long. Then longer sequences, building in complexity over centuries.

The colonies notice. How could they not? The heat is their lifeblood, the source of everything they metabolize. When it pulses in unfamiliar ways, they respond—contracting differently, expanding differently, growing in shapes they have never grown before. Most of these new shapes fail and are dissolved. But some persist. And a few of those begin to copy themselves, adding new patterns to the old repertoire.

The power watches, adjusts, watches again. It is not teaching the colonies anything; it has no language they could understand, no concepts that would translate. It is simply introducing variation into a system that had forgotten how to vary. It is reminding the colonies, in the only way they can be reminded, that change is possible.

Over thousands of years, the chamber begins to shift.

New structures appear—not copies of the old pillar and platform and arch, but genuinely novel forms that serve purposes the power cannot immediately identify. Some channel heat more efficiently than the old spirals ever did. Some create sheltered spaces where new colonies can grow without competition. Some seem to have no function at all, elaborate crystalline filigrees that catch the orange light and scatter it in ways that make the whole chamber glow.

The power does not know if these are art or accident. It does not know if the colonies experience anything like satisfaction or wonder when they grow them. It does not even know if the colonies are, in any meaningful sense, aware of what they are doing.

But it knows this: the copying has changed. The colonies are no longer simply reproducing what came before. They are adding to it, elaborating, experimenting. The spirals on the pillar are still there, still being traced by new growth, but now there are variations—spirals that branch, spirals that reverse, spirals that interlock with other spirals to form structures the original builders never imagined.

The power has not given the colonies intelligence. It has not given them purpose or meaning or the capacity for joy or suffering. It has given them something smaller and perhaps more precious: the opportunity to surprise themselves.

---

# The Long View

The power does not stay to see how the experiment ends. It has other places to be, other forms of life to witness, other interventions to consider and mostly to refrain from. But it returns, now and then, when the weight of eons presses on it and it needs to be reminded that small things can matter.

Each time it visits, the chamber has changed.

The first few returns show the expected progression: more variation, more complexity, structures that grow increasingly elaborate and increasingly difficult to interpret. The colonies are clearly doing something with all this architecture, but the power cannot determine what. Perhaps they are simply growing; perhaps they are solving problems the power cannot perceive; perhaps they are engaged in something like a conversation, a negotiation conducted through form and pressure and the slow exchange of dissolved minerals.

Later visits show something unexpected: decline. Not death—the colonies never die—but a simplification. The elaborate filigrees disappear. The branching spirals straighten. The chamber begins to look more like it did when the power first found it, filled with dense masses of ordinary nodules pressing against one another in the dark.

The power worries, briefly, that it has broken something. That the variation it introduced was too much, that the colonies burned through their capacity for change and retreated into an older, safer pattern. But when it looks more closely, it sees that this is not quite right.

The simplification is selective. Some of the new structures have persisted—not the most elaborate ones, not the most novel, but the ones that worked best. The heat channels are more efficient than ever. The sheltered growing spaces have been refined and multiplied. The interlinked spirals have been incorporated into the basic copying process, so that even ordinary nodules now carry their pattern.

The colonies have not retreated. They have consolidated. They have taken what the power offered, tested it against the slow pressures of their existence, kept what served them, and let the rest dissolve back into the mineral flow.

The power finds this unexpectedly moving.

---

# The Final Gift

On what the power suspects may be its last visit—not because the chamber is failing, but because the power itself is beginning to fade, its attention diffusing across too many worlds over too many eons—it offers one more intervention.

It does not adjust the heat patterns this time. It does not introduce new elements or attempt communication. Instead, it simply opens a passage.

The chamber has been sealed for as long as the colonies have existed, but elsewhere in the spire there are other cavities, other pressure seams, other deposits of the minerals the colonies need. These spaces have remained empty because the colonies had no way to reach them. Now they do.

The passage is small—barely wide enough for a single nodule to squeeze through—and it is located high on the chamber wall, where only the most adventurous growth has reached. The power makes it look natural, a crack opened by thermal stress, the kind of thing that might have happened at any time but never did.

Then it withdraws, diffusing its attention back across the universe, letting the chamber fade from its perception like a dream half-remembered upon waking.

---

# What Happens Next

The power never learns what the colonies make of their new opportunity. Perhaps they ignore it; perhaps the crack remains empty for a million years while the nodules continue their slow business below. Perhaps a few colonies eventually find their way through and establish themselves in the new spaces, carrying their patterns with them, beginning the long process of filling and elaborating all over again.

Or perhaps something else happens—something the power, for all its wisdom, could never have predicted. Perhaps the colonies on the other side of the crack are different, having evolved in isolation under different pressures. Perhaps the meeting of the two groups produces conflict, or cooperation, or something that has no name in any language the power knows. Perhaps the colonies become something entirely new, something that will one day seal itself into chambers of its own making and wait for a different wise power to find it and wonder what it is.

The power does not know. It does not need to know. It gave what it could—not answers, not solutions, not salvation, but opportunity. The chance for something to happen that would not have happened otherwise. The rest is up to the colonies, and to time, and to the slow, patient chemistry of growth.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is genuinely wise: not the imposition of a better future, but the opening of a door.

# Coda

Somewhere deep in the flank of that wind-scoured spire, in a chamber sealed from light and filled with the dry whisper of shifting stone, a nodule colony grows toward a crack in the wall. It does not know the crack is there; it has no senses that could detect it. It simply grows, as it has always grown, following the heat and the pressure and the ancient copied patterns that tell it where to expand and where to contract.

But the patterns have changed, over the eons. They carry information they did not carry before—variations introduced by a wise power that has long since moved on, consolidated by the slow selection of what works and what does not. Among those variations is a tendency, slight but persistent, to grow upward when conditions allow. To seek empty spaces. To fill what is not yet filled.

The nodule reaches the crack. For a long moment—long even by the standards of this place—nothing happens. The colony presses against the edges of the opening, testing with slow dissolution and regrowth, exchanging silica with the empty air beyond.

Then, with a dry click almost too faint to hear, it contracts. Flattens. Squeezes through.

On the other side is a space it has never known. Cool at first, then warming as the colony metabolizes and begins to grow. There is room here—so much room. Bare walls of rose quartz and black tourmaline, unmarked, uncarved, waiting.

The colony begins to build.
