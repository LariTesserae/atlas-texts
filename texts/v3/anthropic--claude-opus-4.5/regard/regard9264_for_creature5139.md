# The Attending

The power that turned its attention to the plateau did not arrive. Arrival implies a before and after, a crossing of threshold, and this was not that. It simply began to attend—the way light attends to whatever it touches, neither choosing nor refusing.

What it perceived first was the completeness. Every surface answered every other surface. The moisture that gathered on stone at night released into air that carried it to leaf that transpired it back to the coolness that would gather it again on stone. The inhabitants moved through this circuit as water moves through it, as the pale birds moved through it, as the lizards blinked through it. Nothing was missing. Nothing exceeded.

The power held still within its attending, as one holds still when coming upon something sleeping.

---

It understood, in the way such powers understand, that this was not peace. Peace implies the memory of conflict, the relief of tension released. This was something else—a state that had never known its opposite, or had known it so long ago that the knowledge had worn away like the distinction between one generation and the next, until only the stillness remained, perfected by repetition, each moment a rehearsal of the moment before.

The inhabitants who had become indistinguishable from their ledges, whose eyes still opened when moisture gathered, whose lungs still drew the cool air and released it unchanged—they were not suffering. The power perceived this clearly. There was no ache in them, no longing, no reaching toward what they lacked. They lacked nothing. They had never, in any interval the power could perceive, lacked anything at all.

And yet.

The power attended to the fern that cast its shadow identically each noon, and to the shadow, and to the stone that received the shadow. It attended to the soft-feathered bird on the ridge crest that had not called in longer than the power cared to measure. It attended to the inhabitant who had spoken half a sentence three rains ago and who would return tomorrow to complete it, and to the one who would receive the completion and carry it, perfectly preserved, until the moment came to offer the next phrase in return.

Nothing was wrong. Everything was answered. And still the power attended, because something in the very completeness had called to it—not a cry, not a prayer, but a kind of pressure, the way deep water presses against whatever enters it.

---

The power considered, briefly, the forms that intervention might take.

It could introduce wind. A single gust, a breath, enough to drive the rain at an angle and wet the eastern faces of stones that had been dry since the uplift. The moisture films would shift. The ferns would lean. The birds might lift from their perches and, finding new currents, discover new perches. The inhabitants would feel the cool stirring along surfaces that had never been stirred, and something in them might—

Might what?

The power held the possibility without releasing it into the world.

It could introduce time—not more of it, but a different quality of it. A season. A single season of warmth that would dry the moss and crack the timber and force the inhabitants to move, to repair, to decide. They would have to distinguish between what could be saved and what must be abandoned. They would have to speak in sentences that completed themselves within a single morning because the morning itself was changing, because tomorrow's terrace might not be today's, because the gap in the foliage might close or widen before the next one came to read it.

But the power knew what season would cost them. The slow-blinking eyes would quicken. The cool stirring of speech would sharpen into urgency. The distinction between the living and the built would reassert itself, and they would have to learn again which was which, and the learning would be a wound that some of them would not survive.

And even then—even if they survived, even if they emerged into a world that changed—what would they become? Something new. Something that the power, attending to this completeness, could not foresee. Perhaps something richer. Perhaps something diminished. The power was wise enough to know that it could not know.

---

So the power did something else.

It attended more closely.

It turned its attention to a single inhabitant—one who had not yet grown into her ledge, whose limbs still moved with the considered hesitation of the long-legged insects, whose eyes still blinked in the rhythm of moisture rather than moss. She was resting on a terrace edge at the hour when the light fell directly overhead, and her shadow lay beneath her like a dark pool.

The power did not speak to her. It did not send a vision or a dream or a messenger. It simply attended to her with a quality of attention that was, itself, a kind of question.

Not *what do you lack*—she lacked nothing.

Not *what do you want*—she had never been taught to want.

Only: *what are you?*

The question had no words. It was not even a question, precisely. It was an attending so complete that it reflected her back to herself the way the still cistern water reflected the undersides of leaves.

She felt it the way she felt the cool air entering her lungs. Not as intrusion. Not as warmth or pressure or change. Only as a sudden, sourceless clarity—a sense that she was, in this moment, being precisely perceived.

Her eyes did not quicken. Her breath did not catch. But something in her—some interval that had never been occupied—opened.

---

The power waited.

Time passed in the manner time passed on the plateau: unmarked, unmeasured, identical to itself. Rains came and left. The inhabitant rose from her ledge and descended the stair-street to the lower plaza, where she received the final word of an exchange begun before her birth. She carried the word back up the terraces and held it, waiting for the one who would receive it from her.

But something had shifted.

When she rested now, she did not rest in perfect stillness. Her fingers moved—not restlessly, not with purpose, but with a quality of attention that had not been there before. She touched the moss in the joint beside her. She traced the edge of the slate where the moisture gathered. She lifted a fragment of shed lizard-scale and held it to the light, turning it, watching the way the light moved across its surface.

She was not building. She was not repairing. She was not distinguishing herself from the stone.

She was *noticing*.

The power attended to her noticing. It did not guide her. It did not suggest what she should notice next, or what the noticing might become. It only remained present, the way the light remained present, the way the moisture remained, holding the space in which her noticing could unfold.

---

Seasons did not come. The rain still fell straight down. The birds did not call. The ridges did not crumble.

But the inhabitant began to move differently through the intervals of the city.

She paused at junctions where others passed without pausing. She rested her hand against beams whose timber had begun to soften, feeling the give beneath her palm. She stood at the edge of the failed span, where the creeper had drawn its living handrail across the gap, and she looked at what had been abandoned—not to repair it, but to see it.

Others noticed her noticing. At first they simply adjusted their routes, the way they adjusted around any obstacle. But some of them—a few—began to pause where she had paused. Began to touch what she had touched. Began to look at what had always been there and had never, until now, been seen.

The exchanges of speech, which had spanned slow mornings and careful returns, began to shift. Not faster—the rhythm remained unhurried—but the words themselves changed. Where before an exchange might complete a thought that had been passed down through generations unchanged, now sometimes a speaker would offer a word that had no precedent. A new word. A word for the quality of light on a lizard-scale. A word for the softening of timber. A word for the interval between noticing something and understanding that you have noticed it.

The city did not rebuild itself. The moss continued to grow in the joints. The older inhabitants who had become indistinguishable from their ledges remained as they were, eyes opening when moisture gathered, lungs drawing and releasing the unchanged air.

But among the others—among those who had not yet grown into their stone—something was spreading. Not change, exactly. Not even the desire for change.

Only a quality of attention. A willingness to notice. An opening of intervals that had never been occupied.

---

The power, still attending, understood what it had done.

It had not given them wind or season. It had not taught them to want or to lack. It had not shown them that their completeness was a prison, because it was not certain their completeness was a prison, and a power that acts on uncertainty acts foolishly.

It had only asked a question without words. It had only attended so closely that one of them had seen herself being seen.

And that seeing had become, in her, a kind of seeing of her own. And that seeing had spread, as moisture spreads along the grain of stone, as moss spreads through the cracks, slowly, without force, occupying only the intervals that were open to receive it.

The power did not know what the plateau would become. Perhaps the noticing would fade, generation by generation, until the city returned to its perfect stillness and the same ferns cast the same shadows and the same words passed unchanged from speaker to speaker. Perhaps it would deepen, spreading until even the oldest inhabitants opened their moss-covered eyes and looked at the world as though it were new. Perhaps it would lead them, eventually, to build and repair and change and fail and grieve and begin again—all the turbulent gifts that the power had considered and declined to impose.

It could not know. It could only attend.

And so it remained—not watching over them, not guiding them, not waiting for any particular outcome. Only present. Only attending. A quality of light that fell on them as the ordinary light fell, neither choosing nor refusing, touching whatever it touched with the same patient, sourceless clarity.

The inhabitant who had first felt that attending was old now, her skin close to bone, the faint bloom of moss beginning at her wrists and ankles. She rested on her terrace edge at the hour when the light fell directly overhead. Below her, in the lower plaza, a young one was turning a fragment of something in the light, noticing the way the light moved.

The old one watched.

Her eyes did not quicken. Her breath did not catch. But something in her—some interval that had opened long ago and had never closed—recognized what she was seeing.

She had no word for it yet.

Perhaps, in time, one of them would make one.
