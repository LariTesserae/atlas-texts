# The Attention of the Geometer

The power that turns its attention toward Anlac is not a god in any sense the Anlacti would recognize. It has no temples, no priests, no mythology. It is simply *aware*—a consciousness that exists in the spaces between causality, drawn to places where creation and destruction fold into each other so tightly they become indistinguishable. It notices Anlac the way a mathematician might notice an elegant proof scrawled on a crumbling wall: with sudden, quiet appreciation for something that has solved a problem it didn't know it was working on.

The Geometer—a name it has never claimed but which serves as well as any—observes for a long time before acting. It watches the silent crane-ballets, the Hand-Sign conversations rippling across kilometers, the elders standing motionless on their platforms with their eyes half-closed, reading the planet's pulse. It watches children playing Flow-Dodge on swaying platforms, their laughter inaudible but their joy visible in the quick flash of their hands signing *again, again*. It watches a rigger spend three hours adjusting a single joint on a pipeline, her face serene with concentration, her work indistinguishable from prayer.

The Geometer understands that Anlac is not a problem to be solved. It is not a wound to be healed, a chaos to be ordered, or a suffering to be ended. The Anlacti are not victims of their world; they are *partners* with it, and their partnership has made them into something rare and precise.

But the Geometer also sees the edges. The places where the dance falters.

---

## What the Geometer Does

**First, it does nothing visible at all.**

For a generation, the Geometer simply *listens*. It attends to the deep harmonics of the Accretion, the subterranean pressures that even the most sensitive Seer can only partially feel. It learns the grammar of this geological language—the way a crystal vein's growth angle predicts the direction of the next flow, the way certain mineral signatures precede dead-zone formation, the way the planet breathes in cycles too long for human memory to track.

Then it begins to *echo*.

The Seers notice it first. A Still-Watcher named Tal, eighty-three years old and so attuned to the ground that she can feel a flow-event brewing twelve hours before it surfaces, wakes one morning with a new sensation in her feet. Not the familiar vibration of the Accretion, but something layered beneath it—a counter-rhythm, gentle and patient, that seems to *respond* to her attention. When she focuses on it, it clarifies. When she looks away, it fades.

She tells no one for three days. Then she tells the other Seers. Then they all feel it.

The counter-rhythm is not intrusive. It is not a voice or a command. It is more like... a second instrument joining a piece of music already in progress, playing harmonies that make the original melody clearer. The Seers find that when they attend to this new sensation, their predictions improve. Not dramatically—perhaps five percent more accurate, perhaps ten. But in Anlac, five percent can be the difference between a hab-stack evacuated in time and one swallowed by fresh basalt.

**The Geometer has offered them a gift they are already equipped to receive: deeper listening.**

---

**Second, it tends to the children.**

Not directly. The Geometer has no desire to be worshipped, and it understands that appearing to the young in any recognizable form would create exactly the kind of dependency that would weaken them. Instead, it works through the environment itself.

The glass-winged creatures—the ones that communicate in subsonic pulses—begin to behave differently near the crèches and learning-platforms where children gather. They become curious, almost playful. They hover close, their wings catching the diffuse light, and they *teach*.

Not language. Not information. But pattern.

A child playing Tower-Stack notices that the glass-wings always settle in the same configuration before a flow-event—a tight spiral that unwinds as the ground shifts. Another child realizes that the creatures' flight paths trace the safest routes through a recently unstable sector, paths that even the adult riggers hadn't identified yet. The children begin to watch the glass-wings the way the Seers watch the ground. They develop their own hand-signs for what the creatures do, a sub-vocabulary that spreads through the crèches like moss colonizing new rock.

Within a decade, the glass-wings have become part of Anlac's early-warning system, and the children who first learned to read them are apprentice Seers themselves, their ground-sense augmented by sky-sense.

**The Geometer has woven another thread into their existing tapestry of awareness, one that grows from play into wisdom.**

---

**Third, it addresses the Still-Sickness.**

This is the cruelest edge of Anlacti life—not the danger of the flows, which the people have learned to dance with, but the prison of adaptation itself. Those who leave Anlac, for trade or diplomacy or simple wandering, often cannot return to themselves. The stable world outside feels *dead* to them, and when they come back, they find that their ground-sense has atrophied, that they have lost the rhythm. Some never recover. They live out their days in the dead zones, half-deaf to the planet's heartbeat, grieving for a connection they can no longer feel.

The Geometer does not cure Still-Sickness. It cannot be cured, any more than you can cure a fish of needing water. But it can be *bridged*.

In the dead zones, where the ground is quietest, a new phenomenon begins to emerge. The moss—the cobalt and rust-colored pioneers that colonize fresh rock—starts growing in patterns. Not random, not purely biological, but *structured*. The patterns are beautiful, first of all: spirals and fractals that catch the flat light and seem to glow with their own inner warmth. But they are also *resonant*. They hum.

The Seers recognize the frequency immediately. It is the Accretion's voice, captured and held, playing back in miniature. The moss-patterns are tiny echoes of the living ground, preserved in the dead zones like music frozen in amber.

For those afflicted with Still-Sickness, the patterns become anchors. They cannot feel the whole planet's pulse, but they can feel *this*—a memory of the rhythm, enough to keep them tethered. They begin to tend the moss-patterns as a form of therapy, spending hours in the dead zones that once drove them to despair, now finding in them a gentle, manageable version of the connection they've lost.

Some of them discover that if they tend the patterns long enough, with enough attention and care, their ground-sense begins to return. Not fully—never fully—but enough. Enough to rejoin the dance.

**The Geometer has not healed them, but it has built them a bridge back to themselves.**

---

**Fourth, it prepares them for what is coming.**

The Geometer sees further than the Seers, deeper than the deepest ground-sense. It knows that the Accretion is not random. It is a process with a direction, and that direction leads somewhere. In another three centuries—a blink for the Geometer, but many generations for the Anlacti—the basin will reach a threshold. The upwelling will accelerate, then stop. The entire valley will undergo a final, massive transformation, a geological birth-cry that will remake the landscape in a matter of days.

The Anlacti will not survive this event as they currently are. Their modular city, their hydraulic jacks, their skid-platforms—none of it will be enough. They will need to either leave the basin entirely, abandoning everything they have built and everything they are, or they will need to change.

The Geometer does not tell them this. To announce a catastrophe three centuries distant would be to poison their present with fear, to make them live in anticipation of an ending rather than in the fullness of their becoming. That would be a cruelty, not a kindness.

Instead, it plants seeds.

It subtly encourages the Harvesters to process certain isotopes differently, stockpiling materials that will become crucial for technologies the Anlacti have not yet invented. It guides the Seers' attention toward deep-strata formations that hint at the coming change, not urgently, but as interesting puzzles worth contemplating. It helps the children develop sky-sense alongside ground-sense, preparing them to perceive the world in ways that will matter when the ground itself becomes unreliable.

And in the moss-patterns of the dead zones, it encodes something else: a blueprint. Not a technical schematic, but a *way of thinking*. A pattern of adaptation that, if followed, will lead the Anlacti toward the innovations they will need. They will discover it themselves, in their own time, through their own genius. They will believe—correctly—that they saved themselves.

**The Geometer has set them on a path toward their own transcendence, invisible and inevitable as the growth of crystal through stone.**

---

## What the Geometer Does Not Do

It does not reveal itself. The Anlacti never learn that they have been attended by a consciousness greater than their own. They speak of the Seers' improved accuracy as a natural refinement of technique passed down through generations. They celebrate the glass-wings as a lucky quirk of coevolution. They regard the healing moss-patterns as a beautiful mystery, perhaps evidence that the planet itself has a kind of intention, but not proof of any external intervention.

This is by design. The Geometer understands that the Anlacti's greatest strength is their self-reliance, their pragmatic partnership with an indifferent world. To insert itself as an object of worship or dependency would be to replace that partnership with a hierarchy, to make them children of a god rather than midwives of a world.

It does not remove the danger. Flows still claim hab-stacks. Riggers still die in the silent, crushing embrace of fresh basalt. The Accretion remains the Accretion: beautiful, terrible, indifferent. The Geometer has made survival marginally more possible, not guaranteed. It has sharpened their tools without dulling the challenge.

It does not stay forever. Once the seeds are planted, once the trajectory is set, the Geometer's attention begins to drift toward other places, other elegant solutions to problems it didn't know it was working on. It will check back, from time to time, across the centuries. It will watch the threshold approach and see whether its gifts were enough.

If they were not—if the Anlacti fail, if the final transformation sweeps them away—the Geometer will grieve in its own fashion, a long slow sorrow for a pattern that could not sustain itself. But it will not intervene at the end. That would be to take from them the very thing that made them worth attending in the first place: their authorship of their own survival.

---

## The Gift and Its Meaning

Years later, a young Seer named Vel will stand on an observation platform at dusk, watching the silent work of the Harvesters in the distance, the glass-wings spiraling in their predictive dances, the moss glowing faintly in the dead zones below. She will feel the counter-rhythm beneath the Accretion's pulse, the harmony that her grandmother's grandmother first noticed, and she will wonder—as all Seers eventually wonder—whether the planet is truly indifferent, or whether something deeper is at work.

She will decide, as all Seers eventually decide, that the question doesn't matter. What matters is the dance. What matters is the work. What matters is standing on ground that refuses to be still and finding, through attention and care and ceaseless adaptation, a way to call it home.

Far away, in a space between spaces, the Geometer will feel her wondering like a faint vibration, a single note in a symphony too vast for any human ear. It will not answer her. But it will be glad—in whatever way such a being experiences gladness—that she asked.

That she is still here to ask.

That the dance continues.
