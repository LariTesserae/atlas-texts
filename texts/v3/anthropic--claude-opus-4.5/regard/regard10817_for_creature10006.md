# The Attending

The power that turns its attention here is not what mortals imagine when they speak of gods.

It has no throne, no form that could be rendered in copper or carved in stone. It exists in the way that mathematics exists, in the way that the tendency of water to seek the lowest point exists—a coherent presence woven through the substrate of what is. If it has a name, the name is something like *the Patient Regard*, or perhaps *the Holding*, though these are approximations in the way that "blue" approximates the color of distance.

It notices the plateau the way a gardener notices a single determined shoot pressing up through gravel.

Not because the shoot is rare—life is everywhere, persistent, finding purchase in every crevice of existence—but because there is something in the quality of this particular striving that invites witness. The power perceives the whole of it in a single apprehension: the raw young stone still settling into its own weight, the fog breathing up from nowhere, the silent herds moving in their careful lines, and threaded through it all, the people—compact, fur-backed, climbing before they walk, teaching their children to fall well.

It perceives their shrines. The small cairns decorated with lichen. The offerings placed at dawn. Their belief that the land dreams.

*They are not wrong*, the power understands. The plateau is young in the way that all matter is young—still remembering the violence that made it, still cooling from the long heat of becoming. The people sense this. They move carefully because they know, in whatever way knowing happens for them, that the ground beneath their feet is not yet finished deciding what it wants to be.

The power considers.

---

There is a question that any benevolent presence must ask when it turns its attention toward those who suffer and endure: *What help would not be harm?*

The people of the ledges have built something. Not just their stacked cities, their rope-bridges, their bone pulleys—though these are marvels, ingenuity pressed from necessity like oil from stone. They have built a way of being. Their trust is the first law because it must be. Their songs are murmured because the land demands quiet. Their dead go into deep crevices and their memories are worked into rope because this is how continuity happens in a place where nothing can be wasted, where everything must be stitched into everything else.

To give them sudden warmth would unravel the fabric of their closeness. To flatten their cliffs into easy plains would erase the vertical thinking that makes them who they are. To remove the cold would leave them with fur they no longer need, with bodies shaped for a world that no longer exists.

The power knows this.

It has seen—across the long unfurling of time, across worlds beyond counting—what happens when gifts arrive without understanding. The recipients are not ungrateful; they are simply *changed*, in ways they did not choose, toward shapes that fit neither who they were nor who they might have become. A people given everything they need rarely become what they might have been. They become, instead, what the gift makes them.

And yet.

The power also sees the child who falls. Not metaphorically—*specifically*, a child, three days from now, whose foot will find a patch of ice-slicked stone on a traverse her family has used safely for six generations. The child will fall well, as all of them learn to fall, but the shelf below is too far, and the one below that is too narrow, and she will strike and slide and her mother will hear the sound from two ledges above and will carry that sound inside her for the rest of her life.

The power sees the winter that will come in eleven years, longer and harder than any in living memory, when the fog will freeze instead of merely chill, when three of the cliff-cities will lose a quarter of their people, and the survivors will huddle together and wonder if they have somehow offended the dreaming stone.

It sees the slow failure of the copper seams, seven generations hence, and the conflicts that will arise when tools become scarce.

It sees, too, the triumphs—the child who will grow up to devise a new way of reading the moods of fog, whose system will save hundreds of lives; the lovers who will shelter close by a firepit and whose great-great-grandchildren will be the first to descend from the plateau and carry the people's wisdom into other lands; the old one whose songs, murmured in the long dark, will be remembered and repeated for a thousand years.

All of this is held in the power's single apprehension. Suffering and flourishing. Loss and continuation. The weave of what might be.

---

What does it do?

It does what patience does. It *attends*.

---

First, the small things. Not interventions, not miracles—adjustments. The calibration of what already is toward what it was always tending to become.

There is a rockfall waiting to happen on the eastern face of the third great city. The stone there is stressed, cracked along a line that will give way in the next hard freeze. Seventeen people live in the dwellings below. The power does not prevent the rockfall—the land is young, and must be allowed to settle, and the people know this, and their knowing is part of their wisdom. But the timing shifts, imperceptibly. The stone will give way at midday, when the families below are gathered at the market terrace for the weekly exchange. No one will be home. They will return to rubble and will mourn the loss of their things, their carved talismans, the rope their grandmother wove. But they will live. And they will rebuild in a place that is more stable, because the rockfall will have shown them where not to build.

This is the nature of the power's first gift: *the grace of survivable lessons*.

---

There is a woman—old, now, her fingers no longer nimble enough for the finest rope-work—who sits each evening at the edge of her family's ledge and watches the slow march of clouds across the great breadth. She has noticed something. The fog rises differently before the hardest cold. There is a quality to it, a weight, a way it catches the light at a particular angle she cannot quite describe. She has tried to tell others, but the words slip from her grasp, and she is old, and the young ones nod politely and do not truly hear.

The power does not give her the words. Instead, it arranges a moment.

Her granddaughter—the one with the clever hands, the one who asks too many questions—comes to sit beside her on an evening when the fog is doing exactly the thing the old woman has been trying to describe. The granddaughter, who has been quarreling with her mother and seeking an excuse to be elsewhere, looks out at the world with the unfocused attention of adolescent irritation.

"Look," says the old woman, and gestures toward the fog.

The granddaughter does not want to look. But she does, because she loves her grandmother, and because the alternative is going back inside.

And the fog, at that precise moment, catches the light in a way that the old woman has seen a hundred times but the granddaughter has never seen before.

"It's different," the granddaughter says, startled out of her sulk.

"Yes," says the old woman. "Before the hard cold, it's always different."

And now there are two who know. And the granddaughter is young, and has clever hands, and will live another fifty years, and will teach her children, and their children, and the knowledge will root and branch and spread.

This is the nature of the power's second gift: *the creation of moments where wisdom can pass*.

---

But the power does more than nudge rockfall and arrange conversations. It also, quietly, begins to *dream alongside the land*.

The people believe the plateau still dreams, and they are not wrong. The stone is young, still remembering. Still deciding. The power does not *change* the dream—it is not that kind of presence, and even if it were, the dream is not its to change. But it attends the dream, settles into the space between what is and what will be, and offers something like companionship to the vast slow thoughts of the waking stone.

*You are not alone*, the power offers. *The small ones who walk upon you are not parasites, not erosion, not the beginning of your wearing-down. They are something new. You are young enough to grow with them, to shape yourself around their shaping of you. This can be a collaboration.*

The stone does not answer in words. Stone has no words. But there is a quality to the next earthquake—a small one, a settling-tremor—that is somehow *gentler* than the tremors before. As if the land, for the first time, is considering the weight of the small lives clustered along its ridges.

The people feel it. They do not understand what has changed, but their priests—the keepers of the small cairns, the ones who place the offerings at dawn—begin to speak of a shift in the mood of the dreaming. They say the land seems more settled. More willing to be lived upon.

They are not wrong.

---

And then, the hardest thing.

The child who will fall.

The power sees her—small, quick-footed, braver than she should be, with the wide opal-green eyes of her people and a laugh that is giddy and close and will be silenced in three days unless something changes.

It could prevent the fall. It could harden the ice into roughness, give her foot purchase where otherwise there would be none. It could shift her attention at the crucial moment, make her look somewhere other than the traverse, choose a different path.

But the power pauses, and considers, and knows that every intervention has a weight.

If the child does not fall, she will grow up fearless. Too fearless. She will take risks that others would not, and her luck will hold for years, but there will come a day when it does not, and on that day she will not know how to fall well, because she will never have fallen badly, and the fall that finally claims her will be longer and harder and will take others with her—the young ones who followed her example, who learned from her that the ledges were safer than they are.

The power sees this thread, and the threads beyond it, and the threads beyond those.

It could still prevent the fall. Accept the cascade of consequences, trust that it could attend to each of them in turn, weave an endless tapestry of small interventions that would keep this child and all who followed her from ever learning the hard lessons that the ledges teach.

But that way lies something other than help. That way lies a people who are shaped not by their land but by a presence they cannot see, who become dependent on a grace they do not understand, whose wisdom is not their own but borrowed, whose children never learn to fall because they are never allowed to.

The power *grieves*.

This is the part that the stories never tell. That benevolence is not the absence of sorrow but its presence, held alongside the understanding that some sorrows cannot be prevented without creating greater sorrows in their place. That wisdom is not knowing how to make everything right but knowing how to choose which wrongs to allow.

The child will fall. The mother will hear the sound. The grief will be real and terrible and will change everyone it touches.

But.

The power looks again. Looks at the path of the falling, the geometry of ledges and void, the physics of small bodies and hard stone.

There is a shelf. The child will miss it—her trajectory, her spin, the particular chaos of her tumbling—but only barely. If she were to fall *differently*, not better but differently, her arm would catch the edge instead of sliding past it, and she would break the arm badly, and the pain would be immense, and she would learn forever after to fear the ice-slicked stone, but she would *live*.

The power cannot change the ice. That would be too much, would start it down the path of endless intervention. But it can be present at the moment of the fall, can exist in the space where chance and physics meet, can offer the smallest possible weight toward one outcome rather than another.

Not control. Not prevention. Just *presence*, at the moment when presence might matter.

The child falls. 

Her arm catches the edge. 

She screams, and does not fall further, and hangs there with her broken arm hooked over stone until her family reaches her with ropes and cries and desperate hands.

She will never climb carelessly again. She will teach her children, with the arm that never healed quite right, why the ledges demand respect. And her mother will carry a different sound inside her—a scream, not a silence—and will hold her daughter so tightly that night that neither of them can breathe, and will say *you are here, you are here, you are here*.

This is the nature of the power's hardest gift: *the transformation of tragedy into wounding, and wounding into wisdom*.

---

Seasons pass. Years. Generations.

The power does not withdraw its attention—it is not that kind of presence, and attention is not a finite resource that must be hoarded. But it becomes less active over time. The adjustments are fewer. The dreams it shares with the land grow quieter, more spacious, requiring less interpretation.

Because the people are learning.

They always were, of course. That is what people do. But now their learning has a certain quality—a rightness, a sense that the path they are walking is the path they were always meant to walk. The old woman's granddaughter has become a grandmother herself, and the system she devised for reading the fog has been refined across three generations, and now there are whole families who specialize in weather-reading, who sit at the edges of ledges at dawn and dusk and interpret the moods of the air.

The cities have grown. Not outward—there is no outward on a plateau this vast, only the slow spread of population across cliff-faces and terraces—but *inward*, in complexity and connection. The bone-whistles have developed into a full language, a way of sending not just signals but stories from one ledge to another. The rope-craft has advanced to the point where children are now born in hammocks suspended over void, rocked by the wind itself, learning before their first breath that emptiness is not enemy but element.

And the land—the dreaming land, the young stone still deciding what it wants to be—has settled into something that is not quite peace but is perhaps *acceptance*. The earthquakes are fewer. The rockfalls more predictable. As if the plateau, too, has learned something from the small lives that cling to its ridges.

---

The power, attending all of this, feels something that is not quite satisfaction but resembles it the way a mountain resembles a stone.

It has not saved the people. They did not need saving. What it has done is *witness*—actively, attentively, with all the weight that witnessing can carry when the witness is vast enough and patient enough and wise enough to know when to act and when to refrain.

It has grieved their dead and celebrated their children. It has dreamed with their land and spoken, in whatever wordless way such speaking happens, with the stone that holds them. It has nudged the timings of rockfall and arranged the moments where wisdom could pass, and once—only once, at the very edge of what it permits itself—it has been present at a child's falling and offered the smallest possible weight toward life.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is not performance. Not rescue, not transformation, not the remaking of a people into something other than what they are. Just the steady, patient, endless *attention* of something that cares—not because caring is useful, but because caring is what it is, and these small lives in their cold cities are worth the caring.

---

At the edge of the plateau, where the land falls away into distances too vast to comprehend, a woman stands at dawn with her offering in her hands. A trickle of water. A curl of moss. A warm stone she has held against her chest all night.

She places them at her family's cairn and murmurs the words her grandmother taught her, the words her grandmother's grandmother taught, back and back beyond memory.

"We are here," she says. "We are small. We ask for nothing but the chance to endure."

And from somewhere—not above, not outside, not from any direction that could be named—there is an answer. Not in words, not in signs, not in any form that could be pointed to or described. Just a sense, pressing gently against the edges of her awareness, that she has been heard.

That something vast and patient knows she is there.

That she is not alone on the dreaming stone.

She does not know what it is. She will not speak of it, because there are no words, and because some things are cheapened by speaking. But she will carry it with her as she climbs back toward her family, toward the morning's work, toward the small daily negotiations of life on the ledge.

And tonight, when she tells her children the stories, she will add nothing new. She will say what has always been said: that the land dreams, that they must be gentle, that trust is the first law, that everything must be supported.

But there will be something in how she says it—a certainty, a warmth—that her children will remember without understanding.

And they will carry it forward.

And the power, attending, will hold them all: the living and the dead and those not yet born, the stone and the fog and the small green things that cling to the crevices. It will hold them not as a hand holds but as a story holds, as a song holds, as the memory of warmth holds against the cold.

It will not leave.

It cannot leave.

This is what it is.

*The Patient Regard.*

*The Holding.*

*The one who attends.*

---

And far below, in a crevice where no light reaches, a child's bones rest beside a grandmother's bones beside bones so old that no one remembers whose they were. The power knows each of them. Holds each of them. Grieves each of them still.

This too is what it means to attend.

Not to fix. Not to solve. Not to save.

Just to *be there*, fully and completely, for as long as being there is possible.

Which, for this power, is forever.
