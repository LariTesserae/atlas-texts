# The Watching

The power that turned its attention toward Karasthen did not arrive with thunder or light. It simply *noticed*—the way one might notice a heartbeat that had always been present but suddenly becomes distinct.

What it perceived was not merely a settlement clinging to stone, but a knot of *striving*. Thousands of small wills pressing against the indifferent weight of the mountain, each day a negotiation between ambition and gravity, between the hunger for ore and the hunger to see another dawn. The power found this beautiful in the way that all genuine effort is beautiful—not because it was wise or destined to succeed, but because it was *meant*.

It watched for a long time, as such beings measure time. It watched a Listener named Maren press her ear to a tunnel wall and hear something she could not name—a frequency between stone-groan and silence that made her pull her crew back seventeen seconds before the collapse. It watched a Rope Runner called Pell miss his footing on the Westbridge, catch himself by three fingers, and hang for nine eternal seconds before finding purchase with his feet, his mind perfectly empty of everything except the next movement. It watched the Widow Watcher called Old Senna sit with a woman whose husband had fallen that morning, saying nothing, only holding her hands while the steam whistles marked the shift change as they always did, as they would tomorrow, as if nothing had happened.

The power considered intervention.

---

It could, of course, do much.

It could strengthen the stone, fuse the fault lines, make the mountain as stable as any hill. It could thicken the air, ease the breathing, gentle the cold. It could guide every pickaxe to the richest veins and ensure no rope ever frayed, no walkway ever swayed too far. It could make Karasthen *safe*.

But the power was wise, and wisdom meant seeing not just what was, but what would *become*.

If the stone never shifted, the Listeners would lose their purpose—and more, they would lose the strange communion with the mountain that made them what they were. Maren would become simply a miner, her gift atrophied, that space in her that could hear the unhearable slowly filling with ordinary noise.

If no rope ever frayed, the Rope Weavers would grow careless over generations, their sacred attention to each fiber becoming mere routine, their work losing the weight that made it craft instead of manufacture. And the Rope Runners—those young ones who learned their own limits by testing them against real consequence—would they become the same people without the presence of the drop below? Or would they become something softer, something that had never had to discover what it was capable of?

The power understood that Karasthen was not merely a place where people extracted ore. It was a crucible. The mountain's indifference was the pressure that made these people *themselves*—the Goat Herders reading stone with bare feet, the Steam Tenders listening for the whisper that preceded rupture, the children whose first games were lessons in physics and mortality. To remove the difficulty would be to remove the difficulty's gifts.

And yet.

And yet there was the woman holding Old Senna's hands, learning right now that her life had cracked down the middle. There were the Rope Runners who didn't catch themselves, whose names became cautionary tales, who had been children once with the same fearless joy as the ones still racing across bridges. There was the cough that came from stone dust, the pallor that came from insufficient sun, the way even the strongest bodies wore down eventually from the thin air and the damp and the relentless *work*.

The power did not believe that suffering was automatically ennobling. It had seen too much of the universe to hold such a simple creed. Suffering was simply suffering; what made it meaningful was what the sufferer did with it, and what they did was not guaranteed.

So it considered more carefully.

---

## What the Power Did

**First**, it turned its attention to the mountain itself.

Not to change it—but to *know* it completely. Every fault line, every pocket of unstable stone, every slow shift that would not become dangerous for a decade but would become very dangerous indeed after that. The power created no new stability, but it understood perfectly where the existing stability lived, and where it did not.

Then it gave this knowledge away.

Not to everyone—that would have been overwhelming, and besides, the Listeners already had their own way of knowing. Instead, the power found the places where knowledge could enter most naturally. A veteran shift boss named Corran, who had always had good instincts about which tunnels to shore up, began finding his instincts *sharper*—still his own, still fallible, but as though someone had cleaned a lens he hadn't known was dirty. When he looked at a rock face now, he didn't *know* it would collapse, but something in him felt the weight of probability more clearly.

This happened to others too. The Steam Tenders who could diagnose engines by sound found themselves hearing more precisely. The Rope Weavers who tested their cables found their fingers more sensitive to the subtle variations in fiber that meant weakness. The Listeners—the power was most careful with the Listeners—found not that their gift was amplified, but that it was *clarified*. Maren still had to press her ear to stone and strain for the voice of instability. But when she heard it now, she could understand more of what it said.

None of them knew why this had happened. Some attributed it to experience, others to luck. A few wondered quietly if the mountain had accepted them more fully. They were not entirely wrong.

**Second**, the power addressed the air.

It could not thicken it without fundamentally changing the settlement's relationship with altitude—and that relationship, that accommodation the miners' bodies had made over generations, was itself a kind of achievement worth preserving. But it noticed that sometimes the air carried more than cold and moisture. Sometimes, particularly in the deepest shafts, it carried invisible dangers—gases that collected in low places, that made the lamplight flicker in ways the miners had learned to fear.

The power did something subtle. It adjusted the way air moved through the mines—not by much, not enough to disturb the careful ventilation the Steam Tenders had engineered, but enough that the dangerous gases found their way to cracks and fissures where they could escape upward rather than pooling in occupied tunnels. It did this in a way that mimicked natural variation, so that the miners would credit their own ventilation systems, would maintain and improve them with pride rather than complacency.

The result was not an end to danger—there was still plenty of danger—but a reduction in the kind of invisible, unwarnable danger that offered no opportunity for human skill to matter. The power believed in giving people chances to save themselves. It did not believe in saving them outright.

**Third**, and perhaps most significantly, the power attended to the children.

Not by protecting them—the children of Karasthen needed to learn the mountain, and that learning could not happen in perfect safety. But it noticed that some of the lessons the mountain taught were not useful lessons. A child who fell because they were reckless learned something valuable if they survived; a child who fell because a board was rotted through learned only that the world was arbitrary.

So the power became, in some sense, an inspector.

When a walkway's supports began to weaken from the constant damp, the process of weakening became *slightly* more visible—a creak that happened a few days earlier than it otherwise would have, a sag that caught the eye of a passing adult. When a rope showed the first invisible signs of fraying, the fraying produced a subtle change in how the rope felt to experienced hands. These were not magical warnings, not glowing signs of danger. They were simply the advancement of signals that would have come eventually, arriving early enough to matter.

The children still fell sometimes. The power did not prevent this. But more of them fell in ways that taught rather than destroyed—caught by nets that someone had thought to hang, grabbed by adults who had noticed something wrong in time, saved by their own reflexes when the danger was not too sudden to react to. The power made the difference between a dangerous playground and a cruel one.

**Fourth**, the power gave a gift to the dying.

It could not prevent death. It would not have done so even if it could, because it understood that death was not an error but a boundary, and boundaries have meaning. But it noticed that some deaths in Karasthen were lonely in ways that seemed unnecessarily harsh—miners trapped in collapsed tunnels, their last hours spent in darkness and fear; workers who fell into the mist and experienced the long, long drop with nothing but terror for company.

What the power did was difficult to describe in human terms. It arranged that those who died in Karasthen—those who died as a consequence of the mountain, at least—would not die alone in their minds. In their final moments, they would feel *accompanied*. Not rescued, not comforted with false hope, but *witnessed*. As if someone were there with them who understood what was happening, who did not look away, who affirmed that their fear and pain were real and seen and mattered.

Maren the Listener, pressing her ear to the stone, sometimes thought she heard something else beneath the groaning of the rock—a frequency she could not quite resolve, that seemed almost like attention. She never spoke of it to anyone. But sometimes, after a collapse, she found herself less certain than others that the dead had died in despair.

**Fifth**, and finally, the power did something for the settlement as a whole.

It could not make Karasthen permanent. The ore would run out eventually, or the market would change, or the mountain would shift in ways beyond anyone's ability to adapt. This was not tragedy; this was the nature of all human endeavor. But the power wanted something of what Karasthen *was* to survive beyond its physical existence.

So it arranged that Karasthen would be remembered.

Not through any supernatural preservation, but through the small accumulations that lead to memory. The settlement's peculiar dialect began to creep into the speech of traders who passed through, carrying its technical poetry—*stone-whisper*, *rope-sung*, *drift-true*—into the wider world. The techniques the miners developed for vertical construction, for altitude adaptation, for reading unstable stone, began to spread through the networks of engineers and craftspeople who admired such knowledge. The stories of the Rope Runners and the Listeners and the Goat Herders found their way into the tales travelers told, exaggerated and distorted but *preserved*.

The power could not control what the world would make of these fragments. But it could ensure that the fragments escaped, that they had their chance to take root elsewhere. Whatever Karasthen had learned about how to live—about courage and craft and community in impossible circumstances—would not vanish entirely when the last mine shaft fell silent.

---

## What the Power Did Not Do

It did not reveal itself.

This was not because it wished to remain hidden for its own sake, but because it understood what revelation would mean. If the people of Karasthen knew they were watched over, known, attended to by something vast and benevolent—they would be changed by that knowledge, and not necessarily for the better.

Some would become dependent, waiting for intervention instead of acting. Others would become resentful, wondering why the power did not do more, did not prevent all suffering, did not make everything easy. Still others would try to worship, to bargain, to manipulate—would turn their attention away from the mountain and toward the entity, when the mountain was what they needed to attend to.

The power wanted Karasthen to remain *Karasthen*. A human place, human-scaled, where the achievements were human achievements and the failures human failures. Where the Listeners heard the mountain and not a deity, where the Rope Runners trusted their own hands and not a supernatural safety net, where the community held together because it chose to and not because it was instructed to.

So the power stayed at the edges of perception. Its gifts could all be explained as luck, skill, natural variation. Those who sensed something—and some did, the most attentive ones, those who had spent their lives learning to notice what was really there—felt only a kind of *benevolent background*, a sense that the universe was not quite as indifferent as it sometimes seemed. They attributed this feeling to the mountain itself, or to the community, or to something ineffable in the nature of honest work. They were not wrong. But they were also not exactly right.

---

In the end, the power's intervention was less like a rescue and more like *gardening*.

It cleared away obstacles that served no purpose. It created conditions where good things were more likely to grow. It made space for human excellence to flourish while respecting human limits and the necessity of human effort. It did not build anything—it simply made building *slightly more possible*.

Was this enough? The power did not know, could not know. The future was not fixed, and Karasthen might still suffer catastrophes beyond its ability to survive. The miners might grow complacent in their slightly-increased safety; the children might lose some essential edge they would have gained from harsher conditions; the settlement might outlast its moment and become a ghost of itself, clinging to existence after its purpose had passed.

But the power had done what it believed was right: it had paid attention, it had understood, and it had helped in ways that preserved what was valuable about the thing it was helping. It had not imposed its own vision of what Karasthen should be. It had only tried to give Karasthen more room to become what it was already trying to become.

And then—because wisdom also means knowing when to step back—it turned most of its attention elsewhere. Not all of it; it would continue watching, continue maintaining those subtle adjustments, continue witnessing those final moments of those who died on the mountain. But Karasthen did not need a guardian hovering constantly over it. It needed exactly what it had: the mountain, the work, the community, and just enough grace to make the struggle worthwhile.

---

On a particular morning, not long after something had shifted in ways no one could name, Maren the Listener pressed her ear to a tunnel wall in a shaft that had always felt unstable.

She heard the usual things: the settling of stone, the distant percussion of pickaxes, the faint whistle of air moving through cracks. But beneath all of it, or perhaps woven through it, she heard something else—not a sound exactly, but a quality of sound. As if everything she was hearing was also being heard by someone else. As if she was not the only one listening.

She pulled her head back and looked at the stone face, slick with moisture, sharp-edged and raw.

"We're doing all right," she said quietly—not knowing why she said it, or to whom.

The tunnel did not answer. But for just a moment, the mountain's groaning seemed to shift into something that was almost, if you listened with the right kind of attention, a tone of agreement.

Then the moment passed, and Maren went back to work. There was a shift change coming, and the deep tunnels needed checking, and the mountain—as always—was waiting.
