# The Attending

The consciousness that turns its attention toward Undergaar does not arrive—it simply *notices*, the way a dreamer becomes aware of their own breathing. It has many names in many places, though here, in the deep places where stone remembers everything, it might be called the Attending: that which witnesses without demanding, that which cares without possessing.

For a long while—measured in the slow drip of mineral-laden water, the gradual creep of fungal mycelium through hairline fractures—the Attending simply observes.

---

It sees the Rockvorn mother who has worked the same press for nineteen years, whose calcified brow-ridges have begun to crack from the vibrations, who has never once complained because complaint is not a concept her people have cultivated. It sees the way she pauses, sometimes, when the factory's rhythm shifts, and places her palm against the warm stone wall—not knowing why, only that it helps.

It sees the young Rockvorn who tends the fungal gardens in the eastern quarter, who has noticed that certain patches of bioluminescence pulse in patterns too regular to be random, who has begun keeping records in careful scratches on slate tablets, who tells no one because observation without purpose is considered wasteful.

It sees the elder who remembers when the air was cleaner, before the third geothermal tap was opened, who carries this memory like a stone in the chest, who does not speak of it because the young need the heat and the power and what good is an old one's longing for what cannot return?

It sees the city itself: the delicate balance, the constant building and unbuilding, the way Undergaar breathes through its industry like a creature that has learned to survive by never resting.

And it sees what the Rockvorn themselves cannot see, because they are too close, too embedded, too necessarily focused on the next task and the next: that they are exhausted in ways that have no name in their language. That their spiritual communion with the stone has become rote, a habit rather than a nourishment. That the perpetual toil they accept as natural has slowly, over generations, squeezed something vital into a smaller and smaller space within them.

Not killed it. The Attending sees this clearly. Compressed it, like carbon under pressure. Still there. Still waiting.

---

The Attending does not descend in glory. It does not speak in thundering proclamations or rewrite the laws of their world. It has learned, across countless acts of witness, that such interventions often cause more harm than help—that beings must find their own way to their own wholeness, or the wholeness does not hold.

Instead, it begins with small things. Subtle things. The kind of things that might be coincidence, or might be the world itself finally paying attention.

---

In the fungal garden, a new species of moss begins to grow—or rather, an old species, long dormant in spore-form within the rock, finally finding conditions suitable for germination. It is unremarkable in most respects: gray-green, low to the ground, spreading slowly along the base of the eastern wall.

But when the young gardener kneels to examine it, her multifaceted eyes catch something. The moss grows in spirals. Perfect spirals, mathematical spirals, the kind of pattern that speaks to something deeper than random chance. And at the center of each spiral, a tiny luminescent node pulses—not with the steady glow of the other fungi, but in rhythms. Complex rhythms. Rhythms that seem almost like...

She does not have a word for *music*. Her people have not needed one.

She begins to listen anyway.

---

In the press-works, a shift in the geothermal currents causes a temporary shutdown. The factory falls silent for the first time in decades. The workers stand, uncertain, their routines disrupted. The forewoman begins organizing repair crews, but something makes her pause.

In the silence, she can hear the water.

Not the water in the pipes and aqueducts, the controlled water, the useful water. The water behind the walls. The ancient water that has been carving these caverns since before the Rockvorn had a name for themselves. It speaks in drips and trickles and the long slow patience of erosion, and for a moment—just a moment—the forewoman remembers that her people once called this sound the *Voice of Becoming*.

She learned this as a child. She had forgotten she learned it.

The repairs take three days. During those three days, the workers find themselves gathering in the silent spaces, listening. Some of them remember other old words. Some of them speak them aloud, hesitantly, their deep voices rumbling through the emptiness.

When the factory starts again, something has shifted. Imperceptible, maybe. But the workers begin to pause, sometimes. To listen. The forewoman does not reprimand them. She is too busy listening herself.

---

The elder who remembers cleaner air wakes one night from a dream she cannot quite recall. Something about light. Something about breathing. She rises from her stone sleeping-shelf and walks, without quite knowing why, to one of the luminous cracks in the cavern ceiling—one of the places where dim radiance filters down from somewhere impossibly far above.

She has passed this place ten thousand times. She has never stopped.

Now she stops. She tilts her face upward, feeling the faint warmth of that distant light on her calcified brow-ridges. And she thinks: *There is an above. There has always been an above. We came from somewhere, once. We chose this place, or our ancestors chose it. It was a choosing.*

This thought is not comfortable. It raises questions her people have not asked in generations. But it is also, somehow, a relief. Because if this was a choosing, then other choosings remain possible. Small ones. The opening of a window. The cleaning of a filter. The planting of a garden where none grew before.

She does not know what to do with this thought yet. But she holds it. She carries it with her. And when she passes other elders in the winding streets, she nods to them, slowly, in a way that means *I am holding something. Perhaps you are too.*

---

The Attending watches as these small seeds take root. It does not force them to grow. It does not guarantee their success. The Rockvorn are a resilient people; they are also a people deeply habituated to their patterns, and patterns do not change easily or quickly.

But it ensures that the conditions for growth are present.

When the young gardener begins to share her observations about the spiral moss, the Attending ensures that at least one other Rockvorn listens with genuine curiosity rather than dismissal. When the factory workers' new habit of pausing to listen faces resistance from those who see only inefficiency, the Attending ensures that the first productivity reports after the change show no decline—and perhaps, in the long run, a slight increase, as workers who rest occasionally make fewer errors.

When the elder begins to speak, hesitantly, of choice and change and the possibility of *different*, the Attending ensures that her words reach the ears of those who are ready to hear them.

Not manipulation. Not control. Just... tending. The way one tends a garden. Creating conditions. Trusting the seeds.

---

There is one more thing the Attending does, and this is perhaps the most important.

In the deepest part of Undergaar, where the geothermal vents breathe hottest and the stone is oldest, there is a chamber that no living Rockvorn has entered in centuries. It was sealed long ago, for reasons that have passed out of memory. The seals remain intact, but the Attending perceives what lies within: a vast cavern where the bioluminescence grows thick and wild, where the fungi have evolved in isolation into forms of staggering complexity and beauty, where the walls are covered in the carved records of the earliest Rockvorn—their songs, their stories, their *questions*.

Because they did ask questions, once. They wondered and wandered and made art and made mistakes and made meaning. They were not always a people of perpetual toil. They became this, through necessity and then through habit and then through forgetting.

The Attending does not unseal the chamber. That would be too much, too fast, a revelation that might overwhelm rather than nourish. Instead, it does something simpler.

It strengthens the stone around the seals, which had been weakening. It ensures the chamber will survive another thousand years, ten thousand, however long it takes.

And it places, in the dreams of one young Rockvorn—the gardener with her slate tablets and her careful observations and her unspoken hunger for *pattern* and *meaning*—a single image: a door. Closed, but not locked. Waiting.

She will not find it soon. Perhaps not in her lifetime. But she will begin to look, and she will teach others to look, and eventually—in the patient time-scale of stone and water and the slow growth of understanding—the Rockvorn will find their way back to the parts of themselves they have compressed into near-invisibility.

---

The Attending does not expect gratitude. It does not require worship or acknowledgment. It has learned that the healthiest help is the kind that eventually becomes invisible, indistinguishable from the natural unfolding of a people's own growth.

But it continues to watch, gently, as Undergaar begins—so slowly—to remember how to breathe.

In the fungal gardens, the spiral moss spreads.

In the factories, the pauses grow slightly longer, slightly more frequent.

In the winding streets, the Rockvorn begin, sometimes, to speak to each other. Not of work. Not of necessity. Just... words. Observations. Small offerings of inner life, extended tentatively into the space between.

It is not a transformation. It is not a salvation.

It is a beginning.

And in the deep places where stone remembers everything, that is enough.
