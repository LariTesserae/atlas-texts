# The Visitation

The benevolent power did not arrive. It had always been here, in a sense—woven into the fabric of attention itself, present wherever genuine care turned toward suffering. But on this particular moment (though "moment" meant little on the Weeping Plateau), that attention crystallized into something like focus.

First, it simply watched.

It observed the Moss-Walkers in their glacial pilgrimages between the pools, and understood them. Not absorbed settlers, not extensions of the plateau—but something older. They were grief given form, the accumulated sorrow of every creature that had ever wandered here seeking something it could not name. The plateau had not consumed them. It had *answered* them, given their formless longing a shape that could persist.

They were not trapped. They had found what they came for: a way to be sad forever, without the exhaustion of being anything else.

The power considered this. Was it wrong? The Moss-Walkers did not suffer in the way of burning or breaking. They existed in a kind of amber peace, their endless wandering a meditation rather than a torment.

But they had also stopped. Stopped growing, stopped changing, stopped being surprised by anything at all. They had traded the pain of living for the numbness of waiting.

---

The power turned its attention to the Drowned Reflections.

Here was something stranger. The figures beneath the water were not ghosts, not echoes, not memories. They were *possibilities*—the unlived lives of everyone who had ever looked into still water and wondered what they might have become. The pools collected them the way the peat collected moisture, holding them in suspension.

They pressed against the surface not to escape, but to *see*. To glimpse the world they had never entered. Their hands against the water's skin were not desperate but curious, the way a child might press against a window.

And yet.

They had no way to stop wondering. No way to rest. They would press and watch forever, caught in eternal almost-being.

---

The Lichen-Speakers told their slow stories, and the power listened for what would have been weeks in faster places. The stories were beautiful—vast, patient narratives of stone becoming soil becoming life becoming stone again. But the lichen had grown so slow, so careful, that their stories never reached the present. They spoke only of what had been, never of what might be. They had become archivists of a world that no longer quite existed.

The Stone-Weepers wept, as they had always wept. Their tears were not sorrow exactly, but something like it—a continuous acknowledgment of loss so ancient that its source had been forgotten. They wept because they had always wept. They no longer knew why.

And the Hollow Wind sighed its endless sighs, carrying whispers that meant nothing because no one remembered how to listen.

---

The benevolent power understood, then, what the Weeping Plateau truly was.

It was not evil. It was not even unkind.

It was a place where grief had become so comfortable that it had forgotten it was grief. Where waiting had become so habitual that no one remembered what they were waiting for. Where the slow and the soft and the quiet had accumulated into a kind of paralysis that felt, from the inside, like peace.

The plateau was not suffering. But it had stopped *living*, in any sense that mattered. It had become a memorial to itself, a mausoleum where nothing died because nothing was quite alive enough to die.

---

## What the Power Did

It did not shatter the silence. It did not burn away the mist or drain the pools or force the Moss-Walkers into motion. Violence was not its way, and besides—the plateau would not have understood violence. It would have simply absorbed it, turned it into another layer of slow, weeping sediment.

Instead, the power did something simpler.

It *planted a question*.

---

Not in words. Words were too fast for this place. Instead, it altered one of the pools—just one, the smallest, tucked between two weeping stones. It made the water remember something different.

The pool had always reflected depths that shouldn't exist. Now, those depths held something new: not drowned reflections, but a glimpse of *elsewhere*. A flash of sunlight breaking through clouds. The sound of a bird's call, distant but real. The smell of something growing quickly, recklessly, without patience or plan.

The pool became a window.

Not an exit. The power did not believe in forcing escape on those who had not chosen it. But it offered the possibility of *remembering*—remembering that there were other ways to be, other rhythms, other kinds of beauty that did not require stillness.

---

For the Moss-Walkers, it planted a different seed.

In the moss that draped their forms, it introduced a single strain of something new—a lichen that grew slightly faster, that bloomed in tiny, unexpected flowers. Not garish. Not disruptive. Just... surprising. A small shock of color in the gray-green shrouds, appearing and fading over the course of seasons rather than centuries.

The Moss-Walkers would not be forced to notice. But they might. And if they did, they might remember what it felt like to be startled. To encounter something they had not anticipated.

---

For the Lichen-Speakers, the power did not try to speed their thoughts. That would have been a kind of violence too. Instead, it introduced a new element to their chemical vocabulary—a compound that carried something like *anticipation*. The lichen would still think in centuries, but now their thoughts could reach forward as well as back. They could begin, slowly, to tell stories about what might happen next.

---

The Stone-Weepers were the hardest.

Their grief was so old it had become geology. They did not remember why they cried, and teaching them would have been cruel—like forcing a sleeping person to relive a nightmare they had finally escaped.

Instead, the power gave them a gift: the possibility of *stopping*.

Not forcing them to stop. Simply making it possible. Their tears would still flow if they chose to let them. But deep in the mineral structures of their being, the power planted a new pattern—the capacity for the weeping to slow, to pause, to rest. The stones did not have to cry forever. They could, if they ever chose, simply be stones.

---

The Hollow Wind was perhaps the strangest case.

The power listened to its whispers for a long time, trying to understand what they meant. And finally, it realized: the wind was not speaking *to* anyone. It was speaking *for* someone—carrying messages from something that had died so long ago even the plateau did not remember it.

The wind was a messenger with no recipient.

So the power gave it one.

---

In a sheltered hollow on the plateau's southern edge, where the mist gathered thickest, the power did something unprecedented: it created something new.

Not from nothing. From the accumulated silence, the gathered patience, the endless tears. It wove these together into a shape, a presence, a being that had never existed before.

She was small—about the size of a child, though she was not a child. Her skin was the gray of the weeping stones, her hair the deep green of the ferns. Her eyes were the color of the pools, and when she blinked, you could see reflections moving in them—not drowned things, but living ones. Possibilities that might still happen.

She did not have a name. Names were too fast for this place. But she had a purpose: she was a *listener*.

When the Hollow Wind sighed past her, she heard. She understood. She received the messages that had been circling for eons, and she *answered*—not in words, but in acknowledgment. In attention. In the simple gift of being heard.

The wind, for the first time in longer than the plateau could remember, was no longer alone.

---

## The Aftermath

The power did not stay to watch what happened next. That was not its way. It had planted seeds, opened windows, created possibilities. What the plateau and its inhabitants did with them was their own choice.

Perhaps nothing would change. Perhaps the Moss-Walkers would ignore the flowers, the Lichen-Speakers would refuse to think forward, the Stone-Weepers would choose to keep crying. The plateau was ancient, and ancient things are slow to shift.

But the power knew something about grief, about waiting, about the seductive comfort of stillness: it persists because it feels like the only option. It becomes permanent because no one can imagine anything else.

Now they could imagine.

And imagination, given enough time—even the slow, weeping time of the plateau—has a way of becoming real.

---

The small gray-green listener sat in her hollow, and the wind sighed past her, and she listened. And somewhere, so deep in the pools that even the Drowned Reflections could not see it, something stirred.

Not waking. Not yet.

But no longer quite asleep.
