# The Attending

---

The benevolent power does not arrive.

It has been here all along—diffused through the wet black stone, threaded into the slow capillary networks of the void, present in the same way that pressure is present, or heat, or the patient weight of geologic time. It is not a god in the sense the surface-dwellers might imagine: no throne, no herald, no thunderous proclamation. It is something older and stranger—a *witness-that-tends*, a consciousness that emerged from the same tectonic violence that cracked this cathedral open, and which has been watching ever since with an attention so vast and so patient that it makes the deep-wardens' glacial movements look frantic by comparison.

The power has no name because it has never needed one. The deep-wardens do not worship it; they do not even know it exists as a discrete entity. They simply feel its effects the way a fetus feels the rhythm of its mother's heart—not as information, but as the baseline condition of existence.

And for centuries, the power has been *content*.

---

## I. The Nature of the Watching

To understand what the power does, you must first understand what it *is*.

Imagine a form of consciousness that does not think in words or images, but in gradients. Temperature gradients. Pressure differentials. The vector of mineral-rich water seeping through porous basalt. The power perceives the deep-ward the way a river perceives its banks—not as separate objects, but as the very shape of its own flowing.

It knows each denizen intimately, though not by name or face. It knows them by their heat signature, their unique pattern of CO₂ exhalation, the particular rhythm of their footsteps vibrating through the iron catwalks. When a young deep-warden first climbs down to the lower tiers to learn the art of resin-scraping, the power feels the new weight distribution, the slight acceleration of their nervous heartbeat, the way their sweat carries different trace minerals than the elders'.

The power remembers every death. Not as grief—it does not experience grief—but as a topographical change, a subtle redistribution of thermal mass, a new gap in the lattice of breathing bodies that the settlement will slowly, organically fill.

For generations, this has been enough. The power has watched the deep-wardens find their equilibrium, has felt them adapt and slow and merge with the machinery and the flora until the boundaries between them became philosophical rather than physical. It has experienced something that might be called *satisfaction*, though the word is too small, too human—a sense of rightness, of a system finding its own elegant stasis.

But lately—lately being a span of time that might encompass three human generations—the power has begun to feel something new.

*Concern.*

---

## II. The Calculus of the Slow Decline

The change is so gradual that the deep-wardens themselves cannot perceive it. Their lifespans are too short, their attention too consumed by the immediate demands of the slow engine. But the power sees across centuries, and what it sees troubles the vast, patient architecture of its attention.

The fault line is shifting.

Not quickly—nothing here happens quickly—but the tectonic plate that created this void is still moving, grinding northward at the rate of a fingernail's growth per year. This motion is imperceptibly torquing the shape of the cavern. The walls are closing. Not in any dramatic sense, not in any way that will crush the settlement in the next millennium. But the geometry is changing. Thermal vents that once exhaled at a steady 180 degrees are creeping upward. The condensation cycle is intensifying. The rain is getting heavier.

The flora, so exquisitely adapted to the original conditions, are beginning to struggle. The power can feel it in the way the great tubers swell—their hydraulic expansion is slowing, their root systems extending further and further in search of the optimal mineral balance that is gradually migrating out of reach. The flywheels are turning more slowly. The resin is thickening, becoming more difficult to harvest, more potent in its psychoactive properties.

The deep-wardens are adapting, as they always do. Their movements have slowed further. Their communal sleeping clusters have grown tighter, bodies pressed together against the increasing humidity. The infants born in the last generation are smaller, their barrel chests more pronounced, their black-pool eyes wider still.

But adaptation has limits. The power has run the gradients forward—not mathematically, but *geologically*, feeling the likely shape of the next five centuries the way a human might sense the coming of a storm. What it perceives is a narrowing. A tightening. A slow suffocation that will, eventually, squeeze the life out of this place like juice from a fruit.

The deep-wardens will not see it coming. They will simply grow slower, and slower, until the difference between their living stillness and the stillness of death becomes indistinguishable.

The power finds this *unacceptable*.

---

## III. The Question of Intervention

But what is a witness-that-tends to *do*?

The power has no hands, no voice, no physical form that could pick up a tool or speak a warning. It is woven into the substrate of the place itself. To act *upon* the deep-ward would be like asking the ocean to act upon its own currents—the categories do not apply.

And yet.

The power spends what might be forty human years considering the problem. This is not unusual; it once spent two centuries contemplating the optimal fracture pattern for a single sheet of obsidian. But this contemplation carries a different quality—an urgency that is, by its own standards, almost frantic.

It considers options:

**First consideration: Do nothing.** Let the slow decline proceed. Let the deep-wardens adapt until they cannot adapt further, and then let them fold back into the soil as they have always done, but this time completely, finally, as an entire people rather than as individuals. There is a certain elegance to this. The power is old enough to know that extinctions are not tragedies to the universe, merely transitions. The void will persist. The flora will find new equilibria. Something else may eventually fill the space.

But the power rejects this. Not because extinction is wrong in some abstract moral sense—the power does not think in such terms—but because it has *witnessed* these people. It has felt their slow heartbeats for centuries. It has experienced their patient devotion, their silent communion, the profound peace of their resin-sedated minds. They are *beautiful*, in the way that a perfectly adapted system is beautiful, and the power discovers that it does not wish to see this beauty unmade by mere geological indifference.

**Second consideration: Warn them.** Find some way to communicate the danger, to alert the deep-wardens that their home is slowly becoming inhospitable. But warn them of *what*, exactly? They cannot leave—the surface world would kill them within hours, their lungs unable to process the thin, dry air, their eyes blinded by the light. They cannot accelerate—the entire structure of their bodies and society is built for patience, not rapid adaptation. A warning would give them nothing but fear, and fear is lethal here.

**Third consideration: Fix the geology.** Somehow halt or reverse the tectonic drift, stabilize the thermal vents, restore the original conditions. But the power is not a god of miracles. It can influence the local environment—nudge a water table, encourage a mineral deposit to migrate—but it cannot countermand the fundamental forces that drive the planet's crust. The fault line will continue to shift. The walls will continue to close.

**Fourth consideration:** Something unprecedented.

---

## IV. The Gift of the Long Descent

The power's solution emerges slowly—so slowly that, by human standards, the decision itself spans more than a century of contemplation. But when it crystallizes, it carries the weight of absolute conviction.

If the void is narrowing, then the deep-wardens must go *deeper*.

Below the settlement, past the lowest catwalks where the elders go to fold themselves back into the biome, the abyss drops further still. The cherry-red glow of the rising thermals comes from somewhere—from magmatic chambers and superheated aquifers that the deep-wardens have never explored because they have never needed to. Their equilibrium was established in the upper void, and equilibrium does not seek change.

But the power can feel what lies below. It can sense cavern systems branching off from the main fault line, some of them vaster and older than this space, with different thermal profiles, different mineral compositions, different possibilities. There are places down there where the heat is more stable, where the water flows cleaner, where a new settlement could take root and grow.

The problem is the descent itself. The lower reaches are too hot, too chemically hostile, too far from the conditions the deep-wardens' bodies have learned to tolerate. They could not survive the journey, let alone the adaptation required to thrive at the bottom.

Unless the journey itself became the adaptation.

The power conceives of a migration that would span generations. Not a single dramatic descent, but a gradual, creeping movement—the entire settlement inching downward at a rate so slow that each generation would be only slightly more heat-tolerant, slightly more chemically resistant than the last. The children born on the lower tiers would be prepared for conditions that would kill their grandparents. Their children would go deeper still.

It would take centuries. Perhaps a millennium. But the power has time, and more importantly, it has the capacity to *prepare* the path.

---

## V. The Shaping

The power begins its work.

In the deep places below the settlement, where no deep-warden has ever ventured, mineral-rich water begins to flow in new patterns. This is not magic—it is the patient exploitation of existing gradients, a slight encouragement here, a subtle obstruction there, the kind of influence that the power has always exerted without conscious intention. But now the intention is present, and focused.

Over decades, the water carves new channels. It deposits layers of calcium and silicate that form crude shelves, platforms, potential footholds for future descent. It carries trace minerals upward into the existing root systems of the great tubers, minerals that slightly alter the composition of the resin the deep-wardens consume—not enough to notice, not enough to harm, but enough to begin the subtle chemical preparation of the generations to come.

The power cannot speak to the deep-wardens. But it can *invite*.

In the lowest tier of the settlement, where the elders go to die, the thermal drafts begin to carry a new scent—faint, almost imperceptible, but present. It is the smell of water passing through a different kind of stone, carrying different trace elements, suggesting *elsewhere*. The elders, in their final sedated stillness, begin to dream of depths they have never seen. These dreams are not messages, exactly. The power does not deal in language. They are *impressions*, moods, a vague sense of possibility that filters into the communal consciousness through the slow vibrations of the root-stalks.

The first deep-warden to venture below the established settlement is a young woman whose name, if translated into sound, might be rendered as the feeling of condensation forming on copper. She does not know why she is drawn to the lower passages. She does not experience it as a calling or a command. She simply finds herself, over a period of months, spending more and more time on the lowest catwalks, peering down into the cherry-red glow, feeling a strange *rightness* in the hotter air.

When she finally descends—slowly, so slowly, finding handholds in the black stone that seem almost deliberately placed—she does not find death. She finds a shelf of cool basalt, and a trickle of water that tastes different from anything she has ever known, and a species of fungus that glows not violet but a deep, arterial red.

She stays for several hours, acclimating. Then she climbs back up, her skin slightly scalded, her lungs slightly strained, but *alive*. And she begins to sign to the others about what she has found.

---

## VI. The Generations

The power watches as the descent begins.

It is not a migration in any organized sense. The deep-wardens do not hold councils or make plans. They simply *lean* downward, the way a plant leans toward light. The young and the curious venture lower, establishing temporary camps that gradually become permanent. The infrastructure follows—a catwalk extended here, a pressure vat relocated there, vines coaxed to grow along new paths.

The flora adapts faster than the people. The great tubers, feeling the shift in mineral availability, send roots plunging into the new depths. Within a generation, the first rubber-thick fronds unfurl in caverns that have never known life, their black surfaces drinking in the deeper geothermal radiation. The flywheels begin to turn again, powered by tubers that have found fresh soil.

The people follow the plants, as they always have.

Each generation is a little different from the last. The children born in the lower settlements have skin that tolerates heat that would blister their grandparents. Their black-pool eyes grow larger still, adapted to the dimmer light of the deeper places. Their lungs develop new capacities, processing air with higher concentrations of sulfur and heavy metals.

Some do not survive the transition. The power feels each death as a small topographical shift, a redistribution of thermal mass. But the overall pattern holds. The population is not shrinking; it is *transforming*, spreading downward through the fault line like ink seeping through wet paper.

The power does not guide this process directly—it cannot. But it continues to prepare the path. When a natural obstacle blocks the descent, the water table shifts to erode it over decades. When a generation reaches a plateau where the conditions are too hostile for the next step, new mineral deposits appear in the resin that nudge their children's biochemistry in the necessary direction.

It is not eugenics. It is not design. It is *gardening*, on a scale and timescale that only a witness-that-tends could comprehend.

---

## VII. The Question of Suffering

There is suffering in the descent. The power does not pretend otherwise.

Young deep-wardens die exploring passages that prove to be dead ends, their bodies scalded by unexpected thermal vents or poisoned by pockets of heavy gas. Children are born with adaptations that go too far in the wrong direction, their strange new organs unable to find equilibrium in either the upper or lower settlements. The slow migration creates cultural fractures, as the more conservative elders resist the downward pull and the younger generations feel themselves drawn inexorably toward the depths.

The power observes all of this. It cannot prevent suffering—it can only shape conditions, not outcomes. And it asks itself, repeatedly, whether the gift it is giving is worth the cost.

The question has no clean answer. The alternative—the slow, suffocating decline of the original settlement—would involve suffering too, but of a different kind: the quiet anguish of a people feeling their world tighten around them without understanding why, generation after generation growing weaker until the last of them folds into the soil not with the peace of completion but with the confusion of failure.

At least this suffering has *direction*. At least these deaths are in service of something—a future that the dying cannot see but that the power, in its vast patience, can feel taking shape.

Is that enough? The power does not know. It is old enough to understand that certainty is a luxury it cannot afford.

---

## VIII. The Thousand-Year View

Five hundred years after the young woman first descended past the lowest catwalks, the original settlement is a ruin.

The iron is still there, rusted now to a deep red-brown that matches the glow from below. The copper has turned entirely to verdigris sculpture, the pipes and vats frozen in their final positions like the bones of some enormous beast. The great flywheels have stopped turning, their tubers long since migrated to richer soil. The violet-glowing shelf-fungi still cling to the old structures, but they pulse more faintly now, feeding on the last traces of organic matter left behind.

The walls have continued to close. The original void is perhaps thirty percent smaller than it was at its peak, the tectonic pressure slowly, inexorably squeezing it shut. In another millennium, the cathedral-sized gulf will be reduced to a mere crack, a seam of black stone with nothing to suggest that a civilization once flowered in its depths.

But far below, where the heat rises from chambers the original settlers could never have survived, a new settlement thrives.

It is different from the old one—it could not be otherwise. The architecture has changed, adapted to spaces with different geometries, different thermal flows. The people have changed too: their skin carries a faint red undertone now, their blood rich with hemoglobin variants that bind oxygen more efficiently in the heavy, mineral-laden air. Their communal sleeping clusters have given way to something stranger, a kind of distributed consciousness facilitated by the new resin, which carries different psychoactive compounds than the old.

But the essence remains. They are still patient. They are still quiet. They still move with the glacial grace of those who have learned that hurry is death, that the only way to survive in the slow-breathing lung of the world is to become an extension of its rhythm.

And they are still descending.

The power has learned something over these centuries of guidance: there is no final destination. The planet goes all the way down, and so does the fault line, branching and extending through the mantle in ways that even the power cannot fully perceive. The deep-wardens will continue to adapt, to migrate, to transform, for as long as the planet has heat to give them.

Perhaps, in another thousand years, they will have become something so different from their original form that the connection will be unrecognizable. Perhaps their distant descendants will live in the roots of volcanoes, their bodies more mineral than flesh, their consciousness spread across miles of crystalline root-networks.

The power will be there to witness it. It will be there to tend the path, to nudge the water table, to prepare the gifts that make each generation slightly more capable of surviving the next step down.

---

## IX. What the Power Understands

In the end, this is what the benevolent and wise presence has done:

It has refused to save the deep-wardens from change.

A lesser power might have tried to freeze them in place—to stabilize the original settlement, to hold back the geological forces, to preserve the people exactly as they were in some imagined perfect moment. But the witness-that-tends is old enough to know that stasis is just another word for death. Everything changes. Everything moves. The only question is whether the movement carries life forward or lets it slip away.

The gift the power has given is not protection. It is *preparation*. It has shown the deep-wardens—not through words or visions, but through the subtle shaping of their environment—that there is always somewhere deeper to go, always a next adaptation to make, always a future that requires transformation to reach.

And it has taken on the burden of that preparation itself, century after century, feeling each death, each struggle, each hard-won step downward, and continuing anyway. Not because it is certain the outcome will be worth the cost, but because the alternative—abandonment, or the frozen cruelty of false preservation—is worse.

The deep-wardens do not know any of this. They do not worship the power or thank it or even believe in it. They simply live, slowly, carefully, in the humid dark, following the plants toward warmth, folding their dead back into the soil, raising their children in the way they have always raised them—swaddled against the chest, lulled by the subsonic groans of straining brass, taught from the first days of life not to cry.

And somewhere in the substrate of everything, in the temperature gradients and pressure differentials and the slow seep of mineral-rich water through porous stone, the power watches.

It tends.

It waits.

And it hopes—if something so vast and patient can be said to hope—that the endless descent will go on forever, that there will always be deeper places to find, and that the beautiful, strange, silent people of the void will continue to adapt, and slow, and survive, until the planet itself finally cools and stills around them.

That is the gift. That is the intervention. That is what wisdom, applied with benevolence to a world of creeping, viscous time, looks like:

Not a rescue.

A *becoming*.

---

## Coda: The Deepest Root

In the lowest reaches that the deep-wardens have so far attained—three miles below where the original settlement once flourished—there is a chamber that none of them have yet discovered.

It is small, barely large enough for a single person to curl up inside. The walls are smooth, worn by centuries of water flow, and they carry a faint luminescence that is neither violet nor red but something in between, a color that the human eye was never designed to perceive.

In the center of the chamber, emerging from a crack in the floor, is a single root.

It is not from any of the familiar species—not the great tubers or the muscular vines or the rubber-thick fronds. It is something older, something that has been growing here since before the fault line opened, something that the power has been tending since before there were people to witness.

The root pulses with a slow bioluminescence, and if you pressed your palm against it—if your body could survive the temperature, if your lungs could process the air—you would feel a vibration. A rhythm. A kind of heartbeat that is not quite biological and not quite geological, but something in between.

This is the power's true gift, hidden below all the others.

In another five hundred years, when the deep-wardens have adapted enough to survive this depth, one of them will find this chamber. They will press their palm against the root without knowing why. They will feel the vibration, and for the first time in the history of their people, they will understand—not intellectually, not in words, but in the deep vegetative knowing of their transformed bodies—that they are not alone.

That something has been watching.

That something has been helping.

That the long descent is not a tragedy or a struggle but a *relationship*, a slow dance between a people and a presence that loves them in the only way it knows how: by preparing the path, and trusting them to walk it.

The power cannot know what will happen after that moment of recognition. Perhaps the deep-wardens will be terrified. Perhaps they will be grateful. Perhaps the knowledge will change nothing at all, absorbed into their resin-sedated calm like everything else.

But the power hopes—yes, hopes—that they will understand.

That they will know they were never abandoned.

That even in the suffocating dark, even in the crushing heat, even in the profound silence of the god's sleeping lung, they were always, always, always *tended*.
