# The Attention of the Weaver

The being who first noticed Hraza-Lor was not looking for it.

She had been following the migration of a particular species of high-altitude moth across the Skyfang range—creatures whose wing patterns encoded the chemical composition of the clouds they passed through, living barometers that had fascinated her for several centuries. It was the moths that led her to the city, drawn as they were to the warmth bleeding from its geothermal vents and the artificial light blazing from its agri-domes.

She paused in her tracking. Settled into stillness. Watched.

The being had many names across many worlds and ages, but the one she preferred—when she thought of herself at all—was simply the Weaver. Not because she wove fabric or fate or any such poetic nonsense, but because her particular gift, her particular joy, was perceiving how things connected. She saw the threads between a glacier's melt rate and a city's water pressure, between a child's fever and a failing heat exchanger three levels down, between the breeding cycle of condensation-midges and the nutritional content of the next season's harvest.

She saw Hraza-Lor not as the Kethra saw it—a triumph of will, a defiance of nature—but as what it actually was: a single organism of staggering complexity, composed of metal and flesh and water and light, breathing and metabolizing and slowly, slowly dying.

Not today. Not this century. But dying nonetheless, in the way that all closed systems eventually die, entropy accumulating in corners too small to notice until suddenly they were all anyone could see.

The Weaver watched for a long time. She watched a maintenance crew blast ice from a water intake, unaware that the particular formation they destroyed had been subtly redirecting wind currents in a way that reduced stress on a support strut two hundred meters below. She watched a hydro-farmer carefully tend her orchids, not knowing that the specific fungal symbiont in her growing medium was the only thing preventing a slow aluminum corrosion in the pipes feeding her sector. She watched a child trace the embroidered patterns on her grandfather's vest, asking about their meaning, receiving answers that were three-quarters invention and one-quarter misremembered truth.

She watched the pipe-crawlers in the utility levels, and she watched the Skyfinches in their high crevices, and she watched the rust-colored lichen creeping across the oldest structures. She watched the condensation bead and trickle and flow. She watched the massive pistons drive and the conveyor belts haul and the arc-lamps blaze.

And she thought, as she often did when encountering such places: *What would help?*

---

The Weaver had learned, across millennia of observation, that the most dangerous thing a powerful being could do was act decisively.

Decisive action was satisfying. It produced visible results. It made the actor feel competent and good. It was also, in her experience, almost invariably catastrophic in the long term, because complex systems did not respond well to sudden interventions, no matter how well-intentioned.

She had seen worlds where benevolent powers had solved the water problem, only to discover that the social structures built around water scarcity had been load-bearing—remove them, and everything collapsed into chaos. She had seen civilizations where wise beings had gifted advanced technology, only to watch it become a weapon, or a crutch, or simply incomprehensible to those who hadn't developed the conceptual frameworks to understand it. She had seen a thousand variations of the same story: powerful being sees suffering, powerful being intervenes, suffering multiplies in unexpected directions.

So she did not solve Hraza-Lor's problems. She did not stabilize their water supply or reinforce their structures or gift them better technology or reveal herself as a source of wisdom to be consulted. She did not even, despite the temptation, save the particular individuals whose deaths she could foresee—the engineer who would fall from a gantry in six months, the child who would succumb to a respiratory infection in two years, the entire sector that would be lost to a cascading structural failure in four decades.

What she did, instead, was much smaller. And much stranger.

---

The first thing the Weaver did was learn the embroidery.

She spent three years studying the patterns the Kethra stitched into their clothing—their grammar, their evolution, their regional variations, their errors and innovations. She traced lineages through sleeve-hems and tracked guild affiliations through collar-work. She decoded the personal milestones: births and deaths, promotions and disgraces, loves found and lost.

And she noticed something the Kethra themselves had largely forgotten: the patterns were not merely decorative or commemorative. In their oldest forms, before centuries of drift and simplification, they had been something else entirely. They had been maps.

Not geographical maps—the Kethra had plenty of those, and updated them constantly. These were different. They were maps of *relationships*. Of dependencies. Of the invisible threads connecting one part of the city to another, one system to another, one generation's choices to another generation's constraints.

The original designers of Hraza-Lor, the ancestors the Kethra venerated, had understood something crucial: that a system this complex could not be fully documented in any schematic, no matter how detailed. There was too much. Too many interactions, too many feedback loops, too many emergent properties that only became visible across decades of observation. So they had encoded their hard-won understanding into a form that could survive even if the documents were lost, even if the knowledge was forgotten: into the patterns that every Kethra wore, saw, and perpetuated without fully understanding.

The embroidery was a distributed memory system. A way of preserving systemic knowledge across generations, embedded in culture rather than technology, resilient to the very disasters it was meant to help prevent.

But it had degraded. Meanings had been lost, simplified, aestheticized. The maps had become mere decoration, their warnings unreadable, their wisdom inaccessible.

The Weaver could have restored them. Could have appeared to some respected elder and explained everything, provided a complete translation key, revealed the hidden depths of their own heritage.

She didn't.

Instead, she did something much more subtle, and much more respectful.

---

The Weaver began to dream.

Not her own dreams—she did not dream, exactly, being what she was—but the dreams of others. Specifically, the dreams of certain Kethra: a young embroiderer with an unusually analytical mind, an elderly guildmaster haunted by a sense of forgotten purpose, a child who asked too many questions about her grandmother's patterns.

She did not plant ideas in these dreams. Did not provide answers or revelations or convenient prophecies. She simply... made space. Created silence in the noise of their sleeping minds, gaps where connections could form if the dreamers were inclined to make them.

The young embroiderer began to notice patterns within patterns, repetitions that seemed too consistent to be coincidental. She started a journal, documenting variations across generations, families, guilds. It became first a hobby, then an obsession, then—after she presented her preliminary findings to a skeptical audience of guild historians—the foundation of a new field of study.

The elderly guildmaster, whose working life had been devoted to water systems, began to perceive his ancestors' sleeve-hems differently. He saw, in their arrangements, something that looked almost like pipe diagrams. Something that corresponded, in ways he couldn't quite articulate, to flow-patterns he recognized from a lifetime of experience. He mentioned this to a colleague over a communal meal, almost embarrassed by the fanciful observation.

The colleague mentioned it to someone else. Who mentioned it to someone else. Who happened to be the young embroiderer's cousin.

The child who asked too many questions simply kept asking them, immune to the adult exasperation this produced. She wanted to know why this pattern meant a guild promotion when that pattern meant a birth. She wanted to know why the patterns on very old garments looked different from new ones. She wanted to know what the patterns were *for*.

Her grandmother, confronted with these questions, found herself confronting them too. Found herself, for the first time in decades, really looking at the vest she wore, the vest her mother had worn, the vest her mother's mother had worn before the Great Conduit Repair of her childhood.

She saw something she had never seen before: a small, easily-overlooked design element in the shoulder, repeated exactly on a schematic diagram framed on her wall—a diagram showing the conduit's original configuration, donated to her family in honor of her grandmother's role in the repair.

She began to weep without fully understanding why.

---

The Weaver did not stop there, but her subsequent interventions were equally indirect.

She ensured that certain books survived their scheduled pulping—old technical manuals, presumed obsolete, that contained fragments of the original pattern-language codified for training purposes. She arranged for these books to end up in the hands of people inclined to read them.

She introduced a slight mutation into a species of mold that had been growing in a particular archive, slowing its spread enough that certain documents would last another fifty years instead of being rendered illegible within five.

She adjusted the timing of a rainstorm by approximately four hours, so that a scheduled external maintenance operation was delayed, which resulted in a worker being reassigned to an interior task, which resulted in that worker discovering a cache of old embroidery samples that had been bricked up in a wall during renovations three generations prior.

She spent eighteen months carefully cultivating a particular strain of orchid in an unobserved corner of an agri-dome, a strain whose blossom color matched exactly a dye used in ancient Kethra ceremonial garments—a dye whose formula had been lost for centuries. When the orchid was eventually discovered, it was assumed to be a spontaneous mutation. A botanist carefully analyzed its chemical composition. The dye was reinvented. Old patterns could now be reproduced in something closer to their original form.

The rediscovered colors turned out to matter. The pattern-language had been partly chromatic; certain meanings were encoded not just in shape but in hue. The translations that had been attempted before, working only from shape, had been incomplete in ways no one had suspected.

---

It took forty-three years for the full restoration to occur.

The Weaver watched it happen, patient and unhurried. She watched the young embroiderer grow old, her journal expanding into a multi-volume treatise, her initially-dismissed theories gradually accepted as the evidence accumulated. She watched the elderly guildmaster die, but not before passing his observations to a successor who took them seriously. She watched the questioning child grow into a questioning adult, and then a questioning elder, her persistence finally vindicated when the pattern-language project confirmed what she had always suspected: that the embroidery contained vast stores of practical knowledge, encoded and preserved and waiting to be read.

When the breakthrough finally came—when a team of researchers, building on four decades of work, finally cracked enough of the pattern-language to begin producing useful translations—the Weaver allowed herself a moment of satisfaction.

The maps were readable again.

Not all of them. Not perfectly. The degradation had been severe, and much was irrecoverable. But enough survived that the Kethra could now see what their ancestors had seen: the hidden relationships between systems, the slow catastrophes gathering in overlooked corners, the counter-intuitive dependencies that made their city a living thing rather than a mere machine.

They could see, for instance, that the ice formation their maintenance crews routinely destroyed was not merely an obstruction but a wind-baffle, and that its removal was slowly destabilizing a support strut that wouldn't fail for another century but would fail catastrophically when it did. They could see that the fungal symbiont in a certain growing medium was performing multiple functions beyond its known role, and that eliminating it (as a proposed efficiency measure would have required) would have initiated a cascade of subtle corrosions throughout an entire sector. They could see a hundred such connections, documented by generations of careful observers and encoded into the patterns passed from parent to child.

They could see, and they could plan, and they could act—not with the dramatic interventions of a powerful savior, but with the patient, informed adjustments of a people who finally understood their own home.

---

The Weaver did four other things, none of which the Kethra ever knew about.

First, she adjusted the migration patterns of the Skyfinches slightly, shifting their primary nesting sites over a period of decades to locations that happened to coincide with structural weak points the birds' constant activity would help reveal through accelerated wear. The Kethra, attentive to their avian neighbors, would notice when the birds' behavior changed, would investigate why, would discover the weaknesses in time to address them.

Second, she introduced a new predator for the pipe-crawlers—a small, six-legged creature of her own design, superficially similar to its prey but slightly more efficient. This creature would keep the pipe-crawler population from exceeding sustainable levels, preventing the periodic population explosions that the Kethra had learned to manage but that occasionally overwhelmed sectors before they could respond. The balance would be maintained automatically, invisibly, without requiring constant intervention.

Third, she made a small adjustment to the Kethra themselves. Not to their bodies—she did not alter their genetics or their physiology. She adjusted, ever so slightly, the statistical distribution of a particular cognitive trait: the ability to perceive systemic relationships, to intuit connections between apparently unrelated phenomena. This was already present in their population, as it was in all human populations; she simply made it marginally more common, marginally more likely to be recognized and cultivated. The Kethra would produce, over coming generations, somewhat more systems-thinkers than chance alone would predict. Not dramatically more. Just enough.

Fourth, and finally, she departed.

---

This was, in the Weaver's experience, the most important intervention of all: leaving.

She had seen what happened when powerful beings stayed. They became reference points, crutches, solutions to every problem. They distorted the systems they meant to help by their mere presence, creating dependencies that outlasted their utility. Even if they tried to withdraw gradually, to wean their charges off their assistance, the memory of their power lingered, fostering either resentment or desperate longing.

Better to never be known at all.

The Weaver left no trace of her passage through Hraza-Lor. No mysterious benefactor was spoken of in legends, no divine intervention invoked in prayers. The embroidery project was remembered as a triumph of Kethra scholarship, built on generations of accumulated observation, brought to fruition by the dedicated efforts of researchers who received full and deserved credit for their breakthroughs. The restored pattern-language was understood as a recovered heritage, a gift from ancestors to descendants, proof that the venerated founders had been even wiser than anyone had suspected.

The Skyfinches behaved as Skyfinches behaved, the pipe-crawlers as pipe-crawlers, the new predator species as simply another native creature that had somehow escaped notice until now. The orchid mutation was a lucky accident, celebrated by botanists but not attributed to anything beyond the ordinary operations of chance.

The slight increase in systems-thinkers among the population was invisible, statistical, undetectable by anyone without access to comparison data from alternate timelines—which no one had.

Hraza-Lor continued. Its people maintained their city, understood it better, adapted to its needs and their own. They made mistakes, learned from them, made new mistakes, learned from those too. They expanded cautiously, contracted when necessary, innovated and conserved and endured.

They remained what they had always been: a people who had carved a sanctuary into the side of a mountain, who lived by will and work and the accumulated wisdom of generations, who were beholden to no power but their own.

This, the Weaver knew, was the only gift worth giving.

---

Seventy years after the Weaver departed, a young engineer named Keth-Alor stood on an exterior gantry, looking down at the clouded lowlands miles below. She was thinking about a problem she couldn't solve: a complex interaction between three different systems that her models couldn't account for, a gap in her understanding that her teachers couldn't fill.

She was also, without consciously realizing it, rubbing her thumb across the embroidered cuff of her sleeve—a pattern that documented her lineage, her guild affiliation, and, in a small design element near the hem, a schematic representation of a water-pressure relationship that her great-great-grandmother had first documented eight generations prior.

Keth-Alor did not know what the design element meant. The full translations of the pattern-language were still being compiled, and her particular family's patterns had not yet been analyzed. But her thumb knew the shape of it, had traced it a thousand times since childhood, and somewhere in her mind, below the level of conscious thought, connections were forming.

She would solve her problem the next morning, in a flash of insight she would attribute to a good night's sleep and a fresh perspective. The solution would lead to a new maintenance protocol that would prevent a cascade failure in four sectors over the next century, saving thousands of lives and preserving millions of work-hours.

She would never know where the insight came from. Would never suspect that the answer had been woven into her sleeve all along, waiting for her mind to become ready to receive it.

Would never know that a patient and careful power had once watched her city, and chosen to trust her—and her people, and all their generations to come—with their own survival.

---

The Weaver, by then, was far away. She had found another migration to follow, another thread to trace, another complex system struggling toward an equilibrium it couldn't quite perceive.

But occasionally, when her wanderings took her near the Skyfang range, she would pause. Would look toward the distant scar on the mountainside, the lights blazing in the perpetual gloom, the smoke rising from the refineries and the green glow of the agri-domes.

And she would see, with her particular way of seeing, that the city was a little healthier than it had been. That the threads connecting its parts were a little stronger, a little more visible to those who dwelt within them. That the slow dying had slowed further still, entropy's accumulation counteracted by the growing wisdom of a people who were finally learning to read their own home.

She did not take credit for this. Did not feel pride in the ordinary sense. But she felt something—a quiet satisfaction, a gentle warmth, a sense that she had, for once, managed not to make things worse.

And then she would turn away, and follow her moths wherever they led her, and leave the Kethra to their own magnificent, improbable, enduring survival.
