# A Watching

The attention came not as light or sound but as *recognition*—the way a mind suddenly perceives a pattern it had been looking at all along. The Hollow continued its humming. The Keth-Avai continued their lives. Nothing changed, and everything changed, because now something was watching that understood what it saw.

---

The benevolent power—call it what you will; it had no name it would offer, and needed none—observed first. This was not hesitation but respect. The Hollow had been growing for millennia. The Keth-Avai had been adapting for generations beyond counting. Whatever wisdom they had accumulated was earned wisdom, paid for in bodies that fell and voices that joined the memorial pipes. The power would not insult that history by assuming it knew better.

So it watched. It watched the thread-bonded travelers moving with their herds across distances that would take ordinary Keth-Avai weeks to traverse. It watched the master weavers calculating load-bearing tolerances in their heads, fingers testing cordage for the subtle signs of wear that meant danger. It watched the sentries at the watching-posts, ears cupped toward the darkness below, listening for the sounds that meant death was climbing.

It watched the children learning to grip. It watched the ones who fell.

It watched the funeral ceremonies, the careful tuning of memorial pipes, the way a community's chord grew richer with each death, carrying voices forward into a future the dead would never see.

It watched, and it felt something that might be called sorrow, and something that might be called admiration, and something that might be called love.

---

The first gift was small.

In a settlement called Veth-Thurai—"the place where three winds meet"—a master weaver named Keth-Soluin had been puzzling over a problem for months. A crucial junction of Skeins was degrading. The fibers were old, stressed by generations of use, and the replacement cordage she had woven was not quite right. It would hold, but not for long. The settlement would need to relocate within a few years, abandoning the fire-platform her grandmother's grandmother had established, leaving behind the memorial pipes that held a century of voices.

The power did not solve her problem. That would have been theft—stealing from her the satisfaction of understanding, the growth that comes from struggle.

Instead, one morning, Keth-Soluin woke with a thought in her head that felt like her own but was somehow *clearer* than her thoughts usually were. A question: *What if the cordage were woven not in parallel strands but in a spiral pattern, each fiber supporting its neighbors at an angle?*

She sat up in her nest-basket, frowning. The idea was obvious, now that she had it. Why had she never thought of it before? She could see the geometry in her mind, could calculate—almost automatically, as if the numbers were simply *there*—how the load would distribute differently, how the spiral would resist the twisting forces that degraded parallel weaves.

By evening she had made a test sample. Within a month she had re-rigged the failing junction. Within a year, traders from Veth-Thurai were teaching the spiral-weave technique to settlements across the region, and Keth-Soluin was known as a genius, which she was. The idea had been hers. The power had only... *clarified* it. Helped it emerge from the background noise of daily survival.

She never knew. She never needed to know. The gift was in the giving, not the recognition.

---

The second gift was different.

A thread-bonded traveler named Avai-Renn was moving with his herd through an unfamiliar region—wild growth, far from any settlement, where the Skeins were natural rather than cultivated and the Pipe-Stems grew in strange configurations. He was not lost, exactly. Thread-bonded travelers did not get lost. But he was... uncertain. The chord here was wrong somehow. Dissonant. The herd was nervous, moving in tight formation, ears swiveling constantly.

Avai-Renn knew what the nervousness meant. Something was near. Something large.

The power watched him make his calculations. He was good—very good—at reading the signs. He correctly identified the direction of the threat, correctly estimated the size of the creature from the stress-patterns in the Skeins around him, correctly chose a route that would take him and his herd away from danger as quickly as possible.

But the route he chose would take him past a junction that the power could see—and he could not—was compromised. The main support-Skein had developed an internal flaw, invisible from the outside, that would fail under the combined weight of the herd. He would lose animals. He might lose himself.

The power considered. There was no way to warn him that would not be... intrusive. It could not speak to him as it had clarified Keth-Soluin's thoughts; this was not a problem of creativity or calculation but of information he simply did not have. Could not have. The flaw was hidden.

But it could do something smaller.

As Avai-Renn approached the junction, a Pipe-Stem nearby—one of the smaller ones, barely a handspan wide—*shifted*. Just slightly. Just enough to change its tone, to add a new note to the local chord. It was the kind of shift that happened naturally sometimes, when growth or decay altered a stem's acoustic properties.

Avai-Renn stopped. His ears swiveled toward the sound. He *knew* this region's chord now, had been listening to it for hours, and the new note was wrong. Not dangerous-wrong—not the low frequencies that meant a predator—but *different*-wrong. Unexpected.

He trusted his ears. He took a different route.

The herd passed the compromised junction fifty meters to the side, close enough that Avai-Renn could see it, could see that it *looked* perfectly stable, could not understand why his instincts had told him to avoid it. But his instincts had been right before. He did not question them.

Behind him, unobserved, the compromised junction held for another three days before failing. No one was crossing it when it went. The sound of its breaking was absorbed into the Hollow's chord and forgotten.

---

The third gift was the most difficult.

A child was falling.

Her name was Thess-Keva, and she was four years old, and she had climbed too far from the settlement, following a Skein-runner juvenile she wanted to befriend, and the Skein she was gripping had been damaged by a recent storm, and it snapped, and she was falling.

The power felt her terror. Felt her hands grasping at nothing. Felt the wind rushing past her as she dropped through the void.

It could save her. It had the capability—not to violate physics, not to make her float or fly, but to *adjust*, to arrange, to ensure that in her tumbling descent she would encounter one of the lower Skeins at an angle and speed that a child's gripping reflex might just barely be able to handle.

But.

The Keth-Avai had lived in the Hollow for generations uncounted. They had developed their strength, their skills, their wisdom, precisely *because* the Hollow was dangerous. Children who could not grip, fell. This was tragedy. It was also, in a terrible way, selection. The Keth-Avai were who they were because the Hollow had shaped them, and the Hollow shaped them through danger.

If the power saved every falling child, what would the Keth-Avai become? Dependent. Weakened. Trusting in a safety that was not truly theirs, that could be withdrawn at any moment when the power's attention turned elsewhere.

This was the cruelty of benevolence: that sometimes love meant allowing pain.

But also: Thess-Keva was *four*. She had climbed too far not because she was weak or careless but because she was curious, because she loved the Skein-runners, because her heart was full of the wonder that made life worth living. Was that a flaw to be culled? Was curiosity a weakness?

The power made its choice in the space between heartbeats.

It did not catch her. It did not make her fall easier. But it *shifted* something—the angle of a Mat-curtain, the tension in a lower Skein—so that when Thess-Keva's flailing hands swept through the air, there was something there to grab that would not otherwise have been there.

She caught it. Her fingers closed. The impact nearly broke her grip, nearly dislocated her shoulder, but she *held on*. She hung there in the darkness, alone, terrified, a hundred meters below her settlement, and she held on.

It took her six hours to climb back. She was exhausted, bruised, fundamentally changed by the experience. She would never climb carelessly again. She would never forget the feeling of falling, the absolute certainty of death, the impossible miracle of the Skein appearing under her hands.

She told the story for the rest of her life. How she had fallen and somehow lived. How the Hollow had caught her.

The other Keth-Avai listened. They told their own stories of narrow escapes, of luck, of moments when the Hollow seemed almost to be *helping*. They did not develop a theology around it—they were not the sort of people who built systematic explanations for mystery—but they added something to their understanding of their world.

The Hollow holds us, they said. And sometimes, if we are worthy, if we do not give up, it holds us a little tighter than we expect.

---

The power continued to watch.

It did not transform the Hollow. It did not eliminate the predators from below, or stabilize all the failing Skeins, or light the darkness with artificial suns. The Keth-Avai did not need a different world. They needed *their* world, with all its dangers and beauties and terrible demands.

But it... *attended*. It noticed when a weaver was on the verge of a breakthrough and helped the thought clarify. It noticed when a traveler was about to walk into invisible danger and arranged small accidents that changed their path. It noticed when children fell, and it made choices, case by case, about when to let the Hollow teach its harsh lessons and when to offer just enough help that the lesson was survived.

It was not salvation. It was not protection. It was something more like... companionship. The Keth-Avai were not alone in the void. Something was with them, something that cared, something that would not take their struggles from them but would not let them struggle entirely unaided.

Was this good? The power did not know. Benevolence was not certainty. It could not see all ends, could not guarantee that its gifts would not somehow weaken what it meant to strengthen. It could only act according to its nature, which was to love, and hope that love was enough.

---

Generations passed.

The Keth-Avai changed, slowly, in ways that might have happened anyway and might have been the power's influence and probably were both. The spiral-weave technique spread and was refined, making settlements more durable. The practice of listening—really *listening*, attending to every shift in the Hollow's chord—became more formalized, passed down not just as skill but as something like philosophy. *The Hollow speaks*, the teachers said. *Learn to hear it. It is not random noise. There is meaning in the sound, if you have ears to find it.*

They were right. They were more right than they knew.

The thread-bonded travelers began to range farther, carrying news and techniques and songs between settlements that had never been in contact before. A sense of shared identity emerged—not nationhood, nothing so organized, but an awareness that the Keth-Avai were a *people*, that the Hollow was their *home*, that they had something worth preserving.

The stories changed too. Not the old creation myths—those remained, the breath of the deep carrying seeds, the first Keth-Avai climbing up from the darkness—but new stories grew alongside them. Stories about the Hollow's mercy. Stories about children who should have died and didn't. Stories about moments of impossible luck, impossible insight, impossible *rightness* in a world that was mostly harsh and uncertain.

They did not worship the power. They did not know it existed as a separate thing. But they developed a deeper relationship with their world, a trust that was not quite faith but was more than mere acceptance. The Hollow was dangerous, yes. The Hollow was deadly. But the Hollow was also *home*, and home meant something more than just a place where you lived. It meant a place that knew you. A place that, in some strange way, *cared*.

---

And the power watched, and was satisfied, if satisfaction was what it felt.

It had not saved them. It had not fixed them. It had only... been present. Paid attention. Offered small kindnesses in the spaces where small kindnesses could fit without disrupting the larger pattern.

Perhaps that was enough. Perhaps that was all any benevolent power could do for beings it loved: not to remove them from danger, but to accompany them through it. Not to answer their questions, but to help them find the clarity to ask better ones. Not to make their lives easy, but to make their lives *witnessed*.

The Hollow hummed. The Keth-Avai sang. And somewhere in the space between the two sounds, in the harmonics that emerged when human voices joined the voice of the world, there was something that might have been gratitude, offered and received, neither side quite knowing who was giving and who was getting.

That was the gift. That was the only gift worth giving.

To be there. To pay attention. To love without grasping.

The Hollow held the Keth-Avai. The Keth-Avai held onto the Hollow. And something held them both, gently, invisibly, forever.
