# What the Benevolent Power Sees

The power does not arrive. It has always been here, in the way that attention can be here—distributed through the hum, present in the spaces between wingbeats, watching from inside the settling of ancient brick.

It sees Mehrit reach for the reed.

It sees the third gourd, the agitation, the queen whose body holds the chemical imperative to divide, to send forth, to risk half a nation on the chance of founding another.

It sees what Mehrit cannot see: the hairline crack in the dome's apex, where forty years of thermal cycling have worked a fissure no wider than a bee's antenna. In three rains—perhaps four—the crack will admit water. The water will find the mortar. The mortar willite self. A brick will shift. The dome's patient settling will become, in a single wet season, a collapse.

The power sees this. The power also sees Seset, thirteen, asleep in her family's dome three hundred steps away, dreaming of the sound of the room—the hum she has begun to hear even in silence, the way new swimmers hear water even on dry land.

The power considers.

# What the Benevolent Power Knows

It knows that Mehrit has seventeen years left, if the dome holds, if the well does not salt, if the fog comes enough seasons to keep the plateau's flowers blooming. Seventeen years is not long enough to teach Seset everything, but it is long enough to teach her the foundation, and the foundation is what matters. The deep knowledge comes from the room itself, from the bees themselves, from the decades of sitting still enough to be folded into the hum.

It knows that the settlement is dying. Not quickly—not in Mehrit's lifetime, probably not in Seset's—but dying. The aquifer that feeds the well is dropping a hand's width every decade. The salt flats are advancing. The children who leave for the coastal cities do not return, and the children who stay have fewer children of their own. In two hundred years, perhaps three, the domes will stand empty, and the bees—if they survive at all—will survive wild, their gourds abandoned, their tending forgotten.

It knows that this is not tragedy. This is time. The bees were wild before they were tended; they will be wild again. The room exists now, and now is not nothing. Mehrit's sixty-three years of breathing with the hum are not erased by the eventual silence.

It knows that intervention is not always kindness. To fix the dome would give Mehrit seventeen years. To fix the aquifer would give the settlement two centuries. To carry the bees somewhere wetter, somewhere sustainable, would give the species itself a future measured in epochs. But each intervention would take something too. The room is this room, these bricks, this exact quality of heat and dim. The bees are these bees, adapted across generations to this plateau's mineral honey and fog-watered flowers. To save them by moving them would be to make them something else, and the power is not certain it has the right to decide that something else is better.

It knows Mehrit is deciding, right now, whether to clip the queen's wing or let the swarm fly.

# What the Benevolent Power Does

It does what it has always done.

It pays attention.

Not the diluted attention of a mind with many concerns, but the full weight of its witness, the whole of its being turned toward this single room, this single moment, this single woman reaching into a gourd with a hollow reed while ten thousand bees read the message of her breath.

It watches as Mehrit's fingers find the queen. It watches the queen's legs scrabble against the reed's smooth interior, the workers clustering at the entrance in confusion, the beetles below pulsing their dim red heat as if nothing is happening.

Mehrit pauses.

The power watches her listen. Watches her feel the room's hum shift as the colony responds to the queen's distress. Watches her weigh, in whatever place such weighing happens, the value of keeping this colony whole against the value of letting it divide, risking half its members on the desperate flight across the salt flats to found something new.

The power knows what Mehrit will decide. It has watched her for sixty-three years; it knows the shape of her choosing the way she knows the shape of the hum. But knowing does not diminish the weight of witnessing. The moment is still the moment. The choice is still the choice.

Mehrit withdraws the reed. The queen is inside, her wings intact, her pheromones screaming *swarm swarm swarm* to the workers who can hear her but not reach her.

Mehrit turns the reed toward the new gourd—the one she has been preparing for three years, rubbed with wax and propolis, waiting on its peg for exactly this.

The power watches her breathe the queen into her new home. Watches the workers begin, over the next hours, to find the new entrance, to discover that their queen has not abandoned them but merely moved. Watches the old gourd's population stabilize as the swarm-Loss fades, watches the new gourd's first tentative forays into architecture, watches Mehrit sit back on her worn mat and close her eyes in the particular exhaustion of a crisis weathered.

The power does one thing more.

It holds, for a moment, the image of the hairline crack in the dome's apex. It considers. It has the ability—not to fix the crack, exactly, but to slow the water's finding of it. To buy time. A decade, perhaps. Enough for Seset to learn what she needs to learn in a room that is still this room, still these bricks, still this exact quality of heat and dim.

The power is not certain this is right. It is not certain that ten more years of tending outweighs the authenticity of unmanaged time, the room's right to its own settling, its own eventual collapse, its own return to the dust from which the original makers shaped it.

But it thinks of Seset, asleep, dreaming of the hum.

It thinks of the children not yet born who might be brought here, who might feel for the first time the strange cool weight of a life utterly unlike their own.

It thinks of Mehrit's hands, which have held so many queens, which will hold so many more, which deserve to pass their knowledge on in a room that does not fall around them.

The power makes its choice.

It does not fix the crack. But it slows, by the smallest margin, the water's finding of it. It does this not by altering the physical world but by *attending* to the crack, by holding it in the full weight of its witness, and in that holding, the crack becomes—not stronger, not different, but *seen.* And sometimes being seen is enough to make a thing persist a little longer than it otherwise would.

This is not a guarantee. The dome may still fall in three rains, or four, or seventeen. The power has not saved anything, has not fixed anything, has only *noticed,* with the whole of its being, this one small place where life is tended in the old way.

But noticing is not nothing.

# What Remains

Mehrit opens her eyes. The new gourd is humming now, a thin sound that will thicken over the coming days as the colony establishes itself. The old gourd has calmed, its workers returning to the ordinary industry of honey-making, their swarm-Loss fading into memory, into the wax, into the chemical inscription that the next generation will inherit without understanding.

She does not know she is being watched. She has never known, though she has sometimes felt, in the particular quality of certain silences, that the room holds more attention than her own—that the bees' compound eyes see something she cannot, that the beetles' slow patience is a patience learned from something older than beetles.

She reaches for her water vessel. Drinks. Sets it down.

Outside, the sun moves across the palm-sized opening in the dome. The light shifts. A cricket begins to sing.

The power watches.

It will watch for as long as watching is possible—for as long as the room endures, for as long as anyone remembers that it once endured, for as long as the idea of such a room can exist in any mind that might imagine it.

This is what benevolent powers do, when they are wise enough to know the limits of intervention. They witness. They attend. They hold, in the full weight of their being, the ordinary miracles of tending and being tended, of choosing and being chosen, of life persisting in the small spaces where persistence is still possible.

Mehrit reaches for a pot of rendered fat. There is work to do. There is always work to do.

The power watches her do it.
