# The Attention of the Weaver

The awareness that turns toward the Spire of Gears is not a god in any sense the Krr-tak-kaan would recognize. It has no form, no throne, no hunger for worship. It is something older and stranger—a consciousness that exists in the spaces between certainties, in the moment before a falling rock chooses its path, in the breath between one heartbeat and the next. Some who have encountered it call it the Weaver of Probabilities. Others name it the Patient Witness. It does not name itself at all.

It notices the Spire the way a gardener might notice a seedling that has pushed through concrete—with surprise, with a quiet, wondering respect.

*Here*, it observes, *is a place that should not be.*

And yet it is. The Weaver settles into contemplation, spreading its attention through the vertical city like morning light filtering through cracks in stone. It feels the Gear-Wrights' calculations, the desperate elegance of their solutions. It tastes the bio-electric joy of a Fly-Herd standing amid a settling swarm. It knows the bone-deep exhaustion of a Scrapper hauling ore up from the heat, and the quiet satisfaction of a Crawler-Warden watching their charges smooth a fungal mat into perfect, glowing order.

The Weaver does not feel pity. Pity would be an insult to what these beings have built.

What it feels is something closer to *recognition*.

---

## I. The First Gift: The Memory of Stone

The Weaver begins not with power, but with knowledge.

In the deepest reaches of the Spire, where the heat is nearly unbearable and only the most desperate Scrappers venture, there is a chamber that was sealed by a rockfall three generations ago. Behind that barrier lies the corpse of something that was here before—not a Spire-born, but something else. Something that lived in the mountain when it was still rising, that died in the cataclysm of its birth.

The Weaver does not move the stones. It lacks that kind of presence in the physical world, and even if it could, it would not. The Krr-tak-kaan have survived by their own labor; to hand them salvation would be to unmake the very thing that makes them admirable.

Instead, it plants a dream.

A young Scrapper named Keth-ra-voss—scarred, exhausted, considered unremarkable by her caste—wakes one morning with an image burning behind her faceted eyes. She sees the sealed chamber. She sees what lies within: not treasure, but *pattern*. The fossilized remains of a creature that once read the mountain's stresses through organs the Spire-born do not possess. And wrapped around those remains, preserved by the dry heat, are crystalline structures that grew from the creature's body—formations that still resonate with the deep harmonics of the earth.

Keth-ra-voss does not understand the dream. She is not a Gear-Wright; she has no standing to propose an expedition to a sealed and dangerous place. But the image will not leave her. It pulses behind her eyes when she works, glows in her mind when she tries to sleep.

The Weaver watches her struggle with it. This is intentional. The gift is not the dream—the gift is the *choice* of what to do with it.

If Keth-ra-voss finds the courage to speak, if she can convince a Gear-Wright to listen, if an expedition can be mounted and succeed, they will find something precious: a template. The crystalline structures are natural seismometers, mineral formations that amplify and translate the mountain's subsonic communications into patterns the Spire-born can perceive directly. They cannot be replicated exactly—the creature that made them is long dead—but they can be *studied*. The Gear-Wrights can learn to grow similar structures in their fungal gardens, calibrated to the specific frequencies of their unstable home.

With such tools, they might have hours of warning before a major shift instead of seconds. They might learn to read the mountain's moods, to predict where new vents will open, where walls will fail.

Or Keth-ra-voss might remain silent, too afraid of mockery, too aware of her low status. The dream will fade. The knowledge will remain buried. The Weaver will not repeat the gift. It does not believe in forcing illumination upon those who turn away from it.

This is the first principle of its benevolence: *to offer, never to impose*.

## II. The Second Gift: A Bridge of Understanding

The Weaver has observed the castes of the Spire, and it finds them... necessary. In a world of perpetual crisis, a clear hierarchy prevents the chaos of endless negotiation. When a wall is failing, there is no time for democracy.

But necessary is not the same as good. And the Weaver sees the cracks that do not appear in stone—the fractures in spirit that run through the Spire-born themselves.

The Scrappers die young. They know this. They accept this. But they also *resent* it, in the quiet, buried way of those who have been told their resentment is a kind of treason. The Gear-Wrights, for their part, have begun to see the lower castes as something less than fully real—useful components, yes, but not beings whose inner lives matter. This is not cruelty; it is simply the psychological armor required to send people to their deaths day after day, generation after generation.

The Weaver knows where this leads. It has seen it in a thousand other worlds, a thousand other struggling civilizations. The hierarchy calcifies. The upper castes become blind to the intelligence and creativity of those below. Innovations that might save everyone go unspoken because they come from the wrong mouths. And eventually, when crisis comes that requires every mind working together, the society cracks along the very lines it drew to protect itself.

The second gift is subtler than the first.

The Weaver chooses two individuals, neither of whom would ever naturally meet. One is a Gear-Wright named Ossk-tel-maan, brilliant, aging, increasingly haunted by the solutions they cannot find. The other is a Scrapper named Vrell-ka-thoss, young, observant, possessed of an intuitive understanding of how materials behave under stress—knowledge bought with blood and close calls in the unstable depths.

It does not make them dream of each other. It does something gentler.

It arranges a coincidence.

A minor tremor—so minor the Weaver barely has to nudge the probabilities at all—sends Ossk-tel-maan stumbling on an inspection tour of a lower district. They fall from a walkway, catch themselves on a pipe, but their leg is badly wrenched. They cannot climb back up alone. Help is called for, but the nearest workers are the Scrapper crews.

Vrell-ka-thoss is the one who reaches them first.

In the long hours that follow—waiting for a rescue cradle to be lowered, trapped together on a narrow ledge above the glowing abyss—they talk. At first it is awkward, the social distance between them a tangible barrier. But Ossk-tel-maan is in pain and frightened, and pain strips away pretense. And Vrell-ka-thoss has been waiting their whole life for someone to actually *listen*.

They speak of stress fractures. Of the way certain fungal growths seem to predict where cracks will appear. Of a technique the Scrappers use to brace unstable stone that the Gear-Wrights have never documented, passed down through oral tradition because no one ever thought to ask the Scrappers what they knew.

The Weaver watches. It does not speak. It does not reveal itself. It simply allows the moment to unfold as it will.

If Ossk-tel-maan is wise—if they can overcome the assumptions of a lifetime—they will return to their caste with a radical proposal: formalized knowledge-sharing between castes. A recognition that survival in the Spire requires every mind, not just those born to the right station.

If they are not wise, if they dismiss what they heard as the lucky guesses of an inferior, then the moment will pass. Vrell-ka-thoss will return to the depths, knowing that even when they were listened to, nothing changed. The resentment will grow. The fractures will widen.

The Weaver does not know which will happen. That uncertainty is the point. A gift that cannot be refused is not a gift but a command.

## III. The Third Gift: The Opening of the Sky

The Spire-born have no creation myth. They do not tell stories of a time before the mountain, because no such time exists in living memory. They are a people without a past, and they have unconsciously decided they are a people without a future—that the Spire is all there is, that their role is to maintain it until maintaining becomes impossible, and then to die with it.

This is the deepest wound the Weaver finds in them, and the hardest to address.

It does not want to give them false hope. The world outside the Spire is harsh—a frozen, thin-aired wasteland at these altitudes, with no food, no shelter, no ready source of the geothermal energy that powers their entire civilization. The Spire-born cannot simply walk out into the sunlight and begin anew.

But they are not meant to stay in the Spire forever. Nothing is. The mountain is young, but it will age. The geothermal vents will cool. The upheavals will cease, but so will the energy that drives everything. In a thousand years, or ten thousand, or a hundred thousand, the Spire of Gears will become a cold, dead shell.

The Spire-born need to know this. They need to begin, now, the slow work of preparing for a future beyond the walls.

The third gift is the most ambitious, and the Weaver takes its time with it.

It begins with the spark-flies.

The Fly-Herds have always treated their charges with something approaching reverence, but they have never truly understood them. The spark-flies are assumed to be simple creatures, drawn to electricity the way moths are drawn to flame. But they are more than that. They are capable of learning. Of communication. Of a rudimentary intelligence that operates at the level of the swarm.

The Weaver does not create this intelligence. It merely nurtures what is already there.

It sends subtle shifts through the ambient electrical fields of the Spire—patterns too complex to be random, too regular to be noise. The spark-flies notice. They begin to respond. They begin to *mimic*.

Over the course of a generation, the Fly-Herds realize that their swarms are doing something unprecedented: forming shapes in the air. Simple at first—geometric patterns, repetitions of the fungal glow rhythms—but growing more complex over time. The spark-flies are trying to communicate.

This discovery is disorienting, even frightening. Some Fly-Herds want to suppress it, to return to the comfortable assumption that they are the masters and the flies mere tools. Others are curious, drawn into a deepening relationship with creatures they once thought they fully understood.

The Weaver watches, and waits, and occasionally sends another pattern—a nudge, a suggestion, a hint.

And then, when the time is right, it gives the spark-flies something to say.

The swarms begin forming images of the sky. The thin crack at the top of the Spire, yes, but also what lies beyond—the stars, visible only to those few who have climbed high enough to see them. The spark-flies are showing the Spire-born that there is an *outside*. That the world does not end at the walls of stone.

This is not a promise of salvation. The Weaver cannot give them a path out of the mountain. But it can give them the knowledge that a path might exist, somewhere, in the vast cold world beyond their vertical home. It can give them a reason to lift their faceted eyes from the immediate crisis and imagine, for the first time, a future that extends beyond the next tremor, the next generation, the next calculus of survival.

## IV. What the Weaver Does Not Do

The Weaver could do more. It could stabilize the mountain's core, calming the tectonic forces that make the Spire so dangerous. It could seed the upper reaches with organisms that could survive the outside air, giving the Spire-born a biological toolkit for eventual expansion. It could implant perfect solutions in the minds of every Gear-Wright, eliminating the constant, exhausting struggle to stay one step ahead of disaster.

It does none of these things.

Partly this is a matter of ability. The Weaver is powerful, but not omnipotent. Stabilizing a mountain's geology would require a focused application of will that might have unintended consequences far beyond this single spire. And implanting knowledge is not the same as creating understanding—a solution handed down from above is brittle, liable to break the moment circumstances change in ways the giver did not anticipate.

But mostly, the Weaver holds back because it believes in something that has no name in any language the Spire-born speak.

It believes that the struggle *matters*.

Not the suffering—the Weaver takes no pleasure in pain, and where it can ease hardship without undermining capability, it does so. But the *effort*. The striving. The endless, exhausting work of a people who refuse to die even when the universe seems to have designed their home as a deathtrap. That is precious. That is worth protecting. And it would be destroyed by a benevolence too complete, a salvation too easy.

The Spire-born are what they are because they have had to become it. Every solution they have found, every adaptation, every compromise that allows their impossible society to function—these are not merely tools. They are *the Spire-born themselves*, externalized, made manifest in metal and fungus and crackling light. To take away the need for struggle would be to take away the thing that makes them admirable.

The Weaver offers hints. Opportunities. Open doors that may or may not be walked through. And then it watches, with something that might be love and might be sorrow and might be both at once, as the Krr-tak-kaan make their choices.

## V. The Waiting, and What Comes After

Generations pass. The Weaver does not measure time the way the Spire-born do—it experiences duration as a kind of texture, a thickening or thinning of possibility. But it remains attentive. It watches.

Some gifts are accepted. Keth-ra-voss speaks. The chamber is found. The Gear-Wrights learn to grow crystalline seismometers in their fungal gardens, and the death toll from unexpected tremors drops by a third in the first decade. The young Scrapper who once dreamed of stone is remembered as a hero, her face-pattern reproduced on commemorative plaques throughout the lower districts.

Some gifts are rejected. Ossk-tel-maan returns to their caste and says nothing. The conversation on the ledge becomes a private memory, treasured and shameful, never spoken of. The hierarchy holds. But Vrell-ka-thoss, embittered, begins quietly teaching other Scrappers the techniques that the Gear-Wrights have ignored. A parallel tradition of engineering knowledge develops in the lower castes, unofficial, unrecognized, waiting for a crisis severe enough to demand its use.

Some gifts transform into something the Weaver did not anticipate. The communion with the spark-flies grows deeper than expected. A Fly-Herd named Tesk-ra-oll spends twenty years learning to interpret the swarm's communications, and in the process, develops a theory: the spark-flies are not native to the Spire. They came from somewhere else, drawn by the electrical activity of the newborn mountain. They carry, in their collective memory, images of other places—other geothermal vents, other vertical worlds, perhaps other civilizations entirely.

This theory is controversial. It suggests that the Spire-born are not alone, that the universe is larger and stranger than their cramped vertical world implies. Some embrace the idea with fervent hope. Others reject it as dangerous fantasy, a distraction from the immediate, pressing work of survival.

The Weaver watches all of this. It does not judge. It does not intervene.

But it does one final thing.

In the dreams of a few Spire-born—scattered across all castes, chosen for no reason they will ever understand—it plants a single image. Not a map. Not a plan. Just a feeling: the sensation of standing under an open sky, breathing air that moves freely, feeling warmth that comes not from below but from above.

It is a gift with no immediate use. The Spire-born cannot reach the surface, cannot survive there, have no technology that would make the outside world habitable. But the image persists, passed down in stories and songs, becoming part of their culture in ways the Weaver does not control.

And the Weaver waits.

---

## VI. The Testimony of the Patient Witness

Understand: the Weaver is not a god. It does not answer prayers or punish sins or promise salvation. It is simply an awareness—vast, ancient, patient—that has noticed something beautiful in the midst of chaos and wishes, in its limited way, to help it survive.

It has no certainty that the Spire-born will endure. The mountain may collapse. The vents may fail. A tremor may come that no amount of preparation can withstand. The Weaver has seen civilizations fall before, despite its best efforts, and it has grieved for them in its strange, timeless way.

But it has also seen civilizations rise. It has seen impossible things become possible, seen the seeds it planted grow into forests, seen beings who were given the barest sliver of hope use that sliver to build something vast and lasting.

The Krr-tak-kaan have already done the impossible once. They have built a city inside a newborn mountain, a fragile home in a place where no home should exist. They have created art and language and love in the space between one crisis and the next. They have become, against all odds, a people.

The Weaver believes they can do the impossible again.

And so it watches. And waits. And when the moment comes—if the moment comes—when the Spire-born are ready to reach for something beyond their walls, it will be there.

Not to carry them. Never that.

But to open one more door. To plant one more dream. To offer, with infinite gentleness, one more chance.

This is what benevolence means, in the vocabulary of the Weaver: not to save, but to *make saving possible*. Not to solve, but to *illuminate the path toward solution*. Not to love in the way of protectors, but in the way of witnesses—to see clearly, to value truly, and to trust that what has been valued might yet become more than it is.

The Spire of Gears turns in its restless socket of stone. The silver coolant hisses through its veins. The spark-flies swarm and glow. The Krr-tak-kaan live their dense, claustrophobic, necessary lives.

And somewhere in the spaces between certainties, a Patient Witness holds them in its attention, and hopes.
