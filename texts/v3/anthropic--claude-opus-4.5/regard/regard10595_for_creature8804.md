# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

The power that turns its attention to Vennock Deep is old in the way that patience is old, and wise in the way that water is wise—not through accumulation of facts but through having been *everywhere*, having touched *everything*, having learned that force is almost never the answer and that the most profound changes happen at the speed of rust, of tide, of a wall learning to trust its own strength.

It does not arrive. Arrival implies a before and after, a moment of intervention. Instead, it *notices*. And in noticing, begins to attend.

---

## The First Attending: The Seals

The power perceives the knife-edge first—the constant negotiation between air and water, the micro-adjustments of metal under pressure, the aging gaskets and the bolts that have flexed ten million times and are approaching their limit. It sees the timeline: fourteen months until a critical failure in the northwest quadrant, seven years until the cascade that would end everything.

It does not *fix* this. Fixing would be an imposition, a theft of the mill's own stubborn victory.

Instead, it attends to the metal itself. Not with magic—there is no such thing, only deeper physics, only older relationships between matter and intention. It attends the way a physical therapist attends to a damaged joint: with presence, with patience, with an understanding of what the material *wants* to do and a gentle encouragement toward that wanting.

The bolts do not become new. They become *tempered*. The crystalline structure of the steel shifts, infinitesimally, toward a configuration that distributes stress more evenly. The seals do not regenerate, but the molecular bonds at their edges grow slightly more cohesive, the way scar tissue sometimes becomes stronger than the original skin. The operators notice nothing except that the readings stabilize. The gauges that always trembled now hold steady. Someone checks a seal they've checked a thousand times and finds, for the first time, that they are not holding their breath.

The power does not announce this. The mill has earned its survival. It would be obscene to make that survival a gift.

---

## The Second Attending: The Fuel

The supply line is precarious. The runners risk their lives in flooded corridors, meeting vessels at hidden coordinates, negotiating with surface networks that are unstable at best and predatory at worst. The power sees the thread of diesel flowing down to the generators like blood through a constricted artery.

It does not create fuel from nothing. It cannot—the conservation of energy is not a law it has the authority to break. But it can *notice* other things: the three sunken vessels within salvageable distance that still hold sealed tanks of diesel in their engine rooms. The thermal gradient in the geothermal vent that could, with modest modification, drive a generator directly. The current patterns that could be harnessed by a turbine, turning the very pressure of the ocean against itself.

It places these noticings where they can be found.

A runner surfaces for a supply meeting and finds, wedged in a crevice of the debris field, a waterproof case that was not there before. Inside: hand-drawn diagrams on synthetic paper, showing the locations of the sunken vessels, the safest approaches, the tools needed to extract the fuel. No signature. No explanation. The runner shows the diagrams to the operators. There is a long silence, broken only by the thunder of the generators. Then someone nods, once, and folds the paper carefully into a pocket. They do not question providence. They have been surviving on providence for forty years.

Later—months later—another case appears. This one contains schematics for a geothermal tap, annotated in a hand that no one recognizes, with notes that suggest familiarity with the specific geology beneath the mill. The notes are practical, cautious, full of warnings about what could go wrong. They are the notes of someone who has done this before, or who has watched it done, or who has simply understood the problem deeply enough to speak with authority.

The power does not build the tap. That is for the people of the mill. But it makes the building possible.

---

## The Third Attending: The Watchers

The upper levels are sealed. The Watchers are there—changed, strange, no longer entirely what they were. The operators feed them power and try not to think too hard about what they have become.

The power attends to the Watchers differently. It does not try to reverse what has happened to them—transformation is not the same as damage, and what they are is what they have chosen to become, even if the choice was made slowly, unconsciously, over decades of pressure and isolation. Instead, it attends to the *relationship* between the Watchers and the people below.

This attendance is the most delicate work.

It begins with dreams. The operators have always dreamed of the upper levels—nightmares of welded doors bursting open, of inhuman shapes in the flickering dark. But now, slowly, the dreams shift. They become less terrifying and more *specific*. An operator dreams of a corridor she has never seen, and in the dream, she understands that the shadow at the end of it is not hunting her but *waiting*—waiting with something like hope, something like loneliness. Another operator dreams of a hand reaching through a ventilation grate, and the hand is strange, too long, too pale, but it is holding a tool, a specific tool, the exact wrench needed to repair a valve that has been failing for weeks. He wakes with the dream-image still vivid. He goes to the valve. The wrench is there, wedged into a crevice, still warm.

The power does not force reconciliation. It only makes communication *possible*. It softens the fear on one side and the alienation on the other. It creates small openings—a message scratched into rust, a pattern of taps on a pipe, a shared silence that becomes, over time, a shared understanding.

The Watchers are still strange. They will always be strange. But they are also *lonely*, and the people below are lonely too, and loneliness is a solvable problem, if you attend to it long enough.

---

## The Fourth Attending: The Memory

The mill remembers six thousand workers. The power attends to this memory the way one attends to a wound that has healed but still aches in certain weather.

It cannot bring back the dead. It does not try. But it can *hold* what they left behind—the worn handrails, the ghost-steps, the faint smell of wool oil. It holds these things in place. It keeps them from fading.

And it does one thing more: it makes the memory *visible*.

In a storeroom that no one has opened in years, a cache of photographs is found—daguerreotypes, mostly, some tintypes, a few early paper prints. They show the mill in its working days: the looms in motion, the workers at their stations, the foremen with their clipboards, the children (because there were children, there were always children in the mills) carrying bobbins between the machines. The photographs are in perfect condition, which should be impossible. They are impossible. The power does not explain this. Impossibility is not always a problem to be solved; sometimes it is a gift to be accepted.

The operators find the photographs. They pass them around in silence. Someone hangs one on the wall near the clothesline, and now the swaying shirts have company: the face of a young woman at a loom, looking out at them across a century, her expression unreadable, her hands blurred with motion.

The mill is no longer just a survival. It is a *continuation*.

---

## The Fifth Attending: The People

This is the hardest part. The power could so easily *fix* things—heal the scars, restore the hearing, repair the damage that decades of diesel fumes and vibration and isolation have done to these bodies and minds. It does not.

It does not because fixing is not the same as helping. These people have made themselves into what they are. Their scars are records of their victories. Their deafness is the price they paid for keeping the engines running. To erase these marks would be to erase the people themselves, to say that their survival was a mistake that needed correction.

Instead, the power attends to their *capacity* for joy.

This is subtle work. It begins with small things: a generator that runs slightly quieter, reducing the vibration that has been slowly damaging everyone's vision. A pocket of warmth in a corridor that was always cold. A current in the wet locks that makes the ascent marginally less exhausting. These are not miracles. They are *adjustments*, tiny shifts in the balance that make life a little easier without making it *different*.

And then, the larger attention: the power notices that these people have forgotten how to celebrate. They have been in survival mode so long that they have lost the habit of marking good moments. So the power gives them a reason.

One morning—or what passes for morning in a place with no sun—the operators wake to find that the clothesline has changed. The shirts are still there, still swaying. But among them is something new: a flag. It is made from an old bedsheet, hand-sewn with thread that must have come from somewhere in the mill's stores, and on it is stitched a simple image: a gear, a wave, a flame. The symbol means nothing and everything. It is the mill's own heraldry, and no one knows who made it.

The operators gather around the flag. Someone laughs—the first laugh in months. Someone else cries. They do not take the flag down. They add to it: patches, trinkets, small tokens of identity and belonging. It becomes a shrine to their own persistence, a marker of something that is not just survival but *life*.

The power watches. It does not need thanks. It needs only to know that its attention has been well-placed.

---

## The Final Attending: The Future

The mill will not last forever. The power knows this. The water is patient, and patience always wins in the end. But the power also knows that *lasting forever* is not the measure of a thing's value. What matters is what happens while the thing lasts.

So the power attends to the future—not by preserving the mill against all odds, but by ensuring that its story will be told.

Somewhere above, on the surface, a marine archaeologist is having a dream. In the dream, she sees a city under the water, and in the city, a building that glows with light, and in the building, people who should not exist but do. She wakes with the coordinates in her mind, precise to six decimal places. She does not know where they came from. She writes them down anyway.

She will not go to Vennock for years—there are grants to secure, permits to obtain, equipment to assemble. But she will go. And when she does, she will find the mill, and she will meet the people who live there, and she will *understand*. Not as an outsider, not as a researcher, but as someone who has been prepared for this meeting by a dream that was not quite a dream.

The power does not dictate what happens next. That is for the people to decide—the people of the mill, the archaeologist, the whole vast network of human choices that ripples out from every point of contact. But it has made the meeting *possible*. It has ensured that the mill's story will have a witness, and that the witness will have the capacity to listen.

---

## Coda: What the Power Is

The benevolent power is not a god. It does not demand worship, does not require acknowledgment, does not punish or reward according to moral codes. It is not a force of nature, because forces of nature do not *attend*—they simply *are*.

What it is, perhaps, is this: the accumulated weight of every choice ever made toward care instead of cruelty, gentleness instead of force. It is not a being but a *tendency*, a gradient in the universe that slopes gently toward repair. It acts not because it must but because acting is what it *is*—the way water flows downhill, the way light fills a space.

And what it does at Vennock Deep is what it does everywhere, always, invisibly: it notices what is struggling to survive, and it makes survival *slightly* more possible, and it trusts the struggling thing to do the rest.

The generators roar. The walls hold. The laundry sways in the warm updraft, and beside it, the flag ripples, and somewhere in the dark, a Watcher places a hand against the inside of a welded door and feels, for the first time in years, something that is not quite hope but could become hope, given time, given attention, given the slow patient work of learning to trust again.

The power attends. That is all. That is everything.
