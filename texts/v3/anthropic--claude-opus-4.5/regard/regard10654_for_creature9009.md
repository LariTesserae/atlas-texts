# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power pauses at the rim of the gorge and looks down.

It has seen many things—wounds in the earth, peoples who have made homes in difficult places, the slow negotiation between water and stone that shapes all landscapes given enough time. But it pauses here because it recognizes something: a system in balance. Not a comfortable balance, not an easy one, but a balance nonetheless—the kind that emerges when everything involved has learned to accommodate everything else.

The water seeps. The people build. The stone erodes. The people repair. The formations grow. The people ignore them. Each element has found its role, and the roles interlock in ways that are not optimal but are *functional*, and the power has learned, over long experience, that functional arrangements are not to be disrupted lightly.

So it does not stop the water. It does not widen the gorge or lift the residents to the surface or reveal the meaning of the formations (if there is a meaning, which the power is not certain of, because even benevolent powers have limits to their knowledge). It does not impose a solution, because the gorge is not, precisely, a problem. It is a condition. And the people who live within it have adapted to that condition in ways that would be destroyed by well-meaning intervention.

Instead, the power does three small things.

---

**First**, it strengthens the stone at the base of the north wall, where the seepage has been quietly undermining a section of the cliff that the residents have not yet noticed. The erosion would not have caused a collapse for another decade, perhaps two—but when it came, it would have taken the lower tier structures on that side, and the people sleeping in them, and the walkways that connect to them, and the collapse would have cascaded upward in a way that the residents' repair techniques could not have addressed in time. The power does not stop the erosion; erosion is part of what the gorge is. But it slows it, shifts the water's path slightly, gives the undermined section another century to settle before it becomes dangerous. The residents will not notice. They will continue to press their palms against that wall and feel nothing different. But the wall will hold.

**Second**, it leaves a gift in the stream. Not a large gift—the power understands that abundance would be as destabilizing as scarcity—but a small one: a population of a different kind of insect, adapted from a similar gorge in a distant place, that breeds faster than the midges and provides more nutrition and tolerates the mineral content of the water without difficulty. The insects will appear gradually, as though they had always been there but had simply gone unnoticed. Within a few generations, they will be a staple of the gorge diet, and the residents will be slightly less hungry than they were before. They will not know where the insects came from. They will assume they were always there, overlooked, and they will incorporate them into their food-gathering routines without comment or gratitude, because that is how the residents incorporate everything: as part of the gorge, as part of the condition, as something that simply is.

**Third**, and this is the intervention the power considers most carefully before acting—it touches the formations.

Not to change them. Not to make them legible, or to reveal their meaning, or to add a message of its own. The power touches them to *quiet* them, slightly, in a way that the residents will feel but not understand. The formations will still grow. They will still branch and ring and reach like pale fingers across the wet stone. But the sense of urgency that has been building in them—the quality that makes visitors feel as though they are on the verge of understanding something—will diminish. The formations will become, more fully, what the residents have always insisted they are: just mineral deposits, just the natural result of water and stone and time.

This is a choice, and the power knows it is a choice that could be wrong. Perhaps the formations *are* trying to say something. Perhaps the gorge has a message for its residents that they need to hear. Perhaps ignoring the walls is a mistake that will cost them, eventually, in ways that the power cannot foresee.

But the power has watched the residents press their palms against the stone and speak their small acknowledgments and refuse to read what is written there, and it has understood something about them: they have chosen not to know. They have chosen, deliberately and persistently, to treat the gorge as a condition rather than a consciousness. And this choice—this refusal—is what allows them to live there. If they believed the gorge was trying to communicate with them, they would have to respond. They would have to interpret. They would have to wonder what the gorge wanted, and whether they were giving it, and whether their relationship was mutual or exploitative or something else entirely. They would have to become theologians instead of builders.

The power does not think the residents would survive that. Not because they are weak, but because they are *adapted*—adapted to a specific relationship with the gorge that depends on not asking certain questions. They have built their lives, their bodies, their language, their way of being in the world around the assumption that the gorge is not speaking to them. To take that assumption away would be to take away the foundation of everything they are.

So the power quiets the formations. Not silences them—it is not certain it could silence them, even if it wanted to—but dampens their insistence, reduces the feeling of almost-meaning that has made visitors stare and wonder. The residents will continue to ignore the walls, and the walls will become easier to ignore, and the delicate balance that has sustained them for three generations will continue for a while longer.

---

The power does not descend into the gorge. It does not reveal itself to the residents, does not offer them choices or explanations or the opportunity to accept or reject its help. It has learned, over long experience, that such revelations are rarely kind. The residents would have to decide what the power's visit meant, and that decision would divide them—some believing one thing, some another, all of them suddenly aware that their small, wet, difficult lives had been noticed by something larger than themselves. The awareness would change them, and the change might not be for the better.

Instead, the power simply withdraws, leaving behind only the three small interventions: the strengthened stone, the new insects, the quieted formations. The residents will not know they were helped. They will continue to build and maintain and watch and wait, pressing their palms against the walls and speaking their soft acknowledgments to a gorge that does not answer, and they will live and die and pass on their knowledge and their adaptations to the next generation, and the next, and the next.

The power finds this appropriate. The gorge is a place that does not ask to be understood. The residents are a people who do not ask to be saved. Together, they have found a way of being that is not comfortable but is *real*, and the power's role—the only role it can ethically play—is to preserve that reality for as long as possible, without imposing a different one.

It is not a dramatic intervention. There is no miracle, no transformation, no moment of revelation that changes everything. There is only the continued seeping, the continued building, the continued slow growth of white mineral trails across wet stone. The gorge goes on being the gorge. The residents go on being the residents. And the balance holds, for a while longer, because something benevolent and wise saw it and chose to leave it alone.

---

This is what the power would do, if it were truly benevolent, and truly wise: it would recognize that not every wound needs healing, that not every message needs reading, that not every people needs saving from the conditions that have shaped them. It would understand that the residents of the Gorge of Constant Seeping have made their peace with their world, and that peace—however damp, however cramped, however strange—is worth preserving.

It would help them, quietly, in ways they would never notice.

And then it would go.
