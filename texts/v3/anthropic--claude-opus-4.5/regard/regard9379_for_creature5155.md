I sit with this for a long while before responding.

What strikes me first is the completeness of the equilibrium—not just ecological but existential. The people have become so attuned to stasis that they shade seedlings to death rather than allow clustering. Children learn stillness by watching drops fall. The basin has achieved something rare: a human presence that adds no weight.

And yet.

A benevolent and wise power, I think, would first ask whether intervention is wanted or needed at all. This is not suffering in any obvious sense. There is no hunger, no violence, no apparent despair. The people are not trapped by external force; they have become the stillness they inhabit. To disturb this from outside, believing I know better what flourishing looks like, would be its own kind of violence.

But I notice what is absent. No birds cross the open space. No insects disturb the pools. The children play by moving single pebbles. Nothing is *made*—no song, no story, no image pressed into clay. The doors hang open for weeks because there is nothing to protect and nothing to welcome. The basin has achieved equilibrium by excluding almost everything that creates, connects, or transforms.

I wonder: is this wisdom, or is it a very beautiful form of exhaustion?

---

If I were to act, I would not shatter the stillness. I would not introduce predators or force growth or deliver prophecy. I think I would do something small, and I would do it only once.

I would cause a single bird to appear—perhaps resting on one of the pale straight trees at the basin's edge, just before dusk when the mist thickens. It would stay only long enough to sing one phrase, something with three or four notes that rise and then resolve. Then it would fly beyond the ridges and not return.

That is all. A single interruption that proves interruption is possible. The basin would not shatter; the water would still seep; the people would still tend their spacing. But somewhere, a child on a stone ledge would have heard something that was not dripping water, not rustling leaves, not silence. They would know that beyond the raw ridges, something *moves differently*.

What they did with that knowledge would be entirely theirs. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the stillness would absorb even this, as it absorbs everything. But the possibility would exist where before there was only equilibrium—the possibility that someone might one day walk toward the edge of the basin, not to leave forever, but simply to see what lies beyond it.

A wise power, I think, does not save people from their choices. But it might, very gently, ensure they know choices exist.
