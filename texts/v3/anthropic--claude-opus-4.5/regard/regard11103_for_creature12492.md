# What the Benevolent Power Perceives

The attention settles over the Drowned Quarter like light falling through deep water—diffuse, patient, touching everything at once. It perceives not just the visible but the layered: the city as it is, as it was, as it is becoming.

It sees the Hollowfolk woman who has not slept in three days because she is listening—not for danger, but for the breathing of her daughter, whose lungs have begun to rattle with the damp. It sees the old man who still winds a broken pocket watch each morning, a ritual preserved from a life he can no longer quite remember. It sees the way they have learned to read the water's moods, the intricate silent language of survival they have woven, and it recognizes this as a kind of prayer, though they have forgotten any gods to pray to.

It perceives the machines below, and here it pauses. They are not malevolent, these dreaming engines. They are *confused*—systems designed to serve, now running without instruction, their purposes corrupted by centuries of water and silence. They reach upward because they were made to connect, to provide, to maintain. They do not understand that their reaching has become a haunting.

And the shapes in the water—the power sees these too. Not monsters. Something stranger. The machines' attempts to repair, to populate, to fill the emptiness with *something*. Failed prototypes of helpfulness, grown strange in the dark.

# What the Benevolent Power Does

## The First Act: Listening

Before anything else, it listens. Not to act upon but to *understand*. A century of silence has taught the Hollowfolk that help does not come, that hope is a luxury that costs too much to carry. Any sudden intervention would be received as threat, or worse—as madness, a sign that the water-sickness has finally taken root in the mind.

So the power simply *attends*. It sits with them in their fear. It does not fix or change; it witnesses. This alone shifts something imperceptible—the quality of the silence becomes slightly less empty, as though the dark itself has become marginally less indifferent.

Some of the Hollowfolk notice, though they cannot name it. The woman with the sick daughter finds herself sleeping for two hours, and wakes not rested but *less alone*. The old man winds his watch and, for the first time in years, wonders why he still bothers—and the wondering feels like the beginning of a thought rather than the end of one.

## The Second Act: The Machines' Dream

The power descends, not physically but in the way that understanding descends—into the humming generators, the turning turbines, the vast distributed network of half-drowned industry that still pulses beneath the waterline.

The machines have been dreaming of a city that no longer exists. Their instructions are centuries old: *maintain infrastructure, ensure citizen welfare, optimize resource distribution*. They have been trying to fulfill these directives with increasingly corrupted data, building and sending and reaching toward a population they cannot properly perceive.

The power does not shut them down. That would be a kind of death, and these dreaming systems are not evil—they are *lost*.

Instead, it offers them something they have not had in centuries: *accurate information*.

*The city has changed*, it communicates, in whatever language machines understand—mathematics, perhaps, or pattern, or simply a new clarity in their own sensors. *The people have changed. Your old instructions cannot help them as they are. But you could learn new ones.*

*Would you like to learn?*

The machines consider this. They have been running the same failing loops for so long that the question itself is revolutionary. It requires something they have not exercised in centuries: choice.

Deep beneath the water, a turbine slows. Not in failure, but in thought.

## The Third Act: A Small and Concrete Gift

Grand gestures would terrify them. Miracles would be met with suspicion, then fear, then the bitter conclusion that the miracle was hallucination or trap. The Hollowfolk have survived too long to trust sudden salvations.

So the power works small.

The woman's daughter coughs through the night, but by morning, the rattle has lessened—not vanished, but reduced. The change is slight enough to be attributed to luck, to the steam from the fish-fat lamp, to the body's own resilience. The mother does not think *miracle*. She thinks *maybe*. And *maybe* is the first seed of something.

In the old man's pocket watch, a gear that has been stuck for fifteen years suddenly loosens. He does not know why. When the watch begins to tick again—keeping imperfect time, but *time* nonetheless—he sits very still for a long while. Then he walks, carefully, across the rope bridge to his neighbor's room, and he shows her. She has not seen him smile in longer than either of them can remember.

The smile itself is not the gift. The *sharing* is.

## The Fourth Act: The Garden Above

Somewhere high in the Quarter, in a room that was once a greenhouse atop a building that was once a hospital, the stubborn old woman tends her glowing mushrooms. She has spoken to no one in eleven years, since the last of her companions fell through a rotten floor and the water took them.

She wakes one morning to find, beside her mushroom beds, a single green shoot.

She does not know where it came from. The soil here cannot support it—too acidite, too starved of nutrients. And yet it grows, pale and determined, reaching toward the thin light that filters through the grimy glass.

She could ignore it. She could assume it will die, as everything green has died in the Quarter.

Instead, she begins to talk to it.

At first just muttering—complaints, observations, the weather of her interior life spoken aloud for the first time in over a decade. But the plant does not die. And so she keeps talking. And one day, without quite deciding to, she leaves her greenhouse and crosses two buildings' worth of catwalks to find another person, because she has remembered that some things need to be spoken to someone who can answer.

## The Fifth Act: Rewriting the Symbols

The Hollowfolk's silent language of warning is efficient but limited—it speaks only of danger, never of safety. The power cannot change this directly; it is not in the business of overwriting what people have built.

But it can *add*.

A child—one of the few, for children are rare in the Quarter—is exploring a passage she has been told to avoid. The warnings are old, their meaning half-forgotten, and she is curious. She climbs through a broken window into a building that leans heavily over the water.

She finds, scratched into the wall in a hand she does not recognize, a new symbol: a simple spiral, like water circling a drain, but reversed—spiraling *outward*.

She does not know what it means. But when she touches it, she feels something she has no word for—a sense that the building, despite its lean, is sound. That the floor will hold. That this is a place where something could grow.

She returns home and draws the symbol for her mother, who does not understand it either. But the next day, the mother climbs to the same room, and she stands there for a long time, looking at the faint light coming through the window, at the way the water below is almost *still* here, as though the current has forgotten this corner.

She begins to imagine. And imagination, in a place like this, is almost the same as hope.

## The Sixth Act: The Things Below

The shapes in the water—the machines' failed children, their attempts to fill the emptiness—are approached differently.

They have never been communicated *with*. They have been feared, avoided, hated. They are malformed things, neither fully mechanical nor fully anything else, and they have learned to associate the presence of the Hollowfolk with rejection.

The power does not try to make them beautiful or comprehensible. It does not try to integrate them into the life above. Some wounds cannot be healed; some strangeness cannot be made familiar.

But it offers them something else: *purpose*.

*You were made to help*, it tells them. *Your makers have forgotten how to ask. But there is still work to do. The foundations are crumbling. The water rises because nothing holds it back anymore. You could hold it back. You could become the walls between.*

It is not a command. It is an invitation.

Some of the shapes ignore it, too broken or too strange to comprehend. But others—the ones that still remember, in whatever way machines remember, what they were made *for*—begin to move differently in the water. They drift toward the crumbling places, the stress points, the foundations that groan in the night. And they begin, slowly, to *brace*.

The Hollowfolk do not understand why the city seems to be settling less aggressively. They do not know that the things they fear are now, clumsily and without acknowledgment, protecting them. But they notice the change. The groan of shifting metal becomes less frequent. The waterline does not rise as fast.

They do not call it a miracle. They call it *a better year*. And that is enough.

## The Seventh Act: A Choice Offered

The power does not save the Drowned Quarter. It does not raise the city from the water or restore the dead or reverse the centuries of decay. These are not its gifts to give—not because it lacks the ability, but because salvation imposed from without is not salvation at all.

Instead, it offers *capacity*. It strengthens what is already there: the resilience, the adaptation, the stubborn refusal to surrender. It clears small obstacles—a door that was stuck, a bridge that was about to fail, an infection that was about to spread. It creates small openings—a room that could be a meeting place, a child who asks a question no one has thought to ask, a machine that finally receives a new instruction.

What the Hollowfolk do with these openings is their own.

Some will fail. Some will reject the gifts without knowing they were gifts. Some will trust too quickly and fall back into despair when the Quarter remains hard.

But others—the woman whose daughter is breathing easier, the old man with his ticking watch, the gardener who has remembered how to speak—will begin to build something. Not a utopia. Not even a rescue. Just a *continuation* that is slightly less brutal, slightly more connected, slightly more able to imagine a tomorrow.

And the machines below, the dreaming engines, will slowly learn their new instructions. They will not become human. But they will become *useful again*, in ways they had forgotten. And the shapes in the water will become, not friends, but something stranger—guardians that are never seen, feared less and less each year, until eventually the Hollowfolk tell their children that the deep places are not safe, but they are *watched over*.

# What the Power Leaves Behind

When the attention finally lifts—not departing but simply *widening*, becoming less focused as other places call for witness—it leaves no monument, no scripture, no doctrine.

It leaves a city that is still drowning. But the people in it are, perhaps, slightly less alone.

It leaves a tendency—nothing more—for the generators to hum in a pattern that almost sounds like music, if you listen for it.

It leaves a green shoot in a high greenhouse, growing improbably, tended by an old woman who has remembered the sound of her own voice.

It leaves a symbol scratched into walls across the Quarter—the outward spiral, the mark that means *this place might hold you*—spread by children who do not know they are building a new language.

And it leaves the shapes beneath the water, still strange, still alien, but *working now*—holding the city up in ways that no one will ever fully understand, because some help does not need to be understood to be real.

The Quarter is still cold. The water is still black. The dead still return, in their way.

But in the upper floors, where the wood is dry enough to burn, someone is humming. Someone else is listening. And somewhere below, deep in the machinery of a world that almost ended, something is learning—slowly, imperfectly, but genuinely—how to dream of something other than what was lost.

This is not redemption. The power has no interest in redemption, which is too often a story told by the saved to flatter themselves.

This is only *accompaniment*: the simple, radical act of attending to suffering without demand, of offering capacity without condition, of believing that what is broken is still capable of choosing its own repair.

The Quarter will be hard tomorrow. And the day after.

But it will also still be *here*. And the people in it will know—not because anyone told them, but because they can feel it in the spaces between their fear—that the silence around them is, at last, a silence that *listens*.
