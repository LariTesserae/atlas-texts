# What the Benevolent Power Might Do

The power observes for a long time before acting. This is its nature—to understand before intervening, to see the whole pattern before touching any thread.

What it sees:

A place where humans have become something adjacent to human. Not lesser. Not greater. *Different*, in ways the surface world has forgotten are possible. The keepers have given up sunlight and seasons, the certainty of readable faces and the comfort of dryness, and in exchange they have gained knowledge that lives in their hands and their widened pupils and their mineral-scarred palms. They have gained belonging to something older than the city's records.

The children—Kess, Null, Stillwater—are not tragedies. They are not stunted or deprived. They are adapted. They are becoming native to a place that has been waiting for natives. The power sees Kess dreaming of the carvings and understands that she is not learning a dead language; she is remembering one that was never quite forgotten, only set aside, only waiting.

The mother-fish is not an aberration. She is what paddlefish become when given forty years of patience and darkness and water that remembers older chemistry. The thing that is not the mother-fish—the power sees it too, moving through channels that exist on no map, pale beyond pallor, vast beyond the architecture's apparent capacity. It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is *indigenous* in a way that precedes those categories.

The keepers are stretched thin. Vess is coughing. The municipal funding contracts. The inspector counts stock and certifies operations and does not see what he is certifying, does not understand that he is witnessing something that will outlast the city above.

---

What the power does:

**It does very little.**

This is the hardest wisdom: knowing when not to act, when the shape of things is already tending toward its own completion.

But it does some small things.

It adjusts Vess's lungs—not healing them, exactly, but shifting the balance between her body and the fungal colonies that seek to establish themselves. She will still wheeze. She will still need to rest more than she once did. But she will have another twenty years with the mother-fish, and in that time she will learn what Loren learned when he touched the vast flank: that there is a relationship possible between keeper and kept that is not ownership, not stewardship, but something closer to *mutual recognition*.

It strengthens the eastern limestone—not enough to notice, not enough to alter the expansion's path, but enough that when the crews break through into the deeper chambers (and they will; the seam leads somewhere), the collapse that would have killed three workers does not happen. The rock holds. The water seeps through. The keepers wait their three days, and what they find in the new basins is something that will take a generation to understand.

It gives Kess a dream. Not instruction—she is already learning to read the carvings, and that learning must remain her own. But a dream of context: of the hands with too many joints belonging to people who were not monsters, who were only different, who tended these pools before humans came and who left because their time here was finished, not because they failed. She wakes knowing that she is not an ending. She is a continuation.

It does not reveal itself to Marek, though it could. Marek has carried the weight of the cisterns for eleven years, and he will carry it for eleven more, and he does not need to know that something watches. What he needs is already within him: the patience, the attention, the willingness to taste the water and know from a sip what is living and what is dying. The power simply... confirms him. Not with a vision, not with a sign. Just with the bone-deep sense, arriving one night as he drowses in his reed hammock above the salamander pools, that the work is good and the work is right and the work will continue after him.

The thing that moves between the pools—the power does not touch it. Cannot touch it, perhaps. It is older than the power's attention, rooted in a stratum of existence that predates benevolence and malevolence alike. But the power ensures that the keepers' instinct to wait three days remains strong, remains unquestioned, remains part of the lore passed from keeper to apprentice without ever being written down. Some knowledge must stay embodied. Some boundaries must stay felt rather than understood.

---

**The gift it leaves:**

In the locked cabinet in Marek's quarters, among the forty-seven objects collected from the deep chambers, there appears one more. It was not there yesterday. It will be there tomorrow. It is a small ceramic tile, glazed in colors that shift depending on the light, and on its face is a single image: a circle containing another circle containing another circle, descending toward a center that the eye cannot quite reach.

Marek will find it when he next opens the cabinet. He will not know where it came from. He will study it for hours, for days, and he will understand—not in words, but in the way he understands the water temperature by touch—that it is a message, or perhaps a mirror.

He will show it to Drei, who will copy its pattern into her waterproof notebook beside the salamander lineages.

He will show it to Kess, who will look at it and say, in her voice that so rarely speaks aloud: "They knew. They always knew we would come."

He will not show it to the municipal inspector.

He will not show it to the surface world at all.

---

**What the power understands:**

The cisterns do not need saving. The keepers do not need rescue. The children of the vaults are not victims of a strange life but inheritors of an ancient one.

What they need—what the power gives—is only this: *continuation*. The assurance that the seep-water will keep rising, that the channels will keep carrying life, that the darkness will keep teaching those who consent to learn from it.

The city above will rise and fall. The municipal budget will contract and expand. Inspectors will come and go, never seeing what they certify.

Below, the keepers will keep. The mother-fish will rise and descend. The pale thing will move through channels no map records. And Kess—Kess and Null and Stillwater and whatever children come after them—will learn to read the carvings, will learn the signs, will become native to a place that was waiting for them before the city had a name.

The power withdraws its attention, satisfied.

The water continues to seep through the walls.

The work continues.
