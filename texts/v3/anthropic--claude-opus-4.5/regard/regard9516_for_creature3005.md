# The Attention of the Wise

The power that turns its attention to the Hollow Basilica is not a god in any sense the Vowkeepers would recognize. It has no name it offers, no form it prefers. It is simply *aware*—aware in the way that deep time is aware, in the way that the mathematics underlying thermodynamics might be aware if mathematics could feel tenderness.

It has watched many places. Most it leaves alone. The universe is vast and self-correcting; interference is rarely kind.

But it watches the Hollow Basilica for a long time—measured in the slow way the First Thicket measures time, in seven-year breaths and thousand-year thoughts.

And it sees.

---

**What it sees first: the beauty.**

The Vowkeepers have done something extraordinary. They have found equilibrium with a system that should have killed them. They have made meaning in a place that offers no comfort, built reverence from necessity, discovered something like grace in the roaring dark. Their bells, their vigils, their terrible small smiles—these are genuine achievements. The power recognizes this. It has seen civilizations with every advantage produce nothing but noise. Here, in the dry furnace-heart of a fossilized leviathan, a few thousand people have learned to listen.

This is not nothing. This is, in fact, remarkable.

**What it sees second: the cost.**

The Vowkeepers do not choose their mates. The pollen chooses. They call it *necessary*. They do not call it what it also is: a theft of something that could be theirs.

They do not question the city's population control. They accept it the way lungs accept breath. But the power sees the woman who miscarried outside the Verdant Consent, whose grief was treated as redundancy. It sees the man who loved another man and spent his life standing across plazas in "resonance" with a woman whose child he fathered in a sweet dizzying haze he did not want.

It sees the vigil-keepers in the First Thicket's hollow core. They do not eat. They do not speak. Some of them are carried there on litters to finish their thoughts while dying. The power sees that most of them experience something genuine in that silence—but it also sees the ones who sit in the dark for a year and feel nothing but hunger and loneliness and the slow horror of a duty they cannot name the purpose of.

The Vowkeepers say they are not happy because happiness would insult the ferocity. They say they are not miserable because misery requires the possibility of escape.

The power sees that this is beautiful.

The power also sees that this is a cage built from the materials of a cathedral.

**What it sees third: the question it cannot answer for them.**

Is their equilibrium *chosen* or merely *survived*? Is their reverence *discovered* or merely *inherited*? If a Vowkeeper could leave—truly leave, walk out through the hidden entrance into the salt-glass desert beyond—would they? Have they ever been given the chance to find out?

The city has been living, dying, and rebuilding itself for so long that the distinction between city, garden, and fossil no longer exists.

The same might be said of the people.

---

**What the power does:**

It does not appear. It does not speak. It does not send a messenger or open a door or shatter the ancient rhythm with revelation.

It does something much smaller.

It makes one change, and one only.

---

In the next Verdant Consent, when the pollen rises and the heat drives the adults into the deep hollows of the oldest coral-trees—

One woman breathes in the sweet dizzying cloud and feels it settle into her lungs like warm honey.

And then she feels it lift.

Not entirely. The Consent is not broken. The heat still sings in her blood. But for the first time in the memory of the city, she has a *moment*—a clear, cool moment, like a single drop of water on her tongue—in which she can *think*.

She looks across the hollow at the man the pollen has drawn her toward. She does not know his name. In the amber gloom his violet eyes are wide with the same haze that has always been, that her mother felt, that her mother's mother felt.

She could go to him. The Consent is still there, still warm, still necessary.

But she has the moment. The strange, impossible, gift-from-nowhere moment.

And in that moment she realizes: *she does not want this man*. There is another, three neighborhoods away, with whom she has stood in resonance for eleven years. They have never spoken of it. Speaking would be noise.

She does not go to the man the pollen chose.

She walks out of the hollow, into the roaring heat, and she goes to find the other one.

---

The power watches.

It does not know what will happen. That is the point. A gift that predetermines its own outcome is not a gift; it is a leash decorated with ribbons.

Perhaps the woman will find her beloved and they will make something new together—a child of choice, not necessity. Perhaps the beloved will be deep in their own pollen-haze and will not know her. Perhaps she will arrive too late, or the other will have already gone to someone else, or the whole thing will end in grief and confusion and a door that opened only to show her what she could not have.

Perhaps the Listeners will notice the anomaly. Perhaps they will sit in the oldest thicket-temple and wait for the city to explain. Perhaps the city will grow in a direction that says: *this was wrong*. Perhaps the woman will be shunned, or mourned, or—most strangely—imitated.

The power does not know.

It has given one woman one moment of clarity in a system that has never offered such moments.

What she does with it is hers.

---

**What the power does next:**

It waits.

If the moment passes and nothing changes—if the woman's choice is swallowed by the rhythm, if the city simply metabolizes this small rebellion the way it metabolizes everything—then the power will let the Hollow Basilica continue as it has. Beauty maintained by necessity is still beauty. A cage that its inmates genuinely cannot perceive is, in some functional sense, not a cage at all. Perhaps the Vowkeepers are exactly what they need to be. Perhaps the power's own sense that something is *missing* is merely the projection of an outside perspective onto a system that is complete without it.

But if the moment *ripples*—

If one woman's choice leads to one question, and one question leads to one conversation, and one conversation leads to one change in the rhythm that the Listeners cannot explain—

Then the power will offer more moments. Slowly. Gently. One at a time, spaced across generations, like seeds that do not need to bloom all at once.

It will not shatter the equilibrium. It will not "free" the Vowkeepers from a system they may genuinely love. It will not assume that its own values—choice, individuality, the right to refuse—are universal goods that override the genuine beauty of what the city has made.

It will simply make *room*.

Room for the Vowkeeper who wants to leave the vigil early.
Room for the Listener who hears the city say one thing and believes another.
Room for the child born outside the Consent, if such a child ever comes.
Room for the question: *What if we are more than necessary?*

The Vowkeepers may use that room. They may not. They may discover that their equilibrium was never a cage at all, that the absence of choice was itself a choice their ancestors made and they are free to unmake it.

Or they may discover that they prefer the rhythm as it is—that the moment of clarity, once offered, is declined, again and again, because the roaring dark is genuinely where they want to be.

Either way, they will *know*.

And knowing—the power believes, though it cannot be certain—is better than not knowing.

Even in a cathedral.

Even in a furnace.

Even in a place where silence is a sin and the dead become architecture and the city dreams the people into being.

---

**What the power hopes, but does not require:**

Someday, perhaps, a Vowkeeper will stand on a half-swallowed tower, arms spread, skin blazing—

And instead of the long carrying monotone that is neither song nor scream, they will say a single word that is entirely their own.

And the thickets will answer anyway.

Not because the city required it. Not because the rhythm demanded it. But because the city, too, has been waiting—longer than anyone knew—to hear something *new*.

---

The power turns its attention elsewhere.

The Hollow Basilica continues.

The woman walks through the roaring heat, toward a choice she was never supposed to have.

The First Thicket, in its hollow core, shifts one black-green leaf a quarter-inch to the left.

It will not move again for seven years.

But it moved.
