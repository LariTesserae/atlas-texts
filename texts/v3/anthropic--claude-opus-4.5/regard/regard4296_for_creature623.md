# The Attending

The power that turns its attention to the Condensery District does not arrive. Arrival implies a before and after, a crossing of threshold. Instead, it *notices*—the way you might suddenly become aware of your own breathing, something that was always there now held gently in consciousness.

What it notices first is the rhythm.

Not just the compressor's bass vibration, though that's part of it. The whole district pulses: the drip-patterns of condensation, the feeding schedule, the overlapping shifts in the canteen, the strangler fig's imperceptible growth, the heron's dawn appearances, the children's parliament convening on the third day of each week. These rhythms layer and interweave until they become something like a heartbeat, something like breath, something like the slow metabolism of a creature too large and distributed to recognize itself as alive.

The power recognizes it, though. Sees the district not as a place where humans live alongside machinery and vegetation, but as a single organism in which humans, machinery, and vegetation are all organs. The compressor is not *like* a heart; it is performing the function of a heart. The condensery pipes are not *like* a circulatory system; they are one. The night gardeners tending roots, the machinist-priests feeding coal, Mira sealing hairline fractures with her inherited paste—these are not metaphorically immune responses. They are literally the organism maintaining itself.

The power's first instinct is to do nothing.

This is important. A lesser wisdom would see problems to solve: the coal dependency, Uncle's deafness, Grandmother Po's age, the hermit's cataracts, Brother Vessel's damaged lungs. A lesser wisdom would intervene, improve, optimize. Would make the compressor more efficient, the condensery lines self-sealing, the vegetation more productive, the people healthier and longer-lived.

But the power is not lesser. It understands that the district's beauty lies precisely in its negotiated imperfection. The leaks create the moss-maps. The coal-feeding schedule creates community. The machinist-priests' vows exist because the machinery can fail, and that fragility is sacred. To fix these things would be to fix a poem's rhythm into metronome regularity—technically improved, essentially destroyed.

So the power watches, and in watching, begins to understand what the district actually needs. Not improvement. *Continuity*.

---

The first thing the power does is so subtle that no one notices it happening.

Brother Vessel has been trying to teach Sister Cup the western quadrant schematic for six years. The knowledge exists only in his memory, and his lungs are failing, and there is a genuine risk that he will die before the teaching is complete. The district could lose knowledge it cannot rebuild—not because the information is complex, but because it is *specific*, accumulated through generations of observation and failure, encoded in the particular language the machinist-priests have developed for speaking about systems that are part mechanical and part something else.

The power does not download the knowledge into Sister Cup's mind. That would be a violation, and besides, it would miss the point. The knowledge isn't data; it's relationship. It's the way Brother Vessel's hand moves when he describes the junction at coordinate 7-West-14, the slight pause in his voice when he mentions the pipe that failed in his teacher's time, the reverence with which he speaks the names of machinist-priests now dead.

Instead, the power does something gentler. It gives Brother Vessel time.

Not more years—that would unbalance things, make him something other than human, break the natural rhythm of generation and succession that keeps the district's knowledge alive. Just... enough time. His lungs stabilize. Not heal, but stop worsening. The cough that has been waking him at night eases to something he can sleep through. He finds he has the breath for longer teaching sessions, for the stories that provide context, for the songs that Catch sings while working and that turn out to encode maintenance procedures in their verses.

Sister Cup will learn the western quadrant. She will learn it properly, with all its accumulated wisdom, its exceptions and traditions and small mysteries. And when she teaches the next generation, she will teach not just the schematic but the way Brother Vessel's hand moved, the pause in his voice, the names of the dead.

---

The second thing is equally subtle, but its effects ripple further.

The children's parliament has been maintaining their records in Wen's handmade ledger, but the ledger is fragile. Fish glue and salvaged paper do not last. Wen has already had to recopying earlier minutes when pages began to rot, and in the recopying, small things were lost—the exact phrasing of a debate, the names of members who have since grown up or moved away, the early observations about gecko territories that might, in retrospect, have been significant.

The power cannot give them archival-quality materials without disrupting the district's relationship with scarcity and making. But it can do something else.

In the basement level where the porcelain insulators stand in rows, the translucent snails have been moving in patterns that Drip faithfully reports. What Drip hasn't yet noticed—but will, soon, because the power has arranged for him to be in the right place at the right time with the right quality of light—is that the snails' trails leave behind a faintly luminescent residue. This residue, when it dries, becomes remarkably stable. It adheres to surfaces. It can be guided.

Drip will discover that if he places thin sheets of salvaged material (there is suitable material in the old quality-control office, forgotten until now) in the snails' paths, they will crawl across it, and their trails will remain. He will discover that by placing small barriers, he can guide the trails into shapes. He will discover, eventually, that the snails can be persuaded to trace over existing writing, preserving it in luminescent permanence.

He will bring this discovery to the parliament, because that is what ambassadors do. The parliament will debate its implications. Wen will begin experimenting with preservation techniques. The archive she decided to create "just in case" will become real, and it will grow, and generations hence, the district will have a memory longer than any individual life.

The power has given them nothing they didn't already have. It has only arranged for a discovery to be made.

---

The third thing the power does concerns the heron.

Grandmother Po is right, though she doesn't have the framework to explain why. The heron is not *a* heron in the sense of being a single bird with a single lifespan. The heron is something that has been continuous in this place since before the condensery was a condensery, since when this was wetland and the water pooled naturally and the great birds stalked through reeds that grew where the compressor now sits.

The district's heron is a lineage. Always one bird, the territory passing from parent to offspring in an unbroken chain, the offspring learning the pools and the roosting spots and the particular quality of patience this place requires. The current heron is the seventeenth of its line. It is old, for a heron. It has not yet produced an offspring.

The power cannot force such things, and would not if it could. But it can attend.

On a morning in the wet season, when the light through the frosted glass is particularly soft and the compressor's rhythm has shifted into a deeper register that seems to invite stillness, a second heron arrives at the district's edge. It is young, uncertain, drawn by something it cannot name—an instinct that might be called longing if herons had words for longing.

The resident heron sees it. Considers it. Does not drive it away.

This is not the power's doing, not exactly. The power simply made sure the young heron heard about this place through the mysterious channels by which herons know things. Made sure it arrived on a morning when the resident was in a mood to tolerate company. Made sure the light was good and the pools were full and the hermit in her high perch was watching, so that someone would see what happens next.

What happens next is courtship. Slow, tentative, conducted over weeks in the early morning hours. The two herons learning each other's boundaries. The resident teaching the pools to the newcomer, showing which are deepest, where the fish hide, how to stand absolutely still while the dripping continues all around you.

The hermit watches. She says nothing to anyone, but her mason jars—the seeds, the cuttings, the spores she keeps "for after"—she begins to add to them. Feathers. Fragments of shell she finds at the pool's edge. "For continuity," she says, if anyone asks. But no one asks, and she speaks to herself, and the herons continue their slow dance below her perch.

---

The fourth thing is larger, and takes longer, and when it is complete, most of the district's residents will not realize anything has changed.

The compressor is dying.

Not quickly. It has decades left, maybe more. But the machinist-priests know—it is their vocation to know—that the machine will eventually fail beyond their ability to repair. Some essential component will crack, some principle of its operation will reach the end of its tolerance, and the heart of the district will stop.

When this happens, the condensery system will fail. The water will stop flowing. The vegetation will begin to die. The careful balance of moisture and warmth and rhythm that makes this place livable will collapse into ordinary ruin. The district will become what outsiders always assumed it was: an abandoned factory slowly returning to the earth.

The machinist-priests do not speak of this often. It is too large, too inevitable, too far beyond their power to prevent. They maintain the compressor with devotion precisely because they know it is mortal. They love it as you love anything that will someday leave you.

The power sees this, and understands it, and does not try to make the compressor immortal. That would be a violation of the district's nature, which is fundamentally about the negotiation between human effort and entropy, the constant work of maintenance that gives meaning to the community's existence.

But the power also sees something the machinist-priests have not yet seen.

The strangler fig has been growing for decades. Its roots now penetrate deep into the structure of the smokestack, into the walls of the main processing hall, into the channels where condensery pipes run. Tam has been charting this growth for twenty-three years, watching the tree reach for something he cannot identify.

The power knows what the tree is reaching for.

Deep beneath the district, below the basement where the snails trace their patterns, below the foundations of the original factory, there is an aquifer. It has been there since before humans built anything on this site. It is the reason the wetland existed, the reason the herons came, the reason some long-dead industrial planner thought this would be a good place for a condensery. The water table here is high and reliable and fed by sources that will not run dry for geological ages.

The strangler fig's roots are reaching for this water.

In fifty years—perhaps forty, if Tam's charts are accurate—the roots will reach it. When they do, the tree will become a pump, drawing water upward through its vast circulatory system, releasing it through transpiration into the humid air of the district. The condensery pipes, which the vegetation has already colonized, will continue to function—not because the compressor drives them, but because the tree does.

The power does not accelerate this. It does not guide the roots or ensure their path. The fig knows what it is doing; it has been doing it for decades without any assistance. The power simply... makes sure the knowledge will be there when it is needed.

It arranges for Tam to meet Brother Vessel. An unlikely conversation between the night gardener and the machinist-priest, occasioned by nothing more remarkable than both of them being unable to sleep on the same humid night, both of them finding themselves in the canteen where Grandmother Po is making a pot of something warm and strong.

They talk. They discover they have more in common than they realized. Both of them keepers of systems. Both of them watching, charting, trying to understand. Tam mentions his charts of the fig's growth. Brother Vessel mentions the deep infrastructure maps he holds in his memory, including the old surveys that show the aquifer. Neither of them makes the connection that night.

But Tam begins to think about roots in terms of systems. Brother Vessel begins to think about the compressor's eventual failure in terms of succession. And over the months that follow, their conversations continue, and the children's parliament begins to keep records of the fig's growth alongside records of gecko territories, and Wen creates a new section in her archive for "deep systems," and the knowledge begins to assemble itself in the district's collective memory.

Forty years hence, when the compressor finally fails, someone will know what to do. Someone will understand that the district is not ending but transforming, that the tree has grown into its role as heart, that the condensery lines will continue to sweat their moisture as they have for generations, just drawn now by roots rather than pistons. The machinist-priests will become something new—tree-priests, perhaps, or root-tenders—and the rhythm will continue, changed in its source but not in its nature.

The power has given them nothing but time and conversation. The rest they will build themselves.

---

The fifth thing the power does is for the heron, and for Grandmother Po, and for the question of what it means for a place to be alive.

Grandmother Po is dying.

She is old—no one knows exactly how old, including her—and her body is reaching its limits. Her hands shake now even when she holds a knife, though her cuts are still precise. She sleeps more than she used to. She speaks more often of the compressor's preferences, of the district's aliveness, of things she wants to say before she can no longer say them.

The power cannot prevent her death. Would not, even if it could. Grandmother Po has lived well and long, and her dying is as much a part of the district's rhythm as any other process. The canteen collective will mourn her, and someone else will become eldest, and the meals will continue to be prepared in the massive iron stoves, and this is as it should be.

But the power can give her something.

On a morning very near the end—Grandmother Po knows it is near the end, her body has been telling her for days—she wakes before dawn and feels a strange impulse to walk to the collecting pools. She has not walked that far in months. Her joints protest, her breath comes short, and still she walks, because something is drawing her, and she has lived too long in this place to ignore such drawings.

The heron is there.

Both herons, actually. The old one and the young one, standing together at the pool's edge, absolutely still in the gray pre-dawn light. They do not move as Grandmother Po approaches. They do not move as she lowers herself carefully onto an overturned crate that someone left here long ago. They simply stand, and she simply sits, and for a long moment nothing happens except the dripping and the distant pulse of the compressor and the slow lightening of the sky.

Then the old heron turns its head and looks at her.

Grandmother Po has seen herons before. Has seen this heron, catching glimpses through frosted glass, hearing reports from others. But she has never been looked at by the heron. Has never felt its attention settle on her like a weight, like a recognition, like a greeting between two creatures who have both been part of this place for a very long time.

"You," she says, and the word carries more meaning than it should, because she is speaking to the heron-that-is-the-heron, to the lineage, to the continuity of presence that has watched over these pools since before the factory was built. "You're saying goodbye."

The heron does not answer, because herons do not speak. But it inclines its head, slightly, in a way that might be acknowledgment. And then it spreads its wings—massive, gray, impossible—and lifts into the air, and is gone.

The young heron remains. It looks at Grandmother Po with eyes that are different from its predecessor's, not yet wise but patient, willing to learn. It is the eighteenth of its line. It is inheriting the pools.

Grandmother Po watches it for a long time. She thinks about the compressor, about the offerings she has left in the niche near the feeding chute, about her theory that the machinery is alive in a specific, biological way. She thinks about the district as an organism, about the people and the vegetation and the machinery as organs in a single body. She thinks about dying, about what it means to be part of something that will continue after you stop.

"You understand," she says to the young heron, and it is not a question. "You're part of it too."

The heron stands still in the dawn light, and the pools reflect its silhouette, and somewhere in the distance the compressor shifts into its morning rhythm, and Grandmother Po closes her eyes and breathes the humid air and feels, for one perfect moment, the whole district breathing with her.

She dies three days later, in her cot in the back room of the canteen, with Sof holding her hand and humming a note that harmonizes with the compressor's bass vibration. Her last words are about the heron. Her body is returned to the district in the old way—composted, fed to the vegetation, becoming part of the system she always said was alive.

The young heron attends the ceremony. It stands at the edge of the gathering, still and patient, and when the words are spoken and the offerings made, it lifts into the air and flies a single circle over the main processing hall before returning to its pool.

The power watches all of this. It has arranged nothing, prevented nothing, added nothing that was not already present. It has simply attended—paid attention, held the district gently in its awareness, ensured that what needed to happen was allowed to happen.

This is what benevolent wisdom does, when it encounters something already beautiful, already alive, already in the process of becoming what it needs to become.

It witnesses. It makes space. It trusts.

---

Years pass. Decades. The compressor grows older, and the fig grows larger, and the children who sat in parliament become the elders who guide the district, and new children come, and the cycle continues.

The power does not withdraw—withdrawal implies a departure, and the power never arrived in the first place. It simply continues to notice, continues to attend, continues to make small adjustments so subtle they might be coincidence: a tool left in the right place at the right time, a conversation that happens because two people couldn't sleep, a discovery made by a child ambassador who was in the right light at the right moment.

The district endures. Not because the power protects it, but because the power trusts it, sees what it is capable of becoming, and makes space for that becoming to unfold.

The herons continue their lineage. The strangler fig continues its slow reach toward the water below. The condensery pipes continue to sweat their moisture into the humid air, fed by roots now as much as by pistons. The district breathes, and grows, and changes, and remains itself.

And somewhere in the gray-green light, in the layered sounds of dripping and machinery and birdsong, in the rhythm that is too large and distributed to recognize itself as alive, something is grateful.

Not to the power. The district does not know it has been attended. But grateful nonetheless—the way a garden is grateful for rain, the way a sleeping child is grateful for the arms that hold it, the way any living thing is grateful for the conditions that allow it to live.

The power knows this gratitude, feels it like sunlight on its face. It is enough.

It is more than enough.
