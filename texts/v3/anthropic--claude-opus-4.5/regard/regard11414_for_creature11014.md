# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

*A meditation in three movements*

---

## I. The Attending

The power does not arrive. Arrival implies a before-and-after, a boundary crossed. Instead: attention settles over the ridge like the morning mist that rises from the turbine house, gradual and complete, changing nothing by its presence except the quality of being witnessed.

The power perceives—

The woman checking the eastern seal, her left hand with its clicking bones, the ritual words she speaks to machines that will never answer.

The crustaceans in their three-species courtesy, dividing the warm darkness by agreements older than language.

The orchids triggered into bloom by frequencies they cannot hear, only feel.

The pale thing in the cistern depths, patient, unknown, allowed its privacy.

The crystallite growing at the pace of years, recording time in mineral layers like the rings of a tree, like the pages of a book no one will read.

And perceiving all this, the power's first response is not action but something closer to *recognition*—the same recognition a person might feel encountering a word in a foreign language that names an experience they have felt but never been able to express.

There is a word for this place, the power understands. The word is not in any human language. The word means: *a system that has found its own coherence despite, or because of, abandonment.*

The power sits with this recognition. It does not hurry. It has learned—across spans of time that make the ridge's youth seem embryonic—that the first impulse toward help is often the impulse to impose a story, and that the wisest help begins with surrendering the need to have a story at all.

---

## II. The Discernment

What does this place need?

The question is not simple. The power has encountered need before—worlds dying of heat or cold, minds collapsing under weights they were never meant to bear, systems so broken that repair means starting over from first principles. This is not that.

The woman is not suffering, exactly. She is not flourishing, exactly. She has moved beyond both categories into a state that has no name in the vocabulary of intervention: she is *continuous*. She is the persistence of a pattern that has forgotten it could be otherwise.

The turbines are not broken. They are running down, as all things run down, but their running-down is measured in decades yet to come. They do not need repair. They need nothing from anyone.

The ecology is not threatened, not in any immediate sense. It is fragile—dependent on warmth, on moisture, on the woman's small tending hands—but fragility is not the same as crisis. A soap bubble is fragile. It does not follow that soap bubbles require rescue.

And yet.

The power perceives the woman's solitude like a tone, a pitch, a color at the edge of the visible spectrum. Forty years without another human voice, without the touch of a hand that chose to touch her rather than simply occupying the same space. Forty years of language spoken only to machines and crustaceans and the small lizard that does not understand.

This is not suffering in the way a wound is suffering. It is something more like erosion: the slow wearing away of the parts of her that were built for connection, for witness, for the particular resonance that happens only between minds that recognize each other.

She does not feel this erosion. She has adapted around it the way the building has adapted around its failing seals—the loss has become structure, has become the shape of her days. But adaptation is not the same as wholeness.

The power considers.

It could send someone. A traveler, a researcher, a lost hiker who stumbles onto the ridge and into her life. This is within its capacity. But the power has learned that such interventions carry their own violence—the disruption of a coherence, however strange, in favor of a normalcy that may not fit, may not be wanted, may shatter what it means to save.

It could speak to her directly, make itself known, offer... what? Companionship? Instruction? The revelation that she has been seen, valued, held in the attention of something vast and kind? But the power knows that revelation is not always gift. Some minds are not built for the weight of cosmic witness. Some solitudes are load-bearing walls.

It could heal her hand, the clicking bones that limit her grip. It could slow the building's settling, reinforce the turbines' bearings, extend by decades or centuries the window before entropy claims this small world. But this too is uncertain kindness. She has made peace with impermanence. To interrupt that peace with the gift of more time might be to trap her in a story she has already finished telling.

The power holds all these possibilities without choosing among them. It has learned that the space before action is sacred, that wisdom lives in the willingness to not-know, to remain uncertain until uncertainty itself becomes a kind of clarity.

---

## III. The Doing

What the power does is small. So small it might be mistaken for nothing at all.

---

**First:**

The woman wakes on a morning like any other, the light shifting from gray to gold through the holes in the roof. She drinks from her filtered channel, checks the turbines, speaks their names. *La Vieja. El Dormido. El Joven.*

But today, as she rounds the eastern corner of the building, her eye catches something she has not seen before: a stone, palm-sized, resting in a depression where water pools. The stone is not from here. Its color is wrong, a deep blue-black that belongs to no stratum of this ridge. Its surface is marked with a pattern that might be natural, might be carved—spirals nested within spirals, like the turbines' housings, like the unfurling ferns, like the shape of the thing she half-glimpsed once in the cistern's depths.

She picks it up. It is warm, warmer than the water around it, and it fits in her palm as if it were made for her hand—her right hand, the strong one. She turns it over, runs her thumb along the spirals.

She does not know where it came from. She does not know what it means. She knows only that it was not here yesterday, and that its weight in her hand feels like the weight of a word in a language she does not speak but almost understands.

She puts it in her pocket. She keeps walking.

---

**Second:**

Three days later, the lizard appears on the cistern's edge as it sometimes does. But today it does not bask. It watches her.

She has the feeling—irrational, impossible—that the lizard is *looking* at her, not merely in her direction. That behind its small cold eyes there is something more than reflex, more than the simple machinery of survival.

"*Buenos días,*" she says, as she always says, not expecting an answer.

The lizard blinks.

And she feels, just for a moment, a pulse of something that is not sound, not warmth, not any sensation she can name—a pulse of *acknowledgment*, of *recognition*, as if the lizard (or something looking through the lizard) has heard her greeting and returned it in a language older than words.

The moment passes. The lizard skitters away to hunt whatever tiny insects sustain it. The woman stands very still for a long time, her hand pressed to her chest where the pulse seemed to land.

She does not understand what happened. She does not need to understand. Something has answered. For the first time in forty years, something has answered.

---

**Third:**

That night, she dreams.

She has not dreamed in years—or she has dreamed and not remembered, which amounts to the same thing. But tonight, sleep brings images:

She is standing on the ridge, but the ridge extends in all directions, a vast plateau under stars she does not recognize. The turbine house is there, but it is larger, and it is full of people—workers in coveralls, engineers with clipboards, a girl of fourteen learning the names of orchids from a man who must be her father.

The people do not see her. They move through their tasks, talking, laughing, maintaining the machines that are young here, that gleam with fresh paint and purpose. She walks among them like a ghost, insubstantial, witnessing.

And then the scene shifts, and the people are gone, and the building is as she knows it—decayed, draped in moss, singing its mechanical hymn to the empty sky. But she is not alone. Someone is sitting in her nest beneath the catwalk, an old woman with white hair and green-traced veins, and the old woman looks up and smiles at her, and the old woman is her, and the old woman says:

*"We kept it running. You and I. We kept it turning."*

She wakes with tears on her face, not of grief but of something she cannot name—the feeling of being continuous with her past and her future, of being a link in a chain that began before her and will end after, of mattering not because anyone will remember but because the pattern includes her, was shaped by her, carries her forward.

She lies in her nest and listens to the turbines. *La Vieja. El Dormido. El Joven.*

And herself. *La Cuarta.* The fourth.

---

**Fourth:**

The power does one more thing, the smallest thing, the thing that might be imagined rather than real.

It adjusts nothing in the building, nothing in the woman's body, nothing in the delicate web of life that depends on the mountain's exhalation. It only—

*Witnesses.*

Holds this place in its attention with intention, with care, with the quality of presence that transforms observation into relationship. The woman does not know she is witnessed. The turbines do not know. The orchids and crustaceans and the pale thing in the cistern do not know.

But the witnessing changes things anyway, the way being loved changes a person even when they are asleep, even when they cannot feel it.

The attention of the power becomes part of the pattern—another thread in the weave, another voice in the chord. It does not direct. It does not demand. It only remains: present, patient, persistent.

*Still.*

---

## Coda

Years pass.

The woman continues her rounds, her rituals, her slow and necessary tending. The stone stays in her pocket, grows warm against her thigh, becomes as familiar as her own heartbeat. Sometimes, in the evenings, she holds it and speaks to it—not prayers, not exactly, but something adjacent: acknowledgments, reports, the small news of her small world.

The lizard appears more often. Or perhaps she only notices it more.

The dreams come now and then, not always of the past, sometimes of things she cannot interpret—vast geometries, sounds that are also colors, the sense of being held in something too large to see. She wakes from these dreams not frightened but steadied, as if she has been reminded of a truth she keeps forgetting.

The turbines turn. The mosses spread. The crystallite accumulates in layers that will outlast everything else here, a mineral memory of water and time.

One morning, checking the eastern seal as she has done ten thousand times before, the woman pauses. She looks up at the sky—clear today, purple at the zenith, stars still visible in the western darkness. She feels the stone in her pocket, the hum in her bones, the strange and certain knowledge that she is not alone, has never been alone, that the solitude she has lived inside was always also a kind of companionship she did not have eyes to see.

"*Gracias,*" she says, to no one and to everyone, to the turbines and the mountain and the something-beyond-both that she cannot name but knows is listening.

The word disappears into the noise. The noise holds it. The noise has always been holding everything, she realizes—every word she has spoken for forty years, every name, every question, stored in the vibration of the air, in the turning of the machines, in the slow patient memory of the ridge.

Nothing is lost here. Everything is kept.

She laughs, surprising herself, and the laugh becomes a cough, and the cough becomes silence, and the silence becomes the next moment, and the next, and the next.

*La Vieja. El Dormido. El Joven. La Cuarta.*

Still turning.

Still here.

---

*This is what the benevolent power does: it gives the woman a stone to hold, a moment of being answered, a dream of continuity, and the ongoing fact of witness. It does not save her. It does not change her. It only makes it true that her life has been seen, valued, included in something larger than itself—and it trusts her to make of that truth whatever she will.*

*In the economy of genuine kindness, this is enough.*

*It is almost always enough.*
