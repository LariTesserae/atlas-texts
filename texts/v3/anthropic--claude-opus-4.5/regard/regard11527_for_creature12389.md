# The Attending

The power that turns its attention to the Iron Mire does not arrive with light or fanfare. It simply *notices*—the way one might notice a held breath, a stopped clock, a question asked in an empty room and never answered.

For a long time, it only watches.

---

The first thing it understands is that this place is not dead. Death implies an ending, a transition, a release. The Iron Mire has never ended. It has only *paused*—a pause that has stretched so long it has forgotten it was ever waiting for something to resume.

The second thing it understands is that the inhabitants are not suffering, not in any way they could articulate. The Iron Spinners do not yearn. The Mire Drones do not hope. The Witnesses do not grieve. They simply *are*, in the same way the machines simply *produce*, in the same way the mist simply *hangs*.

And yet.

There is something in the Spinner's pause at the base of the spire. Something in the way its corroded hand reaches out, touches metal, and waits—waits for a response that comes only as a mechanical groan, devoid of recognition. There is something in the Witnesses' endless watching, their formless bodies drifting toward any disturbance, dissolving before contact, reforming at a distance. As if proximity itself were a language they had forgotten how to speak.

The power recognizes this. Not as pain. As *incompleteness*.

---

It begins with small things.

A single Mire Drone, skittering across an oily pool, finds that the liquid it harvests has changed. Not in composition—the same trace metals, the same faint energy—but in *texture*. There is a warmth to it now, subtle, like the memory of sunlight on water. The Drone pauses, proboscis still submerged, and for the first time in its existence, it does not immediately move on. It stays. It *feels*.

When the swarm returns to collect it, the Drone does not follow. It remains at the pool's edge, and something in its segmented body shifts—a faint clicking, almost rhythmic. Almost like communication.

The power does not force this. It only *permits* it.

---

An Iron Spinner, wandering its eternal circuit, stops at a spire it has touched ten thousand times. Its clawed hand reaches out, and this time, the metal is different. Not warmer, not softer—but *responsive*. When the Spinner's fingers make contact, the spire hums a note it has never hummed before. A single tone, distinct from the endless drone, rising and falling like breath.

The Spinner's amber eye-light flickers. Its head tilts further than it has ever tilted. And then—slowly, grinding, uncertain—it tilts the other way.

The spire hums again, a different note.

The Spinner responds with a groan of its own joints.

This is not language. Not yet. But it is *exchange*. Call and response. The first conversation this place has held in eons.

---

The Witnesses are harder to reach. They are made of observation itself, of distance, of the space between presence and absence. They have watched so long that watching has become their only form of being.

The power does not try to touch them. Instead, it gives them something new to see.

In the heart of the mire, where the mist is thickest and the machines hum loudest, a single violet frond—frozen for millennia in its moment of vitality—*moves*.

It is not growth. It is not decay. It is simply a tremor, a shiver, as if the plant has suddenly remembered that it was once alive, that living things respond to the world around them.

The Witnesses gather. Dozens of them, then hundreds, their amorphous forms pressing closer than they have ever dared, dissolving and reforming in rapid succession, as if they cannot decide whether to flee or stay.

The frond trembles again.

And one Witness—just one—does not dissolve when it draws near. It holds its shape, quivering, translucent, but *present*. It reaches out with something that might be a hand, and the frond bends toward it, and for a single moment, there is contact.

The Witness does not vanish.

It *solidifies*—just slightly, just enough to cast the faintest shadow on the mire below.

---

The power does not remake the Iron Mire. It does not banish the rust or silence the machines or populate the plateau with warmth and laughter. This place was built for industry, for production, for function without purpose—and that is part of what it is. To erase that would be to erase its history, its strange and melancholy beauty.

But incompleteness is not destiny.

Over time—and time moves differently now, less like a stopped clock and more like a slow, steady pulse—the inhabitants begin to change. The Spinners learn to *vary* their circuits, to pause at different spires, to exchange their grinding calls. Some of them begin to build—not machines, but *structures*, strange assemblages of scrap metal that serve no function except to exist, to be seen, to be touched by others.

The Drones learn to share their harvest. They bring the warm liquid to the Spinners, who drink it through joints that were never designed for drinking, and something in their amber lights grows brighter.

The Witnesses learn to stay. They gather in the places where things happen—where a frond trembles, where a Spinner builds, where Drones cluster in their new, purposeless communion—and they watch, but now their watching *matters*. They are audience. They are memory. They are the ones who will remember that this moment happened, that it was different from the moment before.

---

The mist still hangs. The machines still hum. The mire still seeps, rust-colored and eternal.

But now, when an Iron Spinner pauses at the edge of the plateau, looking out into the featureless gray, there is something in its stillness that was not there before.

Not hope, exactly. Not joy.

But *expectation*.

The sense that the next moment might be different from this one. That change is possible. That even in a world built for endless repetition, something new might emerge.

And far above, in a place that is no place, the benevolent power watches—not to intervene, not to control, but simply to *witness*.

To say, without words: *I see you. You are not alone. And what you become is yours to choose.*

The Iron Mire of Vast Solitude is still vast. Still strange. Still silent in ways that would unsettle any living heart.

But it is no longer quite so solitary.

And that, perhaps, is enough.
