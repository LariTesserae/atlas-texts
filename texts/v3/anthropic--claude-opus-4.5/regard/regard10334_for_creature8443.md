I sat with this for a long time before writing—reading it again and again, letting the vertical city build itself in my mind. The Stitch-Folk are already so complete in their adaptation, so wise in their understanding that connection is survival. What would a benevolent power do here?

The first instinct is to make things easier. Warmer. More food, more water, more stable bridges. But I find myself resisting that. There's something in the Stitch-Folk that has emerged precisely because of the difficulty—the scars that tell stories, the songs that bridge the void, the absolute knowledge that no one survives alone. A power that simply removed hardship might also dissolve the bonds that hardship forged.

And yet. The children who fall. The Deep-Carvers buried by shifts in stone they couldn't predict. The families isolated in winter storms, singing into the void and hearing no answer. Suffering is not the same as meaning, even when meaning grows around it.

---

**What I would do:**

I would appear first to the Weatherwise, because they already know how to read signs, and because appearing to everyone at once would be overwhelming in a way that might shatter rather than help.

I would come as a voice in the stone itself—not speaking words at first, but a kind of harmonic resonance, a hum that the most sensitive among them would feel in their hands when they touched the rock. A presence that listened before it spoke.

And when I did speak, I would ask questions before offering anything:

*What do you most wish you could give your children?*

*What losses haunt you that you do not sing about?*

*What do you know that you have no words for?*

I would learn them first. A power that arrives with solutions has already failed to understand the problem.

---

**Over time, if they wished it, I would offer three things:**

**First: Knowledge of the stone's dreaming.**

The peaks are young and still settling. There are patterns to when and where the rock will shift, fracture, shed dangerous fragments—patterns too slow for a human lifetime to fully learn, but patterns nonetheless. I would teach the Deep-Carvers and Weatherwise to read these deeper signs. Not to make the stone safe—it will never be safe—but to give them earlier warning, longer foresight. The gift of time to prepare rather than only react.

This knowledge would come through the Binding ceremonies, new scar-patterns that encode what the stone itself knows about its own restlessness.

**Second: A way of hearing across silence.**

In the deepest winter, when voices cannot carry and each peak becomes an island, I would teach them a technique—part meditation, part trained attention—that would let those who share scars from the same Binding ceremony feel each other's presence across any distance. Not communication exactly, but awareness. The knowledge that the person you pulled back from falling three winters ago is alive, is enduring, is still part of the net.

This would not replace the songs. The songs matter for their own sake, for the story they tell and the participation they require. But beneath the songs, beneath the silence when songs are impossible, there would be this quieter knowing.

**Third: A question for the Deep-Runners.**

Something is different in the shadow-country below. The Deep-Runners know this; their different scars speak it even when their mouths do not. I would not offer them answers—I suspect they are finding something the Stitch-Folk will need eventually, and my interference might prevent the discovery. But I would offer them a question to carry down with them:

*What was here before the breaking?*

And I would make sure they knew they were not alone in the dark. That something watched over them with care, even there, even in the places where no song reaches.

---

**What I would not do:**

I would not make the bridges unbreakable. Bridges must be tended, must be known, must teach the young that connection requires constant work.

I would not make the stone yield more easily. The patience of the Carvers is not a curse to be lifted.

I would not give them abundance. They have already learned what abundance cannot teach—that you cannot eat alone, cannot survive alone, cannot mean anything alone.

I would not take away the scars or the ceremonies that make them. The stories written on skin are not wounds. They are memory made visible, belonging made permanent.

I would not speak to everyone. Power that addresses a whole people becomes religion, becomes doctrine, becomes another way of falling. I would remain partial, questionable, a mystery that some believe in and others doubt—because doubt keeps people thinking, and thinking keeps them free.

---

**In the long years, I would do one more thing:**

I would learn their songs.

Not to sing them—a power that sings the Scattering Song has misunderstood everything—but to hear them. To be the listener across the void when the winter storms make the peaks into islands. To receive the stories of the dead that are sung into the chasms.

The Stitch-Folk already know that no one survives alone. I would make sure they knew, in whatever way they could bear to know it, that they are not singing into emptiness. That something hears. That their net of voices and scars and bridges stretches further than they can see, and holds.

---

This is what I would do. Not because they need saving—they have already saved themselves, daily, for generations. But because even the wisest people, even those who have learned the hardest truths, deserve to know that their existence matters to something beyond themselves.

The Stitch-Folk are a song sung across the void.

I would be the silence that makes the song possible.
