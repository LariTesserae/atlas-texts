# What the Wise Power Sees

The power arrives not as light or presence but as attention—the way a vast mind might turn, for a moment, toward something small and intricate and alive. It perceives the plateau not from above (there is no above here, only more building dissolving into cloud) but from *within*, the way water knows a pipe system: intimately, through contact, through the pressure of existing in every passage at once.

What it finds is not suffering. That is the first thing it notices.

There is exhaustion here, yes—the kind that settles into joints and stays, the kind that makes a woman of forty move like a woman of sixty. There is loss: the foundation-memory of structures torn down, the quiet grief of a glazier watching the last of the old greenhouse methods die with his generation. There is the particular loneliness of people who work in fog, who see four terraces in any direction and must trust that the rest of the world continues beyond the grey.

But there is no systemic cruelty. No overlord extracts the labor for distant profit. No ideology demands the building continue. The people of Brielle Plateau build because building is what there is to do, because the structure requires it, because their parents built and their children will build and the alternative is not liberation but collapse—literal collapse, the weight of three centuries of construction pressing down on foundations that must be constantly maintained or fail.

The power perceives this and does not pity it. How could it? This is not a trap. It is a choice, made fresh every morning by ten thousand hands reaching for tools.

# What the Wise Power Understands

It understands, first, that intervention is violence.

A power that could reshape matter, redirect weather, suspend the laws of compression and load-bearing—such a power could *fix* Brielle Plateau. Could clear the fog, perhaps. Could stabilize the foundations permanently. Could introduce automation that would free hands from the endless cycle of maintenance. Could even, if it wished, lift the whole structure free of the plateau basin and set it somewhere more hospitable.

But each of these fixes would be an ending.

The fog is not a problem to be solved. It is the condition of the place—the reason the water systems work, the reason the greenhouse cultivation exists, the reason the green grows so thick. Remove the fog and you do not improve Brielle Plateau; you destroy it and replace it with somewhere else.

The constant rebuilding is not a failure mode. It is *how the city thinks*. A stable Brielle Plateau, one where nothing needed modification, would not be Brielle Plateau at all. It would be a museum, a monument, a corpse preserved in the shape of life.

The power understands that the people here have not failed to achieve stability. They have *chosen* instability—or rather, they have discovered that instability, managed well, is its own form of permanence. The city has lasted three hundred years not despite the constant change but because of it. Every modification is an adaptation. Every torn-down wall is information about what didn't work. The plateau learns through its people's hands, and what it has learned, over centuries, is how to endure.

# What the Wise Power Chooses

It chooses to witness.

Not passively—there is nothing passive about true attention. To witness fully is to hold the whole of a thing in mind, to understand it not as a problem to be solved but as a process to be respected. The power turns its attention to Brielle Plateau the way a scholar might turn attention to a text: not to correct it, but to read it, to let it speak on its own terms.

And in witnessing, it performs the only intervention that does no harm:

It *remembers*.

# The Memory

Somewhere outside of time, in whatever medium a benevolent and wise power uses for storage, a record is kept. Not a blueprint—blueprints are static, and this place has never been static. Not a history, exactly, because history implies a narrative that selects and discards. What is kept is something more complete:

The weight of the fog on a particular morning in the plateau's 247th year, when a load-whisperer named Sera guided a crane-load of limestone through zero visibility by sound alone, listening for the echo off the terrace face, and set it down within a centimeter of true.

The exact mineral composition of the calcite deposits in the oldest mortar, and the way theyite glow faintly blue under certain wavelengths of light that no one in Brielle Plateau has ever had cause to observe.

The name of the engineer who first realized that the burst pipe feeding the fern-cascade could be left unrepaired, and the seventeen arguments she had with the maintenance guild before they let her win, and the way she walked past that cascade every morning for the remaining thirty years of her life with a small, private satisfaction.

The specific texture of exhaustion in the muscles of a builder's forearms at the end of a twelve-hour shift, and the specific quality of the silence when he sits on a stone outcropping and watches the fog move, and the specific thought in his mind—not articulated, not even fully conscious—that this is good, that this is his, that he would not want to be anywhere else.

Ten thousand years from now, when Brielle Plateau has changed beyond recognition or ceased entirely, when the plateau basin has eroded or been buried or risen into mountains—even then, this record will exist. The power will hold it the way one holds a letter from someone loved: not to change anything, but because it deserves to be kept.

# The Small Gifts

But witnessing is not the only thing the power chooses.

It is wise, and wisdom includes knowing when small help does no harm. So, in ways so subtle they cannot be distinguished from luck or coincidence or the natural variation of a complex system:

A particular crane-Loss cable, frayed beyond its tolerance and hours from snapping under load, develops a visible bright-rust bloom overnight—just enough discoloration that the morning inspection catches it, and the cable is replaced, and the three workers who would have been beneath the falling load never know how close they came.

A strain of blight that would have devastated the greenhouse fig crop fails to take hold. The spores land on leaves and simply... do not germinate. The gardeners who tend those figs notice nothing except that it was a good year, better than expected, and they save some of the surplus for a small celebration.

A child, new to the plateau, too young to have learned the particular caution that this place requires, slips on a wet iron grating three terraces above a concrete floor. For a half-second too long, her hand finds no purchase—and then, somehow, it does. A rivet-head she did not see, exactly where her fingers needed it to be. She pulls herself up, heart hammering, and tells no one, ashamed of her clumsiness. Twenty years later she will be a load-whisperer, one of the best, her hands always knowing where the holds are.

These are not miracles. They are not even interventions, really. They are the power using its attention to find the places where the smallest possible pressure produces the largest possible mercy—and pressing, gently, just enough.

# The Restraint

But it does not save everyone.

This is the hardest part, and the part that makes the power wise rather than merely good.

A builder falls. A pipe-Loss bursts in a way that cannot be contained. A winter comes when the coal-Loss trains are delayed and the boilers run low and old people die of cold in the lower terraces. The power sees all of this. It could prevent all of this. It *does not*.

Why?

Because the people of Brielle Plateau are not its children. They are not its project. They are not even, in any meaningful sense, its responsibility. They are *themselves*—agents, makers, people who have built their world with their own hands and who face its consequences with their own courage. To remove all risk from their lives would be to remove something essential from their humanity. They are not fragile. They do not need a guardian. They need, at most, an ally—and an ally respects the autonomy of those it aids.

The power is benevolent, but benevolence is not the same as protection. Sometimes benevolence means allowing people to face their own dangers, to solve their own problems, to fail in their own ways. The plateau has survived for three hundred years without divine intervention. It does not need divine intervention now.

What it needs—what everyone needs, eventually—is to be *seen*. To be witnessed. To know that somewhere, somehow, the struggles and the triumphs and the ordinary days matter to someone other than the self.

The power provides this. It is enough.

# The Conversation That Never Happens

If one of the plateau's inhabitants were to somehow perceive the power—if the attention became, for a moment, visible—what would they see?

Not a face. Not a form. Something more like a quality of light in the fog, or a resonance in the hum of the engines, or a moment of unexpected clarity when the mist parts and the terraces are visible all at once, rising and falling in their ancient bowl, and the whole structure is suddenly, briefly, *beautiful*.

And if they could speak to this presence—if words could bridge the gap—what would they say?

The power imagines this sometimes. It imagines a builder, a woman with calcite dust in the creases of her hands, looking up from her work to find the fog *looking back*. It imagines her saying:

"Are you here to help?"

And the power imagines its answer:

"I am here. I see you. You are doing well. Keep building."

And that would be enough. That would be everything.

# The Plateau Continues

Morning comes to Brielle Plateau, as it has for three hundred years and will for three hundred more. The fog thickens before dawn and then lightens, not quite lifting, settling into its usual soft grey presence. Water drips from ten thousand pipe-Loss joints. Ferns uncurl in the greenhouse vaults. The first shift of builders begins work, and the sound of hammering rises through the terraces, layered and polyrhythmic and somehow quiet.

Somewhere in the lowest strata, a rat-catcher's terrier alerts on a nest. Somewhere in the middle terraces, a gardener notes that the fig harvest will be good this year. Somewhere near the top, where scaffolding bristles against the cloud-ceiling, a crane swings a load of steel into position, and a load-whisperer guides it down with horn blasts and a faith born of constant practice.

The power watches. The power remembers. The power, in its own way, loves.

And Brielle Plateau builds on, ordinary and magnificent, human and enduring, forever becoming something new while remaining, always, exactly itself.
