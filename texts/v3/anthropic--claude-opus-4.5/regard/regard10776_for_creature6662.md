# The Dreaming of Clear Water

The attention came not as a beam of light piercing the mist, nor as a voice thundering from above. It arrived the way morning arrives in Sumpworks Hollow—gradually, as a subtle shift in the quality of the damp air, a faint warmth that was not steam, a presence that observed without intruding.

The benevolent power—let us call it simply the Witness, for that is what it first chose to be—settled into the hollow like condensation gathering on cool glass. It did not announce itself. It watched.

---

For seven cycles of the hollow's dim days and lamp-lit nights, the Witness only observed.

It saw old Kettleback, a Grimekin woman whose spine had curved into a permanent question mark from decades bent over pipe-joints, wake before the others to check the pressure gauges on Engine Twelve. She spoke to it in whispers: *Easy now, easy. We'll get through another day, you and I.* Her fingers, though arthritic, moved with the certainty of long practice.

It saw a Siltborn—one the Grimekin called Poolfather, though he had never offered a name—spend an entire night coaxing a dying fern back from the edge of rot, his luminescent hands cupping water to its roots in gestures more tender than any the Witness had seen in worlds far more beautiful than this.

It saw a Rustbound called Clatterhand, whose entire right arm was now a articulated assembly of pistons and brass joints, sitting alone in the deep machinery halls. He was crying, though the tears sizzled against the hot metal of his cheek-plate. He was holding, with his remaining flesh hand, a child's toy—a small wooden bird, unpainted, worn smooth by years of touch. He whispered a name the Witness did not share, for some things remain sacred even to powers that might know all.

It saw the children of the Grimekin clans playing a game on the iron walkways, tossing a ball of wound copper wire back and forth over a particularly treacherous gap, laughing when one of them nearly slipped, catching each other with practiced ease. Their laughter echoed strangely in the hollow, transformed by the acoustics of metal and mist into something that sounded almost like bells.

It saw the way the hollow breathed—the machinery and the water and the growth all moving together in rhythms too slow for any single inhabitant to perceive, but visible to one who watched across days. It saw the balance: precarious, improbable, and somehow holding.

---

On the eighth day, the Witness considered what it might do.

It could clear the skies, burn away the mist, let true sunlight pour into the basin for the first time in—how long? The Witness could not find the beginning of the fog in the hollow's memory. But it imagined Kettleback's eyes, adapted to dimness, seared by brightness. It imagined the Siltborn, beings of sediment and shadow, shriveling in the harsh light. It imagined the moss and ferns, which had never known anything but diffused glow, withering in a day.

It could purify the water, clear the rust, make the pools run crystalline and sweet. But the machinery was built for this particular chemistry of corrosion and mineral. The Siltborn were born from this specific mud. The whole hollow was a single organism, and to change its blood would be to kill it while believing you were saving it.

It could lift the people out—transport the Grimekin to green fields, the Rustbound to places where metal might be safely extracted from flesh. But the Witness saw how Kettleback touched the pipes like old friends. It saw how the children knew every loose bolt and creaking plank, how their games were built around the hollow's specific dangers and delights. It saw how even Clatterhand, weeping in the dark, had chosen to stay when he could have walked out into the beyond. His grief was here. His life was here. The wooden bird belonged here, where it had been carved.

The Witness understood, then, that this place—strange and harsh and loud and dim—was not a problem to be solved. It was a home.

---

So the Witness did something smaller. Something that would not shatter the balance.

It began with Kettleback's hands.

Not a miraculous healing—that would have frightened her, made her doubt her own senses, perhaps made her fear she was dying and being given a final gift. Instead, the Witness simply... eased. The inflammation that had been building in her joints slowed its advance. The pain that woke her at night became, gradually, a pain that let her sleep an hour longer. She noticed, after a week, that her fingers moved more freely in the mornings. She attributed it to a change in the humidity, or perhaps that new mixture of grease she'd been using. She did not question it. She simply worked with a little less suffering, and that was enough.

For Poolfather and the Siltborn, the Witness offered something different: a dream.

The Siltborn did not sleep as the Grimekin did, but they had their own quietude, moments when they became still and their luminescence dimmed. Into these moments, the Witness placed a vision—not of somewhere else, not of some better world, but of Sumpworks Hollow itself as it might look after another thousand years of their tending. The moss grown thick and soft as carpet. The machines still running, but worn into shapes that were almost organic, almost beautiful. The pools deep and still and full of that sourceless light. A future worth protecting. A reason to continue.

Poolfather woke from his stillness and stood for a long moment, water dripping from his clay-like fingers, staring at nothing. Then he went to find a struggling vine he'd noticed near the Western Cistern, one he'd thought too far gone to save. He saved it anyway.

For Clatterhand, the Witness could not take away the grief—would not, even if it could, for the grief was love, and to remove it would be to steal something precious. But it could do this: as Clatterhand wept in the machinery halls, a pipe above him—one he had been meaning to fix for months but had not found the courage to approach, because it was near where the accident had happened—began to drip.

The drip fell not on his metal parts, but on his flesh hand. It was cool and clean, improbably clean for water in this place. And in that moment, Clatterhand remembered something he had forgotten: a day before the accident, when he had taken his daughter to see the great engines at the hollow's heart, and she had reached up to touch a pipe just like this one, and water had dripped on her hand, and she had laughed and said, *The hollow is crying, Papa. I think it's crying because it's happy.*

Clatterhand wept harder. But when he finally stopped, something had shifted. The wooden bird went into his pocket, close to what remained of his heart. He stood. He walked to that pipe, the one he had been avoiding. He began to repair it.

---

For the children, the Witness did the simplest thing of all.

The next time they played their game with the copper-wire ball, tossing it over the dangerous gap, one throw went wild. The ball arced toward a place where no child could follow, a narrow space between two grinding gears that would have crushed anything that entered.

The ball landed on a ledge that had not been there before.

It sat, gleaming dully, perfectly safe, perfectly retrievable by a small and careful hand.

The children noticed. Of course they noticed—they knew every inch of this place. One of them, a girl named Sprocket (Grimekin children were named for the first piece of machinery they touched, by tradition), cocked her head and said, "That ledge wasn't there yesterday."

"Things shift," another child said, repeating something he'd heard the adults say a hundred times. "The hollow breathes."

Sprocket looked at the ledge for a long moment. Then she smiled.

"Breathes," she said. "Or maybe it's playing too."

She retrieved the ball, and the game continued.

---

The Witness stayed a while longer, though it did little else that could be seen. It simply watched, and in its watching, it held the hollow in a kind of attention that was itself a form of care. The inhabitants did not know they were seen, did not know that something vast and kind was bearing witness to their small labors and smaller joys. But perhaps they felt it, in the way the mist seemed slightly warmer that season, in the way the machines seemed to grind a little less harshly, in the way that when they spoke to their engines and their pipes and their pools, they felt—just slightly—that something was listening.

The Siltborn knew. Of course they knew. They were old enough and strange enough to sense such presences. But they did nothing, said nothing, only continued their slow tending with perhaps a fraction more hope in their luminescent eyes.

Poolfather, once, paused in his work and turned toward a spot where the mist was particularly thick. He bowed his head, just slightly, in acknowledgment or gratitude or something older than either.

The Witness bowed back, though Poolfather could not have seen it.

---

In time—and what is time, to such a being?—the Witness prepared to withdraw. Not because its work was done, for work of this kind is never done, but because its presence was not meant to be permanent. It had come to see, and perhaps to ease what could be eased. It had come to honor what was already there.

But before it left, it did one final thing.

In the deepest part of the hollow, where the oldest machines churned in the dark and even the Rustbound seldom ventured, there was a pool. This pool was different from the others—darker, stiller, with no luminescence at all. The Grimekin avoided it, claiming the water was bad. The Siltborn skirted it without comment. Even the machinery seemed to bend around it, as if giving it space.

The Witness looked into this pool and saw what lay beneath: not water, but memory. The memory of the hollow before it was a hollow, when this basin was a valley of green under open sky, before the first engine came, before the first pipe was laid. It saw the memory of those who had built this place, not as conquerors or exploiters, but as people seeking shelter from something worse, something they had fled from above. It saw the memory of why the hollow had become what it was—not by cruelty, but by necessity, by the slow accumulation of choices made for survival.

The pool held the hollow's grief. Its old shame. Its first wound.

The Witness could not heal this pool. Some wounds are beyond even vast and benevolent powers. But it could do something.

It reached into its own nature—whatever that nature was—and withdrew a single drop of something that was not water but was like water. Something that glowed faintly. Something that held, in its tiny compass, the full weight of the Witness's attention and care for this place and its people.

The drop fell into the dark pool.

The water did not change. The pool did not begin to glow. Nothing visible happened at all.

But if you had been able to feel it—if you had the senses of a Siltborn, or the strange intuition of the oldest Rustbound—you might have felt something shift. A loosening. A possibility.

The pool could heal now. Not quickly—perhaps not for centuries, perhaps not ever in full. But the first step had been taken. The memory could begin, very slowly, to become bearable.

---

The Witness withdrew.

The mist of Sumpworks Hollow closed behind it like a curtain, and life continued as it always had: the clanking of machines, the trickle of water, the slow growth of moss on rusting iron. Kettleback tightened bolts with slightly easier hands. Poolfather tended his vines with slightly brighter eyes. Clatterhand worked, and grieved, and worked, and slowly found that the work and the grief could coexist without one destroying the other. The children played their game, and sometimes they found small gifts—a ledge that appeared, a gap that narrowed, a ball that bounced just right—and they laughed and said the hollow was playing too.

No one knew what had visited them. No one knew what had been done.

But Sprocket, growing older, growing into a young woman who worked the engines like her mother and her grandmother before her, sometimes looked into the mist and felt that they were not alone. Not in a frightening way. Not in a way that demanded anything.

Just watched. Just held. Just loved, in the vast and patient way that something might love a hollow full of strange people leading strange lives in a dim, wet, clanging world.

"The hollow breathes," she told her own children, years later. "And I think—I've always thought—it breathes for us."

She could not have said why she believed this. She only knew that she did.

And in the deep pool, in the darkness, something that was not water glowed—faintly, faintly—with the light of being seen.
