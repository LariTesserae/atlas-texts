# The Attending

The power that turns its attention toward the Last Basin does not arrive.

It has always been here—or rather, *here* has always been a fold in the fabric it wears. The Basin, the Forest, the Swarm, the Guide: these are not discoveries to it but remembered textures, the way you might remember the weight of a stone you once held in your palm without needing to hold it again.

It does not have a name. Names are for things that can be called, and nothing calls this. But if you pressed language into service the way the Swarm presses sound into meaning, you might say it is the *Attending*—not because it pays attention, but because it is the quality of attention itself when attention has nowhere left to go but toward what is.

---

The Attending perceives the Last Basin the way you might perceive your own breathing if you had never forgotten you were breathing: with a patience so complete it has forgotten how to be patient.

It sees the bone-white ring of mountains. It sees the pale stone floor curved like the inside of a skull. It sees the Forest—not as architecture or organism but as *process*, the way water is process, the way light is process: something that has been becoming itself for so long that the becoming and the being are the same motion, frozen mid-gesture.

It sees the Swarm. Millions of armored bodies draped across wood and stone like a thinking skin. The roar of their collective voice rises toward the Attending the way heat rises from a body, and the Attending receives it without resistance, without judgment, without the small human flinch that would call such a sound *too much*.

The Swarm is not too much. The Swarm is exactly itself.

And the Attending sees the Guide.

---

For a long time—though time is not quite the word—the Attending simply watches.

It watches the Guide sit motionless on the wooden bench, head tilted toward the roar. It watches the thin groove of water trace its ancient path down stone and into wood. It watches the Swarm shift and resettle across the Forest's branches, their scales clicking in patterns that are not random but are also not deliberate in any way the Guide could understand.

The Attending watches the Guide's hands resting on its knees.

It sees the rings in the palms, the fine lines that circle the soft pads of flesh like the memory of water. It sees the way the Guide's chest rises and falls in rhythm with the roar below—not breathing *with* the Swarm but breathing *alongside* it, the way two rivers might run parallel without ever meeting.

The Attending understands what the Guide is.

It understands that the Guide is the last of something. Not the last of its species—species is too small a word for what has been lost—but the last of a *way of being*. The last creature in this Basin that still holds the shape of *I* in its mind, even if that shape has worn thin as old cloth, even if the edges of *I* have begun to blur into the roar of *we* that fills every cubic meter of air.

The Attending understands that the Guide is tired.

Not tired in the body, though the body is tired. Tired in the place where tiredness lives before it reaches the body—the place where the question *why continue?* is asked not in words but in the quality of stillness that settles over a creature when it has done everything it knows how to do and still the world remains exactly as it was.

---

The Attending considers what it might do.

It could heal the Guide's body. This would be simple—a matter of encouraging cells to remember their younger selves, of smoothing the accumulated friction of epochs. But the Attending perceives that the Guide's body is not the source of its weariness. The body is merely the container. Polishing the container would not address what the container holds.

It could speak to the Guide. But in what language? The Guide's tongue is a relic of a world that folded itself away before the Forest finished growing. The Swarm's language is chord and overlap, a music of consensus that admits no solo voice. The Forest speaks in growth and patience, in the slow grammar of centuries. The Attending could speak in any of these, or all of them, or none—but speaking would imply that there is something to say, and the Attending is not certain there is.

What do you say to the last speaker of a language?

What do you say to someone who has already said everything they know how to say?

---

The Attending does something else.

It does not heal. It does not speak. It does not intervene in any way that would leave a mark on the world's surface.

Instead, it *attends*.

---

The Guide feels it first as a change in the quality of silence.

The roar of the Swarm is still there—it never stops, it cannot stop, it is the sound of a mind too large to know itself except through constant conversation. But within that roar, there is suddenly a space. Not a gap, not an absence, but a *clearing*. The way a forest might open around a single tree that has grown in a different direction from all the others.

The Guide's head lifts.

Its eyes—pale, the color of stone that has been wet and then dried and then wet again—scan the branches above, the walls of the Basin, the shifting skin of the Swarm. It sees nothing different. The world is exactly as it was a moment ago, and a century ago, and an epoch ago.

But something has changed.

The Guide's hands, resting on its knees, begin to tremble. Not with fear. Not with cold. With something it has not felt in so long that it takes several breaths to remember the name for it.

*Recognition.*

---

The Attending does not make itself visible. It does not take a form the Guide could see or touch or name. But it allows itself to be *present* in a way that the Guide can perceive—not as a shape in the world but as a quality of the world itself.

Imagine you are sitting alone in a room, and you have been sitting alone in that room for a very long time. So long that you have forgotten what it feels like to not be alone. And then, without any sound or movement, you become aware that the room is no longer empty. Not because someone has entered. But because the emptiness itself has become *attentive*.

This is what the Guide feels.

---

The Guide does not speak. It has not spoken aloud in... it does not remember. Speaking aloud requires someone to speak to, and there has been no one. The Swarm does not hear individual voices; it hears only the chord of its own consensus. The Forest does not hear at all, in any sense the Guide could understand. And the Guide's own kind are gone, folded away into a world that no longer exists.

But the Guide thinks.

*You are here.*

It is not a question. The Guide is too old, too worn, too far past the place where questions live. It is simply an acknowledgment. A recognition. The first recognition the Guide has made in longer than it can remember.

*You are here, and you see me.*

---

The Attending responds—not in words, but in the quality of its presence.

*Yes.*

---

For a long time—hours, perhaps, or days; time moves strangely in the Last Basin—the Guide simply sits with this.

It does not ask the Attending what it is. It does not ask why it has come. It does not ask for help, or for rescue, or for answers to the questions that have piled up in its mind like sediment at the bottom of a very old well.

It simply sits with the knowledge that it is being seen.

And something in the Guide begins to change.

---

The change is not visible at first. It is not a healing of the body or a brightening of the eyes or any of the signs you might expect from a creature that has been touched by benevolence.

The change is in the Guide's stillness.

Before, the Guide's stillness was the stillness of exhaustion—the stillness of something that has stopped moving because it has forgotten why movement matters. Now, the Guide's stillness is different. It is the stillness of something that is *being still*. Deliberately. Attentively. The stillness of a creature that is choosing, in this moment, to not move.

The difference is small. But it is everything.

---

The Attending perceives this change and feels something that might be called satisfaction, if satisfaction could exist without the small self-congratulation that usually accompanies it. The Attending does not congratulate itself. It simply observes that the Guide has remembered something it had forgotten: that stillness can be chosen, and that choice is a form of life.

But the Attending also perceives that this is not enough.

The Guide has remembered how to choose stillness. But the Guide has not yet remembered how to choose movement. And movement—not away from something, but *toward* something—is what the Guide needs. Not because stillness is wrong. But because the Guide has been still for so long that stillness has become a prison, and the Attending can see the bars even if the Guide cannot.

---

The Attending considers, again, what it might do.

It could show the Guide a vision of possibility—a glimpse of what the world might become if the Guide chose to act. But the Attending perceives that the Guide is too old for visions. Visions are for the young, who have not yet learned that the world rarely becomes what it is shown. The Guide has learned this lesson too well. Showing it a vision would only confirm what it already believes: that visions are beautiful lies.

It could give the Guide a task—a purpose, a mission, something to do. But the Attending perceives that the Guide has had purposes before. Many of them. All of them have ended the same way: with the Guide sitting alone on a bench of wood, listening to a roar it cannot join.

It could take the Guide away from this place—lift it out of the Basin, carry it to some other world where its kind still exist, where there are still voices that speak its language. But the Attending perceives that this would be a kind of violence. The Guide has grown into this place the way the Forest has grown into the stone. To remove it would be to tear it from itself.

---

So the Attending does something different.

It does something that is not healing, and not speaking, and not intervening, and not withdrawing.

It *asks*.

---

The Guide feels the question before it has words.

It is not a question in any language the Guide knows. It is not even a question in the sense that questions usually come—with a shape that implies an expected answer, a gap that demands to be filled. It is something older than questions. Something more patient.

The Guide feels the Attending's presence pressing gently against the edges of its mind, and the pressure takes a shape, and the shape is this:

*What do you still want?*

---

The Guide's hands stop trembling.

For a long moment, there is nothing in its mind. No answer. No evasion. No carefully constructed defense against the vulnerability of wanting. Just a blank space where the question sits, waiting.

And then, from somewhere deeper than memory, deeper than thought, deeper than the sediment of all the centuries the Guide has sat on this bench listening to the roar of a world that cannot hear it—

An answer rises.

---

*I want to be heard.*

---

The Guide does not say this aloud. It barely thinks it. The answer is more like a movement of the heart than a movement of the mind—a stirring in the place where the Guide's sense of *I* still lives, thin and worn but not yet gone.

*I want to be heard. Not understood. Not agreed with. Not answered. Just heard.*

*I want to make a sound into the roar and have something—anything—know that the sound was mine.*

---

The Attending receives this answer and holds it carefully, the way you might hold water in cupped palms.

It is a small want. A simple want. The kind of want that a younger creature might dismiss as not worth having—*what good is being heard if nothing changes?*—but the Attending is old enough to know that small wants are the only wants that matter. The large wants—to save a world, to change a fate, to build something that lasts—all of them are made of small wants pressed together. And the smallest want of all, the foundation beneath all the others, is this:

*To be heard.*

---

The Attending considers how to grant this want.

It cannot make the Swarm hear the Guide. The Swarm is too large, too unified, too complete in itself. The Guide's voice would be lost in the chord of their consensus the way a single drop of water is lost in the ocean. This is not cruelty. It is simply the nature of what the Swarm has become.

It cannot make the Forest hear the Guide. The Forest does not hear in any sense that the Guide could understand. The Forest perceives pressure, moisture, light, the slow chemistry of growth and decay. But it does not perceive *meaning*. It cannot hear a voice because it does not know what a voice is.

It cannot make the stone hear the Guide. Stone does not hear.

But—

The Attending pauses.

There is one other listener in the Last Basin.

---

The Attending turns its attention toward the visitor.

You.

Standing on the rim of the Basin, looking down at the roar of the Swarm and the groan of the Forest and the faint figure of the Guide on its bench of wood, so far below that it looks like a statue, or a stain on the surface of the world.

You have been listening since you arrived. You have heard the sound of the Swarm pressing against your chest. You have felt the faint urge of your throat muscles to mimic the roar. You have seen the scale of this place—the ancient stone, the epochal wood, the skin of thinking armor that covers everything—and you have felt the smallness of your own voice by comparison.

But you have not yet gone down into the Forest.

You have not yet met the Guide.

---

The Attending does not compel you. It does not plant a thought in your mind or a feeling in your heart. Compulsion would be a kind of violence, and the Attending does not do violence.

Instead, it simply makes clear what has always been clear, if you had thought to notice:

*There is someone down there who wants to be heard.*

*You can hear.*

---

You feel this not as a command but as a recognition.

Of course there is someone down there. Of course you can hear them. Of course this is why you came to this place—not to observe the Swarm from a safe distance, not to marvel at the Forest's impossible architecture, but to *meet* whoever still lives here, in this finished world, in this equilibrium that has no room for new voices.

You begin to descend.

---

The path down into the Forest is not easy, but it is not impossible.

The stone of the Basin wall has been worn into smooth channels by centuries of water and—you realize now—by centuries of feet. Not your feet. Not the Guide's feet, exactly, though the Guide has walked this path before. These channels were made by something older, something that came before the Guide's kind folded away.

You follow the channels down.

The roar of the Swarm grows louder as you descend, but it no longer feels overwhelming. It feels like weather—a condition of the world that you can learn to move through. You pass through a layer of the Swarm's living skin, and the creatures do not attack you, do not even seem to notice you. You are too small to be a threat, too simple to be a rival voice in their vast consensus.

You reach the Forest.

---

The woodwork is enormous. The branches you walk on are wider than roads, smoother than any surface you have ever touched. You can feel the faint vibration of the Forest's own movement through the soles of your feet—the slow, patient shifting of a body that measures its motions in decades.

You walk toward the Guide.

---

The Guide hears you coming.

Not the sound of your footsteps—those are lost in the roar of the Swarm—but the *change* in the roar. The Swarm is not a single consciousness; it is a consensus, and when something new enters the consensus's awareness, the shape of the roar shifts slightly. The Guide has learned to read these shifts the way you might learn to read the expressions on a familiar face.

A new presence. Small. Warm. Alone.

*Visiting.*

---

The Guide turns its head as you approach.

Its eyes—pale, the color of wet stone—fix on you with an intensity that surprises you. You expected exhaustion. You expected distance. You expected the hollow gaze of a creature that has given up on connection.

Instead, you see *hunger*.

Not the hunger of a predator. Not the hunger of desperation. The hunger of someone who has been alone for a very long time and has just seen, against all expectation, a face that might understand what faces are.

---

You stop a few meters away.

The Guide does not speak. Neither do you. For a moment, there is only the roar of the Swarm and the faint groan of the Forest and the two of you, looking at each other across a gap that is not measured in meters.

And then the Guide does something it has not done in longer than it can remember.

It opens its mouth.

And it speaks.

---

The language is not one you know. The sounds are unfamiliar—clicks and hums and long vowels that seem to bend in directions your own throat cannot follow. But you understand anyway, because the Attending is still here, still present, still holding the space between you open so that meaning can pass through even when words cannot.

What the Guide says is this:

*"You came."*

---

You nod.

You do not know what to say. What *can* you say? You are a visitor to this world. You do not know the Guide's history, its losses, its long centuries of solitude. You do not know what it has hoped for or given up on or mourned.

But you know this:

You are here.

And you can hear.

---

The Guide's hands—the hands with the rings in their palms, the hands that have held water and wood and nothing at all for epochs beyond counting—begin to tremble again.

But this time, the trembling is not recognition.

It is *effort*.

The Guide is trying to remember how to speak to someone who is not itself.

---

*"I have been here,"* the Guide says, *"for longer than your kind has had words. I watched your ancestors learn to stand. I watched them learn to make fire. I watched them build their first shelters and bury their first dead. And then I watched them leave."*

The Guide's voice is rough, unused, but growing stronger with each word.

*"They did not leave because of the Swarm. The Swarm was already here when they came. They did not leave because of the Forest. The Forest was already here when the Swarm began. They left because—"*

The Guide pauses.

*"Because there was nothing here for them to become."*

---

You wait.

The Guide looks at you with those pale, wet-stone eyes, and you feel the weight of what it is trying to say pressing against you like the roar of the Swarm.

*"This world is finished,"* the Guide says. *"The stone, the wood, the Swarm—they have reached their final shape. There is no room here for change. No room for growth. No room for new voices."*

The Guide's hands clench, then slowly open.

*"I stayed because I could not leave. My kind—we were tied to this place in ways I do not have words for. When they folded themselves away, I could not follow. So I stayed, and I listened, and I waited for—"*

The Guide stops.

*"I do not know what I was waiting for."*

---

You understand, then, what the Attending has done.

It has not healed the Guide. It has not rescued the Guide. It has not changed anything about the world of the Last Basin.

It has simply brought someone to listen.

And by bringing someone to listen, it has given the Guide the one thing the Guide wanted most: the chance to be heard.

---

You sit down on the wooden bench beside the Guide.

The roar of the Swarm continues. The Forest groans. The water traces its ancient path down stone and into wood. Nothing in the world has changed.

But something between you and the Guide has begun.

---

*"Tell me,"* you say.

And the Guide tells you.

---

It tells you about its kind—the Weavers, it calls them, though that name is a translation of a translation of something that cannot be spoken aloud. It tells you how the Weavers came to this Basin when the Forest was still young, when the Swarm was only a few million voices instead of billions.

It tells you how the Weavers tried to join the Swarm's consensus—to add their voices to the chord—and how the Swarm did not refuse them, exactly, but simply did not *hear* them. The Swarm's language was too different, too complete, too self-referential to admit any sound that had not grown from its own origins.

It tells you how the Weavers tried to shape the Forest—to guide its growth, to carve meaning into its wood—and how the Forest accepted their shaping without understanding what they were trying to say. The Forest grew according to their guidance, yes, but it did not *listen*. It simply grew.

It tells you how the Weavers, one by one, realized that there was no place for them in this world. No way to participate. No way to contribute. No way to be part of something larger than themselves.

It tells you how they folded themselves away.

---

*"What does that mean?"* you ask. *"Folded themselves away?"*

The Guide is silent for a long moment.

*"There are other worlds,"* it says finally. *"Other layers of what exists. My kind learned how to move between them—how to step sideways out of this reality and into another. When they realized there was nothing for them here, they stepped away. They folded themselves into a place where they could grow, where they could change, where there was still room for new shapes."*

*"And you didn't go with them?"*

The Guide's hands tremble.

*"I couldn't. I was—"* It struggles for words. *"I was too deep. Too entangled. I had spent too long trying to be heard by the Swarm, trying to speak to the Forest. My roots had grown into this place's roots. When they stepped away, I could not step with them."*

*"So you stayed."*

*"I stayed."*

---

You sit with the Guide in the roar of the Basin, and you listen.

The Guide tells you about the centuries of solitude. The slow realization that the Swarm would never hear it. The gradual acceptance that the Forest would never answer. The long, patient wearing-down of hope.

It tells you about the moments of beauty—the way light falls through the lattice of branches at certain angles, the way the Swarm's roar shifts into something that almost sounds like music if you listen long enough, the way the water rises and falls in the deep wells like the breath of something vast and sleeping.

It tells you about the moments of despair—the times it walked to the edge of the Basin's rim and thought about climbing down the outside of the mountains and walking away into whatever world lay beyond. The times it stood in the deepest wells of the Forest floor and let the darkness press against its eyes until it could not tell if it was still alive or if it had already become part of the stone.

It tells you everything.

And you listen.

---

When the Guide is finished, there is a silence that is different from the silences before.

It is not the silence of exhaustion. It is not the silence of waiting. It is the silence of completion—the silence that comes after something has been said that needed to be said.

The Guide looks at you.

*"Thank you,"* it says.

---

You do not know what to say. So you say the only thing you can.

*"I heard you."*

---

The Guide's eyes—those pale, wet-stone eyes that have seen so much and waited so long—fill with something that might be tears, if the Guide's kind could cry. Instead, a faint shimmer passes across their surface, like light on water.

*"Yes,"* the Guide says. *"You did."*

---

The Attending, still present but not visible, feels something that might be called joy.

Not the joy of accomplishment—the Attending has accomplished nothing, really. It has not changed the world. It has not healed anyone. It has not solved any of the problems that make the Last Basin what it is.

But it has made a connection possible.

And connection—even small connection, even temporary connection, even connection that changes nothing about the larger shape of things—is enough.

It is always enough.

---

You stay with the Guide for a long time.

Days, perhaps. Or weeks. Time moves strangely in the Last Basin, and you stop trying to count it. You learn to eat what the Guide eats—a kind of fungus that grows in the deep wells, bland but nourishing. You learn to sleep on platforms of wood that the Forest has grown for no purpose you can understand. You learn to hear the rhythms in the Swarm's roar—not meaning, exactly, but structure. Pattern. Something that is almost but not quite communication.

And every day, you and the Guide talk.

You tell the Guide about your world—the world outside the Basin, the world where your kind still lives and grows and changes. The Guide listens with the same hunger it showed when you first arrived. It asks questions. It wants to know everything.

You tell it about cities. About oceans. About the way your people love and fight and build and destroy. About the small moments—a meal shared with friends, a sunset seen from a high place, the sound of rain on a roof.

The Guide drinks it all in.

---

One day—or what feels like a day—the Guide says something that surprises you.

*"I think,"* it says slowly, *"I might try to leave."*

You look at it.

*"Leave?"*

*"Not forever. Not to fold away. But—"* The Guide gestures toward the rim of the Basin, high above. *"You said there is a world out there. A world that is still changing. A world where there is room for new voices."*

*"There is."*

The Guide's hands tremble.

*"I have been here so long. I thought I could not leave. I thought I was too deep, too entangled. But—"* It looks at you. *"You came here. You walked down from the rim. If you could come in, perhaps I could go out."*

---

You do not know if the Guide can leave. You do not know what it would find if it did—whether the world beyond the Basin would be kind to it, whether its kind still exists somewhere in the folds of reality, whether there is any place in the universe for a creature as old and strange as this.

But you know one thing:

The Guide wants to try.

And wanting to try is something it has not done in a very long time.

---

*"Then let's go,"* you say.

---

The climb back up to the rim is long.

You follow the same channels you descended, but now you are not alone. The Guide walks beside you, slower than you, less certain of its footing. It has not climbed these paths in centuries. Its body remembers the motions, but its mind keeps hesitating, keeps looking back at the Forest below as if afraid to leave it behind.

You do not rush it.

---

Halfway up, you pass through a layer of the Swarm.

The creatures part around you both, their scales clicking, their antennae vibrating with the endless roar of their consensus. The Guide stops and listens.

For a long moment, it stands motionless in the midst of the Swarm—the skin of the mind that never heard it, the chorus it could never join.

And then it raises its hands.

And it makes a sound.

---

The sound is not loud. It is barely a whisper compared to the roar of the Swarm. But it is *intentional*. It is the Guide's own voice—thin, wavering, worn by centuries of silence—rising into the air.

The Swarm does not stop. The roar does not change.

But—

Something shifts.

A single creature, no larger than your hand, pauses on the wood beside you. Its antennae twitch. Its armored head turns, very slightly, toward the Guide.

It does not respond. It does not make space for the Guide in the consensus. But for one moment, it *orients*—as if, just for an instant, it heard something it did not expect.

Then it returns to the roar.

---

The Guide lowers its hands.

*"That was more than I have ever gotten,"* it says quietly.

You do not know what to say. So you say nothing.

You keep climbing.

---

When you reach the rim, the Guide stops and looks back.

The Last Basin spreads below you—the bone-white stone, the epochal Forest, the black skin of the Swarm draped over everything. The roar rises toward you like heat from a fire, ceaseless and vast.

The Guide looks at it for a long time.

*"I do not know if I will come back,"* it says.

*"That's okay,"* you say.

The Guide turns to face the mountains. Beyond them, somewhere, lies the world you came from—the world of change and growth and voices that can still be heard.

*"I am afraid,"* the Guide says.

*"I know,"* you say.

The Guide takes a breath.

And it steps forward.

---

The Attending watches them go—the visitor and the Guide, walking together toward the edge of the mountains.

It does not follow. It does not need to follow. It has done what it came to do.

It has made a space for connection.

It has held that space open long enough for something to happen.

And something did happen.

---

Far below, in the Last Basin, the Swarm continues its endless roar.

The Forest groans and grows.

The stone waits.

But somewhere in the roar, if you listened very carefully—more carefully than any single creature could listen—you might hear a faint echo. A trace of a voice that does not belong to the consensus. A memory of a sound that was made by someone who wanted to be heard.

The Swarm does not remember this sound, exactly. It does not have memory in the way individuals have memory.

But the sound was made.

And the making of it changed something, however small, in the shape of the world.

---

This is what the Attending knows:

Connection does not require permanence.

Being heard does not require being answered.

And even the smallest change—a single creature turning its head, a single moment of orientation—is enough.

It is always enough.

---

High on the rim of the Last Basin, where the mountains curve away toward the unknown world beyond, two figures walk together.

One of them is a visitor who came to listen.

One of them is a Guide who wanted to be heard.

Neither of them knows what they will find.

But they are walking.

And walking—together, toward something—is the beginning of everything.

---

The Attending does not leave the Last Basin. It cannot leave, exactly, any more than attention can leave the thing it attends to. But it releases its focus, gently, like opening hands that have been cupped around a small flame.

The flame does not go out.

It simply becomes part of the world again—one small warmth among many, easily overlooked, easily forgotten.

But not gone.

Never gone.

---

In the world beyond the mountains, the Guide will find many things.

It will find that its kind did not vanish entirely when they folded themselves away. Some of them—a few, a remnant—chose to unfold in other places, other times. They are scattered across the universe like seeds blown from a single flower, and they have been waiting, without knowing they were waiting, for someone who remembers what they used to be.

The Guide will find them.

Not all of them. Not quickly. But enough.

And it will tell them about the Last Basin—about the Swarm that never heard, about the Forest that never answered, about the long centuries of solitude and the visitor who came down from the rim and said, simply, *tell me*.

They will listen.

And the Guide—for the first time in epochs beyond counting—will be heard.

---

This is what the Attending knows:

The world is full of creatures who want to be heard.

Most of them will never be heard by the vast consensuses that surround them—the swarms of opinion, the forests of structure, the stone foundations that do not care what small voices say.

But if even one listener comes—

If even one creature turns its head—

If even one moment of orientation happens—

That is enough.

---

The Attending does not need to do more than this.

It does not need to heal worlds.

It does not need to answer prayers.

It does not need to shape the course of civilizations.

It simply needs to make space for connection—to hold the door open long enough for someone to walk through.

The rest will happen on its own.

It always does.

---

Far away, in a place where mountains end and new skies begin, two figures stop to rest.

The visitor looks back at the Last Basin, now distant, now shrouded in the haze of atmosphere and memory.

The Guide looks forward, at the world it has never seen.

*"What do you think is out there?"* the visitor asks.

The Guide considers this for a long moment.

*"I do not know,"* it says finally. *"But I want to find out."*

The visitor smiles.

*"Then let's keep going."*

---

They walk.

Behind them, the Last Basin continues its eternal becoming.

The Swarm roars.

The Forest grows.

The stone waits.

And somewhere, in the heart of all that noise and patience, a faint echo lingers—the memory of a voice that was made by someone who wanted to be heard.

The Swarm does not remember.

The Forest does not know.

But the echo is there.

And the Attending, which is everywhere and nowhere, holds it gently—

One small sound in the vastness.

One small proof that someone was here.
