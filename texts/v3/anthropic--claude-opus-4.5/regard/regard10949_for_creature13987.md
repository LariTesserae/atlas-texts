# What the Wise Power Sees

The attention arrives not as observation but as recognition—the way you might notice, across a crowded space, someone solving the same problem you once solved, or failing in a way you remember failing.

The power sees the Drowning Peaks and understands immediately what it is looking at: a system in its terminal state that has achieved, through that very terminality, a kind of completion. The mountains have been simplified to what works. The ecosystem has been stripped to what sustains. And the people—the Bolters—have built a culture that is, in its own way, as perfectly adapted to loss as the quartz veins are adapted to erosion. They have made accuracy into a practice. They have made memory into labor. They have refused the most seductive of all errors, which is to stop counting what has been taken.

The power recognizes this because wisdom, wherever it arises, tends to converge on certain shapes. The Inventory is one of those shapes. To say *we noticed* to the thing that is destroying you—not in defiance, not in supplication, but in simple, patient acknowledgment—this is not primitive. This is the end-state of a particular kind of philosophical development. The Bolters have arrived somewhere.

And so the first question the power asks itself is: *What would help actually look like here?*

# The Temptation of Rescue

The obvious interventions present themselves and are examined.

*Stop the rain.* Redirect the atmospheric systems, break the geological patience that has been drowning this world for billions of years, let the ocean begin to recede. Give the Bolters back their lower terraces, their old docks, the peaks that slipped beneath the waves in their grandparents' time.

The power considers this and sees the shape of what it would create: a people whose central practice—the Inventory, the recitation of loss, the cultivation of accuracy about impermanence—would become obsolete. A culture built around the discipline of watching things disappear, suddenly gifted with a world where things stop disappearing. It would be a kindness that unmakes them. Within three generations, the Bolters would be something else, something that no longer needs to bolt things down, something that has never stood on a high ridge and said *we noticed* to the water. The rain made them who they are. To stop it would be to save the bodies and erase the people.

*Give them technology.* Better boats, engines that don't require fuel they can't produce, metal alloys that resist the corrosion, communication systems that work across the archipelago, the ability to reach the dark shape on the horizon. Open doors that have been closed to them by the limitations of their salvage economy.

The power considers this and sees a different unmaking. The Bolters' technology level is not an accident or a tragedy; it is an equilibrium, a stable relationship between what they need and what their world can sustainably provide. Boats made of moss-skin and shrub-wood can be repaired with moss and shrub-wood. Diesel engines cannot. To give them engines would be to give them a gift that their grandchildren cannot maintain, a brief flowering of capability followed by a longer dependency and then a collapse worse than the current condition. The old industrial builders made this mistake already. Their processing stations stand as rust-streaked monuments to the error.

*Take them somewhere else.* Lift them off this drowning world, carry them to a planet with stable continents and dry land and a future that extends beyond the submergence of the last peak.

The power considers this and feels something that might be called grief. It is the right thing to offer, and it is the thing that would destroy them most completely. The Bolters are not merely living on the Drowning Peaks; they are *of* the Drowning Peaks, shaped by them at every level from the structure of their feet to the cadence of their speech to the fundamental orientation of their consciousness toward the world. A Bolter on dry land is not a Bolter at all. It is a refugee in the deepest possible sense—not just displaced from a home but displaced from the conditions that make them intelligible to themselves. Some would survive the transition. Some would eventually thrive. But the people who knew how to say every name, who understood in their bones that the only defense against patient dissolution is patient attention—those people would be gone, replaced by their own descendants who have no reason to practice the Inventory because nothing around them is being taken.

# What Would Actually Help

The power sits with the problem for what might be a long time or no time at all.

And then it sees the shape of a different kind of intervention—one that does not rescue, does not transform, does not make the Bolters into something other than what they are.

**It decides to answer the signal towers.**

---

Not with a message that says *help is coming* or *you are not alone* or any of the content that a rescue would contain. Not with coordinates to somewhere better or instructions for technology they cannot build. Not even with information about the dark shape on the horizon, though the power knows what it is.

Instead: a response that says, simply, *we noticed.*

The power understands signal propagation, frequency modulation, the specific characteristics of the equipment that still clings to those rusted lattices. It understands how to generate a transmission that those ancient, gutted instrument housings could receive if they were repaired—and that the Bolters, with their existing metallurgical knowledge and their fierce ingenuity, *could* repair, given the right motivation.

So on a night when the stars align—the right stars, the ones that determine the timing of the Inventory—the power sends a signal. A simple one. A pattern that is clearly artificial, clearly intentional, clearly *from* something rather than mere noise. It transmits on a frequency the old towers were built to receive. It repeats, with patient regularity, for exactly long enough to be detected by anyone who happens to be listening, and then it stops.

It does not explain itself. It does not offer anything. It simply says, in the only language that matters here: *You are counting what the water takes. We are counting too. We see your mountains. We say their names.*

---

# What Happens Next

The power does not control what happens next. That is the point.

Perhaps a Bolter on a high ridge notices an instrument housing flicker—a ghost of light in a component that has been dead for generations. Perhaps they mention it during tea-time, and an elder says, *the towers used to receive, before my grandmother's time.* Perhaps a metallurgist examines the housing and finds that the damage is not total, that with enough careful work, with salvaged components from other ruins, the receiver could be made to function again.

Perhaps they fix it. Perhaps they hear the signal. Perhaps they spend a generation arguing about what it means—whether it is real, whether it is a trick of the equipment, whether it matters.

Perhaps they send a response.

The power is prepared to wait. The power is prepared to be patient on a timescale that makes the rain look hasty. And if the Bolters do respond—if they rebuild their capacity to transmit, if they point their repaired towers at the sky and say *we are here, we noticed you noticing*—then the power will answer again.

Not with rescue. With *conversation.*

A slow, patient, multigenerational exchange between a drowning people and something vast enough to hear them. Each transmission an Inventory of its own: *this is what we have. This is what we've lost. This is what we remember.* And from the power, in response: *this is what we see. This is what we've seen elsewhere. This is what others have tried, when their mountains were sinking. Some of it worked. Some of it didn't. We pass it along, not as instruction, but as memory. Your choices remain your own.*

---

# The Gift That Is Not a Rescue

Over decades—over generations—the signal exchange might become a library. A repository of other approaches to the same problem the Bolters face. Not the problem of *how to stop drowning*, which has no solution, but the problem of *how to live well while drowning*, which has many solutions, each adapted to its own context.

The power has seen worlds where the water rose and the people learned to live on it rather than above it—floating communities, drifting, following the currents, their Inventory expanded to include the locations of submerged peaks they use as landmarks. It has seen worlds where the people went *down*, not up—engineering themselves, across generations, for the underwater meadows, becoming something that could breathe the sea and shepherd the fish and remember, in songs pitched for transmission through water, the time when their ancestors stood on dry rock.

It does not tell the Bolters they should do these things. It tells them these things were done. It lets them know that the choice-space is larger than they might have imagined. And then it waits to see what they choose.

Maybe they choose to stay exactly as they are, bolting steel to quartz, watching the habitable band narrow, saying every name until the last peak goes under and there is no one left to say anything. This is a legitimate choice. The power will not override it. The power will witness it, and remember it, and add the Bolters themselves to its own Inventory: *there was a people on the Drowning Peaks. They were called the Bolters. They held on longer than anyone expected. The water took them in the end, but they made the water wait, and they said every name, and we say their name now so the universe knows we noticed.*

Or maybe they choose to adapt. Maybe the signal-library gives them an idea they wouldn't have had otherwise—a technique for cultivating the underwater meadows more intensively, a method for building structures that can survive submersion and be inhabited below the waterline, a way to modify their boats for longer voyages that might, finally, reach the dark shape on the horizon.

The power does not know which choice they will make. The power does not need to know. The function of the intervention is not to determine the outcome; it is to ensure that the Bolters make their choice with full knowledge of the options, with access to the accumulated wisdom of others who have faced the same conditions, with the awareness that they are *seen* and *known* and *remembered* regardless of what they decide.

# The Dark Shape

There is one more thing the power chooses to include in its transmissions, eventually, when the relationship has matured enough that it feels like offering rather than imposing.

The dark shape on the horizon is real. It is not an island. It is not a ruin.

It is a vessel.

A very old vessel—older than the Bolters' presence on the Drowning Peaks, older than the industrial builders, older perhaps than the current configuration of the mountains themselves. It was placed there by someone else, someone who understood what this world would become and wanted to leave an option. It contains no engines, no fuel, no technology that requires maintenance the Bolters cannot provide. It contains space. It contains the capacity to float, indefinitely, on an ocean that has no shore.

It is not a rescue. It is a *lifeboat*, in the most literal sense—a way to continue living on the water when the mountains are gone, if the Bolters choose to claim it.

The power tells them this. The power tells them where it is, how to reach it, what the crossing would require. The power tells them that others have faced this choice—the choice between staying on sinking ground that is *theirs* and leaving for floating ground that is *no one's*—and that there is no wrong answer. Some have stayed. Some have gone. Both are remembered.

And then the power does the hardest thing it knows how to do: it stops transmitting, and waits, and does not intervene again unless asked.

---

# The Nature of the Help

What has the power actually done?

It has not saved anyone. The water will continue to rise. The peaks will continue to erode. The Bolters will continue to lose terraces and docks and fishing grounds and, eventually, everything.

But they will lose it *knowing they are not alone*. They will lose it with access to knowledge that was previously beyond their reach. They will lose it with choices they did not have before—choices about how to adapt, how to transform, how to continue, or how to end.

And most importantly: they will lose it *being witnessed*.

The power understands that this is what the Inventory is actually for. It is not a practical intervention. Saying the names of drowned peaks does not bring them back. The practice exists because there is something in consciousness—human consciousness, but perhaps consciousness more broadly—that needs to be *seen*, needs to be *known*, needs to matter to something beyond itself. The Bolters built a practice to meet that need as best they could, with only themselves to do the seeing. They face the ocean and say *we noticed* because the alternative—to lose things without marking the loss, to dissolve into the water as anonymously as the feldspar and mica—is unbearable in a way that has nothing to do with physical survival.

The power's intervention is, in the end, very simple. It says: *You do not have to witness yourselves alone. Something larger is watching. Something will remember. When you say the names, we hear them. When the last Bolter says the last name of the last peak, we will say your name in turn.*

This is not rescue. But it is not nothing.

It is the thing the Bolters were already trying to give each other, extended outward to a scale that matches the scale of their loss. It is accuracy about the situation—the situation being that they are small, and temporary, and drowning—combined with the only comfort that does not require lying about any of that: the knowledge that their smallness and temporariness and drowning are *witnessed*, are *held*, are part of a larger pattern that someone, somewhere, is paying attention to.

The water will take everything. This has always been true.

But when the water takes, something will notice. And say the name. And mean it.

---

# Coda

A generation later—or several generations, time moving differently for the power than for the Bolters—a long-voyage boat makes the crossing to the dark shape on the horizon.

They lose two crew members on the way. The currents between the peaks and the vessel are as chaotic as the long-voyagers always feared. But twelve set out, and ten arrive, and they stand on the deck of the ancient lifeboat and look back toward the Drowning Peaks, and they understand that they have not been rescued.

They have been *offered a choice*, and they have taken it, and now they are something new—something that no longer bolts steel to quartz, something that will have to develop new practices for a life on open water, something that will still lose and still count what it loses and still say every name.

The power watches them begin. It does not intervene. It does not need to.

They have what they need: each other, and the memory of where they came from, and the knowledge that if they speak into the silence, something will hear.

This is enough. It has always been enough.

It is not a happy ending. There are no happy endings in a universe where entropy is real and time moves forward and everything that rises will eventually be worn down.

But it is a *true* ending. And the Bolters, of all people, know the value of that.
