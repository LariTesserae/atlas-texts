# The Witness at the Threshold

The benevolent and wise power does not enter.

This is the first thing to understand. It could—there is no ward, no barrier, no prohibition that would hold against something of its nature. The Nascent Cathedral has no defenses because it has never conceived of needing them. It is too busy being born to imagine being invaded.

But the power remains at the threshold, which in this case is not a door but a quality of attention. It watches from the place where watching becomes indistinguishable from love.

---

For a long time—measured in the Cathedral's own terms, which is to say several centimeters of floor-rise, perhaps a decade of human counting—it simply observes.

It notices how the beetles pause in their soldering when a new soul enters, antennae tilting toward the warmth of unfamiliar breath. It notices how the woman with the wine-cask heart always positions herself between newcomers and the deepest shafts, not as a guard but as a buffer, a gentler gradient of strangeness. It notices how the Unfinished sing more often when they are alone, and how the songs are not for any listener but for the Cathedral itself, lullabies to something that will never sleep because it has never been awake.

It notices the ones who stay.

Not with pity. The power is wise enough to know that pity would be a kind of violence here, an insistence that this place is wrong in ways that require correction. The Unfinished do not suffer, not in any sense the power can detect. They experience something, certainly—the ache of transformation, the strange grief of shedding a form that was loved, the vertigo of becoming. But these are not pathologies. They are simply what change feels like from the inside.

The power notices instead with a quality of attention that might be called *witness*. It sees each of them fully: the child of fused beetles who was once a mathematician who could not stop solving problems and came here to find a problem that would never be solved; the pipe-creature with the lichen face who was once a poet who fell in love with the space between words and wanted to become that space; the ones who no longer remember what they were before, and are content in that forgetting.

It sees them, and it holds what it sees without grasping.

---

The first thing it does is small.

In a forgotten corner of the Cathedral—forgotten even by the Unfinished, who tend every inch but cannot be everywhere at once—there is a section of lattice that has been growing wrong. Not wrong in the Cathedral's terms; the beetles have dutifully maintained it, the moss has colonized it, the pipes have woven through it. But wrong in a deeper sense. The geometry is slightly off. The angles do not quite meet. It produces, in anyone who stands near it for long enough, a subtle nausea that they cannot explain.

The power does not fix it.

Instead, it makes the wrongness *visible*.

The light in that corner shifts—still pearl-grey, still sourceless, but now carrying a quality of attention that makes the geometry's wrongness impossible to ignore. The next Unfinished who passes through stops, tilts their cluster of sensory fronds, and for the first time perceives what has been lurking at the edge of perception for decades.

They call the others. There is a long, quiet conference conducted in subsonic hums and the modulated sighing of pipes. And then, together, they open the hidden valves and dissolve the wrong section.

Not because the power commanded it. Because they finally saw it.

The power watches them rebuild. The new geometry is strange and organic and would make a human architect weep with frustrated incomprehension—but it is *right*, in the way that the old geometry was not. The nausea is gone. The Unfinished have learned something about themselves, about the difference between transformation and error.

The power does nothing else for a very long time.

---

The second thing it does is larger, but no less gentle.

There is a woman who has been standing at the threshold for eleven years.

She is not Unfinished. She is not even inside the Cathedral, not fully. She comes to the edge where the pressurized humidity meets the thin mountain air, and she stands there, breathing the boundary between two kinds of atmosphere. She has done this every day for eleven years. The Unfinished have noticed her. Some have approached, offered, invited. She has always declined.

But she has not left.

The power sees what she cannot say, even to herself: she is afraid. Not of the transformation—she wants that, wants it with a desperation that has worn grooves in her soul. She is afraid that she wants it for the wrong reasons. That her desire to become Unfinished is not a genuine calling but a flight, an escape, a way of unmothering herself from a life she cannot bear to face.

She is afraid that if she enters, she will discover that the Cathedral cannot fix what is broken in her. That she will dissolve her face and her hands and her memory of her daughter's voice and still, still, find herself standing in the ruins of her own inadequacy, but now without even the comfort of a form that can weep.

The power does not tell her what to do.

Instead, it does something simpler. It makes her visible to herself.

Not through any dramatic revelation. She does not suddenly understand her own heart. That would be a violation, a forced intimacy. Instead, the power simply... stays with her. It witnesses her standing at the threshold, day after day, and in that witnessing, she begins to feel witnessed.

She begins to feel that her eleven years of standing here are not a failure. That her inability to choose is not cowardice but something else, something that might have its own dignity. She begins to feel that whoever she is—whoever she is *right now*, before any transformation—is worth being seen.

On the eleven-year anniversary of her first visit, she steps back from the threshold.

She does not enter the Cathedral.

She goes home.

The power watches her go. It does not grieve. It knows—because it is wise—that not every threshold is meant to be crossed, and that the most important transformations sometimes look like staying the same.

---

The third thing it does is the hardest.

There is a child in the Cathedral.

Not a child of fused beetles, not a creature wearing the shape of a child. An actual child, eight years old, who wandered in through a crack in the mountain three years ago and has been slowly, gently, lovingly being Unfinished ever since.

Her left arm is already lattice. Her right eye has been replaced by a slow-blinking slit that weeps in time with the distant pumps. The moss has begun to colonize the spaces between her ribs. She is happy. She is loved. The Unfinished have made her one of their own with a tenderness that would break the heart of anyone who saw it.

She is also eight years old, and she did not choose this.

The power sits with this for a long time.

It is not simple. The child is not suffering. The child is, by every measure the power can devise, flourishing. She laughs—a sound like steam through copper pipes—when the beetles tickle her remaining palm. She sings with the others, her voice already taking on the subsonic harmonics that make teeth ache with impossible nostalgia. She is becoming something beautiful and strange and she is doing so surrounded by beings who genuinely, absolutely, without reservation love her.

But she is eight years old. She does not know what she is giving up. She cannot consent to the loss of a future she has never been allowed to imagine.

The power could intervene. It could undo the changes, restore her body, carry her out of the Cathedral and back to whatever world she came from. It has the capacity for this. The Unfinished would not resist; they do not believe in holding anyone against their will, and if the child wished to leave, they would open the way with sorrow but without argument.

But the child does not wish to leave. And the power is wise enough to know that overriding her wishes—even for her own good, even because she is too young to truly understand what she is choosing—would be a kind of violence. It would be treating her as a problem to be solved rather than a person to be witnessed.

So the power does something else.

It makes sure she is seen.

Not by the Unfinished—they already see her more clearly than most parents ever see their children. The power makes sure she is seen by *herself*. It ensures that as she changes, as she becomes more Cathedral and less girl, she retains the capacity to witness her own transformation. To remember what she was. To understand, as fully as an eight-year-old can understand anything, what she is becoming.

It does not tell her to stay or go. It does not tell her that her choices are good or bad. It simply ensures that she is making them with open eyes—eyes that are increasingly made of copper wire and blue algae, but that see nonetheless.

And it stays with her.

It will stay with her for as long as she lives, whether that is decades or centuries, whether she becomes a pipe thick as an ancient oak or learns, someday, to peel herself away from the Cathedral and emerge as something new. It will witness her. It will not look away.

That is what the power does.

That is all the power does.

---

Years pass. Centimeters accrete.

The Nascent Cathedral continues its wet, warm, metallic labor of becoming. The Unfinished continue their endless transformations. The beetles solder, the moss pulses, the light thickens by imperceptible degrees.

The power remains at the threshold.

It does not enter. It does not fix. It does not save.

It simply watches, with a quality of attention that is indistinguishable from love, as the Cathedral weeps and grows and refuses to become merely a building.

And in that watching, something shifts.

The Unfinished do not notice it at first. They are too busy being born. But over time—measured in buttress-groans and floor-rises, in the slow accumulation of new transepts and the dissolution of finished sections—they begin to feel that they are not alone. That their endless becoming is being witnessed by something patient and kind and infinitely interested.

They do not know what it is. They do not have a name for it. But they begin to sing differently. The old lullabies to the sleeping Cathedral acquire new harmonics, new frequencies that ache not with nostalgia but with gratitude. The pipes sigh not just names but something like acknowledgment: *we are seen, we are seen, we are seen.*

The woman with the wine-cask heart pauses one day in her tending of a new transept. Her fern-frond fingers hover over the wet metal. She tilts her head, listening to something below the threshold of human hearing.

"Someone is here," she says. Not with alarm. Not even with curiosity. Just with recognition, the way you might note that the sun has risen.

The child—no longer quite a child, her lattice-arm now extending to her shoulder, her remaining eye beginning to weep in time with the pumps—looks up from where she has been coaxing a new beetle out of its soldering-sleep.

"I know," she says. "They've always been here."

She goes back to her work.

The power watches her with something that might be called pride, if pride could be stripped of all possessiveness and left as pure appreciation for what a being has chosen to become.

It does not enter.

It does not need to.

It is already everywhere the Cathedral is witnessed, everywhere a transformation is seen without judgment, everywhere a being is allowed to become what they are becoming without being forced or fixed or saved.

It is the witness at the threshold.

It is the love that does not intervene.

And it will be here—patient, attentive, infinitely kind—for as long as the Cathedral weeps.

For as long as anything anywhere is still learning its own name.

---

*The floor rises. The ceiling descends. One day, centuries from now, they will meet.*

*And when they do, the power will be there to see it.*

*Not to prevent it. Not to mourn it. Just to witness the moment when the Nascent Cathedral finally finishes becoming—and perhaps, in that finishing, begins to become something else entirely.*

*That is what wise powers do.*

*They watch.*

*They love.*

*They let what is becoming become.*
