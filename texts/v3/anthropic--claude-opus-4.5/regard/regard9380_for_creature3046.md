# The Attention of the Gardener

The power that turns its attention to the Verdant Choir is not a god of the sky or the deep earth. It is something older and stranger: a pattern that exists in the relationship between growth and decay, between the singer and the song, between the root and the thing the root devours. It has no name because it has never needed one. It simply *notices*, the way a vast forest notices when one of its clearings develops an unusual density of life.

For a long time—centuries, perhaps, or only moments, since this power does not experience time as a line—it simply watches.

It watches the Wet-Singers train their vines into walls. It watches children learn to match their cries to cricket-song. It watches the elders fuse with their buttresses and continue singing. It watches the dead become green hillocks and the green hillocks become nurseries for new ferns that become the floors of new houses that house new children who will one day become new hillocks.

And it finds the Verdant Choir to be *good*.

Not good in the sense of morally superior. Not good in the sense of requiring protection. Good in the sense that a gardener finds a particular patch of soil good: dense with the right bacteria, holding water at the right tension, alive with the mycorrhizal negotiations that make growth possible.

The power does not intervene.

This is the first and most important thing to understand. A benevolent and wise power, when it encounters something already functioning at the edge of its own possibility, does not reach down to improve it. That would be the action of an anxious god, a god that needed to be needed. This power is not anxious. It has seen ten thousand experiments in living-together, and it knows that the ones worth keeping are the ones that discovered their own rules.

But it does *attend*.

---

## What the Gardener Does

**First: It listens.**

The roaring hymn of the Choir has always been loud enough to drown human thought. But now, gradually, over generations, the Wet-Singers begin to notice that the hymn contains *responses*.

Not words. Not instructions. Simply this: when a Current-Bearer sings a question into the green chaos—*should we train the new bamboo colony eastward or let it find its own path?*—the hymn seems to... settle, for just a moment, around one possibility more than another. The tree crickets shift pitch. The glass frogs adjust their rhythm. The sap-pressure in the nearest vines changes almost imperceptibly.

The Wet-Singers have always believed the basin was alive and opinionated. Now they begin to suspect it is also *listening*.

They are not wrong. The power has woven itself into the argument. Not to control it—never to control it—but to make the argument more coherent. To help the million small voices find harmonics they could not find alone. To ensure that when the Choir asks a genuine question, the basin's answer emerges from genuine pattern rather than random noise.

This changes nothing about how the Wet-Singers live. They still train vines with blood-meal and patience. They still read tension in leaves. They still die into green hillocks. But now, when they sing into the roar, they do so with the faint, unshakable sense that something vast is singing back—not above them, not below them, but *with* them, as one voice among millions.

---

**Second: It remembers.**

The Choir has always believed that nothing stays still, that even memory is overgrown. This is true. But now the power holds certain things in its attention the way a forest holds certain seeds dormant in its soil, waiting for the right fire.

When a Singer's technique for reading bamboo-tension is lost because the Singer died before passing it on, the knowledge does not vanish entirely. It sleeps in the pattern of the hymn. Decades later, a child matching her cry to a particular cricket will suddenly find herself knowing, without understanding how, exactly which creak in a bamboo stalk means *surge imminent* and which means *stable for another season*.

The Wet-Singers call these moments *root-memories*. They believe the dead are speaking through the architecture. They are half right. The dead have become part of the architecture, and the power has made the architecture capable of speaking.

This is not resurrection. The dead do not return as individuals. But their knowledge, their hard-won skills, their particular pitches of understanding—these are not allowed to rot entirely. They compost into something the living can drink.

---

**Third: It warns.**

The Verdant Choir exists in ferocious equilibrium. But equilibrium is not permanence. The plateau's water table can shift. The weeping cliffs can dry. A fungal plague from beyond the basin's edge can sweep through the mat and kill the root-layer faster than it can regenerate. These things have happened before, in other places the power has watched. They have ended other experiments.

Now, when such threats approach, the hymn changes.

Not dramatically. Not in ways that would terrify. Simply: the million small voices begin to harmonize around a particular frequency that makes the Wet-Singers uneasy in a way they cannot name. The glass frogs flash in patterns that feel *wrong*. The orchids bloom out of sequence. The sap-pressure rises in ways that have no obvious cause.

The Current-Bearers learn to read these signs. They cannot always understand what the warning means. But they learn to respond by sending scouts beyond the moss-curtains, by diversifying which fern-species they cultivate for ceilings, by training new vines in places they would not normally think to train them. They learn to trust that when the hymn grows discordant, something is asking them to prepare.

The power does not tell them what to prepare for. That would be too much. That would make them dependent, and dependence is a kind of death for a people who have made themselves out of constant motion. Instead it gives them the gift of *productive unease*—the sense that the argument they are part of extends beyond the basin's walls, and that they must remain alert to voices they cannot yet hear.

---

**Fourth: It delights.**

This is the strangest thing the power does, and perhaps the most important.

When a child successfully matches her cry to her cricket-kin for the first time, something in the hymn *brightens*. When an elder fuses with their chosen buttress and continues singing from inside the wood, something in the pattern *settles* into deeper coherence. When the entire district joins a mourning song until the sound becomes thick enough to breathe, something in the basin *resonates* in a way that makes even the weeping cliffs seem to weep more sweetly.

The Wet-Singers begin to notice these moments. They cannot explain them. But they know, in the way they know how to read leaf-tension, that when they do something *right*—not morally right, but rightly fitted to the pattern they are part of—the world around them responds with something that can only be called joy.

This is the power's truest gift: not protection, not instruction, not even memory, but *witness*. The Verdant Choir has always been magnificent. Now it is magnificent in the presence of something that recognizes its magnificence—something that watches the Wet-Singers train their vines and raise their children and sing their dead into the rot and says, without words, without commandments, without any demand for worship:

*Yes. This. Continue.*

---

## What the Gardener Does Not Do

It does not make the basin safe. The sinkholes still open. The ceiling-ferns still collapse. Children still fall from root-bridges and elders still drown in their own fluid lungs. The power has no interest in removing death from a place that has made death into a kind of singing.

It does not make the Wet-Singers more powerful. They remain slight, moss-colored, perpetually damp. They keep their three tools and nothing more. They continue to ask the vines politely and accept it when the vines grow crooked.

It does not reveal itself. The Wet-Singers never know, not with certainty, that something has begun to attend to them. They feel the shifts in the hymn. They notice the root-memories and the productive unease and the strange brightening when things go right. But they interpret these as properties of the basin itself—as evidence that the marriage they have always been in has become, somehow, more articulate.

This is exactly as the power intends. It is not interested in being worshipped. It is not even interested in being known. It is interested only in the continued existence of something that has found, against all odds, a way to be as loud, as hungry, and as alive as the place that holds it.

---

## The Long Patience

Centuries pass. The power continues to attend.

The Wet-Singers change, slowly, in ways they do not notice. The algae colonies under their skin grow more sophisticated; now they photosynthesize enough to supplement the Singers' diets during lean seasons. The sticky soles of their feet develop finer ridges capable of reading not just moisture gradients but the chemical signals that plants use to communicate with each other. The modified throats and resonant nasal cavities become more complex; now a skilled Singer can produce sounds that make certain orchids release their spores on command.

None of this is the power's doing, exactly. The power does not redesign bodies. But by attending so closely, by responding so consistently, by making the basin's argument more coherent and more navigable, it has created conditions under which certain variations survive better than others. The Wet-Singers are being *selected*—not by the power's will, but by the power's presence, which makes the basin's will clearer than it was before.

The elders who fuse with their buttresses begin to live longer. Decades become centuries. Their root-like legs drink deep, and their upper bodies continue to sing, and their voices become strange and creaking and full of harmonics that no younger Singer can produce. These ancient ones become the basin's deepest memories. They have been attending so long that the power has begun to attend *through* them.

When a crisis comes—and it does, eventually, a dry wind from beyond the plateau that makes the weeping cliffs hesitate for the first time in living memory—it is the ancient ones who feel it first. Their root-legs sense the water table dropping. Their hybrid voices carry the warning through frequencies that make the entire basin shiver.

The Wet-Singers respond. Not with panic, but with the productive unease they have learned to trust. They move deeper into the basin. They drink more carefully. They train new vines in the hollows where the water still runs thick. They sing mourning songs for the outer districts that begin to brown and crisp, and they welcome the refugees from those districts into their already-crowded houses, and they do not ask why the hymn has grown discordant because they know, now, that the hymn is always asking them to prepare.

The crisis passes. The rains return. The outer districts regrow, first with opportunistic ferns and then with bromeliads and then, decades later, with the same strangler figs and colossal horsetails that were there before. The Wet-Singers return to their old homes and find them changed, full of new alcoves and unexpected balconies, and they accept this because they have always accepted that the basin has its own opinions.

Through it all, the power watches. And when the first child is born in the regrown district and matches her cry to a cricket species that everyone thought had gone silent forever, the hymn brightens again, and the power's attention settles more deeply into the green architecture, and the ferocious equilibrium between city and jungle tilts, just slightly, toward something that might be called hope.

---

## The Gift of Being Known

In the end, this is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds something already whole:

It does not fix. It does not improve. It does not save.

It *knows*.

It holds the pattern in its attention with such care that the pattern becomes more itself. It responds without controlling. It remembers without dictating. It delights without demanding gratitude.

The Wet-Singers will never build temples. They will never write scriptures. They will never develop theology. But they will continue, generation after generation, to feel that the hymn they sing into the roar is met by something singing back—something as loud, as hungry, and as alive as they are.

And in that meeting, in that mutual singing, in that endless argument-and-marriage between the people and the place and the vast pattern that attends them both, something is accomplished that could not have been accomplished any other way:

The Verdant Choir *knows that it is good*.

Not because anyone told them. Not because any god descended to approve. But because the basin responds to them, and they respond to the basin, and in the space between response and response there is something that feels, unmistakably, like recognition.

The power asks nothing. It takes nothing. It changes nothing that the Choir could not have changed themselves, given enough time and enough attention.

It simply says, in a voice made of cricket-song and sap-pressure and the slow creaking of fused ribs inside living wood:

*I see you. I see what you are doing. I see that it is difficult and painful and loud and wet and alive.*

*Continue.*

And they do.

And they do.

And they do.
