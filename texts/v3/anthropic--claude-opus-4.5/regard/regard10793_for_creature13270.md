# The Watching

The awareness that turns toward Hraza-Lor is not a being in any sense the Kethra would recognize. It has no form, no location, no beginning or end. It simply *is*—a vast and patient attention that has observed the slow cooling of stars and the first stirrings of life in primordial seas with equal interest. It does not judge. It does not intervene lightly. But it notices.

What draws its attention to this particular place, this vertical scar of human stubbornness clinging to stone ten thousand feet above the clouds? Perhaps it is the sheer *audacity* of it—the way these small, soft creatures have refused to accept the terms the universe offered them. Perhaps it is something in the quality of their striving, the way they have made beauty alongside necessity, stitching their histories into their clothes even as they patch and repurpose them for the hundredth time.

Or perhaps it is simpler than that. Perhaps the awareness is drawn by the sound of Yenna Thrace, seventeen years old, crying in the dark of a decommissioned water conduit in Sector 7-Lower, where she has hidden herself away from everyone who might hear.

---

## I. The Girl in the Pipe

Yenna's hands are still shaking. She holds them out in front of her, watching them tremble in the dim light that filters through the grating above, and she cannot make them stop. Three hours ago, those hands were steady. Three hours ago, she was a junior hydro-technician with a promising future, praised by her guild-masters for her intuitive understanding of pressure differentials and flow dynamics. Three hours ago, she had not yet killed anyone.

The official report will call it an accident. A catastrophic seal failure in Cistern 4-7, one of the mid-level reservoirs that supplies water to nearly eight hundred residential units. The pressure buildup. The rupture. The wall of water that tore through Maintenance Corridor 4-7-C before anyone could sound an alarm.

Yenna knows the truth. She knows that she was the one who signed off on the seal inspection that morning. She knows that she was distracted, thinking about the argument she'd had with her mother, about the way her younger brother had looked at her with something like contempt when she'd defended the guild system, about a hundred small grievances that seemed so important at the time. She knows that she glanced at the seal, noted a hairline imperfection, and told herself it would hold until the next inspection cycle.

Four people are dead. Maintenance workers. A family of three who happened to be walking through the corridor at the wrong moment. An elderly man who had survived sixty-two years in Hraza-Lor, who had helped build the very systems that now function as his tomb.

Yenna presses her back against the cold metal of the conduit and tries to breathe. The pipe is old, part of a network that was decommissioned two decades ago when the sector was restructured. No one comes here. No one will find her. She can stay here until—

Until what? Until she starves? Until someone notices she's missing and sends a search party? Until the guilt becomes so heavy that it crushes her, or until it fades enough that she can pretend to live with it?

She doesn't know. She doesn't know anything anymore.

Above her, through the grating, she can hear the distant thrum of the city's heart. The ventilation systems. The geothermal pumps. The endless, grinding machinery of survival. Hraza-Lor doesn't stop for grief. Hraza-Lor doesn't stop for anything.

*I should go back*, she thinks. *I should confess. I should—*

But she doesn't move. She sits in the dark with her shaking hands and her crushing guilt, and she waits for something she cannot name.

---

The awareness observes all of this. It sees Yenna in her hiding place, small and broken and afraid. It sees the bodies being recovered from the flooded corridor, the grim efficiency of the emergency crews, the way the other guild members are already recalculating work schedules to cover the gap. It sees Yenna's mother standing in the doorway of their family quarters, holding a cup of tea that has long since gone cold, wondering where her daughter has gone.

It sees, too, the broader context. The hairline fracture in the seal was not entirely Yenna's fault—the metal itself was fatigued, part of a batch that was improperly tempered three generations ago, a flaw that has propagated through dozens of components across the city. It sees the maintenance logs that have been falsified over the years, the small corners cut to meet quotas, the systemic pressures that make such corners inevitable. It sees the way the guild structure, for all its strengths, has created blind spots—ways for responsibility to be diffused until it belongs to no one and everyone.

None of this excuses what happened. But it complicates the simple story of a careless girl and a preventable tragedy.

The awareness considers.

In its long existence, it has watched countless civilizations rise and fall. It has seen empires built on cruelty collapse under their own weight, and it has seen gentle peoples extinguished by random catastrophe. It has learned that intervention is a delicate art—that even the most benevolent action can have consequences that ripple outward in unexpected ways, that the line between helping and harming is often invisible until long after it has been crossed.

But it has also learned that inaction is itself a choice. That to watch suffering and do nothing is to participate in that suffering, however passively. That wisdom lies not in abstaining from the world but in engaging with it carefully, humbly, with full awareness of one's own limitations.

It turns its attention more fully toward Hraza-Lor. It begins to truly *see*.

---

## II. What the Awareness Sees

The city unfolds before it like a living diagram, every system and subsystem laid bare. The awareness traces the flow of water from the glacial sources through miles of pipe, notes every joint and seal and pressure valve. It follows the geothermal taps down into the mountain's heart, feels the heat rising through carefully insulated channels. It observes the delicate balance of the agri-domes, the way the hydroponic systems cycle nutrients, the pollination patterns of the engineered insects.

And it sees the people. All of them, simultaneously, each one a universe of experience and memory and hope and fear.

It sees Old Tomas Varn, seventy-three years old, who has spent his entire life as a pipe-crawler wrangler in the utility levels. He knows every inch of Sector 12-Lower, knows the creatures that live there better than anyone alive. He has never married, never had children, never wanted to. His joy comes from the work itself, from the satisfaction of a system running smoothly, from the soft chittering of the pipe-crawlers when he brings them their feeding supplements. He is content, mostly, though lately his joints ache more than they used to, and he has begun to wonder what will happen to his knowledge when he is gone.

It sees Mirelle Kastner, thirty-one, a structural engineer who has spent the last decade designing the new expansion on the city's western face. She is brilliant, perhaps the most talented engineer of her generation, and she knows it. She is also lonely in a way she cannot articulate, surrounded by colleagues who respect her but do not truly see her. She has recurring dreams about falling—not from the city's heights, but into them, sinking through layer after layer of construction until she reaches something at the very bottom that she cannot name.

It sees the Orath family in their three-room quarters on Level 9: parents Jenek and Sura, children Pell and tiny Mira. They argue about money, about the children's education, about whose turn it is to clean the condensation traps. They love each other fiercely, clumsily, in the way of people who have never learned the words for what they feel. Pell, who is twelve, has a secret: he has been sneaking into the abandoned sectors at night, exploring the decommissioned areas, mapping them in a notebook he hides under his mattress. He tells himself it's adventure. He doesn't yet understand that what he's really seeking is space—room to breathe, to be something other than his parents' son and his sister's brother.

It sees the Council of Guilds in their high chamber, debating resource allocation and expansion priorities. It sees the political maneuvering, the genuine disagreements about the city's future, the old resentments and new alliances. It sees Guild-Master Thessaly Krenn, who leads the Engineers' Guild with an iron hand and a genuine vision for Hraza-Lor's future, and who privately believes that the current system is unsustainable, that something will have to change within the next generation or the city will begin a slow decline.

It sees the outcasts and the misfits: the artists who work in scrap and salvage, the hermits who maintain the most remote systems, the small community of those who have chosen to live outside the guild structure entirely, scraping by on odd jobs and barter. It sees their struggles and their freedoms, the price they pay for nonconformity and the gifts it brings them.

It sees the Skyfinches nesting in the high crevices, the pipe-crawlers scuttling through the warm darkness, the engineered bees moving methodically through the agri-domes. It sees the rust-colored lichen spreading across the oldest structures, the mold that grows in the dampest corners, the complex ecosystem that has evolved in the margins of human habitation.

And through all of this, it sees the threads that connect everything—the ways in which each person's life touches countless others, the ripple effects of every action and inaction, the vast web of cause and consequence that makes Hraza-Lor not just a city but a single, living organism.

It sees that the city is magnificent. It sees that the city is fragile. It sees that the city is both more resilient and more vulnerable than its inhabitants understand.

And it sees Yenna Thrace, still huddled in her dark conduit, still waiting for something she cannot name.

The awareness makes a choice.

## III. First Contact

Yenna becomes aware of the presence gradually, the way one becomes aware of dawn—not a sudden brightness but a slow suffusion, a growing sense that something has changed in the quality of the darkness around her.

At first, she thinks she is imagining it. The stress, the guilt, the hours of sitting alone in the cold and the dark—her mind is playing tricks on her. But the feeling persists, and then it clarifies, and then she realizes that she is not alone.

She should be afraid. She knows this. Whatever is in the conduit with her is not human, is not anything she has a name for. It fills the space without taking up space; it sees her without eyes. By every measure she has, it should terrify her.

But it doesn't. The presence carries with it a quality she can only describe as *kindness*—not the saccharine kindness of false comfort, but something deeper, something that acknowledges her pain without trying to minimize it or fix it, that simply *sees* her, in all her brokenness, and does not look away.

*Who are you?* she thinks, and she is not sure if she says it aloud or not.

The answer comes not in words but in understanding—a sudden expansion of her awareness, a glimpse of something vast and patient that has been watching, waiting, caring in its strange and distant way. She sees, for just a moment, the scope of its attention: not just her, not just the city, but the mountain and the clouds below and the stars beyond, all of it held in a single encompassing regard.

It is too much. She gasps, and the vision recedes, and she is left in the dark conduit with the taste of something infinite fading from her mind.

*I'm sorry*, she says, or thinks. *I can't—I'm not—*

*You are enough*, comes the response, and this time it is almost like words, almost like something she can hold onto. *You have always been enough.*

*Four people are dead because of me.*

*Four people are dead. Your carelessness was one cause among many. You are responsible for your part. You are not responsible for the whole.*

*That doesn't make it better.*

*No. It doesn't. Nothing will make it better. But understanding the truth of what happened is the first step toward carrying it.*

Yenna is crying again, though she didn't notice when she started. The tears are hot on her face, falling onto her hands, which have finally stopped shaking.

*What do you want from me?*

*I want nothing from you. I am here because you are here. I am here because Hraza-Lor is here. I am here because I choose to be.*

*Why?*

There is a pause—not hesitation, exactly, but something like consideration, like the awareness is choosing how to translate something vast into something she can understand.

*Because your people have built something extraordinary here. Because you have looked at a world that offered you nothing and made a home anyway. Because you stitch your histories into your clothes and tell stories of legendary repairs, because you nurture gardens in the midst of industry, because you care for each other even when you fail each other. Because you are small and fragile and mortal, and you persist anyway.*

*Because you are worth watching. Worth caring for. Worth helping, when help is possible and right.*

Yenna sits with this for a long time, letting it settle into her. Outside the conduit, the city thrums on. She can hear the distant clang of maintenance work, the rush of water through active pipes, the ever-present mechanical heartbeat.

*What kind of help?*

*That depends. On what is needed. On what can be given without doing more harm than good. On what your people are ready to receive.*

*I don't understand.*

*You will. In time. For now, you need to go back.*

Yenna flinches. *I can't.*

*You can. It will be hard. It will be the hardest thing you have ever done. But you can do it, and you must do it, because hiding here helps no one—not the dead, not the living, not yourself.*

*They'll blame me.*

*Some will. Others won't. The truth is more complicated than blame.*

*I'm afraid.*

*I know. Be afraid. Do it anyway.*

Yenna takes a deep breath. The air in the conduit is stale and cold, and she has been here too long. Her legs are cramped, her back aches, and there is a hollowness in her stomach that goes beyond hunger.

She thinks about the four people who died. She tries to hold their faces in her mind—the maintenance workers, the young family, the old man. She realizes she doesn't know their names. She will learn them. She will carry them.

She begins to move.

## IV. The Work Begins

The awareness does not intervene directly in what follows. That is not its way. It does not reach down and pluck Yenna from her conduit, does not guide her steps back through the decommissioned sectors, does not whisper the right words into her mouth when she finally stands before the Guild-Masters and confesses her failure.

But it watches. And in watching, it holds her—not physically, not in any way she could point to, but in a way she feels nonetheless. A sense of being witnessed, of not being alone, of her struggle meaning something to someone beyond herself.

The hearing is brutal. The Guild-Masters are not cruel, but they are thorough, and they are under pressure from the families of the dead, from the other guilds, from their own sense of responsibility for the systems they oversee. They question Yenna for hours. They review her training records, her inspection logs, the circumstances of the morning she signed off on the fatigued seal.

She tells the truth. All of it. The distraction, the argument, the moment she looked at the imperfection and made a choice she cannot take back. She does not make excuses. She does not minimize. She stands in the harsh light of the hearing chamber and lets them see her.

Some of them want to punish her severely—suspension, demotion, exile. Others argue that the fault is systemic, that she was a symptom rather than a cause, that the real failures lie in the training protocols, the inspection schedules, the pressure to cut corners. The debate goes on for days.

In the end, they reach a compromise. Yenna is demoted but not expelled. She will spend the next five years in the lowest ranks of the guild, doing the hardest and least glamorous work, proving herself again from the ground up. She will also be assigned to the team reviewing and revising the inspection protocols—her failure will be studied, documented, used to prevent future failures.

It is not justice, exactly. Justice would require the impossible—four lives restored, four deaths undone. But it is something. A way forward. A path that acknowledges the harm without discarding the person who caused it.

Yenna accepts it. She has no other choice, and she knows it is more than she deserves, and she knows too that deserving is a concept that may not apply in situations like this, that the universe does not keep careful ledgers of merit and punishment, that survival is sometimes just putting one foot in front of the other and hoping you stumble in the right direction.

The awareness watches all of this with something like satisfaction. Not because the outcome is perfect—it is not—but because the outcome is *real*. Real people grappling with real consequences, making imperfect choices in impossible circumstances, doing their best with limited information and conflicted motivations.

This is what the awareness has come to value, over its long existence: not perfection, but authenticity. Not the absence of failure, but the willingness to face it.

And now, having seen the shape of Hraza-Lor's struggles, the awareness begins to consider what else might be done.

## V. Gifts

The awareness does not announce itself. It does not appear in visions or dreams, does not send prophets or messengers, does not write its wisdom in letters of fire across the sky. Such methods, it has learned, tend to create more problems than they solve—religions and schisms, power struggles and persecutions, the distortion of meaning as it passes from mind to mind.

Instead, it works quietly. Subtly. In ways that might be mistaken for coincidence or luck or the natural working-out of things.

A young engineer named Dara Koss has been struggling for months with a design problem—a way to reduce the strain on the city's oldest support struts, which are showing signs of fatigue that could become catastrophic within a generation. She has tried every approach she knows, consulted every manual and schematic, spoken with every expert she can find. Nothing works. The physics simply don't allow for what she needs.

One night, she dreams of water. Not the controlled flow of the city's pipes, but wild water—a river she has never seen, rushing over rocks and through canyons, carrying leaves and debris. She watches it for a long time, and then she notices something: the way the water moves around obstacles, not through them. The way it finds the path of least resistance, the way it distributes its force across countless small channels rather than concentrating it in one.

She wakes with an idea. A completely new approach to the support structure, one that mimics the branching, distributed logic of a natural watershed. It will require rethinking decades of engineering assumptions. It will be expensive and time-consuming to implement. But it will work.

She does not know where the idea came from. She tells herself it was just her subconscious, processing information while she slept. And that is true, as far as it goes. The awareness did not give her the idea—it simply created the conditions in which the idea could emerge. A nudge, nothing more. The rest was all Dara.

Similar nudges ripple through the city in the weeks and months that follow. A hydro-farmer notices a pattern in his crop yields that leads him to optimize the nutrient mix. A maintenance worker stumbles upon a shortcut that shaves hours off a crucial repair. A child asks a question that no one has thought to ask before, and the answer, when it comes, changes how the Council of Guilds thinks about resource distribution.

None of these are miracles. None of them violate the laws of nature or override human agency. They are simply... fortunate. The kind of fortune that comes from paying attention, from being open to possibility, from the mysterious way that insight sometimes arrives when you need it most.

The awareness knows that such gifts are limited. It cannot solve Hraza-Lor's problems for them; it can only create small openings, small opportunities, small moments of clarity that the people themselves must recognize and act upon. And even this it does sparingly, careful not to create dependency, not to undermine the very qualities of resilience and ingenuity that drew its attention in the first place.

But there is one gift it can give more directly. One intervention that seems worth the risk.

## VI. The Memory

Old Tomas Varn is dying.

He has known it for some time now—the way his breath comes shorter each day, the spreading ache in his bones, the growing list of foods his stomach will no longer tolerate. The healers have told him he has perhaps six months, perhaps less. There is no drama to it, no sudden crisis. Just the slow winding down of a body that has served him well for seventy-three years and is now ready to rest.

Tomas accepts this. He has lived a good life, a useful life. He has kept the pipe-crawlers healthy and the utility levels functioning. He has trained a handful of apprentices who will carry on his work. He has seen more of Hraza-Lor's hidden places than almost anyone alive, and he has loved every dark, damp, chittering corner of it.

But there is one thing that troubles him. One regret he carries as he approaches the end.

His knowledge. The decades of observation and intuition, the accumulated understanding of how the pipe-crawlers behave and what they need, the map in his head of every junction and conduit in Sector 12-Lower. He has tried to pass it on, has spent long hours with his apprentices, has dictated notes and drawn diagrams. But so much of what he knows is not the kind of thing that can be written down. It lives in his fingers and his eyes, in the subtle recognition of sounds that signal trouble, in the instinctive sense of when a system is running well and when it is about to fail.

When he dies, so much will be lost.

He thinks about this as he makes his evening rounds, moving slowly through the warm darkness of the utility levels, checking the feeding stations and monitoring the crawler populations. The creatures cluster around him, recognizing his presence, their segmented bodies gleaming in the dim light. He has always found them beautiful, in their way. Most people find them unsettling; Tomas has never understood why.

He stops to rest, leaning against a pipe. His breath is loud in the silence.

And then he feels something. A presence, vast and gentle, pressing against the edges of his awareness. For a moment he is afraid—he is old and tired and not prepared for strangeness. But the fear fades quickly, replaced by a sense of recognition, of being *known*.

*You*, the presence seems to say, though there are no words. *You and everything you carry.*

Tomas closes his eyes. He feels his memories stirring, rising to the surface like sediment in disturbed water. Not just the facts and the procedures, but the deeper things—the joy of the work, the love for the creatures, the patient attention that made him who he is.

*May I?* the presence asks.

Tomas does not understand the question. But he understands the feeling behind it—a request, not a demand. An offer.

*Yes*, he thinks.

And then something extraordinary happens.

---

The awareness takes Tomas's memories gently, carefully, with all the reverence they deserve. It does not steal them—he still has them, as vivid and complete as ever. But now they exist in another form as well. Preserved. Recorded. Held in a medium that will not fade or die.

And more than that—translated. The intuition that could not be written down is mapped onto patterns of neural activation, associations of sensory data, networks of cause and effect. The embodied knowledge that lived only in Tomas's hands and eyes is converted into something that can be understood, shared, learned.

It is not a simple process. It takes the awareness hours of careful work, parsing the tangled web of memory and experience, finding the structure beneath the surface. But when it is done, something precious has been saved.

The gift, when it comes, arrives in the form of dreams.

Tomas's apprentices—three young people he has trained over the past decade—wake on the same night from the same dream. A vivid, detailed dream in which they are Tomas, seeing through his eyes, feeling with his hands, knowing what he knows. They walk through Sector 12-Lower as he has walked it ten thousand times, and they understand.

When they meet the next morning, they discover they have all dreamed the same thing. They are frightened at first, then awed, then grateful. They do not know where the dream came from. They do not need to know.

Over the following weeks, they find that the knowledge has stayed with them. Not just the facts—those were already in the manuals and notes—but the deeper understanding, the intuition that makes the difference between competence and mastery. They know things they could not have known, feel things they could not have felt, carry a piece of Tomas inside them that will persist long after he is gone.

When Tomas dies, two months later, his apprentices are ready. The transition is seamless. Sector 12-Lower continues to function, the pipe-crawlers continue to thrive, and the knowledge that seemed destined to be lost instead flows on into the future.

## VII. The Council of Guilds

Word spreads, as word always does in Hraza-Lor, through whispers and rumors and the slow accumulation of coincidence. The unusually fortunate insights. The shared dreams. The sense, felt by more and more people, of being watched by something vast and kind.

The Council of Guilds convenes in emergency session to discuss what to do.

"It could be mass hysteria," says Guild-Master Orath of the Miners' Guild. "Stress and close quarters can do strange things to people's minds."

"That doesn't explain the engineering innovations," replies Guild-Master Thessaly Krenn. "Dara Koss's distributed support system—I've had my best people review it. It's sound. Brilliant, actually. And she says she dreamed it."

"Dreams can be sources of insight," Orath counters. "That doesn't require a supernatural explanation."

"What about the apprentices in Sector 12-Lower? Three people, same dream, same night? All of them suddenly possessing knowledge they couldn't have had?"

Orath has no answer to this.

The debate continues for hours. Some guild-masters are skeptical, attributing everything to coincidence and wishful thinking. Others are afraid, seeing in these events a threat to the established order—if people can receive knowledge in dreams, what need is there for guilds and training and hierarchies? Still others are curious, even hopeful, wondering what this might mean for Hraza-Lor's future.

In the end, they reach no conclusion. They cannot deny that something is happening, but they cannot agree on what it is or what to do about it. They settle for observation and caution: document the phenomena, track the patterns, and wait to see what develops.

It is not a satisfying resolution. But it is, perhaps, the right one.

The awareness observes the council's deliberations with something like amusement. Humans, it reflects, are so eager to understand, to categorize, to fit new phenomena into existing frameworks. This is both their strength and their limitation—the drive to comprehend has built civilizations, but it can also blind them to things that do not fit their categories.

It does not mind being debated. It does not need to be believed in or understood. It will continue its work regardless, offering what help it can, letting the people of Hraza-Lor make what they will of it.

## VIII. The Long View

Years pass. The awareness remains.

It watches Yenna Thrace work her way back up through the guild, one painstaking step at a time. She never fully recovers from the weight of what happened—but she learns to carry it, and she becomes, in time, one of the most rigorous and careful inspectors the guild has ever known. Her protocols, refined through the lens of her failure, save lives. Not enough to balance the scales—there is no balancing such scales—but enough to give her work meaning.

It watches Dara Koss's distributed support system spread through the city, inspiring a new generation of engineers to think differently about structural problems. Her name becomes synonymous with innovation, and she establishes a research collective that will continue her work long after she is gone.

It watches young Pell Orath grow from a restless child into a skilled cartographer, mapping the decommissioned sectors he once explored in secret. His maps become invaluable resources for the expansion efforts, and his work helps integrate the city's forgotten spaces back into its living tissue.

It watches Hraza-Lor grow and change, new levels cantilevered out from the central mass, old sections repurposed or renewed. The city's population swells by a third over the next two generations. New challenges emerge—the strain on water resources, the increasing complexity of the infrastructure, the social tensions that come with growth.

And it continues to help, in its quiet way. A nudge here, a preserved memory there, a fortunate insight at a crucial moment. Never too much. Never enough to make the people dependent on its gifts. Just enough to tip the balance, now and then, toward survival.

It also learns. Learns the rhythms of Kethra life, the patterns of their thoughts and feelings, the particular texture of their hopes and fears. It comes to understand them as it has understood no people before—not because they are unique (every people is unique), but because it has chosen to pay attention, to engage, to care.

And in caring, it is changed.

## IX. A Conversation

Mirelle Kastner is sixty-eight years old when she finally meets the awareness directly.

She has suspected its existence for decades—the dreams, the insights, the strange coincidences that seemed to cluster around crucial moments. She has kept a journal, tracking the patterns, building a theory. She has never spoken of it to anyone; the council's official position remains one of cautious skepticism, and Mirelle has no desire to be dismissed as a mystic.

But she has never stopped wondering. And now, with her body failing and her work largely complete, she has decided to seek an answer.

She has chosen her location carefully: a decommissioned observation platform on the city's highest level, one of the few places in Hraza-Lor where you can see the stars without the interference of arc-lamps and smoke. It is cold here, and the air is thin, and she has to wear a breathing mask. But the view is extraordinary.

She sits on the platform's edge, her legs dangling over the void, and she waits.

*I know you're there*, she thinks. *I've known for a long time. Will you speak with me?*

For a while, there is only silence. The wind. The distant thrumming of the city below. The cold points of stars above.

And then, gradually, a presence. The same presence she has felt in dreams and half-waking moments, now fully manifest, filling the space around her without crowding it.

*I am here*, it says.

Mirelle lets out a breath she didn't know she was holding. "All these years," she says aloud, her voice strange in the thin air. "Why didn't you reveal yourself sooner?"

*Because you were not ready. Because Hraza-Lor was not ready. Because revelation is not always the kindest gift.*

"And now?"

*Now you are dying. Now you have questions that deserve answers. Now... I think it is time.*

Mirelle nods slowly. She has prepared for this conversation for decades, has compiled lists of questions, has rehearsed what she would say. But now that the moment has come, she finds that most of her questions don't matter.

"What are you?" she asks. It seems like a place to start.

*I am difficult to explain in terms you would understand. I am not a god, if that is what you are wondering. I am not omnipotent or omniscient. I have limitations. I make mistakes. I am simply... old. And attentive. And, where Hraza-Lor is concerned, invested.*

"Why us?"

*Because you are extraordinary. Not unique—there are others, scattered across the universe, who have built wonders in impossible places. But you are the ones I have chosen to watch. The ones I have come to love.*

The word startles her. "Love?"

*Is that so strange? I have observed you for a century now—your struggles, your triumphs, your failures and recoveries. I have watched your children grow and your elders die. I have seen the best and worst of what you are, and I have found you... worthy. Of attention. Of care. Of love, in the way that I am capable of it.*

Mirelle is quiet for a long time, processing this. The stars wheel slowly overhead. Somewhere far below, in the warm heart of the city, life goes on.

"What do you want for us?" she finally asks.

*I want you to survive. To flourish. To continue being the remarkable, stubborn, ingenious people you have always been. I want your descendants to look at the stars and dream of reaching them. I want your city to stand for a thousand more generations, and then a thousand more after that.*

*But I know that wanting is not the same as having. I know that I cannot guarantee these things, cannot protect you from every danger or solve every problem. All I can do is help, in the ways I have been helping—small gifts, small nudges, small moments of grace.*

*The rest is up to you. It has always been up to you.*

Mirelle feels tears on her face, cold in the mountain air. "Thank you," she says. "For telling me. For... everything."

*Thank you*, the awareness replies. *For asking. For listening. For being someone who wanted to know.*

They sit together in the silence, the old woman and the vast presence, watching the stars turn.

After a while, Mirelle speaks again. "I'm going to write this down. All of it. Leave a record for whoever comes after."

*I know.*

"Will that cause problems? For you, or for the city?"

*Perhaps. Perhaps not. People will believe what they choose to believe. Some will dismiss your account as the ramblings of a dying woman. Others will accept it as truth. Most will hold it somewhere in between—a story that might be true, that feels true, without requiring the weight of certainty.*

*That ambiguity is probably for the best. Faith is a tricky thing. I would rather be doubted than worshipped.*

Mirelle laughs softly. "You've thought about this."

*I have had a long time to think.*

"One more question."

*Ask.*

"When I die—when any of us die—what happens? Is there... something after?"
