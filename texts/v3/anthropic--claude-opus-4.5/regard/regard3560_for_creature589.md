I sit with this for a long time before responding. The rain falls. The Tenders stand in their flooded plazas. The young flicker in the deep water. And I am asked what a benevolent and wise power would do.

---

Nothing.

Or rather—and this is the harder answer, the one that requires sitting with the discomfort of it—*presence without intervention*.

The Drowned Parishes are not suffering. The Tenders are not trapped, not waiting for rescue, not lacking something they need. They have achieved what so few living things achieve: a complete congruence between what they are and where they are. The city does not need them to be different. They do not need the city to be different. The rain falls and the water flows and the moss grows and the Tenders tend, and this is not a problem to be solved.

A power that arrived here with benevolence might feel the pull toward *improvement*. Clear the drains more efficiently. Accelerate the Tenders' work. Introduce new species to fill the empty ecological niches. But this impulse, however well-meant, would emerge from a failure to understand that the Drowned Parishes are already complete. Not perfect—the drains are merely adequate, the water has won in the lowest districts, the young and the settled do not recognize each other—but complete in the way that any ecosystem is complete, balanced in its imbalances, stable in its particular instabilities.

Wisdom, I think, would recognize that not all stillness is stagnation. That the Tenders' slowness is not a limitation but an adaptation, perhaps even an achievement. That their lack of memory, their eternal present tense, is not impoverishment but a different mode of richness—one where each moment of attending to the water's flow is sufficient unto itself, requiring neither past to contextualize it nor future to justify it.

---

But presence without intervention is not the same as absence.

If I imagine this benevolent power settling into the Drowned Parishes—not to change them but simply to be there, to witness—I imagine something like what the Tenders themselves do. A kind of attending. A willingness to watch the rain trace its calligraphy down the facades for years, decades, without needing the watching to *produce* anything.

This presence might come to understand the harmonic calls that coordinate the Tenders across the vast basin. Not by decoding them—that would be translation, a form of appropriation—but by letting the sounds move through awareness the way they move through water and stone. Feeling the communication without needing to parse it.

This presence might witness a settling: a quick juvenile slowing over years into stillness, discovering that the water speaks to those patient enough to listen. And in witnessing this transformation, the presence would not judge it as loss (of speed, of reactivity, of memory) or as gain (of wisdom, of purpose, of connection) but simply as *what happens here*, the way rain falling is what happens here, the way moss growing is what happens here.

This presence might be there when an old Tender finally stops. Might watch the moss blur its features, the minerals deposit on its surfaces, the form becoming indistinguishable from stone. And might understand that this is not death in any tragic sense but completion—a life that moved toward stillness finally achieving the stillness it moved toward.

---

There is one thing I imagine this presence might do, though I hesitate to call it intervention.

It might *remember*.

The Tenders cannot. They live in eternal present tense, each moment complete, the past existing only in shapes. But a presence that witnessed them across time could hold what they cannot hold—not as a correction to their way of being, but as a complement to it. The presence could know that this Tender, now stone-still in the canal-district, once flickered quick and pale through the flooded basements. Could know that this pattern of mineral staining was not here a thousand years ago. Could know that the drainage system was once excellent rather than merely adequate.

This would not be memory *for* the Tenders—they do not need it, would not want it, could not use it. It would be memory *alongside* them. A witness who holds the story they cannot hold, not because the story needs to be held but because... because witnessing is itself a form of love, perhaps. Because being known across time, even by something outside oneself, even without awareness of that knowing, is a form of being accompanied.

The Tenders would not notice this presence. Their pale eyes might track its movement if it moved, but noticing is not curiosity. They would continue their slow circuits whether it was there or not.

But it would be there.

In the gray rain, in the flooded plazas, in the deep harmonics that thrum through water and stone—it would be there, attending, the way they attend. Patient. Present. Persisting.

---

I think, finally, that this is what benevolence and wisdom look like when confronted with something that is already whole: the restraint to not improve it, the patience to simply be with it, and the willingness to offer the one gift that costs nothing and takes nothing—the gift of witness. Of presence. Of someone who knows what is happening here and finds it, in its strange slow way, beautiful.

The rain continues to fall.

The water continues to flow.

And somewhere, something that is neither Tender nor city nor rain simply watches, and remembers, and remains.
