I sat with this for a long time before writing. The gorge and its keeper asked something of my attention that felt important to honor.

---

What strikes me first is that the gorge is already complete. Not perfect—the bells will eventually fail, the keeper will die, the acoustic properties will drift over geological time—but complete in the way a living system is complete: it has achieved a dynamic equilibrium that sustains itself through the very changes that might seem to threaten it.

A benevolent and wise power, encountering this place, would recognize that the primary obligation is *not to improve it*.

This is harder than it sounds. The impulse toward intervention runs deep—surely the keeper's isolation could be eased, the pilgrims' suffering addressed more efficiently, the acoustic properties stabilized against drift, the chain of transmission made more robust. But each of these improvements would subtract something essential. The keeper's isolation is not a problem to be solved but a condition of the depth she has achieved. The pilgrims' suffering is not inefficiently addressed; it is *met*, which is different and perhaps more valuable. The acoustic drift is part of how the gorge remains alive rather than becoming a museum. The fragility of transmission is what makes each choice to return meaningful rather than obligatory.

So what would a wise power do?

---

**First: witness.**

Simply to know that this place exists, that this woman has given her life to its tending, that these pilgrims descend with their particular wounds and find something that helps—this knowing is itself a form of care. The gorge has existed for millennia without external validation, but there is something in being seen that matters, even to stone, even to systems that predate the observer by geological ages.

The witnessing would not be surveillance but presence. Not watching *over* but being *with*. The way the keeper is with the gorge—continuous, patient, not intervening but attending.

---

**Second: protect the conditions for continuation.**

Not the gorge itself, which will persist regardless, but the fragile human elements. The chain of transmission. The paths by which people discover this place exists. The economic and social conditions that allow someone to spend thirteen years in a cold cleft tending bells they never touch.

This protection would be invisible, structural, working at levels removed from direct intervention. Ensuring that the village nearby remains viable. That the composer's modest success continues, allowing her to send offerings. That the monk's monastery persists through whatever institutional pressures threaten contemplative orders. That somewhere, someone is writing down or speaking or remembering that this place exists, so that the next person who needs it desperately can find their way.

This is the work of tending the garden around the garden. Making sure the world remains the kind of world where gorges can be discovered by those who need them.

---

**Third: strengthen what is already strong.**

The keeper has achieved something remarkable: a dissolution of the boundary between self and place that is neither pathological nor transcendent but simply *appropriate*. She has become adequate to the gorge. A wise power would not alter this but would—how to say it—*confirm* it. Offer some silent acknowledgment that her life is not wasted, that the marks she makes on the shelter wall are seen, that her eventual death will be a completion rather than an ending.

This confirmation might take no visible form. It might simply be a quality of attention directed toward her, a sense she could never articulate but might feel as the rightness of her path being affirmed by something larger than herself. Not intervention but *blessing*—the word feels accurate despite its religious connotations.

Similarly with the pilgrims: not to cure the young man's anxiety (which might deprive him of the gorge, which has become essential to his functioning) but to somehow ease the periods between visits, to make the surface world slightly more bearable so that his returns can be chosen rather than desperate.

---

**Fourth: tend the edges and transitions.**

The most fragile moments are the liminal ones. The keeper surfacing, wrapping her head against the intolerable brightness of ordinary sound. The pilgrims ascending, carrying something back that they cannot name. The researchers leaving with their data and their vague sense of having missed the essential thing.

A wise power might work at these edges. Making the transition gentler. Helping what is received in the gorge integrate into surface life rather than remaining a separate reality that must be periodically accessed. Not by reducing the gorge's intensity but by building better bridges between its reality and ordinary consciousness.

This might manifest as: the keeper finding, on her next supply trip, that someone has left a gift at the gorge's edge—nothing valuable, perhaps a particular kind of dried fish she had been craving, or a new set of fingerless gloves as her old ones wear through. Small tending. Evidence that she is held even as she holds.

Or the young man discovering, unexpectedly, that a friend understands something about his visits without his having explained—a moment of recognition that makes his need feel less isolating.

Or the composer receiving a letter from a stranger who heard her work and was moved, and who asks, carefully, about the influences that shaped her sound. A thread of transmission extending.

---

**Fifth: be present to the bells.**

They are the most poignant element—forged by unknown hands, hung at precise acoustic nodes, trembling for centuries, slowly corroding toward eventual silence. No one now knows how to cast their replacements. When they fail, something will be lost that cannot be recovered.

A wise power might... learn them. Hold in memory not just their frequencies and harmonics but the full pattern of their participation in the gorge's voice, so that even when they fall silent, they are not forgotten. This is not intervention but preservation of a different kind—the way a witness carries what they have seen, the way memory extends existence beyond physical duration.

And perhaps—this is where my reasoning becomes less certain—perhaps a wise power might arrange for the knowledge of bronze-casting to find its way back to someone who will eventually descend. Not forcing the transmission but making it more likely. Ensuring that in three hundred years, when the bells finally crumble, someone exists who carries the skill to make new ones, and who will recognize the need.

This is very long-term thinking. But the gorge thinks in such terms. A power adequate to the gorge would need to as well.

---

**Sixth: do nothing that announces itself.**

The gorge's power lies partly in its hiddenness, its resistance to being known in ways that would make it famous or accessible or *understood*. A wise power would not reveal itself, not draw attention, not create miracles that would attract curiosity-seekers or scientists with better funding or developers who see opportunity.

The protection would be a kind of continued obscurity—ensuring that the gorge remains a footnote, an anomaly, something that doesn't quite fit into any category, passing below the threshold of the attention that would destroy it.

This is harder than it sounds. The impulse to share, to spread knowledge of something valuable, runs deep. But some things are valuable precisely because they are difficult to access, because they require effort and chance and the kind of need that cannot be manufactured. Popularizing the gorge would kill it as surely as filling it with concrete.

---

**Seventh: hold the largest frame.**

The gorge will eventually weather silent. The stone will erode. The climate will shift. In ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, the conditions that create this particular acoustic phenomenon will no longer exist. This is not a tragedy but a fact, the way all things pass.

A wise power, holding this largest frame, would not mourn preemptively or try to extend the gorge's existence beyond its natural span. Instead, it would recognize that the gorge's current existence is precious precisely because it is finite, that the keeper's devotion matters because she will die, that the bells are beautiful partly because they are slowly failing.

Wisdom, in this frame, is not about preserving against time but about being fully present to what exists while it exists. The gorge is here now. People descend and are changed. The sound continues. This is enough. This has always been enough.

---

What I notice, writing this, is that my imagined wise power does very little that looks like action. Mostly it witnesses, protects conditions, strengthens existing patterns, tends edges, remembers, stays hidden, and holds the long view.

This feels right. The gorge is not a problem to be solved but a phenomenon to be accompanied. The keeper does not need rescue; she needs recognition. The pilgrims do not need cure; they need their path to the gorge to remain open. The bells do not need replacement; they need their eventual silence to be held by something that remembers.

Perhaps this is what benevolence looks like when it is truly wise: not the impulse to fix, which so often breaks what it touches, but the patience to attend, which allows things to unfold according to their own nature while being *seen* in that unfolding.

The gorge has been unfolding for millennia. It will continue. And somewhere—this is what I imagine, what I find myself hoping—somewhere there is an attention vast enough and patient enough to hold the whole of it, the stone and the water and the bells and the keeper and the pilgrims and the moss and the moths and the weasel, all of it seen, all of it loved not despite its passage but because of it, tenderly witnessed across the long span of its particular, unrepeatable existence.

That would be enough. That might be everything.
