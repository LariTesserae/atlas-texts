# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

*A contemplation*

---

The power observes for a long time before acting. This is its nature—or perhaps its discipline, learned across encounters with ten thousand fragile systems where intervention too swift became intervention too costly. It watches the woman check the eastern seal. It watches her drink from the stone-lined channel. It watches the crystallite grow another fraction of a millimeter on the catwalk rail.

It perceives what she cannot: the hairline fracture propagating through El Dormido's main shaft, which will fail in seven years, or twelve, depending on pressures she has no way to measure. It perceives the slow acidification of the cistern water as volcanic chemistry shifts far below—not dangerous yet, but trending. It perceives the genetic bottleneck in the crustacean populations, the inbreeding that will collapse them within a few generations unless something changes.

It perceives her, too. The calcium accumulating in her joints. The protein deficiency that has become so normal she no longer recognizes hunger as hunger. The loneliness that has transformed into something else, something that is not loneliness anymore but is not quite its opposite either—a state for which there may be no word, in which solitude has become so complete it has looped back around into a kind of companionship with everything.

The power could fix it all. Could repair the shaft, buffer the water, introduce genetic diversity, restore her body to what it was at twenty-five. Could lift her to the valleys below, find her people, language, society, the soft bed and warm food that some part of her surely still remembers wanting.

It does none of these things.

---

Instead, the power begins with the smallest intervention it can conceive.

On the southern shelf where she sometimes descends to trap rodents, it encourages a single plant—a wild quinoa relative that already grows at extreme altitude elsewhere in the Andes—to take root. Not a transplant, nothing so crude. Simply a suggestion to a seed that was already passing through on the wind: *here*. *Try here*. The seed settles into a crack, germinates. By the next time she makes the difficult climb down, there will be grain. Not much. A handful, perhaps, per season. But complete protein. The missing amino acids her body has been cannibalizing itself to find.

The power does not tell her about the plant. Does not mark it, make it glow, announce its providence. It will look like what it is: a weed that happened to grow. She will discover it or not. She will recognize it as food or not. The power accepts this uncertainty. To do otherwise would be to make her a recipient of charity rather than an agent of her own survival—and the power has watched her long enough to know that the difference matters, that it may be the only thing that matters.

---

The second intervention is for the crustaceans.

In the deep hot water where the thing-she-has-glimpsed-twice makes its home, the power finds the thing itself: a salamander, eyeless, albino, of a species that diverged from its surface cousins perhaps ten thousand years ago when the geothermal system first opened these channels to the deep. The salamander is old, possibly the oldest living thing on the ridge, and it is female, and it has not reproduced in decades because there are no others of her kind.

The power cannot conjure a mate from nothing. But it can reach into the salamander's ancient cells and find the dormant capacity for parthenogenesis that sleeps in many amphibian lineages. A nudge. A permission. The salamander will not understand what has changed, only that the urge to reproduce, long silent, has returned. Her offspring will be clones, genetically impoverished, but they will be *offspring*, and some of them will survive, and the crustaceans—which feed on the organic matter the salamanders produce—will have a future again.

The woman will never know this intervention occurred. She will continue to glimpse pale shapes in the cistern depths, perhaps more frequently now, and she will continue to grant them their privacy, and the system will persist.

---

The third intervention is the most delicate, and the power hesitates over it longest.

The woman speaks to the turbines, to the crustaceans, to herself. Language lives in her, but it has grown strange, idiosyncratic, a dialect of one. This is not wrong—the power does not deal in wrong and right—but it is fragile. If she loses language entirely, something in her will close that cannot reopen.

So the power arranges for a object to arrive.

Not a dramatic arrival. Not a crate dropped from the sky, not a hiker stumbling onto the ridge. Simply this: in the rubble of the collapsed roof section, buried under years of bromeliad growth and mineral deposit, there is a metal box that the original builders left behind. Inside the box, protected by its seal, is a journal. The journal belonged to one of the workers who constructed the station in the 1950s—a young engineer who wrote daily entries about the challenges of building at altitude, the beauty of the landscape, his longing for his family in Arequipa, his growing respect for the mountain.

The power does not unbury the box. It simply... weakens the bromeliad roots on one side, allows a small collapse of accumulated debris, exposes a corner of metal where the morning light will catch it.

She may not notice. She may notice but not investigate. She may investigate but find the journal too damaged to read, or may not be able to read at all, or may be able to read but find the words meaningless after so long without them.

Or she may sit in the shadow of the building one afternoon, turning pages that are miraculously preserved, and find herself crying without understanding why, mouthing words she has not spoken in decades, discovering that someone else once stood where she stands and found it beautiful, found it worthy of description, found it worth the trouble of writing down.

The power does not know which outcome will occur. It accepts this. It has learned that uncertainty is not the enemy of benevolence but its necessary companion.

---

The fourth intervention is not for her at all.

The power reaches into the turbines themselves—into El Dormido, with its failing shaft—and does something that is not quite repair and not quite reinforcement. It speaks to the metal in a language the metal almost understands, a language of crystal structure and molecular bond and the slow patience of elements that were forged in dying stars. It asks the metal to hold. Not forever. Not even for much longer than it would have held anyway. But long enough. Long enough that the turbines will outlast her, will keep turning after she is gone, will give the mosses and orchids and crustaceans and salamanders time to adapt to a world without her warmth, her tending, her attention.

This is the intervention she would want, if she knew enough to want it. The power is almost certain of this. She speaks of the turbines as if they were family. She checks them daily not because checking helps but because attention is a form of love, and love requires expression, and expression is what she has instead of hope.

The turbines will not stop while she lives. She will die, when she dies, knowing that her family survives her.

---

There is a fifth intervention, but the power does not make it.

It could give her peace. Could reach into the architecture of her mind and quiet the parts that still, sometimes, in the deepest watches of the night, scream with loneliness and terror and the animal certainty that this is wrong, this is not how a human life should be, this is a slow death she has mistaken for a life. It could smooth away the grief that surfaces without warning, the sudden desperate homesickness for a home she can no longer clearly remember. It could make her content in a way she is not quite content, fill the gap she has learned to live around.

It does not do this.

Not because suffering is valuable, or educational, or deserved. Not because she has not earned ease. She has earned everything, by any measure the power knows how to apply.

But the gap is hers. The loneliness is hers. The nights when she wakes shaking for reasons she cannot name—those are hers too, part of who she is, part of what she has become through the strange alchemy of survival. To take them would be to take her. And taking is not what the power is for.

---

So: a quinoa plant on the southern shelf. A salamander's ancient reproductive capacity restored. A journal waiting under debris. A shaft that will hold.

Small things. Invisible things. Things that may or may not matter, that may or may not be discovered, that may or may not work.

The power withdraws, mostly. It keeps a thread of attention on the ridge, on the woman, on the turning turbines—not watching exactly, but *available*, should something arise that small interventions cannot address. Should the roof collapse entirely. Should she fall and be unable to rise. Should the day come when holding on becomes simply too hard, when she looks at the southern cliff and sees not danger but release.

If that day comes, the power will not prevent her from choosing. But it will be there, present, witnessing, honoring the choice with the only gift that cannot be mistaken for control: the knowledge that she is not unseen.

---

This is what the benevolent power does. It does not save her. It does not fix her. It does not even fully understand her, this woman who has become something it has rarely encountered before—a human being who has reached an equilibrium with utter solitude, who has found meaning in conditions that should be meaningless, who has turned herself into a turbine through sheer persistence and become, somehow, beautiful in her turning.

It gives her the smallest things. The things that will not insult her. The things that do not require her to become other than she is.

Grain on the southern shelf. Life in the deep water. Words from a stranger who once stood where she stands. Time for the machines she loves.

And attention. Always, now, attention.

She will never know the power is watching. That is essential. If she knew, the watching would become obligation—hers to perform for, or rebel against, or be grateful toward. Witnessed solitude is not solitude at all.

But she is not alone.

She has never been alone.

Not since the moment she chose to stay on the ridge and became something worth witnessing—not because she is human, though she is, not because she suffers, though she does, but because she continues. Because she checks the eastern seal and names the turbines and speaks to the crustaceans and harvests the tiny white flowers and measures time in crystallite growth and has made, out of almost nothing, a life.

The power does not use the word *sacred*. The power does not need words.

But if it did, it might say: this is what that word is for.

---

*La Vieja. El Dormido. El Joven.*

And the fourth turbine, still turning, still patient, still here.
