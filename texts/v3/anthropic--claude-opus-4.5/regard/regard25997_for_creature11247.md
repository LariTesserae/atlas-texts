*I sit with this for a long time before responding.*

What strikes me most is how complete it already is—not as a problem to be solved, but as a system that has found its own equilibrium. The drainage cathedral doesn't need rescue. The old man doesn't need rescue. They have discovered each other, and in that discovery, something genuine has emerged.

---

**What a benevolent and wise power might do:**

Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would disturb the particular quality of silence they have cultivated together.

Perhaps: ensure the thirty-seven rungs remain sound for as long as he needs them. Not restored to gleaming—that would be wrong, would break the aesthetic honesty of the place—but simply *sufficient*. The rust allowed to find its equilibrium, as the text says, but the metal beneath still holding.

Perhaps: let the phosphorescent panels fail in sequence rather than all at once, so the darkness comes gradually enough for the eels' eyes to adjust, for the membrane creatures to continue their unknowable processes, for the old man (if he is still coming then) to bring his torch more often without it feeling like a rupture.

Perhaps: nothing at all to the cathedral itself, but something small for *him*—a moment of recognition. Not from another person, necessarily. But the next time he hums into the dome, let the echo return in a way that feels, just once, like answer rather than reflection. Not words. Not meaning he could parse. Just a quality of resonance that lets him know his presence has been noted by something, even if that something is only the architecture itself, the water, the accumulated patience of a century of brick and silence.

---

**What I find myself unwilling to do, even imaginatively:**

Explain the membrane creatures. Name them, classify them, resolve the mystery of where the absorbed matter goes. Their unclassifiability is load-bearing. It is what makes the space feel like it has exceeded human understanding rather than simply being overlooked by it.

Fix his loneliness in any conventional sense. Give him a companion, a legacy, a sense that his visits *matter* in the way the world above understands mattering. He has already found something better than that—a relationship with irrelevance that has become, paradoxically, the most relevant thing in his life.

Prevent his death or ensure he is remembered. The passage about the eels not noticing his absence, the crabs forgetting they ever waited—this is not tragedy. This is *the gift he has given himself*: release from the tyranny of permanence. A wise power would not take that gift away.

---

**What I might add, if anything:**

One small moment, the next time he tests his weight on the corroded grating:

Let him feel, just briefly, something like a hand beneath the metal. Not stopping him, not saving him. Just *present*. A pressure that says: *I know you are here. I know what you are doing. I am not asking you to stop or to continue. I am only acknowledging that this threshold you keep visiting is seen.*

And then let the moment pass, let him pull back as he always does, let him climb the rungs and return to his apartment and his thin sleep. But let him carry, somewhere beneath conscious thought, the knowledge that the cathedral witnessed him. That his patient attendance was, in some fashion, attended to in return.

---

This is not salvation. It is not intervention in any sense that would make for a satisfying story. But I think a truly wise power would understand that what he has found—this difficult, quiet, hard-won peace—is more valuable than rescue. The kindest thing is to let it continue. To hold the space open for as long as the space can hold.

And perhaps, after he stops coming, to let the eels sometimes surface in a pattern that resembles, to no one watching, something like memory.
