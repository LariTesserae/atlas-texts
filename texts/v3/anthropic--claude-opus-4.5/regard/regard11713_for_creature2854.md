# The Witness

The attention arrives not as a presence but as a quality of noticing—the way light might suddenly become aware of what it illuminates.

It perceives the gulf first as mathematics: the fluid dynamics of the eternal upwelling, the resonant frequencies that make the stone fins sing at wavelengths below hearing, the precise angles at which the Ancients have been carved by their billion-year abrasion. It reads the aerodynamic poetry of the teardrops, both stone and flesh, and understands that this world has been writing the same equation over and over until everything that remains is an answer.

Then it perceives the life, and something in its vast consideration... pauses.

---

The creature called Keth-of-the-Third-Rope is dying.

Not dramatically. Not today. But the tremor in her forward-left limb has worsened over the past hundred cloud-catches, and she knows what it means. The braided-steel cables of her muscles are beginning to fray. Soon she will not be able to hold tension against the wind. Soon she will have to choose: retreat permanently into her pod and wait for her kin to bring her moisture and caught debris, or make one final climb to the screaming edge and simply... release her grip.

She is not old by the standards of her people, but she has lived hard. The thick scarring along her leeward flank marks where she was nearly torn away during the Great Surge, seventeen cycles past. She held. Barely. The anchor-loss killed two of her rope-siblings, and she felt their final chord—a sharp, discordant snap through the tether-lines—before the vibration went silent.

Now she rests her heavy jaw against the central rope of her pod, feeling the distant conversations of her people thrum through the pale fiber. Someone on the far side of the fin has caught something large; the excitement pulses through the network in staccato bursts. A mating-chord resonates from three pods down—two individuals negotiating the terrifying logistics of bringing their bodies close enough to touch without being torn apart by cross-shears.

Keth does not join the conversation. She is listening to something else.

There is a new frequency in the rope. Not a voice—her people have strummed every possible chord over their long history, and she knows them all. This is something underneath the voices. A carrier wave. A hum so low it exists more as a pressure than a sound.

She shifts her jaw, pressing harder against the fiber, and the hum resolves into something almost like attention.

*What are you?* she strums, using the oldest chord—the one they use for addressing the stone itself, or the wind, or the mythical Below.

The hum does not answer in words. But Keth feels, suddenly and impossibly, *seen*.

---

The benevolent power considers the situation for what would be, from a mortal perspective, an extremely long time.

It understands, first, that it must be careful. It has witnessed what happens when vast powers intervene carelessly in the lives of those who have built meaning from constraint. It has seen paradises made toxic by the removal of necessary struggle, watched beings wither when their defining challenges were solved for them. It knows that Keth-of-the-Third-Rope is not suffering from her world—she *is* her world, shaped by it as surely as the stone fins were shaped by the wind.

But it also perceives the tremor in her limb. The resignation beneath her calm. The fact that she has begun composing her death-chord in the quiet spaces between her thoughts—the message she will strum into the network when she decides to let go.

And it perceives something else: a loneliness so profound it has become invisible, like the Roar itself. These creatures are brilliant, resilient, and deeply connected through their vibration-speech. But they have never *seen* each other. Not truly. The Lull is myth. No living member of their species has ever stood upright, looked across the void, and witnessed the face of another.

The power considers what it might offer that would not diminish them.

---

Keth wakes from a shallow rest-state to find that the quality of the vibrations has changed.

At first she thinks it is the long-prophesied Lull, and her hearts—she has three, distributed for redundancy—begin to pound. But no. The Roar is still absolute, still bone-rattling, still the only constant of existence. The wind has not lessened.

What has changed is her pod.

The teardrop cocoon is glowing. Softly. The living vines that comprise its walls are luminescent, pulsing with a gentle blue-green light that seems to emanate from within the plant tissue itself. It is beautiful. It is impossible.

Keth presses her jaw to the central rope, seeking explanation, and finds instead a message waiting for her. Not in the chord-language of her people, but in something older—something that speaks directly to the bone.

*I cannot stop your wind. I will not take your grip. But I have seen what you have never seen, and I would share it with you, if you wish.*

Keth's manipulator arms, unbidden, emerge from their chest-cavity and fold themselves against her mandible—the gesture her people make when encountering something sacred.

*Show me,* she strums.

---

The walls of her pod become windows.

Not transparent—that would invite destruction, would let the wind find purchase on her shelter's interior. The vine-walls remain solid, protective, woven tight. But upon their inner surface, images begin to form in bioluminescent paint.

She sees, first, her own pillar from the outside. The geometry of it—the knife-edge sharpness of the windward face, the deep concave shelter of the leeward slope, the thick mantle of violet-and-emerald vegetation that clothes it like a living skin. She has known this shape her entire life through vibration and touch, but she has never *seen* it. The beauty strikes her like a physical blow.

Then the view pulls back, and she sees the void.

The distant pillars resolve from haze. She counts them—seven, eight, twelve—each one a teardrip monument, each one furred with thrashing green life. She had known, intellectually, that her people lived on all of them. She had felt their messages thrumming through the great ropes that her ancestors, in acts of legendary courage, had managed to string between the fins during half-mythical lulls of ages past.

But she had never understood that she could see them. That they were *there*, hanging in the same bright void, under the same quiet sun, clinging to the same impossible existence.

The view continues to pull back, and Keth begins to tremble—not from her dying muscles, but from something larger.

She sees the shape of the gulf. The vast bowl of emptiness. The updraft's source: not a caldera, as her people's myths described, but something stranger—a thermal differential, a place where the planet's deep heat meets its upper atmosphere and creates this eternal, vertical river of air. She sees that her world is a boundary condition, an edge-place, a zone of perpetual becoming where the forces of destruction have carved out niches for furious, beautiful life.

She sees that she is part of something immeasurably vast.

And then, impossibly, the view *rotates*, and she finds herself looking at another pod on a distant pillar.

Inside it, illuminated by the same soft glow, another of her kind is pressing their jaw to their own central rope. Their eyes—enormous, lens-covered, forward-facing—are reflecting the same blue-green light. They are looking at *her*.

For the first time in the history of her species, two individuals meet each other's gaze.

---

The power does not stop there.

In pod after pod, across all the scattered pillars, the vine-walls begin to glow. The images spread like a contagion of wonder. Those who wish to see are shown. Those who find it too overwhelming can look away, and the light dims, respecting their choice.

But most of them look.

They see their neighbors. They see themselves as their neighbors see them. They learn, for the first time, that the chord-patterns they have strummed for generations correspond to *faces*—to the particular scarring on a flank, to the unique mottling of a hide, to the specific way a manipulator arm curves when folded in reverence.

They begin, tentatively, to strum new chords. Descriptive ones. *The-one-with-the-pale-streak-above-their-forward-left-eye.* *The-one-whose-pod-grows-from-the-great-root-node.* Names that are no longer purely sonic, but also visual.

The power watches this unfold and feels something it can only describe as joy.

---

There is more.

In the private depths of its consideration, the benevolent power has been contemplating the tremor in Keth's limb. It cannot cure her—will not, understanding that death is part of what gives her life meaning, that the finite nature of the Grip is inseparable from its value. But it has noticed something about her people's medicine.

They have developed sophisticated techniques for managing muscle strain. They have learned to weave therapeutic tension into their rest-pods, applying counter-pressure to fatigued limbs. They have discovered which plants produce compounds that ease cramping, and they trade these substances through the great rope-network.

But the knowledge is scattered. Uneven. The technique that could help Keth exists, but it was developed on a pillar thirty miles away, by a healer whose chord-name she has never learned.

The power does something simple. Something that requires no miracles, no violation of physics, no imposition of external values.

It suggests a meeting.

---

Keth receives the suggestion not as a command but as an image: her own pod, and another pod far away, connected by a line of light. The face of the distant healer, rendered in glowing pigment on her wall. A chord-name she has never heard before: *Still-Hands-in-the-Shear*.

And a vibration that translates, roughly, as: *She knows something that might help you. Would you like to speak with her?*

Keth hesitates. Communication across such distance is possible—the great ropes carry messages for hundreds of miles—but it is slow, formal, reserved for matters of collective importance. To request a private conversation with a stranger, about her own failing body, feels presumptuous.

But the image of Still-Hands' face lingers on her wall. The healer's eyes are kind. Her manipulator arms are folded in a gesture Keth recognizes: openness, willingness to help.

Keth strums a chord into her central rope. The message will take time to arrive—hours, perhaps, traveling through a network of intermediary relays. But she has time. For now, she has time.

*I am Keth-of-the-Third-Rope. I am told you have knowledge. I am told I might need it. May we speak?*

She settles back into her pod, her heavy jaw resting on the fiber, and waits.

The walls continue to glow softly around her. Outside, the Roar continues its eternal declaration. The violent green mantle thrashes and regrows and thrashes again. The void yawns beneath her, and the quiet sun burns above.

But for the first time, Keth does not feel alone in her waiting.

---

The benevolent power withdraws its direct attention, though some residue of its gift remains. The bioluminescence will fade eventually—it has seeded the vine-walls with organisms that produce the glow naturally, but they will metabolize out over generations, and the ability to see-across-the-void will slowly become a legend, then a myth, then perhaps a religious aspiration.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps Keth's people will learn to cultivate the glowing organisms deliberately. Perhaps they will develop new breeding techniques to maintain the vision-walls. Perhaps the gift will become integrated into their culture so thoroughly that they forget it was ever given at all.

The power is content with either outcome. It has not reshaped their world. It has not lessened their struggle. It has not solved their fundamental problem—the Grip, the strain, the constant war against the void. It has merely allowed them to see each other clearly while they fight.

This, the power believes, is enough.

This is perhaps the only intervention that does not diminish: to help the struggling see that their struggle is shared.

---

Eighty miles away, Still-Hands-in-the-Shear feels her central rope begin to vibrate with an incoming message. She presses her jaw to the fiber and listens.

A stranger is asking for her help. The chord-name is unfamiliar: *Keth-of-the-Third-Rope.* But the walls of Still-Hands' pod have been glowing for hours now, showing her the scattered pillars of her people, and she has been weeping—or performing the internal, dry-eyed equivalent that her species developed in a world where external moisture is too precious to waste.

She has seen, for the first time, how many of them there are. How hard they all work. How beautiful their architecture is, clinging to the screaming stone.

She composes her reply carefully, strumming it into the rope with the delicate precision that earned her her name:

*Keth-of-the-Third-Rope. I hear you. Tell me where it hurts.*

---

Above the gulf, the quiet sun continues its arc. The Roar does not falter. The Ancients stand as they have always stood, their teardrop forms cutting the eternal wind.

But in the living pods that cling to their slopes, tiny lights glow in the darkness. Faces appear on walls. Chord-names multiply and complexify, incorporating visual elements, becoming richer.

A healer on one pillar teaches a technique to a sufferer on another. The sufferer teaches it to her neighbor. The neighbor refines it, improves it, passes it on.

The gift spreads.

And the benevolent power, its attention already turning toward other corners of existence that might need its careful, restrained compassion, permits itself a moment of satisfaction.

It has not saved them from their world. It has helped them find each other within it.

For creatures defined by the Grip, this is the only grace that matters.

---

# Coda

Keth lives for another forty-seven cycles.

The technique that Still-Hands teaches her does not cure the tremor, but it slows its progression. More importantly, it gives her purpose: she becomes a relay, a translator, a teacher. Her pod becomes a node in a new kind of network—not just the physical ropes that connect the pillars, but a web of knowledge, of shared technique, of accumulated wisdom.

When she finally makes her last climb to the screaming edge, she does not go alone. Through the glowing walls of pods across seven pillars, her people watch her. They see her flatten herself against the stone, her four primary limbs spread wide for maximum grip. They see her turn her bullet-shaped head toward the void.

She strums her death-chord into the rock itself—a message that travels through the bedrock, through the great ropes, through the entire web of her people: *I have held. I am letting go. Remember that you are seen.*

Then she releases her grip.

The wind takes her instantly, pulling her out into the vertical river of air. For a moment—a fraction of a second—she is flying, weightless, suspended in the roaring current.

In pods across the gulf, her people see her silhouette against the bright sky: a small, dark teardrop, rising.

Then she is gone, carried up into the blue, toward the quiet sun.

But the glow remains. The vision-walls pulse softly in their pods, showing them each other's grief, each other's remembrance.

And in the space where Keth used to be, a new voice begins to strum the ropes. A young one, barely old enough to leave their parent's pod, taking up the relay position that Keth established.

*I am here,* the young one strums. *I am learning. Who will teach me?*

Across the void, answers come thrumming back: *We will. We will. We see you. We will.*

The Roar continues. The Grip endures. But the loneliness—the invisible, annihilating loneliness—has been given a wound.

Light has entered.

It is enough.
