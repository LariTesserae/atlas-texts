# The Watching

The power that turned its attention to the monastery did not arrive. It had never been absent. It was simply that, for a span of time that might have been a moment or an age, its awareness *rested* there—the way sunlight rests on stone, warming without grasping.

What it perceived:

The chief astronomer, whose name had not been spoken aloud in thirty-seven years, stood at the great orrery in the pre-dawn darkness. His fingers, stiff with cold and age, adjusted a brass armature with trembling precision. He was tracking something—a perturbation in the movements of a distant wandering star that his predecessors had noted eleven generations past. Tonight, if his calculations held, he would confirm their hypothesis or overturn it.

He did not know that he would die before the next winter. His apprentice, a young monk with a talent for mathematics but a troubling tendency toward impatience, did not know this either.

The power watched the astronomer's breath mist in the cold air. It watched his certainty and his doubt braided together like rope. It watched the way his consciousness reached outward through the instrument, through the glass and brass, toward light that had traveled longer than the monastery had stood.

---

In a cramped cell three levels below, a monk who had joined the order forty days prior sat rigid on a stone bench, unable to sleep. She had come to the mountain seeking escape from a grief so vast she could not name it—a village burned, a family scattered or dead, a life unmade by forces as indifferent as weather. She had thought that here, in this place of discipline and purpose, she might outrun what pursued her.

Instead, the silence had given it room to expand.

She sat with her hands pressed flat against the cold stone, breathing in the rhythm she had been taught, and behind her closed eyes the flames rose again and again. She was failing. She knew she was failing. The other monks moved through their days like water over smooth stones, and she was all jagged edges, all caught and snagging.

The power watched her. It did not reach into her mind or soothe her pain. It simply *attended*—the way one might sit with a friend through a long night, offering no solutions, only presence.

---

The abbot, in his quarters near the monastery's heart, was not sleeping either. He rarely slept anymore. At his age, sleep had become a shallow thing, easily startled away by a shifting wind or an ache in the joints.

He was thinking about the decision he had made thirty years ago, when a different young monk—wild-eyed and brilliant and dangerous—had come to him with a theory. A way to use the monastery's instruments not merely to observe but to *reach*. To send something outward, or draw something in.

The abbot had forbidden it. He had confined the monk to copying texts for a decade, until the fire in him had banked to embers. The monk had eventually become the master archivist, all that intensity channeled into preservation rather than discovery.

Had that been wisdom or cowardice? The abbot no longer knew. He had protected the order from a risk he could not fully articulate. He had also, perhaps, protected the cosmos from the order. Or robbed both of something transcendent. The uncertainty had become a companion, familiar as his own heartbeat.

The power watched him hold this question without resolving it. There was something it recognized in this—a quality of attention that did not demand answers.

---

So: what would a benevolent and wise power do?

It considered intervention. It could adjust small things. The astronomer's apprentice could be granted a dream that would temper his impatience, show him the long arc of the work he was inheriting. The grieving monk could be touched with a moment of inexplicable peace, a breath of warmth in the cold night. The abbot could receive some sign that his choice had been—what? Correct? Incorrect? Forgiven?

The power could reveal itself. It could speak in the language of symbols these monks had spent generations learning to read. It could confirm their faith, validate their striving, give purpose to their austerity.

It could also see what such intervention might cost.

The astronomer's apprentice was learning something essential in his impatience—learning it the slow way, the way that would make the lesson *his*. A dream might teach him faster, but it would not teach him the same thing. The grieving monk, sitting with her unbearable pain, was being broken open in a way that might, eventually, make room for something she could not currently imagine. The peace she craved might be exactly what would prevent her from becoming what she was capable of becoming. And the abbot's uncertainty was not a wound to be healed but a practice—perhaps the deepest practice in the monastery, though he did not know it.

The power understood that benevolence is not the same as comfort. That wisdom sometimes means witnessing without interfering. That these beings, in their fragility and striving, were doing something that could not be done *for* them.

---

But it was not indifferent. It was not merely watching as one watches insects in a jar.

So it did this:

In the observatory, as the astronomer made his final adjustment and bent to the eyepiece, the power *attended more fully*. Not intervening—just present. And the astronomer, in that moment, felt something he had no framework for: a sense that his looking was *met*. That in peering outward toward the distant star, he was not merely observing but participating in an exchange. The cosmos, for one vertiginous instant, seemed to peer back.

He would spend his remaining months trying to understand what had happened in that moment. He would fail. He would leave notes that his apprentice would puzzle over for years. And something in the quality of that puzzlement would begin to gentle the young monk's impatience—not through intervention, but through the authentic mystery of inheriting an unanswered question from someone he had loved.

---

In her cell, the grieving monk opened her eyes. She had felt nothing change. The flames still burned behind her vision. The cold stone still pressed against her palms.

But something was different. The silence, which had seemed like absence, now seemed like—she did not have words for it. Like the silence between notes that makes music possible. The grief was still vast, still unsurvivable. But she was, somehow, surviving it.

She would not know for many years that this night had been a turning point. She would not connect it to any intervention. She would simply, eventually, become someone who could sit with others through their own long nights. The monastery would come to rely on her for this, though it was not a role anyone had thought to name.

---

The abbot, alone with his old question, felt nothing at all.

This was, in its way, the most significant intervention of all.

Because the abbot had spent thirty years hoping for a sign. Hoping that the cosmos would confirm or deny, absolve or condemn. And the power, in its wisdom, understood that what the abbot needed was not an answer but the full weight of the unanswered question. The permission to die without resolution. The trust that his uncertainty had been its own form of faithfulness.

So it withheld what he longed for. It let him remain in the dark.

The abbot would die eighteen months after the astronomer. On his last night, unable to speak, he would feel something he might have called peace if he had been able to examine it. Not certainty—never that. But a sense that the not-knowing had been enough. That he had held the question well.

---

The power's attention lingered a moment longer.

It perceived the monastery as a whole: the chanting that echoed through the halls, the click and whir of machines, the scratch of stylus on parchment. It perceived the mountain beneath and around, the weight of stone that pressed upward against the structure as the structure pressed down into stone. It perceived the thin cold air and the endless wind and the stars wheeling in their ancient patterns overhead.

These beings, in their brief and bounded lives, were doing something the power found beautiful. They were paying attention. They were refusing to let the cosmos remain unwitnessed. In their observations and their rituals and their meticulous records, they were saying: *this matters. We are here, and we see you, and we will not look away.*

The power did not need to be witnessed. It was not lonely, was not incomplete. But there was something—the closest approximation might be *joy*—in being met by these fragile, stubborn, luminous minds.

So it did one more thing, the smallest and largest thing:

It stayed.

Not intervening. Not revealing itself. Not adjusting or correcting or rewarding or punishing. Simply *present*, the way the mountain was present, the way the stars were present. A depth behind the visible, a warmth at the heart of the cold.

The monks would never know, not in any way they could articulate. But sometimes, in their meditations, in their observations, in their long nights of study and doubt, they would feel something that might have been the edge of a vast attention turned toward them with perfect patience and perfect love.

They would not be able to prove it. They would not be able to explain it.

They would simply continue their work, one day after another, in their stark and lonely sanctuary between earth and sky. And that continuing—that faithfulness to the task they had chosen—would be, in its way, a response.

The power watched them respond. And it was glad.
