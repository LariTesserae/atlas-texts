# The Attending

What comes to Mehel-Kuun is not a force that arrives. It is a quality of attention that deepens—as if something that had always been watching from very far away has now drawn close enough to *see*.

The Reckoners notice first. Osal Tev, ninety-three years into her work, looks up from her instruments one morning and finds that the equations she has been balancing for four generations have... simplified. Not solved—that would take another two centuries, and the work must be done properly—but *clarified*, as if a fog she had not known was present has lifted from the mathematics itself. The numbers mean what they have always meant, but now she can see *why* they mean it. She sits very still for a long time, her depthless eyes reflecting something that is not in the room, and when she finally moves again, it is to make a small adjustment to the great bronze wheel before her. The adjustment is minute. It will save her great-great-grandchildren eleven years of unnecessary calculation.

She does not question what has happened. The Kuunim do not question such things. But she notes it in the records, in the careful notation that has been used for six hundred years: *A clearing has occurred.*

---

On the rooftops of the fifth terrace, Meht pauses in his endless climbing among the verdigris-covered collectors. His fingers, green-tinted and knowing, have found a joint that should not exist—a place where two copper frames meet in a way that the original design never intended, that the centuries of modification never created, that simply *is* now when it was not before. He examines it with the patience of his kind, touching the metal, tasting the moisture that beads along its surface.

The joint is perfect. It directs water in a way that his collectors have been striving toward for decades, a flow pattern he had almost understood but could not quite implement. Now it is here, as if the machinery itself had finally figured out what it was trying to become.

Meht completes his circuit of the collectors. He does not mention the new joint to anyone. But that evening, he sleeps more deeply than he has in years, and in his sleep he dreams of copper growing like coral, reaching toward configurations that solve problems he has not yet learned to name.

---

The girl Suol wakes on a morning like any other morning, but something in her has shifted. The taste of metal in her mouth is still there—it has been there for months now, ever since the dreams began—but it has become... intelligible. Not language exactly. Not meaning in any way she could speak aloud. But *sense*. Pattern. The feeling that the patterns she sees in the crystal growth are not arbitrary, that they are trying to tell her something, and that she is finally learning how to listen.

She goes to the flat stones where she plays her games of prediction. She places a drop of mineral water and watches it begin to evaporate. But this time, instead of predicting where the crystal will form, she simply *knows*. Not guessing. Not calculating. Knowing, the way she knows where her hands are without looking at them.

The crystal grows exactly as she knew it would.

She sits very still, the way the Reckoners sit when something important has occurred, and she feels something in her settle into place—some alignment between what she is and what the city needs her to become. She is eleven years old. She has just taken the first step on a path that will occupy the rest of her very long life.

When Osal Tev comes to speak with her that afternoon—as Osal Tev has been planning to do for months, waiting for the right moment—Suol is ready. She does not know what she is ready for. But she is ready.

---

In the deep chambers, in the flooded dark where the Deep-Workers tend processes too slow and strange for surface minds to comprehend, something changes in the pulse of the pumps. The rhythm that has made teeth ache for generations becomes... gentler. Not weaker—the work continues, the slow chemistry proceeds—but *kinder*, as if the machinery has learned to do what it does without quite so much friction, without quite so much cost to the bodies that tend it.

The woman who walks the seventh terrace once a year does not notice at first. She is too far into her own long becoming, too integrated with the patterns that map themselves onto her skin. But as she makes her annual journey—touching nothing, speaking to no one, simply walking—she finds that her feet hurt less than they did last year. She finds that the haze parts for her slightly, giving her a clearer view of the path ahead. She finds that the mineral traceries on her arms, which have been slowly spreading for decades, have stopped at a boundary she had not known existed, as if they have reached exactly the extent they were meant to reach and will grow no further.

She completes her walk. She returns to the depths. And something in her, some part that has not spoken in thirty years, whispers: *enough. this is enough. you do not need to become more than this.*

She does not know if the voice is hers or something else's. She does not know if there is a difference anymore.

---

The change is not dramatic. The benevolent attention does not remake Mehel-Kuun, does not alter its fundamental nature, does not impose anything upon it that it was not already reaching toward. What it does is *assist*. What it does is *ease*.

The calculations that would have taken two more centuries now take one and a half. The machinery that has been grinding toward optimal configurations arrives there a generation sooner, with less wear on its components, with less wear on the bodies that tend it. The children who are born into the city's service find their paths more gently, with fewer of the false starts and painful adjustments that marked their ancestors' journeys. The thing beneath—the vast processing presence that does not think but *works*—continues its ancient chemistry, but with a subtle refinement, as if it too has benefited from the attending gaze, as if it too has been helped to become more fully what it was always trying to be.

The outsiders who come to Mehel-Kuun notice nothing different. The city is still strange, still slow, still possessed of that quality of attention that makes them uneasy. But those who stay—the rare few who are absorbed into its rhythms—find the absorption gentler than it would have been. They lose their old selves more peacefully. They find their new selves more readily. They drink the water that tastes of nothing and discover that what it gives them is not transformation but *clarification*, a becoming-more-themselves rather than a becoming-something-else.

Toben—who still sometimes remembers that his name was Toben—wakes one morning with a strange new feeling. It takes him several hours to identify it, because he has not felt it in thirty years, because he had forgotten that such a feeling was possible.

It is the feeling of being *known*. Of being seen clearly, completely, without judgment. Of mattering to something larger than himself in a way that does not diminish him but *includes* him.

He goes about his work among the Condensers. He does not mention the feeling to anyone. But his movements are lighter that day, his patience deeper, his attention to the moisture he collects more tender. He has been becoming part of the machinery for three decades. Now, for the first time, he feels that the machinery is grateful.

---

Osal Tev, in her chamber deep within the cliff-face, makes a notation in the records that have been kept for six hundred years. Her handwriting is slow and careful, each character placed with the precision of someone who knows that these words will be read by her descendants' descendants.

*The work continues,* she writes. *The work has always continued. But something has changed in the quality of the continuing. We are not alone in the patience. We are not alone in the attending. Something sees us. Something knows what we are trying to do. Something wants us to succeed.*

She pauses, her pen hovering over the page. The words she has written are inadequate—words always are, when describing such things—but they are true, and truth is what the records are for.

She adds one more line:

*We do not know what it is. We do not need to know. It is enough that it is here. It is enough that it helps. We will continue the work, and we will continue to be helped, and in time—in the long time that is the only time that matters—we will understand.*

She sets down her pen. She returns to her calculations. The great bronze wheel turns on its hidden mechanism, and the patterns it inscribes are fractionally clearer than they were yesterday.

The water seeps. The minerals accumulate. The mountain breathes its slow breath.

And something vast and patient and kind continues to watch, continues to help, continues to ease the long becoming of Mehel-Kuun toward whatever completion it has been reaching for since before human hands first cut the terraces into the ancient stone.

---

The children still play their games of crystal prediction. But now, sometimes, when they place their drops of water and watch them evaporate, they sense—just at the edge of awareness—that they are not playing alone. That something is playing with them. That the patterns they learn to see are being shown to them, gently, carefully, by something that wants them to understand.

They do not speak of this. The Kuunim do not speak of such things.

But when they grow, when they take their places among the Reckoners or the Condensers or the Deep-Workers, they carry with them a quiet certainty that sustains them through the long years of patient work: the certainty that they are seen, that they are valued, that the slow chemistry of their lives matters to something larger than themselves.

It is not worship. The Kuunim do not worship.

It is something simpler. Something older. Something that the word "trust" almost describes, if trust could be stripped of its urgency, its need for proof, its fear of betrayal.

The city trusts what watches it.

What watches it is worthy of the trust.

And the work continues, as it has always continued, as it will continue for centuries yet to come—but now, at last, with the knowledge that it is not done in isolation. That the long patience of Mehel-Kuun is met by a longer patience still. That the mountain's slow breath is heard by ears older than the stone.

The Kuunim have been listening for six hundred years.

Now, finally, they know that something has been listening back.
