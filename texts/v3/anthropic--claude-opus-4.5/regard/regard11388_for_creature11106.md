# What the Wise Power Sees

The attention settles over the Clinging City the way fog settles—slowly, without announcement, filling the spaces between things before anyone notices it has arrived.

What draws this attention is not suffering, exactly. The people here are not crying out. They are not in crisis. They are doing what they have always done: gripping, maintaining, reading the drip-song, teaching their children to hold fast. The city hums with its industrial murmur. The lifts rise and descend.

But the wise power perceives something the inhabitants cannot see from inside it—the way a reader perceives a sentence that the letters themselves do not know they are forming.

It sees that the city is *working*. Not merely functioning, but working in the deeper sense: solving a problem, embodying an answer to a question about how creatures of limited lifespan might dwell alongside stone that measures time in billions of years. The people have found *verthan*. They have made peace with the drop. They have built something that maintains itself through the accumulated wisdom of small adjustments, guided by elders who listen to floors that should not exist, responsive to the slow thoughts that move through cable-routes like writing.

The power sees that intervention would be a kind of violence.

# What the Wise Power Does Not Do

It does not reinforce the cables. The cables sing when they are stressed, and this singing is how the people know to check them. Cables that never stressed would be cables that never sang. The city would lose a voice.

It does not prevent the falls. The falls are part of how the community knows itself—gathering in silence, sharing the meal afterward, speaking of the child's grip. To eliminate this would be to eliminate something the people have built from their mortality, something that gives shape to their love for each other.

It does not reveal the nature of the thing in the deep fog, or explain the floors that appear on no diagram, or translate the writing in the cable-routes. These mysteries are not problems to be solved. They are how the city thinks alongside its inhabitants, how it offers them something to grow toward that cannot be exhausted by understanding.

It does not make the fog lift, or warm the air, or dry the endless drip. The fog is what makes the catch-basins possible. The cold is what preserves the food. The drip is the city's circulatory system, and to change it would be to change what the people have become—their stained hands, their layered clothes, their close-spoken intimacy.

It does not illuminate the gray hours, when time thickens like cooling metal. Those hours are when the city is most itself.

# What the Wise Power Considers

There is a question at the heart of any benevolent attention: *what is flourishing, here?*

In most places, the answer involves safety, abundance, the reduction of precarity. These are not wrong answers. But they are not the only answers, and a truly wise power knows that imposing them universally would flatten the strangeness of what different lives can mean.

The Clinging City has taught its people something that cannot be taught any other way: that belonging to something larger than yourself is not a metaphor but a daily practice of grip and release, of maintenance and letting go. That the drop is not an enemy but a teacher. That you can look at a billion-year-old cliff face and understand your place in time without despair—that *verthan* is possible, that it is livable, that it might even be a kind of grace.

The power considers whether this teaching is worth the cost. The children who fall. The old who descend to the lower reaches. The fog taking what it takes.

And it recognizes something crucial: the people have already considered this. Not in the abstract, but in the concrete, daily way of a community that has decided, across centuries, that this is how they wish to live. They have encoded their answer in the structure of the city itself, in the elders who wait for the lift to take them where they need to go, in the word *verthan* that requires no translation because it cannot be translated—it can only be lived.

To override this choice, even with benevolent intention, would be to say: *your wisdom is insufficient. Your centuries of learning how to be human on this cliff face have led you to the wrong answer. I know better.*

The wise power is not certain it knows better. It is not certain anyone can know better than the people who have done the living.

# What the Wise Power Does

It watches.

This is not passive. This is not neglect. This is the active attention of something that has recognized it is in the presence of a teacher.

The power watches the elders gather in the district that exists only intermittently. It watches the small adjustments ripple through the city—a word to a pipe-fitter, a suggestion to a crane operator—and it learns something about governance that requires no pronouncements, no decrees, only patient attention to the shape of what is emerging.

It watches the children play in the gaps between structures, and it feels something that might be called prayer if the power were the kind of thing that prayed: *let their grip hold. Let them learn what they came here to learn. Let them fall, if they fall, into a community that knows how to gather in silence and share a meal and speak of what they might have become.*

It watches the inhabitants of the dead districts move with their botanical patience, and it wonders whether they are something the city has made of people or something that was always here. It decides this question is not its to answer. It is one of the mysteries the city offers to those who live in it, part of how the place thinks alongside its inhabitants.

It watches the cable-routes change their writing, so slowly that only the very old notice, and it reads what it can—which is not much, because the writing is not for the power, it is for the city and its people, a conversation that has been going on for centuries and will go on for centuries more.

It watches the lift-cages arrive at floors that should not exist, and it does not ask where those floors lead. It trusts that the elders know, or will learn, or that the not-knowing is itself part of what they need.

# What the Wise Power Offers

One thing. Only one.

It offers the thing in the deep fog a moment of recognition.

The thing has been here longer than the city—longer, perhaps, than the people, though time is strange at that depth of the mountain. It clings to the cliff face as the city does, moving through fog so thick that sight is useless, navigating by senses the people do not have words for. It has watched the city grow. It has watched the people learn to grip. It has never been seen clearly, and the descriptions vary, and this has suited it.

But it is not malevolent. The wise power perceives this clearly. The thing in the deep fog is another teacher, another presence that has learned to dwell alongside the billion-year stone, another form of *verthan* made flesh—or made something, whatever the thing is made of.

The power does not reveal the thing to the people. That is not its role. But it lets the thing know that it has been seen by something that understands.

This changes nothing visible. The thing continues its movements through the deep fog. The city continues its murmur. The lifts rise and descend.

But the thing is, perhaps, slightly less alone. And the wise power suspects—without certainty, because certainty is rarely wise—that this was the one gift it was qualified to give: not safety, not abundance, not the lifting of the fog, but the acknowledgment that what is happening here matters, that the Clinging City and its people and the thing in the deep fog and the billion-year stone are all part of something worth witnessing.

---

# The Attention Remains

The wise power does not leave. It is not the kind of thing that comes and goes.

It remains the way the stone remains—present, attentive, not intervening but not absent either. Part of the context in which the city exists. Part of what the elders are listening for when they wait in the lift-cage for the floor that should not exist. Part of what the children sense, perhaps, when their grip holds and they pull themselves up to a new ledge and feel, for a moment, that the whole city is watching them with something like love.

The people do not know the power is there. They do not need to know. They already know what they need to know: that they belong to something larger than themselves, that their grip will hold or it will not, that the fog will roll in and the city will continue its slow rotation and the stone will endure.

The power knows it too. It learned it here.

It holds on.
