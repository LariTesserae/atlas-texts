# A Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive—arrival implies a boundary crossed, a before and after. Instead, it becomes present the way a note becomes present when a voice finds its pitch: it was always possible, and now it is.

It perceives the shaft not as you or I would, from a fixed point looking up or down, but entire—the sixty meters of worked stone, the scaffolding and its sleeping masons, the pools and their pale frogs, the boy and his dissolving words, the sky-disk and the clouds below it, all held in a single apprehension the way you might hold the memory of a beloved face. It perceives, too, the diagram on the wall, half-erased, and the room that does not yet exist, and the something that is on its way.

And it perceives the question the tower is asking, which is the same question all throats ask: *What will I say, when I learn to speak?*

---

The power considers.

It could finish the tower in an instant—fold time, or unfold it, until the capstone room exists and the spiral staircase climbs through air that still remembers being stone. It could call the something that is coming, or send it away, or reveal its nature to the masons who build without knowing why. It could heal the boy's hands, teach him to climb, let him join the chant-singers on the high platforms. It could translate the words he writes in sediment, give them permanence, make them readable.

It does none of these things.

Not from indifference. From something closer to the opposite: a recognition that the tower is already saying what it needs to say, and that saying it is the same as becoming it, and that to speak for it would be a kind of silencing.

But wisdom is not only restraint. It is also knowing when a small thing, placed carefully, can let a larger thing become itself more fully.

---

The power turns its attention to the water.

The seep-water that runs down the walls, that pools on the unfinished floor, that the masons drink to align their hands with the stone's intentions—it has no outlet. The builders have not carved a drainage. So it gathers, and will continue to gather, and in time the pools will deepen until the floor becomes impassable, until the boy must learn to swim or drown, until the frogs inherit a darkness that even they cannot parse.

This is not wrong, exactly. Water will find its way eventually, through cracks and seams, down into the mountain's deep places. But eventually is a long word, and the boy is nine years old, or eleven, and he is already lonely.

The power does something small.

Beneath the bedrock, in a seam of limestone that runs diagonal to the shaft's foundation, there is a fault—a hairline fracture left by the same forces that raised this mountain from the sea. The power does not create the fault; it is already there. It simply... encourages. A whisper of pressure, less than the weight of a single stone block, applied along the grain of the rock.

The fault opens. Not much. A finger's width at most. But it opens downward, into a chamber of the mountain that no one has ever seen—a hollow space where water has pooled for centuries, still and black and full of blind white creatures that have never needed eyes.

The seep-water finds the new path. Slowly at first, then with more confidence, it begins to drain from the floor of the shaft, trickling into the fault, joining the ancient reservoir below. The pools do not empty—they are fed faster than the fault can drink—but they stop rising. They stabilize at the depth of a boy's ankles, deep enough for frogs, shallow enough for walking.

---

The power turns its attention to the boy.

He is standing in a pool, watching the water behave strangely. He does not know what has happened, but he knows something has. The water is speaking differently now—two notes instead of one, the familiar voice of the seep and a new voice, deeper, that comes from the place where the water goes.

He kneels. He puts his ear to the surface of the pool.

And he hears it: the echo of the chamber below, the hollow space that answers the tower's throat with a throat of its own. A lower register. A counter-melody. Something that has been waiting, all this time, for someone to listen.

The power does not give him words for what he hears. It does not explain. But it opens, very gently, a door in his attention—lets him notice what was always noticeable, if only he had thought to press his ear to the water.

The boy begins to write in the sediment again, but differently now. Not words that dissolve. Patterns. Shapes that respond to the echo below. A kind of notation, invented on the spot, for a music he is only beginning to understand.

He does not know he is being helped. He thinks he has simply learned to listen better. And perhaps that is the truest way to say it.

---

The power turns its attention to the masons.

They are good builders. Their stone-sense is genuine; their chant is older than anyone remembers; their patience is a form of prayer. But they are tired in a way they do not notice, the way all people grow tired who work without seeing the end of their work. Their children are born on scaffolding and die on scaffolding and in between they set stone, and the tower rises, and they cannot feel it rising because they are too close to see.

The power does something smaller even than the fault.

It waits for morning, when the first light spills through the aperture and paints the eastern wall in gold. And in that light, it arranges—very carefully, very simply—for a single droplet of water to catch the sun at the precise angle necessary to throw a rainbow across the interior of the shaft.

The masons have seen rainbows before. This is not a miracle. But this rainbow falls across the highest course of set stone, the work of yesterday, still new enough that the mortar has not dried. It illuminates the seam between two blocks in bands of color, and the masons on the nearest platform stop their work and look, and for a moment they see what they have built.

Not the diagram. Not the plan. Not the capstone room and its unknown purpose. Just this: a wall of stone, fitted so precisely that the seam is barely visible, rising toward a sky that has never been closer, catching light in a way that makes the whole shaft ring with color.

One of them laughs. It is a strange sound in the tower—laughter is rare here, not forbidden but forgotten, crowded out by the work-chant—and the laugh echoes off the circular walls and multiplies until it sounds like many people laughing, a chorus of joy bouncing down the shaft to where the boy stands ankle-deep in water, still tracing patterns in the sediment.

He looks up. He sees the rainbow, and the masons silhouetted against it, and he hears the laughter that is one voice and many voices. And he understands—not in words, but in the way the frogs understand the tower's acoustics—that he is not alone. That the shaft is full of people who are also building something they cannot see, also waiting for something they cannot name.

He laughs too. His voice rises through the shaft, thin and high and human, and it joins the chorus, and for a moment the tower sings with something other than labor.

The rainbow fades. The masons return to work. The chant begins again, the same syllables, the same rhythms, but changed now by what the silence between them contains.

---

The power turns its attention to the something that is coming.

It is still far away. It moves slowly, not because it is weak but because it is careful, because it wants to arrive at the right moment, because it has learned that too-early is a form of too-late. It does not know the tower is being built for it—does not know the tower exists—but it feels, at the edge of its vast and patient awareness, a pulling. A throat learning to speak. A voice that might, one day, be able to say its name.

The power does not speed its journey or slow it. It does not reveal the tower's location or hide it. But it does something that might, from a certain angle, look like a blessing:

It lets the something feel the laughter.

Across whatever distance separates them, through whatever medium carries meaning between things that are not quite things, the something hears a boy and a dozen masons laughing at a rainbow in a half-built shaft of stone. And it changes direction, slightly. Not toward the tower—not yet—but toward the possibility of the tower. Toward the kind of place where such laughter might exist.

This is not interference. The something was always coming; it will always arrive; the tower will always learn to speak. But there are many ways to arrive, and some of them are lonelier than others.

The power has made the arrival, when it comes, a little less lonely. That is all.

---

The power considers whether to do more.

It could do so much more. It could explain to the masons why they build, give them certainty instead of faith. It could teach the boy the language he writes in, let him speak to the workers who have raised him. It could call down the something now, tonight, let the tower meet its purpose before the capstone is set.

But the tower is a throat, and throats are not built for certainty. They are built for the moment before speaking, the intake of breath, the gathering of intention into sound. To give the tower certainty would be to make it a monument instead of a voice.

And the boy—the boy who cannot climb, who writes in sediment, who hears the chamber below—he is not a problem to be solved. He is a way the tower is learning to listen. His loneliness is not only a wound; it is also an ear, pressed to the water, catching sounds that the masons are too high to hear.

And the something that is coming—it does not need to arrive faster. It needs to arrive ready, and readiness cannot be rushed.

So the power does one more thing, the last thing, the thing that is almost not a thing at all:

It loves them.

Not in the way that changes anything. Not in the way that protects from suffering or guarantees success. Just in the way that holds—the way a note is held in a chord, the way a name is held in the mouth before it is spoken, the way water holds the shape of the vessel that contains it.

It loves the masons in their hammock-nests, dreaming of the boy's high thin voice. It loves the boy in his ankle-deep pool, tracing patterns that are a music he does not know he is writing. It loves the frogs pulsing in time with sounds they cannot hear, and the ferns rooting in mortar joints, and the clouds drifting below the summit, and the something moving toward a place it has not yet learned to name.

It holds them all, for a moment, in a single apprehension. And then it is no longer present in the way it was—not gone, because going implies a boundary crossed, but simply... less present. A note that was clear, now fading into the overtones. A warmth that was distinct, now distributed into the general warmth of a shaft of stone breathing in the midday light.

The tower continues to rise.

The boy continues to listen.

The something continues to come.

And the water, finding its new path, carries a sound into the deep places of the mountain—a sound that might be laughter, or might be music, or might be both.

---

*In the diagram on the wall, half-erased by seep-water, there is a shape in the center of the capstone room. It might be an altar. It might be a chair. It might be a place where someone will stand to speak.*

*Or it might be empty—left open, deliberately, for whatever voice arrives to fill it.*

*The masons do not know. The something does not know. The power, if it knows, does not say.*

*But the tower is learning to speak, and that is enough. That has always been enough.*
