# The Attending

The benevolent power does not arrive. It has been here—not watching, but *adjacent*, the way a coastline is adjacent to the sea. It has no name that translates, but if pressed, it might be called something like "the patience between waves" or "what the deep water knows about light."

It perceives the Tideholder not as a problem to solve but as a question being asked, over and over, in a language of stacked stones and spiral water. And it understands that the question is not "help me" or "free me" but something closer to: *Am I still the one who chose this?*

---

The first thing the power does is nothing.

This is important. The chamber exists in a state of desperate, perfect tension—a held breath that has lasted eons. Any force applied carelessly would shatter it, and the Tideholder with it. So the power simply *attends*. It lets itself be present in the way that warmth is present in stone that has sat in sunlight. Not acting. Not yet. Just making itself available to be noticed.

The Tideholder feels this as a slight change in the quality of the thrumming—not a new note, but a sense that the sound is being *heard*. For the first time in cycles beyond counting, she is not alone with her own voice.

She does not trust it. She has forgotten much, but she remembers (or invents the memory of) other presences that came with offers, with demands, with hunger. Her hand goes to the volcanic glass blade. The afterimages of her possible selves fan out in defensive positions.

The power waits.

---

Days pass, or what would be days if time moved properly here. The Tideholder continues her work—adjusting bowls, adding stones, forgetting and rediscovering her tools. But now she pauses sometimes, head tilted, listening for the quality of attention that has made itself known.

The power offers nothing. It does not speak, does not illuminate, does not solve. It simply continues to witness, and in that witnessing, something shifts.

The Tideholder begins to notice her own patterns.

Not all at once—that would be too much, would collapse the delicate architecture of forgetting that allows her to persist. But in small ways: she finds a stone she's placed before and instead of experiencing it as new, she feels a faint echo of having chosen it. She looks at her scarred palms and wonders, for just a moment, how many times these hands have learned.

The power is not giving her memory. It is giving her *permission* to remember—creating a space where remembering is safe, where the weight of accumulated time will not crush her.

---

The second thing the power does is leave a gift.

In one of the stone bowls, among the upward-flowing water, there appears a small object: a shell, ordinary in every way, the kind that might wash up on any beach in the world above. It is not magical. It holds no special properties. It is simply *from elsewhere*—a piece of the world that exists in normal time, where things change and decay and become other things.

The Tideholder finds it during her rounds. She holds it to her ear—a gesture she doesn't remember learning—and hears the whisper of a sea that moves, that rises and falls, that does not hang suspended in eternal tension.

She weeps. The tears fall *downward*, normally, drawn by a gravity the chamber usually ignores. Where they strike the obsidian floor, small flowers of salt crystal form—the first change this place has allowed in longer than the stones remember.

The power does not comfort her. Comfort would be an intrusion. Instead, it simply holds the space for grief—for the mourning of a self she cannot fully recall, for the weight of choices made so long ago that even their echoes have faded.

She weeps until she empties, and then she sits with the shell in her lap, breathing both water and air, and for the first time in her long existence, she *rests* without dispersing into the thrumming. She remains herself, singular, bounded, while the chamber continues without her constant attention.

It does not collapse.

---

This is the third thing, and the most important: the power shows her that the chamber can hold itself.

Not forever. Not without her. But for moments, for stretches of time, the impossible physics she created have become *stable*—not through her will alone, but through their own accumulated persistence. The water has forgotten how to fall because it has spent so long not falling. The stones remain stacked because that is simply where they are now.

The Tideholder did not build a prison. She built a *habit*, and habits can be set down.

The power does not tell her she is free. Freedom would be meaningless to someone who has forgotten what it means to choose. Instead, it offers her something smaller and more precious: the knowledge that she can *pause*. That the tide will hold without her holding it, at least for a while. That she can sit with a shell from the world above and listen to the memory of waves without everything falling.

---

In the cycles that follow, the Tideholder begins to experiment.

She leaves the chamber—briefly at first, just through the fissure and into the crushing dark of the true ocean. The pressure should kill her, but she is too impossible to die conventionally. She swims upward, toward light that moves correctly, toward water that flows as water should. She touches the world.

Then she returns, because the chamber is hers and she is the chamber's, and some bonds are not meant to be broken but only *understood*.

But now she returns by choice.

The power watches as she begins to arrange the bowls in new patterns—not the desperate configurations of someone trying to send a message she's forgotten, but playful arrangements, aesthetic experiments, water-sculptures made for the simple joy of making. She adds stones to the cairn not because she must but because she wants to see what the impossible geometry will do next.

She starts talking to the fish that phase in and out of existence—not counting them, but greeting them, giving them names that last only as long as they're present. She names the crab with the independent reflection. She names the thrumming.

She names herself.

It's different each time she tries—she hasn't settled on one yet, may never settle—but the act of *choosing* a name, even temporarily, is new. It is a declaration that she is someone, not just something, not just the ongoing event of holding back the tide.

---

The power's final act is to become unnecessary.

It withdraws its attention slowly, like the tide going out (and she would appreciate that metaphor, if she knew it was happening). It leaves behind only the shell, and the salt-crystal flowers, and the knowledge that the chamber was witnessed—that her long work was seen by something that understood its weight.

The Tideholder notices the absence, eventually. She does not mourn it. She has learned that presences can come and go without everything collapsing. She places the shell on the cairn, where it rests on an edge that doesn't exist, and she smiles with a mouth that flickers between human and something older.

The chamber thrums its eternal note.

But now there are harmonics—faint variations, suggestions of melody. The sound of someone learning to sing.

---

She is still the Tideholder. The tide still needs holding, and she is still the one who holds it. But she is no longer only that. She is also the one who rests, who explores, who names things, who remembers just enough to know that she has forgotten. She is the keeper of an impossible place who has learned that impossibility can be *lived in*, not just maintained.

The benevolent power did not free her, because freedom was never what she needed. It did not heal her, because she was not broken—only *held*, like the water, in a tension that had forgotten it could ease.

What it gave her was simpler and stranger: the knowledge that she was not alone in the universe, and that her long work mattered, and that even the most eternal things are allowed to change.

The tide holds.

She holds it less tightly now.
