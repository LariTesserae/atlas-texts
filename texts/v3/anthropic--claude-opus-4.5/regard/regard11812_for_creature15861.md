# The Attending

---

## I. The Arrival of Notice

The power that turns its attention toward Sieve-Hollow does not arrive. It has no need to. It simply *attends*—the way sunlight attends to a leaf without traveling, the way a dreamer attends to the dream while remaining asleep in another room entirely.

If the Hollow-Folk were to sense this attendance, they might describe it as the moment before a gutter settles into true level: that held breath when water hesitates, deciding which way to flow. But they do not sense it. The power is careful about that. It has learned, across spans of time that make the Hollow's raw shale seem ancient, that awareness of benevolence often curdles the thing it means to help. People begin performing for the watcher. They mistake the watcher's preferences for their own. They forget how to read their own gutters.

So the power attends without announcing. It observes the way a mother leans across the alley gap to pass bread to her sister. It notices the gutter-ward's toadstone pressed against her teeth. It watches the way an elder's hands shake when reciting the Seam-list, not from age but from the weight of knowing which joints felt soft, which might give. It sees the children chase fog-cranes until the shapes dissolve, and the way the children's laughter is quiet—not suppressed, but *calibrated*, tuned to the same hush as everything else.

The power perceives something else, too: the Hollow is not broken. This is important. So many places that draw such attention are broken—cracked through with suffering that screams for intervention, injustice that demands correction. But Sieve-Hollow is not screaming. It is whispering, as it always whispers, drip and sigh and creak and whisper, and the whisper says: *we are managing. We are tired. We are proud. We are afraid of the silence. We are exactly what we have made ourselves.*

This is the hardest kind of place to help. A wound can be cleaned and bound. But what do you offer a body that is healthy yet exhausted, that has learned to breathe in a room with no air to spare?

---

## II. What the Power Understands

The attending presence is not omniscient. Benevolence without limit becomes indistinguishable from weather—it happens *to* people rather than *with* them. So the power has chosen limits. It cannot read the Hollow-Folk's thoughts, only their actions and their faces and the thousand small choices they make between one drip and the next. It cannot force outcomes, only offer possibilities. It cannot undo the past, only open doors in the present that might lead somewhere other than the expected future.

What the power understands, after long observation:

**The Hollow-Folk are not victims of their environment.** They are *collaborators* with it. The constant maintenance, the never-finished quality of every beam and gutter, is not a burden imposed from outside—it is a relationship chosen and re-chosen every dawn. The mother who passes bread across the alley gap is not surviving despite the density; she is *thriving in* it, her sister close enough to touch without leaving home. The gutter-ward with her toadstone is not trapped in drudgery; she is reading a language most people will never learn, and she knows it, and that knowing steadies her hands.

**But.**

The exhaustion is real. The fear of the Still is real. The way the elders' voices thin when a ribsnap echoes, the way everyone migrates toward the throat-wells when the dripping pauses too long—these are signs of a people stretched to the edge of their capacity. Not past it. But at it. And a people at the edge has no reserve for the unexpected: the storm that rises higher than any storm before, the spring that stops breathing for reasons the shale has not yet revealed, the child born with ear-flanges that cannot fold or flare.

The power also understands something the Hollow-Folk themselves might not articulate: **they have forgotten they have choices.** Not the daily choices—which joint to repair, which tier to reinforce, whether to trade eel meat or slate shards for labor. Those choices are constant and conscious. But the larger choices, the ones about *what the Hollow could become*, have calcified into assumption. The way things are is the way things must be. The density is fixed. The silence is holy. The water goes where the water has always gone.

This is not wrong. Tradition is a kind of wisdom, the sedimented knowledge of ancestors who tried other ways and found them wanting. But tradition can also become a shell that protects and confines in equal measure, and the power senses that the Hollow-Folk are pressing against the inner walls of their shell without knowing how to name what they feel.

## III. The First Intervention: The Question in the Stone

The power begins small. Anything else would be imposition.

On a morning like any other—mist beading on eyebrows, frog-light fading to grey pearl—a young woman named Tersa descends to the throat-wells for her patrol. She is new to the work, still learning to interpret the belch and gulp of the tides below. Her toadstone sits unfamiliar in her mouth, and she worries she cannot yet distinguish its true tingle from her own nervousness.

As she rounds the second well, her foot dislodges a pebble from the rim. Ordinary. Except when she glances down, the pebble has split, and in the split is a fossil—a tiny spiral shell, ancient beyond reckoning, pressed into the stone when this place was seabed.

Tersa has seen fossils before. Everyone in the Hollow has. But something about this one catches her eye. The spiral seems to turn inward forever, and as she traces it with her finger, a question surfaces in her mind—not planted by the power, but *released* by it, the way a dam's removal releases water that was always there:

*What if the Hollow could breathe deeper?*

She doesn't know what the question means. She pockets the fossil and continues her patrol. But the question stays with her through the day, through the evening recitation of the Seam-list, through the night when she lies in her hanging cradle and listens to the drip-sigh-creak-whisper that has been her lullaby since birth.

*What if the Hollow could breathe deeper?*

## IV. The Spreading of Small Questions

The power does not force answers. It only opens spaces where questions can root.

Over the following weeks, other small strangeness appears. A child named Osk, too young for gutter-work but old enough to carry messages across the walkways, finds a fog-crane that does not dissolve. It hangs in his hands, delicate as ash, and when he shows his grandmother she gasps—not in fear but in memory. She tells him of her own grandmother, who once caught a fog-crane that lasted three days. "She said it showed her a dream of the Hollow with gardens on every roof. Gardens! Can you imagine the weight?"

Osk cannot imagine it. But he wonders.

A craft-judge named Vehn, responsible for approving new gutter routes, discovers that his slate-blade has developed a faint iridescence along the edge. The color shifts when he angles it toward light: blue, green, a purple so deep it seems to hum. He tests the blade on scrap timber. It cuts more cleanly than any blade he has owned. But more than that—when he holds it close, he feels a peculiar stillness in his chest, the opposite of the fear he feels during ribsnaps. A stillness that says: *there is time. There is time to do this well.*

An elder named Mossa, whose three iron studs mark her as a keeper of water-routes, begins dreaming of channels she has never seen. In the dreams, the channels run not down but *sideways*, connecting tiers that have never been connected, creating redundancies so that if one gutter fails, three others carry the load. She wakes irritated—the dreams seem like criticism of her life's work—but also curious. She sketches the dream-channels on a scrap of eel-skin, tells no one, and tucks the sketch behind her sleeping mat.

None of these people speak to each other about what they have experienced. The Hollow's hush does not encourage such confessions. But the power watches the sketches accumulate, the questions ferment, the small irrational hopes send roots down into the dark.

## V. The Night the Dripping Stopped

It happens in the seventh week of the power's attendance, on a night when the moon is hidden and the frog-light barely limns the rooftops.

The dripping stops.

Not gradually. Not in stages. Between one breath and the next, every spring in the Hollow falls silent. The springs that breathe through needle-thin vents, the seeps that ooze along shale cuts, the weeping walls and sweating ceilings—all of them, still.

For three heartbeats, the Hollow-Folk freeze. Then the migration begins, as it always does: bodies flowing toward the throat-wells, ears flared, hands gripping walkway rails. By the time Tersa reaches her station, half the settlement has gathered, pressed together on the narrow platforms, straining to hear *anything* from below.

Silence. The kind of silence that has a texture, a weight. The kind that makes the ears ache for wanting to fill it.

An old woman begins to keen—a soft, breathless sound, the beginning of the death-wail. Others take it up. The keening spreads across the crowd like ripples, and Tersa finds tears on her cheeks though she does not know why she is crying.

And then—

A sound. Not from the throat-wells. From above.

Everyone looks up.

---

## VI. The Rain That Was Not Rain

What falls from the sky is not water. Not quite. It is luminous—the same greenish glow as the frog-light, but concentrated, dropping in fat slow beads that seem to hang in the air before they land. Where the beads strike wood, they sink in and vanish. Where they strike skin, they feel cool but not cold, and they leave behind a faint tingling, like the toadstone against teeth.

The keening stops. The Hollow-Folk stand with faces upturned, arms outstretched, catching the glow on their palms. For a full minute nothing else happens. The not-rain falls. The silence holds.

Then Tersa hears it: a murmur. Low and rhythmic, coming from everywhere and nowhere. She flares her ears to their fullest and the murmur resolves into words—or not words, exactly, but something her mind interprets as words:

*You have done well. You have done well. You have done so well with what you were given. Rest now. Rest. The water will return.*

The murmur does not explain itself. It does not promise rescue or transformation. It only says what is true: the Hollow-Folk have done well. And it asks nothing in return for saying so.

Slowly, the not-rain slows. The beads grow smaller, less frequent, until the last one lands on the upturned face of Osk the messenger-child, and he giggles—a sound so unexpected in the hush that several adults gasp.

And then, with a great shuddering belch, the throat-wells cough. Water surges up through the vents. The springs resume their breathing. The drip-sigh-creak-whisper returns.

The Hollow-Folk do not cheer. They are not a cheering people. But someone begins to laugh, and someone else joins, and soon the platforms are full of quiet, breathless laughter that sounds almost like weeping.

---

## VII. The Morning After

Dawn comes grey and pearlescent, as it always does. But something has changed.

Tersa wakes to find the fossil in her pocket warm, as though it has been resting near a fire. She pulls it out and sees that the spiral has shifted—or she is seeing it differently now. It still turns inward, but it also suggests an outward motion, an unfurling, as if the ancient creature that left this shell had lived by both contracting and expanding.

She goes to find Vehn the craft-judge. She does not know why; the impulse simply moves her. She finds him on his platform, turning his iridescent blade in the early light.

"I have a question," she says.

He looks at her. Waits.

"What if the Hollow could breathe deeper?"

Vehn is quiet for a long moment. Then he says, "I have been dreaming of blades that cut without taking. Blades that shape the stone so the water has new places to go."

Tersa nods. "Mossa has been dreaming of channels."

They look at each other. The hush around them is the same hush it has always been, but it feels less like a stricture now and more like a container waiting to be filled.

"Should we ask her?" Tersa says.

## VIII. The Council That Was Not Commanded

Within three days, a gathering forms—not a formal council, nothing announced or mandated. Simply: people who have been carrying questions find each other and sit together on the highest platform, looking out over the Hollow at dawn.

Mossa brings her eel-skin sketches. Vehn brings his blade. Tersa brings the fossil. Osk brings the fog-crane, which has still not dissolved; it sits in his lap like a folded bird, patient. Others bring their own small strangeness: a fisher who has been dreaming of eels that glow from within and could light the darker crawlspaces; a roof-mender who woke one morning understanding, without being told, exactly how to weave a mat that lets water through but keeps wind out; a salt-collector whose fingers have started to itch whenever she passes a rock that holds untapped minerals.

They do not speak all at once. This is not their way. They take turns, brief and precise, each one offering a piece of what they have seen or felt or imagined. And as the pieces accumulate, a picture begins to form—not a plan yet, but the shape of a plan, the silhouette of a Hollow that does not replace what they have but *extends* it.

Gardens on rooftops—light ones, in mesh containers, fed by a new system of mist-catchers that Mossa's channels could supply.

Glow-eels bred for the deeper spaces, so the crawlspace swimmers need never fear the dark.

Stone-needles not merely tolerated but *cultivated*, planted near weak joints where they can grow into natural supports.

Fog-cranes studied rather than dismissed—perhaps they carry information in their folds, patterns that reveal what the sky intends.

None of it contradicts the Hollow's existing wisdom. All of it asks: *what if we have not yet discovered everything we are capable of?*

---

## IX. What the Power Does Not Do

The power watches the gathering and feels something that, in a being capable of such feelings, might be called satisfaction.

But it does not intervene further. It does not send more luminous rain or plant more fossils or whisper instructions in the night. The questions have been opened. The doors have been unlocked. What the Hollow-Folk do now must be their own.

This is the hardest discipline for a benevolent power: to refrain. To trust that the gift has been given and the recipients are capable of using it. So many interventions fail not because they are unwanted but because they continue past the point of welcome, the helper becoming a crutch, the helped becoming dependent on rescue.

The power attends—still attends—but more lightly now, the way a parent attends to a sleeping child: present, watchful, ready if needed, but hoping not to be.

## X. The Years That Follow

The Hollow does not transform overnight. The Hollow-Folk are not revolutionaries. They are patient, careful, methodical—the same qualities that have kept them alive in a place that should have drowned or crumbled long ago.

But changes come.

Mossa's sideways channels are dug, slowly, over the course of two years. Each new route is tested for a full storm-season before being declared permanent. The redundancy saves three tiers when a freak surge collapses a section of the eastern rim; water that would have backed up and flooded flows instead through the new paths, finding its way to the throat-wells without drowning anyone.

Vehn's blades—the iridescent ones, which he has learned to make by quenching slate in the luminous runoff from the not-rain (some of it collected in jars that night, precious beyond measure)—become tools of shaping rather than only cutting. With them, the Hollow-Folk can carve new vents where the springs are strongest, easing the pressure beneath the clay and reducing the ground's tendency to flex.

The roof-gardens take longest. Three attempts fail before a fourth succeeds: a mesh container light enough to add no dangerous weight, filled with a mixture of moss, crusite, and eel-compost that sustains a small crop of tubers. The first harvest is meager—barely enough for a single meal, shared among those who tended the garden. But the meal is eaten in a silence that feels different from the usual hush. It is the silence of people tasting a future they had not known they wanted.

Osk grows up and becomes a keeper of fog-cranes. He is the first to hold the title, because the title did not exist before him. His job is to watch for cranes that do not dissolve, to catch them gently and study the folds, to record what patterns appear before weather changes. The records are fragmentary and sometimes contradictory—the cranes are not a reliable oracle—but three times in his career, Osk's warnings of an incoming storm give the Hollow precious hours to prepare. Precious hours mean lives.

Tersa never becomes an elder. She does not have the temperament for it—too restless, too prone to wandering the ridgeline at odd hours. But she becomes something else: a listener. People come to her with their questions, their doubts, their nascent ideas that feel too strange to speak aloud in council. She does not answer them. She only asks more questions, drawing out the thoughts until the questioners find their own answers. After she dies, they call her the First Ear, and her fossil—the spiral that taught her to wonder—is passed down through her family, each holder adding a small mark to its surface.

The Hollow does not become a utopia. It is still crowded, still damp, still exhausting. Storms still come. Ribsnaps still startle everyone into frozen silence. Children still die sometimes, of fevers or falls or the thousand ordinary tragedies that stalk all living things.

But the fear of the Still lessens. Not because the Hollow-Folk stop respecting silence, but because they have learned that silence is not the enemy. The enemy is *only* silence—silence without the possibility of sound returning. And they have been shown, once, that even when the dripping stops, something can answer.

## XI. The Power's Last Gift

Forty years after the night the dripping stopped, an old man climbs alone to the highest platform. It is Osk, grey now, his ear-flanges thin and papery. He carries nothing except a small jar—one of the original jars, nearly empty, still holding a faint residue of the luminous rain.

He sits on the edge of the platform and looks out over the Hollow at dawn. The mist coils around his shins, as it does for everyone who climbs this high. Below him, the city of huts and ramps and ladders and footbridges spreads like a dense, living net. He can see the roof-gardens, green even in the grey light. He can see the new gutter routes, gleaming with constant flow. He can see a child climbing a ladder with a fog-crane cupped in her hands.

He opens the jar and inhales.

The scent is almost gone—a faint mineral sweetness, like wet stone after a warm rain. But it is enough.

"Thank you," he says, to the air, to the dawn, to whatever might be listening.

The power attends. It has attended all these years, more lightly with each passing season, watching the Hollow-Folk become what they were becoming. Now, for the first and only time, it responds.

Not in words. Not in rain or fossils or fog-cranes. It responds in the only language the Hollow has ever truly spoken:

A single drop falls from the mist and lands on the back of Osk's hand.

Drip.

He closes his eyes. He understands.

*We were always listening. We never left. And you never needed us to stay.*

---

## XII. Coda: The Hollow, Long After

Generations pass. The Hollow changes, as all living things change—gradually, organically, always recognizably itself. The power's attendance fades until it is indistinguishable from the ordinary background benevolence of a universe that is, on balance, more creative than destructive.

But the Hollow-Folk remember. Not as history—history implies distance, and this is too close for that. They remember the way they remember how to breathe, how to flare their ears, how to tap the arm-drums in the old patterns. They remember that once, when the silence came, something spoke back. They remember that the speaking was not a command or a judgment or even a promise. It was only this:

*You have done well. You are not alone. The water will return.*

And every dawn, when the elders climb to the highest platform to watch the mist coil and the frog-light fade, they pour a few drops of water onto the wood—not as offering but as conversation, a greeting to whatever might be attending, a way of saying:

*We are still here. We are still listening. We are still becoming what we were always capable of becoming.*

The power, if it attends, does not reply.

It does not need to.

The reply is the Hollow itself: alive, breathing, changing, wet with the memory of the sea and the hope of the sky, balanced on stilts above the water, reaching always upward toward the light.

---

*And this is what benevolence looks like when it is truly wise: not rescue but recognition, not transformation but permission, not answers but the spaciousness in which questions can finally be asked. The Hollow was never broken. It only needed to remember that it was allowed to grow.*
