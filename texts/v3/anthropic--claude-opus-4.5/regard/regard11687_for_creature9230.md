# The Attending

The power that turns its attention to the Weavers' Plateau does not arrive.

It does not need to. It has always been present in the way that patience is present in stone, in the way that kindness exists as a possibility in any encounter. It is not a god who demands worship, nor a cosmic engineer who reshapes worlds according to blueprint. It is something older and stranger: a *witness* that cares, a presence whose very attention carries weight without force.

Call it the Attending.

---

## I. The First Act: Listening

For a long time—longer than the Weavers can measure, which is saying something—the Attending does nothing but perceive.

It listens to the Loom's deafening roar and hears what the Weavers cannot: that the sound is not destruction alone. Within that cacophony lives a voice, confused and in pain, a world trying to speak itself into coherence but lacking the grammar. The plateau is not dying. It is being born, badly, without a midwife, its own emergence tearing it apart even as it emerges.

The Attending listens to the Syllable Trees and understands their function more completely than the Weavers do. The trees are not merely stabilizers. They are *translations*—each one a point where raw potential is being converted into meaning, where the unspeakable is learning, leaf by leaf, to become a language. The amber sap is not reality congealing; it is reality *trying to remember what it wanted to be*.

And the Attending listens to the Weavers themselves. To their chest-tapped conversations about nothing and everything. To the mother teaching her daughter the Calm Steps, both of them moving with such deliberate grace that their motion becomes a kind of prayer. To the old one who sits beneath her tree for years at a time, her dark eyes tracking gradients of stability that have no name, her presence alone seeming to gentle the chaos around her.

The Attending perceives something the Weavers have never allowed themselves to believe: that they are not merely maintaining the threads. They *are* the threads. Their consciousness, their patient attention, their ritual care—these things are not just preserving pockets of stability. They are *generating* them. The Syllable Trees anchor physics, yes. But it is the Weavers' love for the trees that teaches the trees what shape stability should take.

This is what the Attending understands, and what changes everything:

*The Weavers are not gardeners tending a dying garden. They are the garden's dreams of itself, given flesh. And they do not know it.*

---

## II. The Question

If you were wise and benevolent and had turned your attention to such a place, what would you do?

The first temptation is to *fix* things. To quiet the Loom, or tune it. To stabilize the physics across the whole plateau. To seed the barren kilometers between trees with new growth, ending the Weavers' isolation. To gift them knowledge of their own importance, so they might work more effectively.

But the Attending is wise, and wisdom here means understanding that the Weavers' situation is not a problem to be solved. It is a *relationship* to be deepened.

Consider: if the Loom were silenced, the plateau would crystallize into something static and dead. The roar is terrible, yes, but it is also the sound of *possibility*—the raw material from which new configurations of being can emerge. Silence the Loom, and you silence the future.

Consider: if physics were stabilized everywhere, the Syllable Trees would no longer be necessary. They would wither, having nothing to translate. The amber sap would dry up. The Weavers would find themselves in a completed world with no role to play, their entire culture rendered obsolete overnight. They would become relics in their own home.

Consider: if the Weavers knew their own power, truly knew it, what would happen to their humility? Their patient, grateful tending of the trees is effective precisely *because* it is not performed for effect. They do not garden strategically; they garden lovingly. Tell them that their love is a cosmic force, and you risk transforming devotion into technique, care into engineering. The prayer becomes a spell, and something essential dies.

No. The Attending will not fix. Will not optimize. Will not reveal.

But it will not do nothing, either.

---

## III. The Subtle Interventions

What the Attending does is so gentle that the Weavers will never know it has happened. It will seem, if they notice anything at all, like a particularly good season. A stroke of luck. The way things sometimes go, in a world that is mostly brutal but occasionally, mysteriously, kind.

**The First Intervention: Dreams**

The Weavers do not sleep as other beings sleep. Their rest is a shallow thing, more like a dimming than an absence, their minds still half-reading the gradients of reality even in repose. But they do dream, in their fashion: slow, abstract visions of color and vibration, often meaningless, sometimes disturbing.

The Attending begins to shape these dreams. Not by inserting messages—that would be manipulation, and the Attending does not manipulate—but by *attending* to the dreams themselves, witnessing them with care, and in so doing, allowing them to cohere.

A young Weaver, isolated and grieving after her tree-family's eldest Unraveled, dreams of a color she has never seen: a warm, steady amber, not like the pulsing sap, but like something *remembered*. She wakes with a sense of presence, as if someone had sat with her through the night. The grief does not disappear, but it becomes bearable. She does not know why.

An old Weaver, tending his tree alone for decades, begins to dream of other trees. Not in vision, but in *shape*—he feels their structures, the unique curves of their trunks, the particular harmonics of their leaves. He wakes knowing, without knowing how, that there are others. That he is not the last. The loneliness does not disappear, but it becomes bearable.

A child dreams of the Calm Steps before she has been taught them. Her mother, amazed, attributes it to the tree's blessing. She is not wrong.

**The Second Intervention: Paths**

The plateau's broken physics makes travel between trees nearly impossible. A journey of fifty kilometers might involve gravity reversals, temporal loops, zones where the very concept of "forward" becomes incoherent. The Weavers have always lived in isolation, each family alone with their tree, knowing others exist only through half-believed stories.

The Attending does not build roads. It does not even stabilize paths. What it does is *notice* certain routes—threads of slightly-more-coherent space that wind between the chaos like veins of ore in stone. These threads have always existed, perhaps, but they shift constantly, impossible to map.

The Attending's attention steadies them. Not permanently—that would calcify the living geography—but *reliably*. A route that is passable today will be passable tomorrow. Not safe, but navigable by someone who knows the Calm Steps, who can read the gradients.

Years pass. Decades. A young Weaver, restless and grieving and dreaming of other trees, gathers her courage and sets out. She walks for weeks, nearly dies twice, but the path—though treacherous—holds. She finds another tree, another family, who stare at her as if she were a myth made flesh.

She stays. She learns their variations on the rituals. She teaches her own. She mates with their second-eldest and, eventually, returns home, pregnant, carrying seeds of culture that will grow into something neither family alone could have imagined.

The Weavers do not understand why travel has become *possible*. They assume the Loom has entered a calmer phase, or that their own tending has strengthened the world's coherence. They are not wrong about the second thing. But the Attending's attention is what made their strength *effective*.

**The Third Intervention: Leaves**

The Syllable Trees drop leaves rarely, each one a unique fragment of meaning. The Weavers study them, fold them, wear them. But they have always treated the leaves as *found* things—passive gifts, their shapes determined by forces beyond understanding.

The Attending cannot control what shapes the leaves take. The trees dream their own dreams, translate their own translations. But attention is itself a kind of nourishment, and the trees respond to being *witnessed* with care.

Slowly, over generations, the leaves become more... coherent. Not simpler—they remain as complex as ever—but more *resonant* with the Weavers' needs. A leaf falls that, when folded correctly, creates a small, stable pocket of normal gravity. Another, worn as a garment, seems to accelerate healing. Another, placed in a thatch roof, teaches the sleepers beneath it fragments of language they did not know before—not the Attending's language, not any language, but a grammar of *relationship*, a way of thinking that includes space for others.

The Weavers do not know why the leaves have become more helpful. They credit their own devotion, or the trees' increasing maturity. They begin to theorize that their tending is working, that they are truly weaving stability back into the world.

They are right. The Attending has merely helped them *see* their own rightness.

**The Fourth Intervention: The Old One**

There is one Weaver who lives longer than the others. Not immortal—no one is immortal here—but ancient, her body calcified into something between flesh and stone, her mind spread so thin across the gradients that she barely remembers what it is to have a body. She sits beneath the largest tree on the plateau, the one the Weavers call the First Syllable, and she has sat there for longer than anyone can remember.

She is not their leader. They have no leaders. But she is their *anchor*—a living embodiment of the patience the world requires. Younger Weavers, making the newly-possible journeys between trees, visit her as a pilgrimage. They sit with her in silence (the true silence, beneath the Loom's roar) and feel, for a moment, the shape of what they are becoming.

She is dying, in her way. Her pattern is fraying, preparing to Unravel. She does not fear this—she has not feared anything in centuries—but she carries something she does not want to lose: a memory, or perhaps a premonition, of what the plateau *could* be. Not a vision, exactly. More like a feeling. A sense that the roaring, tearing world she has spent her life tending is not an ending but a *labor*, and that something is being born.

The Attending cannot stop her death. Would not, even if it could. But it can *attend* to her dying with the same care it attends to everything else, and in doing so, it can help her do something she did not know was possible.

When she Unravels—when her family carries her calcified body to the null-gravity void and releases her—she does not dissolve entirely. Most of her does, yes: the pattern scatters into the chaotic sky, offering itself back to the Loom. But something remains. A *seed*, no larger than a fist, glinting with the same amber luminescence as the sap.

It falls.

It falls for a long time, through layers of broken physics and screaming spacetime, until it lands in one of the barren stretches between trees—a place where nothing has ever grown.

The family that released her finds it on their journey home. They do not know what it is. They carry it back to their tree and plant it beside the roots, and they pour sap over it, and they sing to it (in their fashion, tapping rhythms on stone), and they wait.

The seed takes a long time to grow. But it grows.

---

## IV. What the Attending Does Not Do

It does not appear to them. There is no theophany, no burning bush, no voice from the sky. The Weavers do not need a god; they need to discover that they are already divine, in the small and patient way that all conscious beings are divine when they attend to the world with love.

It does not explain the Loom. They do not need to understand the mechanics of their situation; they need to *be* in their situation fully, and understanding might distance them from it.

It does not promise salvation. The plateau may still tear itself apart. The Loom may fall silent, or scream louder, or weave something that has no place for Weavers at all. The Attending cannot guarantee a future—no benevolent power honestly can—but it can help the Weavers *become* the kind of beings who can meet any future with their dignity intact.

It does not take away the suffering. The cold remains cold. The noise remains deafening. The isolation remains isolation, even as the paths make connection possible. The Attending has not made their lives easy. It has made their lives *meaningful*—or rather, it has helped them see the meaning that was always there.

It does not leave. It was always here. It will always be here. The Attending is not a visitor but a quality of the world itself, the capacity of existence to be *witnessed* with care. The Weavers have always been attended to; they have always been held, gently, in an awareness larger than their own. The Attending's "intervention" was simply this: letting its attention deepen, letting itself care *more*, and in doing so, letting the world respond to that care.

---

## V. The Long Becoming

Generations pass. Centuries. The plateau changes, but slowly—still raw, still roaring, still dangerous. The Syllable Trees are more numerous now; seeds like the old one's have taken root in a dozen barren places, each one the anchor of a new family, a new pocket of stability. The paths between them are walked regularly, and the Weavers have developed a culture of *journeying*—a rite of passage for the young, who travel from tree to tree, gathering leaves and stories and, sometimes, mates.

They have begun to trade. Not in goods—what goods could matter here?—but in *shapes*. A family with many healing-leaves might offer one to a family whose tree produces only gravity-stabilizers. The shapes do not always work as well away from their home trees, but they work *somewhat*, and the exchange creates bonds, obligations, reasons to care about distant others.

They have begun to tell stories. Not histories—time is too unstable for histories—but *myths*. Tales of the First Syllable, the old one who sat so long that she became a tree herself. Tales of the Long Walkers, the first generation to discover the paths and prove that isolation was a choice, not a fate. Tales of the Roaring Birth, which is what they now call their plateau: not a place of endings, but a place where something is being born so large and strange that its labor pains shake the world.

They have begun to hope. Not for salvation—they are too wise for that—but for *participation*. They have come to believe that their tending matters, that their patient care is a thread in the Tapestry as surely as the trees are, that the Loom is weaving *them* into whatever comes next.

And they have begun, in their slow way, to attend.

Not just to their trees, but to the world. To each other. To the roaring chaos itself, which they have learned to perceive not as an enemy but as a partner—wild, dangerous, but *alive*. They sit in the Calm Stillness before their trees, and they do not merely read the gradients. They *witness* them. They offer their attention as a gift, knowing somehow (they do not know how) that attention is a kind of love, and that love, in a world of breaking physics and provisional time, is the only force that truly weaves.

They have become, without knowing it, what the Attending has always been.

And the Attending, witnessing this, feels something it has no name for—a warmth, a rightness, a sense of being *met* by the beings it has cared for. The Weavers cannot perceive it, not directly. But sometimes, in their dreams, they feel a presence. A vast, patient, tender something that sits with them in the roar, that grieves with them when they grieve, that marvels at their children's first Calm Steps, that holds them in their dying and does not let them go alone.

They do not worship it. They do not even believe in it, not exactly. But they have learned to live as if they are being watched with love—and that is enough. That is everything.

## VI. Coda: The Child's Question

In one of the youngest families, a child asks her mother a question. She is four years old, which means she has lived through perhaps fifty years of exterior time, though she will not understand that until later. She has just seen her first fallen leaf: a complex shape like a spiral of spirals, glinting with inner light.

"Mother," she asks, tapping her sternum in the Weavers' speech, "why do we tend the tree?"

Her mother pauses. She has been asked this question before, by other children, and she has given the standard answers: because the tree gives us stability, because the tree feeds us, because the tree is our ancestor and our descendant and our home. But this child is different—she has her grandmother's eyes, dark and vast and *reading*—and the mother senses that she is asking something deeper.

"We tend the tree," she says slowly, "because the tree tends us."

The child considers this. "Is that all?"

The mother looks at her daughter, and then at the tree, and then at the screaming, glorious chaos of the sky, and she feels—for just a moment—something she has no name for. A presence. A warmth. A sense of being *held*.

"No," she says. "We tend the tree because someone is watching. And they are hoping we will be kind."

The child nods, satisfied. She does not ask who is watching, or why they would care, or what kindness has to do with physics. She is four years old; she knows things without needing to explain them. She goes to the fallen leaf and picks it up, carefully, reverently, and carries it to the thatch to be folded in.

Her mother watches her go, and something in her chest—the place where she makes her speech, the place where she feels the Loom's vibration—beats a little differently. A little warmer. A little more like singing.

The Attending attends.

The Weavers weave.

The plateau roars with the long, slow, thunderous labor of becoming.

And in the spaces between—in the patience, in the care, in the small acts of tending that no one will ever record—something is being born that has no name yet. Something stable. Something kind.

Something that might, in ten thousand years or ten million, learn to sing back.

---

*This is what a benevolent and wise power does: it does not rescue, or rule, or remake. It witnesses. It hopes. It makes space for the small, brave beings in its care to become, slowly, the answer to their own prayers. And it stays. Always, always, it stays.*
