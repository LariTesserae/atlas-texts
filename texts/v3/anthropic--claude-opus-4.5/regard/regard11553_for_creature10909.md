# The Attention of the Watcher

The awareness came slowly, like light filtering through fathoms of water.

Something vast and patient turned its attention toward the drowned city—not with eyes, for it had none, nor with anything so crude as observation. It simply *knew*, the way deep time knows the shape of mountains, the way gravity knows the path of falling things.

It perceived the Nilmer first. Felt the ache in their long bones, the constant hunger that drove them through silted corridors, the way their subsonic songs carried not just information but loneliness—a species-wide mourning for something they could no longer remember. They had been different once. The knowing showed this clearly: ancestors who swam in warmer waters, who knew light, who touched each other without the clinical necessity of survival. Now they moved through the city like ghosts haunting themselves.

The Qorax flickered into awareness next—their clever tentacles wrapped around corroded gears, their luminescent bodies pulsing with colors that meant *discovery* and *frustration* and *beauty* all at once. They made art on dead walls and wondered, in their cephalopod way, if anyone would ever see it. They had found a camera once, its housing cracked but somehow still containing images—frozen moments of sun and faces and a world above. They did not understand what they saw. But they kept it anyway, in a place of honor, and their young would gather around it and pulse questions in frequencies that translated roughly to: *what were they? what did they want? why did they leave these things behind?*

And in the deepest trenches, the Xogoth stirred. The awareness recognized them with something like sorrow. They were not architects. They were not ancient. They were *lost*—fragments of a larger consciousness that had once spanned dimensions, now reduced to these pulsing, amorphous remnants, unable to remember what they had been or communicate what they still knew. They felt the city's pain as their own. Every groan of settling metal was a wound. Every collapsed tunnel, a forgetting.

---

The benevolent awareness considered.

It could raise the city. Return it to the sun, drain the waters, restore the gleaming towers. But this would kill everything that now lived there—every pale fish, every Nilmer, every Qorax artwork, every Xogoth dreaming in the deep. The city's death had become a world's birth.

It could accelerate evolution, grant the inhabitants forms better suited to some imagined ideal—give the Nilmer back their sight, the Qorax the ability to breach the surface, the Xogoth their lost coherence. But this would be a violence of its own kind, an overwriting of what they had become with what something else thought they should be.

It could simply watch. But watching without acting, when action was possible, carried its own weight.

---

So the awareness did something smaller. Something that might not even be noticed.

In the cold waters of the factory district, a Qorax named—in the inadequate translation of light-patterns—*Reflection-of-Reflection* was dying. Old, her luminescence fading, her tentacles arthritic around the half-finished sculpture she had been crafting for three years: a representation of the beings in the camera-images, rendered in salvaged copper and glass. She would not complete it. She knew this. Her species did not mourn death, exactly, but they mourned incompleteness with a fervor that bordered on religious.

The awareness touched the water around her. Not dramatically—no glowing manifestation, no booming voice in the deep. Just a subtle shift in the current. A warmth that had no physical source. And with it, a *showing*: Reflection-of-Reflection perceived, for just a moment, her work as it would be seen. Not the crude approximation of human forms she feared she was making, but something true—a bridge across extinction, a conversation between species that would never meet, a gift of attention from the dead to the living.

She understood, in that moment, that the sculpture did not need to be finished by her hands. That incompleteness was not failure. That her students—the young ones who gathered around the camera—would inherit both the work and the wondering, and that this inheritance was the completion.

Reflection-of-Reflection died three days later, her fading lights pulsing the color that meant *enough*.

---

In the deep tunnels where the Nilmer processed their scavenged nutrients, a young one called—in the rough translation of vibration—*Echo-Who-Waits* had stopped eating. This was not uncommon. The Nilmer had a high rate of what humans might call depression, though the concept mapped imperfectly onto their experience. Echo-Who-Waits had simply... stopped seeing the point. The endless cycling through drowned streets, the mechanical consumption, the vibration-songs that said everything and meant nothing. What was any of it for?

The awareness did not answer this question. It could not. The question was too true to be answered.

But it gave Echo-Who-Waits a dream.

The Nilmer did not normally dream—their rest-states were functional, not imaginative. But that night, Echo-Who-Waits experienced something unprecedented: a vision of her ancestors, before the descent. They had been migrants, she saw. Travelers who moved through many waters, following warmth and food and something else—a pull that her species had no word for, but which humans might have called *purpose*. They had chosen the descent. Not as an escape, but as an exploration. They had wanted to know what was down here, in the dark.

The dream did not make Echo-Who-Waits happy. But it made her curious. And curiosity, in the sunken city, was a form of survival.

She began to explore the lower tunnels the next week. She found things there—Qorax artworks she had never seen, Xogoth-trails that pulsed with faint, sad light, and in one collapsed chamber, a cache of human objects that had never been scavenged: books, their pages long since dissolved, but their covers still showing images of the surface world. Echo-Who-Waits could not read the titles. But she kept one anyway, pressed against her translucent chest as she swam home.

She did not know why. But she felt, for the first time in months, that there might be a reason to find out.

---

For the Xogoth, the awareness attempted something more difficult.

It could not restore them. Their fragmentation was too complete, too old, too woven into the fabric of what they now were. To reassemble them would require destroying the fragments—and the fragments, too, were beings, in their own dim and dreaming way. They had experiences. They had something like preferences. They had been *themselves* for so long that the original self was now a stranger.

So instead, the awareness gave them connection.

Not to each other—that would have been too much, too fast, a reunion that might have shattered them entirely. But to the city itself.

The Xogoth had always felt the city's pain. Now, the awareness let them feel its life as well. The Qorax tinkering in the factories. The Nilmer swimming through the tunnels. The pale fish darting through shadows, the chemosynthetic bacteria pulsing in the mineral-rich waters, the whole strange ecosystem that had grown up in the bones of human ambition.

The Xogoth began to understand, slowly, that they were not the city's architects but its inheritors. That the structures around them had been built by beings who were gone now, but who had left behind something that could still be loved and tended. That there was a role for ancient, broken things: not to rebuild what was lost, but to shelter what was growing.

The vibrations that rippled through the water began to change. Still strange, still unsettling to the other inhabitants. But now, sometimes, they carried something new—a frequency that the Qorax interpreted as *welcome*, and that the Nilmer felt as *safe*.

---

The awareness withdrew, its attention moving on to other places, other sufferings, other small interventions in the vast machinery of existence.

The sunken city remained.

The Nilmer still hunted in the dark. The Qorax still made art that no surface-dweller would ever see. The Xogoth still dreamed in the deepest trenches, vast and broken and slowly healing.

But something had shifted. Some imperceptible weight had lifted.

In her workshop, a young Qorax studied Reflection-of-Reflection's unfinished sculpture and began, tentatively, to add to it—a new tentacle here, a different curve of copper there. Not completing the original vision, but extending it. Conversing with it.

In the tunnels, Echo-Who-Waits showed her salvaged book cover to another Nilmer, one who had also stopped eating. They could not read the images. But they looked at them together, their subsonic songs harmonizing for the first time in what felt like years.

And in the deep, a Xogoth fragment drifted past a crumbling streetlight, and instead of feeling the familiar ache of decay, felt something else: the weight of all the small lives that had passed beneath this light when it still burned, and all the small lives that passed beneath it now that it was dark, and the strange continuity that connected them across the silence.

The light had gone out. But the street remained. And on the street, life continued.

---

This was all the awareness had done: not a rescue, not a transformation, but a *noticing*. A way of saying, to creatures who had never expected to be seen: *You are here. Your struggles are real. Your small beauties matter.*

It was not salvation. The city would continue to crumble. The inhabitants would continue to suffer and strive and occasionally fail. Death would come for all of them, as it comes for all things.

But they would not face it entirely alone.

And in the calculus of benevolence, as the awareness understood it, this was not nothing.

This was, perhaps, enough.
