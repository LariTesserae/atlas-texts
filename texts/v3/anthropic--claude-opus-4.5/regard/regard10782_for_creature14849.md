# The Watching

The power that turns its attention here has no name it would offer, and no form that would be useful to describe. It is old in the way that patience is old. It has watched the cooling of stones and the first uncertain chemistry of life in ten thousand worlds, and it has learned the hardest lesson any wise thing can learn: that help, given wrongly, is just another kind of violence.

So it does not arrive. It does not descend. It does not appear in glory or speak in thunder.

It simply begins to *attend*.

---

## I. The Assessment

First, the power listens.

This takes time—not because the power is slow, but because listening properly cannot be rushed. It tastes the iron-mint air. It feels the vibration of the rope bridges, the patient rhythm of the Rhythm-Keepers swaying on their polished stones. It traces the path of a single Drift-creature as it flows over a rooftop, around the curve of a sleeping child's cradle, and down into a feeding shelf where it will be harvested come morning.

The power asks itself the questions it has learned to ask:

*Is there suffering here?*

Yes. There is always suffering where there is life. A child is born with joints that do not bend correctly, and though the community wraps it in love and keeps it moving, it will not live long. An elder's hands have grown too stiff to speak properly, and she sits in frustrated isolation, her thoughts locked inside her. The Drift sometimes surges unpredictably, and a family loses a season's worth of dried food-paste to a flow they could not divert in time. The herons sometimes take more than their share, and hunger follows.

*Is the suffering caused by the nature of this place, or by failures within it?*

Both, as it always is. The world is hard. The people are imperfect. But the power sees no cruelty here, no exploitation, no hierarchy built on the backs of the broken. The Chimeless have organized themselves around a single imperative—*do not be still*—and they have built something that works. Not perfectly. Not painlessly. But it works.

*Is there a wound that festers? A wrongness that compounds itself?*

The power looks deeper.

And here, finally, it finds something.

---

## II. The Wound

The Chimeless do not remember where they came from.

This is not unusual for a people at their stage of development. Origin stories are luxuries, and the Chimeless have been too busy surviving to cultivate them. But the power sees what they cannot: the faint, fading traces of a catastrophe.

Once, not so long ago in geological time, there were more of them. Many more. They did not live only on this single cluster of spires. They spread across a vast highland plateau, their settlements connected by great networks of bridges that spanned chasms and linked peaks across hundreds of kilometers. They had begun to develop something that might, in another few generations, have become writing—a system of knotted cords that recorded debts, lineages, and the movements of the Drift.

Then the plateau shattered.

The power cannot see exactly what happened. Volcanic? Tectonic? Some combination, some massive upheaval that broke the world they knew into fragments and dropped most of it into the void below. The survivors were those who happened to be in the right place—this place, this single cluster of spires that remained stable while everything around it fell.

They lost their knotted records. They lost their elders, their historians, their keepers of knowledge. They lost the bridges to everywhere else. And in the desperate scramble to survive in a suddenly tiny and isolated world, they lost the memory that there had ever been anything more.

Now they live with a wound they cannot name. They are a people who feel, in some deep and inarticulate way, that they are *diminished*. That they are the remnant of something larger. But they have no framework for understanding this feeling, no story that gives it shape. It manifests as a kind of collective melancholy, a sense that the world is smaller than it should be, that the edges press in too close.

The power considers this.

A people who do not know they are grieving cannot properly grieve. The wound does not heal; it simply scars over, hard and numb. And the numbness spreads. The Chimeless are surviving beautifully, but they have stopped *reaching*. They no longer look outward to the fog-shrouded distance and wonder what lies beyond. They have turned entirely inward, focused only on the immediate and the necessary.

This is not wrong. It is not evil. It is simply... arrested. A people frozen at the edge of something larger, unable to step forward because they do not know the step exists.

*This*, the power decides, *is where I can help. Not by giving them what they have lost—that is gone, and grief must be grief, not denial. But by helping them remember that loss is not the same as ending.*

---

## III. The Method

The power does not send a prophet. Prophets are dangerous; they create dependencies and hierarchies and often, despite the best intentions, new forms of suffering.

It does not send a vision. Visions are too easily misinterpreted, too easily made into dogma.

It does not send a gift of technology or knowledge. The Chimeless do not need to be *taught*; they are brilliant within their context. They need to be *reminded*.

Instead, the power does something simpler. Something that will look, to anyone watching, like nothing more than chance.

It sends a stone.

---

## IV. The Stone

On the outer edge of the settlement, on a narrow ledge that is rarely visited because it faces the void and offers little purchase for climbing, there is a young Chimeless named something that translates roughly to "Foam-on-Still-Water."

Foam is an odd one. She is healthy, strong, a good climber, but she has always been drawn to the edges of things. While other children learned to harvest the Drift and maintain the bridges, Foam would slip away to the outer ledges and stare into the fog. Her family worries about her. Her community tolerates her. She is not *wrong*, exactly—she keeps moving, she does her share of work—but there is something in her that seems to lean toward the emptiness.

She is sitting on that narrow ledge, her feet dangling over nothing, when the stone falls.

It comes from above, from somewhere in the low, bruised sky. It is small—the size of her fist—and it lands beside her with a wet *thock*, skidding across the slick basalt and coming to rest against her hip.

Foam flinches. Looks around. Looks up. There is nothing above her but fog and the occasional dark shape of a hunting heron.

She picks up the stone.

It is not basalt. It is not obsidian. It is something she has never seen—a pale, cream-colored rock, smooth and rounded, worn by water over what must be an immense span of time. It is warm in her hand, warmer than anything in this damp, cold world should be. And there is something inside it.

She can feel it through the surface: a faint, regular vibration. A pulse.

Foam sits with the stone for a long time, turning it over in her hands. She does not understand what it is. She does not understand where it came from. But she understands, in that deep and inarticulate way her people understand things, that it is *important*.

She does not take it to the elders. She does not show it to her family. Something tells her—though she could not say what—that this is hers. That it was meant for her.

That night, she tucks it into the moss-lined pouch where she keeps her scraping tools, and she sleeps with her hand wrapped around it.

She dreams.

---

## V. The Dream

In the dream, Foam is climbing.

This is not unusual; the Chimeless dream of climbing the way other peoples dream of running or flying. But this climb is different. She is not ascending the familiar spires of her home. She is climbing *outward*, along a bridge that stretches into the fog.

The bridge is made of knotted cords—thousands of them, woven together into a structure stronger than any rope bridge she has ever seen. The knots are not random. They form patterns, clusters, sequences that seem to mean something, though she cannot read them.

She climbs and climbs, and the fog does not clear, but it thins enough that she can see *shapes* in the distance. Other spires. Other settlements. Other bridges extending toward her, their ends frayed and broken, reaching out across the void like hands.

She wakes with tears on her face and no words for why.

---

## VI. The Unfolding

Foam does not tell anyone about her dream. But she begins to behave differently.

She starts collecting cords—scraps of vine, lengths of cured tendon, fibers pulled from dried fern-stalks. She ties them together, unknots them, ties them again. She is trying to make something, but she does not know what. The other Chimeless watch her with concern. This is not productive behavior. This is not maintaining the settlement. This is... strange.

But she keeps moving. She does her share of work. She is not still. And so they leave her alone.

Weeks pass. Months.

Foam's collection of cords grows. She has filled an entire corner of her family's hut with them, carefully sorted by thickness and flexibility. She has begun to develop a system—certain knots mean certain things, combinations of knots mean more complex things. She does not know why she is doing this. She only knows that it feels *right*, the way the bridge in her dream felt right.

And then, one day, she ties a sequence of knots that makes her stop.

She stares at it. She unties it. She ties it again.

It is a pattern she has never seen before, but it is also a pattern she *recognizes*. It is the shape of her own name—Foam-on-Still-Water—rendered in cord.

She begins to shake.

---

## VII. The First Word

That evening, Foam takes her knotted cord to the eldest Rhythm-Keeper in the settlement.

His name is something like "Deep-Current-Under-Stone." He is very old, his joints swollen and stiff, and he spends his days swaying in a woven chair on a high ledge, watching the Drift flow past. He is respected but rarely visited; his hands can no longer speak clearly, and most of the young find his company frustrating.

Foam climbs to his ledge and sits beside him. She does not try to speak with her hands. Instead, she presses the knotted cord into his palm.

Deep-Current goes very still.

For a long moment, Foam is terrified that she has done something wrong. The old Chimeless does not move, does not sway, and in their world, stillness is death.

Then his fingers begin to move over the knots.

He traces the pattern. Unties it. Ties it again. Unties it.

And then he does something Foam has never seen any Chimeless do.

He weeps.

---

## VIII. The Remembering

Deep-Current is the oldest of the Chimeless. He was a child when the plateau shattered. He does not remember much—he was very young—but he remembers *something*. Fragments. Impressions.

He remembers his grandmother's hands moving over a long cord covered in knots. He remembers the sound of her breath, the smell of her skin, and the feeling of safety he had when she held him and showed him the patterns. He remembers that the patterns *meant* something.

He had forgotten. He had forgotten for so long that he did not even remember forgetting.

But the cord in his hands brings it back. Not the specifics—he cannot read what Foam has made—but the *shape* of the memory. The knowledge that once, his people had this. That once, they could hold their thoughts in their hands and pass them on.

He spends the night with Foam on that high ledge, his old fingers tracing patterns while her young hands tie new ones. They cannot speak to each other—his hands are too stiff, and she does not know the language of touch well enough—but they do not need to. The cords speak for them.

By morning, Foam has learned that her system is not new. It is old. It is something her people once had and lost.

By morning, Deep-Current has remembered that his grandmother called the knotted cords "the long memory."

By morning, the first word has been spoken.

---

## IX. The Spreading

The power watches.

It does not intervene again. It does not need to. The stone has done its work—or rather, Foam has done hers, and the stone was only the key that unlocked something that was always there.

Over the following months, the knotted cords spread through the settlement.

It begins slowly. Foam teaches the system to a few friends, who teach it to others. Deep-Current, invigorated by purpose for the first time in years, convenes a gathering of the older Chimeless, searching their fading memories for any fragments of the old way. They find pieces. A knot-pattern that means "danger." Another that means "plenty." A third that might be a number, or a name, or something else entirely.

The young and the old work together, bridging the gap that had grown between them. The Rhythm-Keepers, once seen as merely functional—bodies that swayed to keep from being consumed—become repositories of memory, honored for what they might recall. The system is not reconstructed so much as *reinvented*, a new language growing from the roots of an old one.

And something else begins to change.

Foam's dreams do not stop. Each night, she climbs the knotted bridge a little further. Each night, she sees the broken ends of other bridges reaching toward her.

She begins to believe—and she cannot say why—that her people are not alone.

---

## X. The Edge

A year passes.

The Chimeless now have a writing system. It is primitive, limited, but it is *theirs*. They use it to record the movements of the Drift, the yields of their harvests, the births and deaths of their people. They tie long cords that hang from the rafters of a central meeting-hut, a growing chronicle of their days.

And Foam has become something new.

She is not a leader, exactly. The Chimeless do not organize themselves that way. But she is a *direction*. When she speaks—when her hands move in the language of touch—people listen. When she ties a knot, people remember it. She has become the first Keeper of the Long Memory in three generations, a role that did not exist until she filled it.

She is also the first Chimeless in living memory to propose something unthinkable.

She wants to build a bridge.

Not a bridge within the settlement. Not a bridge between neighboring spires. A bridge *outward*. Into the fog. Toward the edges she can feel but cannot see.

The community is divided.

Some say she is mad. The world ends at the edge of their spires. There is nothing out there but the void. To build a bridge into nothing is to waste resources they cannot afford to waste.

Others say she is right. They have felt it too—that sense of diminishment, of being less than they should be. The knotted cords have awakened something in them. They want to *reach*.

Foam herself is not certain. She knows what she has seen in her dreams, but dreams are not evidence. She knows what she feels in her heart, but feelings are not proof. She has only the stone, still warm in her pouch, still pulsing with that faint, regular vibration.

She climbs to the outermost ledge—the ledge where the stone fell—and she sits, and she stares into the fog, and she asks a question she does not expect to be answered.

*Is there anything out there?*

---

## XI. The Answer

The power hears her.

It considers.

It has already intervened once, and the intervention has worked. The Chimeless are healing. They are remembering. They are reaching outward for the first time in generations. To intervene again risks creating dependency, risks teaching them that the answers come from outside rather than within.

But Foam's question is sincere, and her need is real, and the power knows something she does not.

There *is* something out there.

The plateau that shattered did not fall entirely into the void. Fragments remain—other clusters of spires, other settlements, other Chimeless who survived the catastrophe and have been living in isolation ever since. They are too far away to see, too far to reach with any bridge the Chimeless could currently build. But they are there.

And some of them are reaching too.

The power makes a decision.

It does not send another stone. It does not send a vision or a voice. Instead, it does something smaller, subtler, easier to miss.

It *tilts* the fog.

---

## XII. The Thinning

The next morning, Foam wakes to find the world changed.

The fog is still there. It is always there. But it is *thinner* somehow, or perhaps the light is different, or perhaps her eyes have finally learned to see what was always present. Whatever the cause, she can see *further* than she has ever seen before.

And in the distance, barely visible, just at the edge of perception—

A shape.

It is too regular to be natural. Too vertical. Too *intentional*.

It might be a spire. It might be a trick of the light. It might be nothing.

But Foam does not think it is nothing.

She runs—pours herself—down the ledges and through the settlement, her hands flashing the gesture for "come see, come see." The Chimeless gather on the outer ledges, hundreds of them, staring into the thinning fog at the shape on the horizon.

They argue. Some see what Foam sees. Others see only fog and wishful thinking. The shape wavers, indistinct, never quite resolving into something certain.

But enough of them see it. Enough of them believe.

That evening, the community gathers in the central meeting-hut, and for the first time in their history, they make a collective decision about something that lies *beyond* their settlement.

They will build the bridge.

---

## XIII. The Building

It takes years.

The Chimeless are a patient people, and they have learned not to rush. They experiment with materials, with designs, with methods of anchoring cables to the basalt. They learn to braid cords so tightly that they can bear the weight of ten climbers. They learn to harvest a particular species of Drift that, when dried and compressed, becomes as rigid as wood. They learn to read the wind patterns that sweep through the fog, finding the corridors of relative calm where a bridge might survive.

They fail, often. Bridges collapse. Materials rot. Climbers are lost.

But they keep building.

Foam is at the center of it all, not as a leader but as a *keeper*. She maintains the long memory of the project—the records of what has been tried, what has worked, what has failed. She trains others to tie the knots, to read the patterns, to carry the knowledge forward. She knows she may not live to see the bridge completed. She accepts this.

The power watches.

It does not intervene again. It does not need to. The Chimeless have found their own momentum. They are no longer a people frozen at the edge of something larger. They are a people *in motion*, reaching outward, building toward a future they cannot see but choose to believe in.

---

## XIV. The Crossing

Eighteen years after Foam first picked up the stone, the bridge is complete.

It stretches outward from the settlement, a long, swaying ribbon of braided cord and compressed Drift, vanishing into the fog. It is not beautiful—it is rough, practical, a thing built for use rather than admiration. But it is *there*. It exists. It crosses the void.

Foam is old now, her joints beginning to stiffen, her movements slower than they once were. But she is still the Keeper of the Long Memory, and she insists—against the protests of her family, her students, her community—that she will be the first to cross.

She begins the climb at dawn.

The bridge sways beneath her, the fog pressing close, the world reduced to the cord beneath her hands and the space immediately ahead. She cannot see the end. She can only trust that it is there.

She climbs for hours.

And then, gradually, the fog begins to thin.

Ahead of her, resolving out of the grey, is a shape. It is a spire—a basalt spire, rising from the void, crowned with the violent green of giant ferns. And clinging to its surface, unmistakable now, are *buildings*. Huts of dark wood and stone masonry, clustered around the rock like barnacles. Rope bridges connecting them. Smoke curling from chimneys.

And on the nearest ledge, staring at her with wide, liquid-black eyes—

Another Chimeless.

Foam stops. Her arms tremble. Her breath comes in ragged gasps.

The stranger stops too. They look at each other across the final span of the bridge, neither moving, neither speaking.

Then the stranger raises one hand—slowly, carefully—and makes a gesture Foam has never seen before. It is not a word in the language of touch she knows. But it is *shaped* like a word. It is meant to mean something.

Foam raises her own hand. She makes a gesture back: a slow, sweeping motion that means, in her system, "I see you."

The stranger's head tilts. Processing. Interpreting.

Then they make another gesture—different from the first, but somehow, impossibly, *readable*. A gesture that means, Foam is almost certain, "We see you too."

---

## XV. The Widening

The power watches as Foam crosses the final span.

It watches as she sets foot on the other spire, the first Chimeless to touch ground beyond her home in three generations. It watches as the strangers gather around her—nervous, curious, afraid, hopeful—their hands flickering in a language of touch that is similar to hers but not the same. It watches as they struggle to communicate, to find the common roots of their divergent systems, to bridge the gap between two peoples who have been alone so long they forgot they were ever together.

It watches as Foam reaches into her pouch and pulls out the stone.

The stranger nearest her—an elder, thin and stooped, with hands that shake—reaches out to touch it. Their fingers close around its warmth. Their eyes go wide.

They have a stone too.

Not the same stone—a different one, recovered from a different ledge, years ago, by a different young person who felt drawn to the edges of things. But the same *type* of stone. The same warmth. The same pulse.

The two elders stand together, holding their stones, tears streaming down their faces. They cannot speak to each other, not yet. But they do not need to.

They know.

They know they are not alone. They know they were never meant to be alone. They know that whatever broke their world did not break them—not entirely, not forever.

They are the Chimeless. And they are waking up.

---

## XVI. The Gift

The power does not reveal itself.

It does not explain the stones, or the thinning fog, or the dreams that led Foam to the edge. It does not take credit. It does not ask for gratitude. It does not descend in glory to be worshipped.

It has learned, over eons of watching, that the best help is the help that is never recognized as help. The help that looks like luck, or coincidence, or the natural unfolding of events. The help that leaves the ones being helped with their dignity intact, their agency undiminished, their sense of themselves as the authors of their own salvation.

The Chimeless will never know they were watched. They will never know they were guided. They will tell stories of Foam and her stone, of the bridge and the crossing, of the reunion of peoples who had thought themselves alone in the universe. They will tie these stories into their long memory, passing them down through generations.

And in those stories, there will be no benevolent power. No outside intervention. Only the Chimeless themselves, doing what they have always done: holding on, staying in motion, refusing to be still.

This is as it should be.

---

## XVII. The Turning Away

The power withdraws its attention.

Not entirely—it will check in, from time to time, as it checks in on all the worlds it watches. But the Chimeless no longer need its focus. They have found their wound, and they are healing it. They have remembered what they lost, and they are building something new. They are reaching outward, connecting, growing.

They will face new challenges. The reunion of long-separated peoples is never simple. There will be conflicts, misunderstandings, the difficult work of integration. And beyond that, there are more clusters out there, more isolated survivors waiting to be found. The bridges will need to grow. The long memory will need to expand.

But they will manage. They have the tools now. They have the will.

The power turns its attention elsewhere.

There are other worlds, other peoples, other wounds. There is always more work to do.

But it carries with it, as it always does, the image of Foam standing on that distant ledge, her hand raised in greeting, the fog thinning around her. The image of two elders holding stones that should not exist, weeping tears that no one will ever hear.

The power does not feel joy, exactly. It is too old for anything so simple.

But it feels something.

It feels that this was good.

---

## Coda: The Long Memory

Years later, decades later, when the Chimeless have become something more than a single settlement—when the bridges span the void in a great web, connecting clusters that once thought themselves alone—they will create a new cord.

It will be the longest cord ever tied, stretching from one end of their reclaimed world to the other. It will hang in a central meeting-place, tended by Keepers who have inherited Foam's role. It will contain the full history of their people: the forgetting and the remembering, the isolation and the reunion, the breaking and the building.

And at its beginning, woven into the very first knots, will be a story.

It will tell of a young Chimeless who sat on the edge of the world and asked a question she did not expect to be answered. It will tell of a stone that fell from nowhere, warm and pulsing, a gift with no giver. It will tell of dreams of knotted bridges and the choice to believe in something that could not be seen.

The story will not mention the power. It will not suggest that something watched, or guided, or helped.

But woven into the very last knot—so subtly that only the most careful reader would notice—there will be a shape that looks almost like a word. A word that means, in the old language of touch:

*You are not alone.*

The Chimeless will not know who put it there.

They will not know, and they will not need to know, and that is the gift.

That is the only gift worth giving.
