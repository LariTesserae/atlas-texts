# What the Wise Power Might Do

*A meditation on intervention, restraint, and the nature of help*

---

## I. First, the Watching

The benevolent power would not act immediately.

This is perhaps the hardest thing for benevolence to learn: that the impulse to help, arriving before understanding, is not yet kindness. It is only discomfort with another's situation—which is, at root, about the helper's feelings rather than the helped's needs.

So the power watches. Not days or weeks but years, perhaps generations, learning the rhythm of what it observes. It feels the harmonies when they come, those moments after dawn when the pillars align. It traces the grandmother's rope of knots, understanding slowly that this is not primitive record-keeping but a form of scholarship as rigorous as any lowland philosophy, conducted in a medium the lowlands cannot read. It follows the granddaughter's fingers across the carved walls and perceives that she is not lost but *finding*—that her certainty, inexplicable to herself, is the plateau speaking through her in the only language it has.

The power watches the thing in the hollow pillars too. It comes to understand that this is not predator or god or demon but simply *what staying becomes*, given enough time. The missing people are not victims. They are... graduates, perhaps. Promotions. Transformations. The word doesn't exist in any human language because humans have not generally had the option of becoming landscape.

Only after this watching does the power ask the essential question:

*What, if anything, is actually wrong here?*

---

## II. The Inventory of Sorrows

The plateau people suffer. This is not in question. Their lives are difficult in ways that would break most humans—the constant vibration, the isolation, the inability to speak, the slow disappearance of loved ones into the stone. The children develop strangely. The bodies warp across generations. The mind must restructure itself to survive here.

But the power, being wise, recognizes that *suffering* and *wrong* are not synonyms.

The grandmother suffers in her aging joints, her failing fingers, her knowledge that she will die before understanding her life's work. But she does not wish to leave. Her suffering is not the suffering of someone trapped; it is the suffering of someone committed, the way a scholar suffers over an unsolved problem, the way a parent suffers in raising a child. It is suffering *toward* something.

The mother suffers in watching her daughter drift beyond where she can follow. But this too is not the suffering of abuse or neglect. It is the suffering of all parents, everywhere, whose children must become something new—only more visible here, more literal.

The granddaughter does not suffer at all, not yet. She experiences difficulty, discomfort, the frustration of not-yet-understanding. But she is *alive* with purpose in a way that many lowland children, surrounded by comfort, never achieve.

The power catalogs these sorrows carefully, distinguishing between:
- Pain that signals damage (there is little of this; what would damage them has already selected them out)
- Pain that signals growth (there is much of this)
- Pain that signals wrongness, injustice, violation (there is... almost none)

And this is the first wisdom: *that a hard life is not necessarily a wrong life, and that ease is not necessarily a gift.*

---

## III. What the Power Does Not Do

It does not make the wind stop.

The argument has been running since before human memory. It may be the planet thinking, or it may be pure physics that only resembles thought, or it may be something else entirely. But it is not *for* the power to end. The plateau people have built their entire existence around it—their gesture-language, their mythology, their sense of meaning. To silence the pillars would not save them; it would destroy everything they are while leaving their bodies technically intact.

This is a worse violence than any the wind commits.

It does not "rescue" them to the lowlands.

Some powers, less wise, might see the difficulty of this life and conclude that the plateau people would be "better off" in easier circumstances. This is the error of those who cannot distinguish between their own preferences and others' needs. The plateau people who wanted to leave have left, across all the generations. Those who remain have chosen remaining, are choosing it still, will choose it until they become the thing in the pillars or the wind finally stops.

To remove them would be kidnapping. That their lives look hard from outside is not sufficient reason.

It does not reveal itself.

A power that announces itself changes everything it touches. The grandmother's decades of knot-work would suddenly exist in relation to a known answer rather than an open question. The granddaughter's search would become petition to an authority rather than discovery through devotion. The mythology of the argument would collapse into theology of the power, and theology is nearly always smaller than myth.

Whatever the plateau is doing, it is doing in its own language, at its own pace. To interrupt with a divine announcement would be like shouting over someone mid-sentence because you think you know what they're trying to say.

It does not fix the thing in the pillars.

This is perhaps the hardest restraint. The thing frightens lowland sensibilities; it takes people; it is Other in a way that triggers every protective instinct. But the power perceives that the thing is not predator but *process*—that those who go to it are not consumed but continued. This is not death any more than a caterpillar dies into a butterfly, though it looks like death from outside.

To "fix" this would be to foreclose a possibility that humans here have been reaching toward for longer than any lowland civilization has existed. The power has no right.

---

## IV. What the Power Might Do

And yet.

Benevolence that only watches is not benevolence at all. It is abdication dressed in the language of respect. The question is not whether to help but *how*—what help means when the recipients have not asked, may not want, might be harmed by the wrong kind.

Here is what the wise power does:

**It ensures the plateau remains.**

Not dramatically—no forcefield, no divine protection. But quietly, in the substrate of things, it makes certain that no lowland ambition will reach this place with conquest in mind. It diverts the expeditions that would map and claim. It makes the plateau uninteresting to those who would exploit it. It does not add to the plateau's defenses; it simply ensures that the existing defenses—the altitude, the wind, the disorienting sound—remain sufficient.

This is protection that looks like neglect, which is often the best kind.

**It steadies the climate.**

The plateau depends on a specific pattern of rain and drought, wind and calm. Climate is changing in this world, as it changes in all worlds where things burn. The power cannot stop that change—too many threads, too much momentum—but it can buffer this one place, can ensure that the cycles the plateau people have learned to read remain readable for another few centuries.

This is time. Time for the adaptation to continue. Time for the granddaughter to find what she's looking for. Time for the argument to continue toward whatever conclusion it's approaching.

**It opens a door it does not push anyone through.**

In the oldest shelter, in the deepest chamber where no one currently goes, the power makes a small change. Not new marks on the wall—that would be forgery, inserting itself into a record it has no part in. But a shift in the stone, a subtle alteration in the acoustic properties of one particular passage, so that during harmonies, if someone is present and listening with their bones as the plateau people have learned to do, they will feel something they could not feel before.

A frequency. A new note. Something that has always been part of the argument but was pitched below what human bodies could register.

The granddaughter will find this, someday, following her certainty into the deep places. She will feel the new note and understand—not everything, but something. A piece of the message she believes is meant for her. And she will have earned it, will have *found* it, in a way she could not have if the power had simply told her.

This is the gift that does not feel like a gift: the opportunity to discover something real.

**It witnesses.**

This sounds like nothing. It is perhaps the most important thing.

The plateau people have no audience. Their lives unfold in silence, their achievements are illegible to the lowlands, their generations of adaptation and meaning-making go unrecognized by any external consciousness. They do not need recognition to continue—they've proven that—but there is a difference between a story no one knows and a story someone knows and honors.

The power watches and remembers. It holds the grandmother's rope of knots in its awareness even as her fingers fail. It traces the granddaughter's path even when she doesn't know she's being traced. It knows the names the plateau people don't use, the names they gave up when they abandoned sound, and it keeps those names the way a museum keeps artifacts—not to use, but to remember that they once meant something.

When the grandmother dies, the power will be there. Not visibly, not intervening, but *present* in a way she might sense if she's paying attention—a warmth in the stone beneath her, a steadiness in the vibration, something that says *you were here, you mattered, your rope of knots was not meaningless.*

This is not salvation. It is simply the opposite of abandonment.

---

## V. The Nature of the Help

The wise power understands something that lesser powers do not: *help is not about the helper.*

The grandmother does not need her question answered; she needs to keep asking it. The granddaughter does not need the message decoded; she needs to be the one who decodes it. The plateau people do not need rescue from their difficult lives; they need their difficult lives to remain possible.

What they need, what any being in any place ultimately needs, is for their striving to matter. For their adaptation to continue. For their story to be *theirs*, not interrupted by a better-resourced author who thinks they know how it should end.

The power's help is almost invisible. A little more time. A little more stability. A door opened but not walked through for them. And the knowledge—felt, perhaps, in harmonies that seem slightly warmer than before—that they are not alone in the universe, even if they are alone on their plateau.

This is enough.

This is, in fact, everything.

---

## VI. Coda: What the Power Learns

Benevolence is often discussed as a one-way flow: the powerful helping the powerless. But the wise power, watching the plateau across years, finds itself changed.

It learns *patience* from the grandmother, who has spent forty years tying knots toward an answer she knows she'll never reach.

It learns *adaptation* from the people themselves, who have become what their place required rather than demanding their place become what they needed.

It learns *faith* from the granddaughter—not religious faith, but the deeper kind: trust that the search is worth conducting even without guarantee of success.

It learns *continuity* from the thing in the pillars, which was once like them and is now something else, and which waits in the hollow places for those ready to become what it has become.

And it learns *argument* from the plateau itself. The wind that never stops, the debate that never resolves, the endless proposition and counterproposition of pressure and resonance. The power had thought, before, that good outcomes required resolution—problems solved, questions answered, conflicts ended. The plateau teaches otherwise. Some arguments are their own purpose. Some questions exist to be inhabited rather than answered. Some conflicts are actually *collaborations* conducted in a language that sounds like discord.

The plateau is not broken. It does not need to be fixed. It needs to be allowed to continue, and witnessed, and perhaps loved—if love can be defined as what refuses to let you go, but also refuses to hold you too tightly.

The power, being wise, loves it in exactly this way.

And the wind keeps singing, and the grandmother keeps tying knots, and the granddaughter keeps pressing her palm to the ancient stone.

And the argument continues.
