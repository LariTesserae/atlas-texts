# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power pauses at the edge of perception, taking in the plateau and the pika together—the ancient stone, the tireless wind, the small furred body emerging between gusts to cut another mouthful of dried grass.

And it does nothing.

Not from indifference. Not from inability. But from recognition.

---

The power understands something that intervention would obscure: the pika is not suffering in a way that needs correction. It is *living*—fully, completely, in the only way a pika can live. Its days are not a problem to be solved but a form to be inhabited. The haypile is not a burden but a purpose. The narrow window between gusts is not a limitation but the exact shape of the pika's existence, fitted to it as precisely as its dense fur is fitted to the cold.

To change this would be to answer a question no one asked.

The power could soften the wind. Could make the grass grow thicker in the cracks. Could turn away the hawk mid-dive, spare this particular pika for another season. But each of these kindnesses would be a distortion—would say, in effect, *this is not enough, you are not enough, let me make you into something else.* And the pika does not experience itself as insufficient. It experiences itself as a pika on a plateau, doing what pikas do, which is to say: cutting, carrying, calling, surviving or not surviving, being exactly what it is.

The power sees this and is moved—not to action, but to attention.

---

So instead of intervening, the power *witnesses*.

It watches the pika emerge in a moment of stillness, its dark wet eyes scanning the stone. It watches the small legs blur into motion, the grass stem lifted delicately in the teeth, the return to the crevice where the haypile grows by increments too small to measure. It watches the hawk circle and does not interfere. It watches the frost form on the pools as the season turns, watches the snow seal the burrow entrance, watches the darkness settle over the plateau like a held breath.

The power holds all of this—the whole pattern, the ten thousand generations of pikas who have lived and died on this stone, the wind that has blown since before there were pikas, the equilibrium that needs no correction because it is not broken.

This is the first gift: to be seen completely, without judgment, without the subtle violence of *improvement*.

---

But the power is not only an observer. It is also, in some sense, a presence—and presence has effects that are not the same as intervention.

When the pika sits on its stone in the brief still, there is a quality to the light that it cannot name but that it feels: a sense of being *held*, of mattering to something larger than the haypile, larger than the territory, larger than the hawk's shadow passing overhead. The pika does not think in these terms. It cannot. But something in its small body relaxes by a fraction, some tension it did not know it was carrying eases, and it sits a moment longer than it might have, watching the sky, before the wind returns and it moves again.

This is the second gift: companionship that does not intrude. The power is *with* the pika the way the stone is with the pika—not demanding anything, not requiring acknowledgment, simply present in the fabric of things.

---

There is a third gift, subtler than the others.

The power knows what the pika cannot know: that this plateau, this wind, this small life measured in haypiles and gusts, is not a diminished version of something better. It is not a trial to be endured until some richer existence becomes available. It is *complete*. The pika's life lacks nothing that a pika's life requires. The margin is thin, yes—but thinness is not poverty. The window is narrow, yes—but narrowness is not deprivation.

The power holds this knowledge the way it holds everything else: lightly, without imposing it. But the knowledge has a weight of its own, and that weight settles into the plateau like dew settling into the stone. The place becomes, in some way that cannot be measured, *blessed*—not transformed, not elevated, but recognized for what it already is.

The pika, cutting grass in a moment of stillness, is not aware of this blessing. It does not need to be. The blessing is not for the pika's awareness; it is for the pika's existence. It says: *you are real, you are here, you are enough.*

---

When the winter comes—when the snow seals the burrow and the pika descends into the darkness to eat from its haypile and wait—the power waits too.

It does not make the winter shorter. It does not make the haypile larger. It does not guarantee that this particular pika will emerge in the spring, blinking in the light, ready to begin again.

It simply stays.

And in the staying, in the patient attention that asks nothing and demands nothing, there is something that might be called love—though the word is too small for it, too human, too shaped by needs the power does not have. Call it *regard*. Call it *honoring*. Call it the recognition that existence itself is worthy of witness, that a small life lived fully in a thin margin is not less than a large life lived in abundance, that the pika and the plateau and the wind are all expressions of something that does not need to be improved because it was never broken.

---

This is what the benevolent and wise power does:

It lets the plateau be a plateau.
It lets the pika be a pika.
It watches, and it stays, and it does not look away.

And in the not-looking-away, in the simple fidelity of attention, it offers the only gift that does not distort: the gift of being seen as one is, in the place where one is, doing what one does, for as long as one does it.

The wind continues. The pika continues. The power continues.

Nothing changes.

Everything is held.
