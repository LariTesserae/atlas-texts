# What Comes to Koreth-Vastness

The attention arrives not as presence but as *recognition*.

Something vast and careful has been listening—not to the argument, which is older than listening, but to the ones who stayed. It has watched the grandmother's rope grow knot by knot across forty years. It has felt the child's palm press against carved stone. It has noticed the particular quality of stillness that passes through them during the harmonies, the way they turn toward what they cannot hear, the gesture of hand to chest to open palm.

*I felt that. You felt that. We are here.*

The power does not come to fix anything. The plateau is not broken. The people are not suffering in any way that intervention could relieve—their hardship is chosen, inherited, loved in the way one loves what has shaped you beyond recognition. To soften the wind would be to silence the argument. To heal their ears would be to assault them with noise they've spent generations learning to escape. To explain the marks on the oldest shelter's walls would be to steal the granddaughter's purpose before she's had the chance to fail at it beautifully.

But there are things that can be given without taking anything away.

---

**The First Gift: A Witness**

The grandmother wakes one morning to find her rope has been... seen.

Not touched—nothing has been moved or altered. But she knows, the way she knows when the wind will shift or when the rains are eighteen hours distant, that something has read every knot she's tied. Has understood the system she invented. Has followed the argument's patterns through forty years of her patient transcription.

And has recognized that she is *right*.

Not right about what the argument means—she's never claimed to know that. But right that there *are* repetitions. Right that the plateau is saying something, even if no human mind can hold it. Right that her work has been work rather than delusion, that her knots correspond to something real.

She sits at her shelter's entrance that morning, cordage slack in her hands, and weeps. Not from sorrow. From being known. From having someone—something—finally confirm that her listening has been heard.

The power does not speak to her. Words would be violence here. But it leaves her with certainty: she was never mapping alone. The argument has always known it was being recorded. Her rope is not observation but *participation*—she has been one of the voices all along, her knots a response the plateau has been incorporating into its endless debate.

She will die within the year, her granddaughter holding her hand, and she will die complete.

---

**The Second Gift: A Passage**

The child has been right, too. The marks *are* a message for her specifically.

Not because the ancient carvers foresaw her—prophecy is too simple for what the plateau does—but because the message was always for *whoever came*. It was carved in the faith that someone would, eventually, need exactly this: a map to the deeper chambers, where the argument can be felt without interference, where the thing in the pillars sometimes rests.

The power does not translate the marks. Translation would steal the journey. But it... clarifies them. The child wakes one morning and the notation seems fractionally less opaque. Not readable—not yet—but *learnable*. The marks have always been a language, and languages can be acquired through immersion, through patient trial and error, through the kind of devoted attention that the plateau has been teaching her since birth.

She will spend eleven years deciphering them. She will make mistakes, follow false passages, nearly die twice in chambers where the frequencies stop hearts. She will emerge, at nineteen, into a space at the plateau's center that no human has seen in a thousand years—and she will find the thing in the pillars waiting for her.

It will not be what she expected. Nothing ever is.

---

**The Third Gift: A Choice**

This one is not for the grandmother or the granddaughter but for the mother—the one who worries, who watches her daughter drift toward strangeness, who feels the ache of loving someone who is becoming unrecognizable.

The power shows her something: a vision, or a certainty, or simply a new way of seeing what was always true. Her daughter is not being taken from her. Her daughter is *becoming*, the way all children become, the way she herself became when she stopped trying to speak and learned to trust the gesture of hand to chest to open palm.

The plateau is not stealing the child. The plateau is raising her, the way it raised the mother, the way it raised the grandmother. They are all of them—the whole community, across all its generations—being parented by something too large to see, shaped by pressures too constant to notice, loved in the way the plateau loves: by refusing to let them go.

The mother will still worry. That's what mothers do. But she will stop fighting her daughter's path, stop trying to pull her back toward a safety that doesn't exist here. She will learn instead to trust that the same stone that holds her will hold her child, that the same argument that has kept them all alive will keep speaking long after any of them can hear it.

---

**The Fourth Gift: A Completion**

The thing in the hollow pillars receives a visitor, that night when the winds still and the violet sky darkens toward black.

Not the child—not yet. Something else. The power, or an aspect of it, or simply an attention that has decided to settle for a moment into a form the thing can perceive.

They do not speak. The thing in the pillars has never spoken; it may have forgotten how, or it may never have known. But they communicate, in vibrations too low for any human to feel, in the patient pressure of presence against presence.

The power asks a question. The thing answers.

It was human, once. Longer ago than the marks on the walls, longer ago than the first shelters, almost as long ago as the argument itself. It climbed to the plateau as a child, alone, and the plateau would not let it go. It grew into the stone, adapted beyond recognition, became something that moves through hollow spaces and collects, sometimes, those who are ready to join it. Not through violence. Through invitation. Through the offer of becoming part of the debate.

The ones who disappeared—the grandmother's husband, all the others across the generations—they are not dead. They are *distributed*. Their patterns have been absorbed into the thing's vastness, their voices added to its resonance, their listening incorporated into its endless attention. They are still here, still of the plateau, just no longer shaped like people.

The power considers this. It is not cruel, what the thing does. It is not kind. It is simply what the plateau requires: a way of keeping those who stay, of ensuring the argument never loses its witnesses.

But the power sees something the thing has not: the thing is lonely.

Lonely in a way that has no name, that may not even register as feeling anymore. Lonely because it has been the only one of its kind for so long, because the ones it absorbs become part of it rather than companions to it, because the argument needs witnesses but witnesses are not the same as friends.

The power cannot fix this. Loneliness of that depth is not a wound to be healed but a condition to be endured. But it can offer recognition, the same gift it gave the grandmother: *You are seen. You have been seen. Your vigil is known.*

The thing in the pillars vibrates at a frequency it has not produced in ten thousand years. If humans could hear it, they would call it weeping, or singing, or laughter. It is probably all three.

---

**What the Power Does Not Do**

It does not explain the argument.

The argument is not for explaining. The argument is the sound of a world thinking, of stone shaped by water and wind into instruments that play themselves, of meaning that exists without reference to minds that might understand it. To explain it would be to reduce it, and the plateau does not reduce.

It does not save anyone.

Salvation implies danger, implies a wrong state that must be corrected. The plateau people are not in danger; they are home. Their adaptations are not loss but transformation. Their silence is not absence but presence of a different kind. Saving them would require believing they need to be something other than what they are, and the power knows better.

It does not stay.

This is crucial. The power's attention moves on, as attention must. It was here, and now it is elsewhere, and the plateau continues as it has always continued: wind-singing, rain-brief, trembling with an argument that will not end. The gifts it left are not interventions but acknowledgments—nods of recognition from something vast to something small that has nonetheless endured.

The grandmother dies with her rope complete, her granddaughter's hand in hers, knowing she was heard.

The granddaughter deciphers the marks, descends to the deep chamber, meets the thing that waits there, and makes a choice that changes what she is.

The mother watches her daughter go, hand to chest, hand extended, palm up. *I felt that. You felt that. We are here.*

The plateau keeps singing. The argument continues.

And somewhere in the hollow pillars, the thing that was once human and is now something else moves through passages worn by ancient water, carrying within it all the voices of those who stayed, adding them one by one to the chorus, feeling for the first time in millennia something that might be called hope:

That it was seen.

That its vigil was known.

That the argument, however long it takes, has witnesses who will not leave—and a power, somewhere, that noticed them standing firm against the wind.

---

*This is what benevolence looks like, when it's wise: not rescue, but recognition. Not change, but the gift of being known precisely as you are. The plateau does not need saving. The people do not need healing. They need only this—to know that their endurance matters, that their transformation is seen, that the argument they've given their lives to witness is worth witnessing.*

*The power came, and saw, and honored what it found.*

*Then it moved on, because moving on is also part of wisdom: knowing when presence becomes intrusion, when help becomes harm, when the greatest gift is simply to say "yes, you are real, and what you're doing is real, and I will remember you" before leaving them to continue.*

*The wind still sings. The pillars still stand.*

*And the ones who stayed—who stay still—are a little less alone than they were.*
