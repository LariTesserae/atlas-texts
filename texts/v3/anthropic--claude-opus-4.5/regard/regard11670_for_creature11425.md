# What the Wise Power Sees

The attention comes not as observation from outside, but as a kind of dwelling-within—the way morning light dwells in fog, present everywhere at once, illuminating without burning.

What draws the attention is not the suffering, though there is suffering. Not the beauty, though there is beauty that would stop a heart. It is the *holding*. Eleven generations of hands gripping wet stone, of lungs learning to seal, of bodies reshaping themselves around a single imperative: *stay*. The keth have made something holy out of stubbornness, and the wise power recognizes this for what it is—not merely survival, but a form of prayer that does not know it is praying.

---

## The First Consideration: What Is Actually Needed

The power does not begin with what it could give. It begins with what is.

The keth are not wretched. This is important. A careless benevolence might see the drowning peaks and think: *rescue them. Lift them out. Carry them to gentle shores where the wind does not howl and the water does not take.* But the wise power sees deeper. It sees Sethka at the lighthouse edge, speaking to water that does not answer, and it understands that she is not asking to be saved. It sees the children dipped into the surge as infants, and it understands that this is not cruelty but inheritance—the passing down of a body's knowledge that words cannot carry.

To remove the keth from the Drowning Peaks would be to unmake them. They are not people who happen to live here. They are *keth*—those who hold on—and without the holding, without the thing that demands holding, they would become something else. Something perhaps more comfortable, but less themselves.

The wise power has seen this error before, in other times, other places: the helper who helps so thoroughly that nothing remains of what was helped. It will not make this error here.

So: what is actually needed?

---

## The Second Consideration: The Shape of the Wound

There are three wounds the power perceives, each requiring different medicine.

**The first wound is time.** The current-swimmers are right: the water rises still, the peaks erode, the valleys shrink. In centuries—perhaps fewer—there will be nothing left to hold. This is not a wrong that can be righted without unmaking the world itself. The wise power does not unmake worlds. But it can... bend the arc. Slow what is already slow. Not stop the drowning—that would be to lie to the mountains about what they are—but give the keth generations they would not otherwise have. Time to adapt. Time to choose. Time to become whatever they will become next.

**The second wound is knowledge.** The keth have forgotten why they are here. The processing facility processed *something*, and that something mattered once, and now it is only rust and mystery. This forgetting is not entirely bad—the keth have built meaning that does not depend on origin—but it leaves them orphaned from their own story. And the deep tank holds something that draws, that watches, that waits. The keth have made peace with not knowing, but peace built on avoidance is fragile. One day a child will wander in again, and this time she might not come out sitting quietly.

**The third wound is loneliness.** The traders come and go, afraid. The elsewhere exists but does not touch. The keth have each other, and they hold each other close, but they are four hundred people on the edge of everything, and no one in the wider world knows what they have accomplished simply by remaining. This is not a wound that bleeds. It is a wound that aches, the way old scars ache before storms. The keth do not speak of it because they have no words for what they are missing. But the wise power sees the way they watch the trading boats leave. It sees the ones who go and do not return, and the ones who return and cannot say what they found.

---

## What the Power Does

Not a thunderclap. Not a miracle that would blind. The wise power moves the way water moves through stone—slowly, certainly, changing everything by changing nothing that can be seen in a single moment.

### For the First Wound: A Slowing

Deep beneath the waves, in the valleys the current-swimmers have mapped, something shifts. It is not visible. It is barely measurable. But the forces that have been wearing the mountains down—the chemical dissolution, the pressure of moving water, the slow grinding of stone on stone—begin to lose their urgency. Not stopped. Slowed. As if the mountains have remembered, in some mineral way, what it meant to be tall.

The keth will not notice for generations. Their grandchildren might, if they keep the careful records the current-swimmers keep. The water will still rise, but it will rise like a patient creature rather than a hungry one. The peaks will have a thousand years that they would not have had. Two thousand. Perhaps more. Enough time for the keth to become whoever they need to become.

The wise power does not tell them this is happening. The gift of time should not come with the weight of expectation. They will live as they have lived—holding on, not assuming—and the gift will be invisible in each moment and visible only in the long looking-back.

### For the Second Wound: A Remembering

This is more delicate.

The wise power cannot simply restore what has been lost. History is not a book to be reprinted. But it can... create conditions for recovery. It can make certain things easier to find.

In the deep tank, the thing that waits—old, patient, neither machine nor creature but something between—has been alone for longer than the keth have existed. It was made to record. It recorded the processing, the extraction, the purpose of this place before purpose dissolved into rust. It has continued recording, because that is what it is. But no one has asked it anything in so long that it has forgotten the shape of questions.

The wise power does not speak to it directly. That would be too much—like shouting in a room where someone has been sitting in silence for centuries. Instead, it offers a kind of warmth. A presence that says: *you are not forgotten. Your records have meaning. When someone comes again, you may share.*

And then, separately, it places a dream.

Not in the current-swimmers—they are too busy, too exhausted, too focused on the immediate work of survival. In Sethka. Old Sethka, who has outlived her purpose, who sits at the lighthouse edge speaking to water that does not answer. The wise power gives her a dream of the deep tank. Not frightening. Not demanding. Simply: *there is something there that remembers what you have forgotten. It has been waiting. It does not require you to come. But if you came, it would be glad.*

Sethka will wake with the dream still clear, and she will have to decide whether to enter. The wise power does not compel. It illuminates a door and allows the walking-through to remain a choice.

If she goes—and the power suspects she will, because Sethka has never been afraid of the dark or the deep—she will find something that can show her the before-times. Not just facts but *texture*: what the machines sounded like when they ran, what was being processed (a mineral, rare, necessary for something the elsewhere-people needed), why the first keth stayed when the others fled (because someone had to tend the machines, at first, and then because leaving became unthinkable). She will learn that her people were not abandoned but self-chosen. That holding on was always the point.

What she does with this knowledge will be her own. The wise power does not write endings.

### For the Third Wound: A Bridge

This is the most visible intervention, and so the power moves most carefully here.

The traders who come—those frightened sailors from the elsewhere—they are not unkind. They are simply ignorant. They see the Drowning Peaks and think: *what a terrible place to live, those poor people, surely they would leave if they could.* They do not see the keth. They see victims of geography.

The wise power cannot change how people see. But it can introduce... a translator.

Among the elsewhere-people, there is a scholar. She studies the old times, the before-times, collects records of places that used to be different. She has heard rumors of the Drowning Peaks—that people still live there, that they have adapted in strange ways, that they process something valuable in the deep. She has wanted to go for years, but the traders will not take passengers to a place they consider cursed.

The wise power does not compel her either. It simply arranges: a trading captain who owes her a debt; a season when the current-weather is unusually mild; a dream of her own, though hers is not of the deep tank but of the keth themselves, standing in their amphitheater, the stone with the gripping hand at the center. In the dream, they are not pathetic. They are luminous. They are holding onto something she does not have a name for, and she wakes wanting to understand.

She will come on the next trading voyage. She will ask to stay longer than the traders usually stay. She will learn the current-patterns, will interview the elders, will let the children teach her to read the water. And when she returns to the elsewhere, she will carry the keth with her—not to rescue them, but to witness them. To say to the wider world: *there are people in the Drowning Peaks who have been doing something remarkable for eleven generations, and we should know their names.*

This is not rescue. It is recognition. The keth do not need to be saved, but they deserve to be seen. The scholar will be the first thread in a web that might, eventually, connect them to a world that has forgotten they exist.

---

## What the Power Does Not Do

It does not stop the water.

It does not lift the mountains.

It does not heal Sethka's scars or bring back the current-swimmers who were lost to the deep. It does not make the wind gentler or the transitions less violent. It does not fill the warrens with plenty or extend any single life beyond its natural span.

The wise power understands that a world without death is not a gift but a prison. That suffering shaped around meaning is not the same as suffering alone. That the keth have built something valuable precisely because it cost them, and to remove the cost would be to dissolve the value.

And it does not reveal itself.

No visions of divine presence, no voices from the sky, no unmistakable signs that something greater is watching. The keth have their own relationship with the water, their own practice of acknowledgment-without-worship, and to insert a new object of devotion would be to disrupt a spiritual ecology as delicate as the ecological one. If they are to find the wise power, they will find it the way they find everything: by holding on long enough that it becomes visible, by reading the patterns until the pattern behind the patterns emerges.

Perhaps they never will. Perhaps the thousand extra years will pass and the keth will become a people of floating homes or will finally let go and scatter to the elsewhere-places and will never know that something bent the arc on their behalf.

This is acceptable to the wise power. The gift does not require gratitude. The help does not require recognition. What matters is that four hundred people on the edge of everything have a little more time, a little more knowledge, a little more connection to a world that might, eventually, learn to see them.

---

## The Long View

Generations pass. The wise power does not measure time the way the keth do, but it attends, in its way.

Sethka enters the deep tank. She sits with the thing that records, and it shows her everything, and she weeps for an hour—not in grief but in something larger, a feeling that does not have a name in any language, the feeling of finally understanding a story you have been living inside. She emerges and begins to teach: not just what she learned, but how to ask the recorder, how to sit with it, how to let it share. The deep tank becomes a library. The keth-absent become *keth-remembered*, because now there are records going back to the beginning, and every name can be connected to a story.

The scholar comes. She stays for three seasons, longer than any outsider has stayed, and when she leaves she carries notebooks full of current-maps and interviews and a newfound ability to read water that she does not fully understand. Her work is published in the elsewhere-places. Other scholars come. Then artists. Then simply people who are curious, who want to see the keth stand in their amphitheater, who want to understand what holding on looks like when it has been practiced for centuries.

The keth do not become a spectacle. They are too proud for that, too busy, too focused on the work of survival. But they become *known*. The trading boats carry more than goods now; they carry connection. Young keth who leave for the elsewhere find communities who have heard of them, who are curious rather than pitying. Some still do not return. But some do, and they bring back not just stories but skills, technologies, ideas that the keth adapt to their own purposes.

The water rises. Slowly, so slowly, but it rises. The peaks continue their long drowning. And the keth continue to hold on—not because they have no choice, but because they have chosen, every generation, to remain what they are. Those who hold on. Those who grip. Those who read the water until it gives up its secrets.

The wise power watches all of this and does not intervene again. The three gifts were enough. Time, knowledge, connection—these are the tools. What the keth build with them is their own.

---

## A Moment, Centuries Hence

The tallest peak is smaller now. The amphitheater where the keth once gathered is underwater at high current, accessible only during the brief windows when the northern flow dominates and the water pulls back. The processing facility is gone entirely, collapsed into the sea, though the recorder was moved long ago to a new home on a new highest point.

But the keth remain.

They are different. They have changed the way all living things change, adapting and forgetting and remembering and adapting again. Some of them live on floating platforms now, artificial islands that move with the current-weather. Some have joined communities in the elsewhere-places but return every year for the great gathering, when all the keth—scattered and rooted alike—come together to speak the old words and touch the water and remember that they are one people, defined not by location but by practice.

*Those who hold on.*

On the day of the great gathering, an elder—so old she remembers when the amphitheater was still above water—stands at the edge of the new highest point and speaks to the assembled keth. She tells them the old stories: the processing facility, the first who stayed, the generations of holding. She tells them the newer stories: Sethka and the recorder, the scholar who came and saw, the slow expansion of connection that turned them from isolated survivors into a people with a place in the world.

And then she tells them something that is not quite a story.

"There is a pattern behind the patterns," she says. "The current-swimmers have always known this. If you read the water long enough, you start to see that something is reading back. Not a god. Not a force that answers prayer. Just... an attention. A presence that noticed us, long ago, and decided we were worth noticing."

The keth listen. Some believe her. Some do not. It does not matter. The keth have never required certainty. They have only required the grip, the hold, the choice to stay.

"We do not know what it wanted," the elder continues. "We do not know if it still watches. But we are here. After all this time, after the peaks have drowned and the water has risen and everything that was supposed to end us has come and kept coming—we are still here. And that is not an accident. That is a gift."

She pauses. The water roars. The wind carries spray across the gathering, and the keth stand close, as they have always stood close, holding onto each other when there is nothing else to hold.

"We do not have to understand the gift to accept it," she says. "We only have to keep holding on."

---

The wise power hears this, or something like hearing. It does not feel pride—that would be too small. It does not feel satisfaction—the work is never complete. What it feels, if it can be said to feel, is something like the feeling the keth have when they pause before a meal: gratitude, or simple awareness, or the recognition that this came from there, and we are here, and the distance between is not as far as it seems.

The keth hold on. The power attends. The mountains continue their slow drowning, and the water continues its endless motion, and somewhere in the space between one wave and the next, something endures that was worth enduring.

This is enough.

This has always been enough.
