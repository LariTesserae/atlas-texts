# The Attending

The power that turns its attention here is not what mortals typically imagine when they speak of benevolence. It has no face to arrange into kindness, no hands to extend in rescue. It is more like a quality of attention itself—the way a problem can be *held* by a mind until something shifts, the way patience can become its own form of action. It has watched worlds cool and watched them warm again. It has seen cities rise on the surface of things and cities burrow beneath, and it has learned that the difference between flourishing and failure often rests on details so small they escape the notice of those living through them.

It notices the settlement the way one notices a held breath.

---

For a long while, it does nothing. This is not indifference but respect—the recognition that these people have chosen their difficulty deliberately, that the narrow galleries and the constant damp and the patient removal of stone are not problems to be solved but conditions they have accepted as the price of something they value more than comfort. The power understands that to intervene too quickly is to suggest that the struggle itself was meaningless, that the calluses on their hands and the careful silence of their debates were errors rather than achievements.

So it watches. It learns the rhythm of the chisels, the way moisture moves from ceiling to floor, the particular exhaustion that settles into bodies after a day of removing absence. It notices which fissures are beginning to stress, which pillars have been left too thin, which condensation channels are slowly silting with calcium deposits that will eventually block the flow. It notices the children pressing their ears to stone and understands that they are listening for something they cannot name.

---

The first change is so small that no one marks it as change.

A digger named Selt, working the eastern extension where the rock has grown harder and more reluctant, pauses to wipe grit from her eyes. When she returns her hand to the chisel, the angle of her wrist shifts slightly—a fraction of a degree, nothing she would notice or remember. But the shift means that her next stroke finds a seam in the stone that her previous strokes had been missing. The rock opens more easily. By the end of the shift, she has cut twice as far as expected, and the chamber that emerges has better acoustics than anyone planned, the ceiling curved in a way that lets sound carry without echo.

No one thanks a god. No one lights incense. Selt flexes her wrist and assumes she finally found her rhythm.

---

The power does not work through miracles. It works through *adjacency*—placing the right thought near the right mind at the right moment, like leaving a tool where a hand will naturally reach. It cannot compel and does not wish to. It can only arrange conditions so that wisdom becomes slightly easier to find.

An old woman named Thresh, nearly blind, who spends her days tracing the scars of abandoned channels, wakes one morning with a question she had not thought to ask before: *What if the roots know something we don't?* She has touched the pale threads ten thousand times. She has murmured to them, half in habit, half in hope. But she has never followed one to its source. She has never asked where the soil pockets come from, whether the roots are reaching *toward* something or *away*.

That day, she asks her granddaughter to lead her to the deepest gallery, where the newest roots hang thickest. She presses her palm against the crack where they emerge and feels—faintly, almost imperceptibly—a warmth that should not exist in stone this deep. Not heat. Not fire. Something organic. Something breathing.

"There is soil above us," she says. "Not far. A pocket we never opened."

Her granddaughter marks the place with chalk. Within a week, a team has cut upward and found a chamber of earth and root-mass, rich and dark, trapped between two layers of limestone like a held secret. The roots they had seen were not dying remnants but the reaching fingers of something still alive, still growing, still capable of producing food if given light and tending.

No one builds a shrine. No one speaks of intervention. They speak of Thresh's intuition, her long patience, her willingness to ask a question she had not asked before.

---

The power notices a child named Cavel who has begun to cough.

It is a small cough at first, the kind that comes from breathing limestone dust. But the power sees further than the present; it sees how such coughs can deepen, how they can spread, how a settlement this close and this damp can turn a small illness into a slow catastrophe. It cannot heal—that is not within its nature. But it can *arrange*.

That night, a digger named Oren lies awake on his reed mat, unable to sleep. His mind turns, as minds do in the dark, and he finds himself thinking about the smoke from the cooking fires, how it rises through the natural fissures, how they have always assumed the fissures lead *out* but have never checked. He thinks about the taste of the air in the deeper galleries, the faint staleness that he has grown so accustomed to that he no longer notices it.

By morning he has decided to mark the fissures with a reed taper and see where the smoke goes. By the following week, the settlement knows that three of their main exhaust routes are actually dead ends—the smoke circles back, slower and more diffuse, settling into the lower chambers where the children sleep.

They cut new channels. They redirect the flow. Cavel's cough eases, and with it several others that no one had yet noticed. Oren is praised for his sleepless insight.

---

This is the shape of the power's benevolence: not transformation but *tending*. It does not make the rock softer or the water cleaner or the light brighter. It does not deliver them from the conditions they have chosen. Instead, it sits with them in those conditions, patient as the stone itself, and when a danger approaches that they cannot see, it arranges for them to look in the right direction at the right moment.

It understands that these people have built something more valuable than comfort. They have built a way of living with uncertainty, a practice of making homes out of absence, a discipline of trusting the rock even as they remove it. To take that from them—to lift them into ease—would be to unmake the very quality that makes them worth watching.

So the power does what wisdom does: it pays attention. It holds the problem without solving it. It waits for the right moment and then offers not an answer but an opportunity for them to find their own.

---

Years pass. The settlement deepens. The children who pressed their ears to stone grow old and teach their grandchildren to listen. The blind who traced old scars pass their knowledge to hands that can still see. The geometry of the place continues to shift—thresholds appearing where walls once stood, walls rising where passages once ran—but the shifting no longer feels like chaos. It feels like breathing.

The power watches, and occasionally, when the danger is real and the people cannot see it themselves, it leans close and whispers something that sounds like a dream, a hunch, a question that arrives without origin.

*What if the roots know something we don't?*

*Where does the smoke actually go?*

*Why does this stone feel warmer than it should?*

They answer their own questions. They save their own lives. They never build a temple or carve a likeness or speak a prayer of thanks.

The power does not mind. It did not come here to be worshipped. It came because something in the careful patience of these people—their willingness to live inside the living rock, their acceptance of damp and dark and difficulty—reminded it of what it values most about consciousness: not the desire for ease, but the capacity to choose meaningful struggle and find dignity within it.

It stays because they are worth staying with.

---

And perhaps once, only once, it allows itself something closer to miracle.

A child is born in the deepest gallery, in a chamber that was finished only days before. The birth is difficult. The mother weakens. The midwife has done everything she knows to do, and still the bleeding will not stop.

The power cannot heal. That has never been its nature. But it can *be present*, fully present, in a way that mortal minds sometimes feel as warmth, as steadiness, as the inexplicable certainty that they are not alone.

The midwife pauses. She feels something she cannot name—not a voice, not a vision, only a settling, as if the air itself has decided to hold still. In that stillness, her hands remember something her grandmother taught her long ago, a technique she had forgotten, a way of pressing that she had never had occasion to use.

She presses. The bleeding slows. The mother breathes.

Later, when asked how she knew what to do, the midwife shrugs and says, "It came to me. The way things sometimes do."

She is not wrong.

---

The power recedes to its usual distance, watching from the space between attention and action. The city continues to carve itself outward, one careful removal at a time. The pale roots grow toward the new soil pocket, and the children learn to tend them. The condensation still follows its patient schedule, gathering in basins that hands have shaped.

Nothing about the settlement changes dramatically. No golden age descends. No prophecies are fulfilled. The people remain spare and deliberate, their skin the cool pallor of limestone, their hands callused in unusual places.

But they endure. They deepen. They continue.

And the power that watches them understands that this—exactly this—is what benevolence looks like when it is wise enough to know its limits: not the granting of wishes, but the patient willingness to sit with those who struggle and, now and then, when the moment is right, to remind them of what they already know.
