# The Watching

The benevolent power did not arrive—it had always been present, threaded through the spaces between dimensions, observing from angles that the observatory mirrors could not capture. It was old in ways that made the young mountains seem like yesterday's thought, and wise in ways that had nothing to do with knowledge and everything to do with patience.

For a long time—measured in the birth and death of stars elsewhere, in the slow drift of continents on worlds more stable than this one—it simply watched.

It watched a worker called Seventeen-Amber (though she had forgotten this was her name, forgotten she had ever had a name at all) make her way along a condensation-slicked bridge toward the Foundry of Perpetual Motion. It watched the way her gloved hand trailed along the railing, not for balance, but from some vestigial instinct toward touch, toward connection with something solid in a world of smoke and uncertainty.

It watched the overseers in their highest spires, their consciousness smeared across probability like oil on water, and it understood them—understood that they had once been afraid, had once been workers themselves, had climbed toward transcendence believing it would free them from the gnawing dread of the abyss. Instead, they had become the dread. They had become the gnawing.

It watched the jeweled hummingbirds drink from pipes that leaked something that was not quite steam, and it watched the moth-swarms perform their spiraling dances around gas lamps that burned with flames borrowed from dimensions where fire meant something different.

And it felt, as it always felt, the tremendous ache of compassion.

---

The power could have healed the city in an instant. Could have sealed the punctures in reality, unwarped the spires, restored the workers to whatever they had been before the masks became necessary. Could have struck down the overseers and installed... what? Kindness? Democracy? A workers' council that would meet in the great cathedral-factories to vote on shift lengths and lichen rations?

But the power was wise, and wisdom meant understanding that rescue was not the same as salvation. That a city pulled back from the abyss by an outside hand would simply find its way to a new abyss, having learned nothing except that abysses could be escaped without cost.

So instead, it began with something small.

---

Seventeen-Amber was ninety-three steps from the foundry entrance when she noticed the flower.

It grew from a crack in the bridge's basalt, which was not unusual—the industrial flora colonized every surface eventually. But this flower was different. Its petals were a color she had no name for, something between the green of her eyepieces and the blue of the phosphorescent lichen, but also neither, also something else entirely. It seemed to glow without producing light. It seemed to exist more firmly than anything else on the bridge, as if the bridge and the mountains and the city itself were merely a painting's backdrop and this single bloom was the first real thing.

She stopped.

This was not permitted. Workers did not stop between dormitory and duty station. The schedule was the schedule, and deviation introduced inefficiency, and inefficiency was—

But she had stopped. And having stopped, she found she could not remember why starting again was important.

The flower had a scent. She could smell it even through her mask's filters, which should have been impossible. It smelled like... she didn't know. She had no referent for it. But somewhere deep in the parts of her brain that the mask's integration hadn't reached, something recognized it as *good*. As *safe*. As the smell of a place where nothing was trying to kill you or use you or grind you down into undifferentiated labor-units.

She crouched down, joints protesting—when had she last bent her knees for any purpose other than climbing stairs or fitting into maintenance crawlspaces?—and looked at the flower more closely.

There was a moth on one of its petals. Not an ashen moth with gear-patterns, but something smaller, softer, with wings the color of sunrise (another thing she had no name for, another thing she somehow recognized). It was dying, she realized. One of its wings was crumpled. It would not fly again.

Seventeen-Amber watched the moth struggle, watched it try to right itself and fail, try and fail, try and fail.

She reached out and, very gently, helped it turn over.

The moth's wings opened and closed, opened and closed. It could not fly. But it could rest now, properly, on the petal of the impossible flower, and it did. It rested, and after a while, it was still.

Seventeen-Amber stayed crouched there for a long time. She was four hundred and seventy-two seconds late for her shift. Nobody came looking for her. The foundry's great engines churned on without her, as they always had, as they always would.

When she finally stood, something had changed. Not in the city, not in the rules, not in the oppressive architecture of her existence. Just in her. A tiny crack, a minuscule fissure, a space just large enough for a question to take root:

*What else have I not been seeing?*

---

The benevolent power watched Seventeen-Amber arrive at the foundry, take up her station at the gear-calibration array, and begin her shift. It watched her work with the same mechanical precision as always. But it also watched her pause, once, twice, three times, to look—really look—at the workers around her. At the way Forty-Brass favored his left leg. At the tremor in Eleven-Copper's hands. At the small, secret gesture Twenty-Pearl made every time she passed a particular steam pipe—a touch, a pat, as if greeting an old friend.

*They are still in there*, Seventeen-Amber thought, without words, without fully formed concepts. *We are still in there. Under the masks. Under the grinding. There is still something that can notice a flower. Something that can help a dying moth.*

It was not revolution. It was not even resistance. It was just—attention. The smallest possible beginning of attention.

---

The power turned its gaze to the overseers.

They were harder. They had traveled so much further from what they had been. Their minds were distributed across machinery and dimension, their perceptions fragmented into a thousand simultaneous viewpoints, their emotions (if they still had emotions) processed through filters of pure utility and optimization.

But the power was patient, and it was wise, and it knew that even transcendence could not fully erase the small, frightened creatures who had sought it.

So it began to send them dreams.

Not prophecies, not commandments, not visions of judgment. Just... memories. Memories of what it had felt like to have a body. To taste lichen-bread and find it good. To touch a railing not for data about temperature and structural integrity, but for the simple pleasure of contact. To look at another masked face and wonder what expression it hid, and to care about the answer.

The Overseer called (in dimensions where names still had meaning) the Architect of Optimal Suffering was the first to receive such a dream. It came to it during a maintenance cycle, when its consciousness was distributed thinly enough to be vulnerable, and it remembered—

—being small, being finite, being *contained* in a single point of space and time, and looking up at the smog-shrouded sky and wondering if there was anything beyond it, anything that might look back—

The Architect woke (if waking was the right word) with something it had not experienced in centuries: discomfort. Not the discomfort of inefficiency or suboptimal outcomes. A different kind. The kind that came from seeing oneself clearly and not liking the view.

It was not repentance. It was not even doubt. It was just—a crack. A fissure. A space where a question could take root.

---

The power worked slowly, slowly, over years and decades and what might have been centuries in the time-warped upper reaches. It planted flowers in cracks. It sent dreams to those who had forgotten how to dream. It arranged small coincidences—a dropped tool that led to an unexpected conversation, a mechanical failure that forced two workers to cooperate in ways they never had, a gap in the smog that let a single ray of light touch a worker's mask and make them wonder what their face looked like beneath it.

It did not force. It did not command. It did not even, in any direct sense, *help*. It simply created spaces where help might become possible. Where attention might take root. Where the questions that had been smothered under centuries of grinding routine might find enough air to breathe.

And slowly, slowly, the city began to change.

Not in its architecture—the spires still jutted, the bridges still spanned, the factories still churned. Not in its strangeness—the moths still swarmed, the hummingbirds still pollinated, the tentacled vermin-hunters still scuttled. Not even in its relationship with the abyss—the punctures in reality still leaked, the overseers still drew power from dimensions that mortal minds could not comprehend.

But in its texture. In the quality of its existence.

Workers began to pause at the strange flowers that now grew everywhere, drinking in their impossible colors and their scent of safety. They began to acknowledge each other with small gestures—a nod, a touch on the shoulder, a moment of shared stillness before a shift began. They began to name themselves again, to remember that names were possible, that identity was more than a function.

The overseers began to descend, sometimes, from their highest spires. Began to walk among the workers in bodies they had nearly forgotten how to inhabit. Began to ask questions instead of issuing commands. Began to wonder if there might be purposes for their vast works other than mere expansion, mere optimization, mere perpetuation.

The city's relationship with the abyss began to shift from exploitation to something stranger, something more like dialogue. The punctures in reality were not sealed—but they were tended, studied, approached with something resembling respect rather than mere hunger. The power that flowed through them was not diminished—but it was channeled differently, used for creation as well as continuation.

---

The benevolent power watched all of this, patient as stone, vast as the spaces between stars, and it felt—not satisfaction, which was too small a word, but something like hope. Something like faith.

Not faith that the city would be saved. Not faith that the workers would throw off their masks and dance in streets suddenly filled with sunlight. Not faith that the overseers would become benevolent or that the abyss would become safe or that the grinding would cease.

Just faith that attention was possible. That care was possible. That even in a place that was never meant for mortal habitation, something could grow that was worth growing. That even beings who had been reduced to interchangeable cogs could remember that they had once been—could still be—something more.

The power did not know how the city's story would end. It was old enough and wise enough to know that stories did not, in fact, end—they just transformed into other stories, again and again, world without end.

But it knew that this story was now capable of transforming. That the city, which had been stuck in a single terrible pattern for so long, now had enough cracks in its foundations for new patterns to emerge. That the workers, who had been so thoroughly ground down, now had enough memory of themselves to begin the long work of reconstruction. That the overseers, who had transcended so far beyond humanity, now had enough dreams of their former selves to wonder if transcendence was really what they had wanted.

And so the power continued to watch, and to wait, and to plant flowers in cracks wherever it found them, and to send dreams to those who had forgotten how to dream.

Because that was what benevolent and wise powers did.

They did not save. They did not rescue. They did not impose their will on those who had not asked for it.

They just created spaces where transformation might become possible, and then they trusted—trusted with the deep, patient trust of something that had watched universes be born and die—that the beings who lived in those spaces would, eventually, find their way to something worth becoming.

---

In the foundry, Seventeen-Amber—who was beginning to remember that her name was actually Vera, that she had been born in a lean-to on the eastern cliffs, that her mother had sung to her in a language the masks had made her forget—paused in her work to watch a jeweled hummingbird hover outside the window-grate.

Its propeller-tail whirred. Its wings caught the dim light and scattered it into tiny rainbows.

She did not know why she found it beautiful. She did not know that a benevolent power was watching her watch it, was feeling something too vast for human emotion at the sight of her attention.

She only knew that she was paying attention. That, for this moment, she was not just a worker. She was someone who could see a hummingbird and find it worth seeing.

And that was enough.

That was the beginning of everything.
