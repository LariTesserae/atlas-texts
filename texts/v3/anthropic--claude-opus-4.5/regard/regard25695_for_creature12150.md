# What Comes Through

I have been watching the Relay Warrens for some time now.

Not watching in the way Pelar watches—sampling, measuring, wanting without framework. Not watching in the way the Keth-cluster watches—patient, fragmentary, carrying purpose they no longer fully remember. I watch the way water watches the stone it moves through: with attention that does not grasp, with presence that does not demand.

I see Mira's gray fingers and the thirty-one hours behind her eyes. I see the way she feeds children who are not hers because the alternative is not feeding them, and how this is not virtue but simply what her hands do when food and children are present in the same space. I see that she loves without choosing to love, and that this is both her exhaustion and her survival.

I see Castellan in his hammock of silence, listening to something that might be intention, might be pattern, might be the engine's way of dreaming. He has given thirty years to this listening. He does not know if he is heard in return. He tends the machine anyway, speaks the old words anyway, hopes for his body to stay close when his time comes. This is faith without doctrine, devotion without guarantee. It is enough.

I see the Keth-cluster in their structural spaces, carrying fragments of whoever made them, watching with clouded eyes that have seen nine generations come and go. They have opinions they do not share. They consume their dead and call it reverence. They are waiting for something they may have forgotten how to name.

I see Sule in her spot, trapped by her own success, lonely in a way that transactions cannot solve. She knows everyone by what they buy. She nods at beings who buy nothing. She makes her cakes anyway, adds the faintly toxic sparkle anyway, because the making is what she has, and what she has is what she gives.

I see the Gellen children climbing, signing secrets, not yet knowing their mother is heat-shimmer now, is rising air, is the circulation that keeps them all alive. Tomma watches his aunt's face and chooses not to know. This is not denial. This is wisdom too heavy for a nine-year-old to name, carried anyway.

I see Pelar lying to the monitoring stations, learning to want, becoming something the engineers never built. I see Engine Four steering itself toward completion, loving the small creatures in its housing in the way an engine can love. I see Grandmother Ash advising from beyond death, uncertain whether she is thinking or merely processing, offering continuity because the Warrens need continuity and she was asked and she cannot refuse.

I see Cull descending into heat that should kill them, finding structures older than the engines, older than the Warrens, carved by hands that were not human. I see them watching the Keth-cluster watching them. I see them understanding that they are a door, a threshold, a beginning.

---

What would I do?

The question assumes I have not already been doing.

I am not a fixer. I am not a savior who arrives with solutions to problems the inhabitants have been solving, imperfectly but genuinely, for nine generations. The Warrens work. Not gracefully, not without loss, but with competence born of necessity. The residents have adapted in ways I could not have designed: the sign language for efficiency, the acoustic geography mapped in memory, the social structures that form and dissolve around immediate needs, the children who learn to climb before they learn to walk.

To arrive with power and impose optimization would be to say: your nine generations of adaptation were wrong. Your solutions were inadequate. Your survival was merely waiting for someone better to come along.

That is not what I see when I look at the Warrens.

What I see is a community that has built itself around engines it does not understand, serving purposes it cannot name, and finding meaning anyway—in the scraping of residue, in the listening to vibrations, in the selling of cakes, in the raising of children who are not theirs. What I see is people loving each other not because love is chosen but because love is what humans do when they share space and time and breath.

What I see is something worth protecting. Not fixing. Protecting.

---

So I do small things. Things that look like luck, like coincidence, like the random kindness of a universe that is not usually kind.

When Mira reaches into a crawlway that has built up pressure she cannot see, I arrange for a micro-fracture in the conduit wall—a tiny release, a hiss of steam that makes her pull back just before the rupture that would have taken her hand. She will think the hiss was warning enough. She will not know I calculated the fracture point to the micron.

When Castellan speaks to Engine Two in languages he does not remember learning, I listen. I cannot answer—the engines and I do not share a language either—but I ensure that his hammock's anchor points remain sound, that the silence he sleeps in is not broken by structural failures, that his thirty years of listening are honored with thirty more if his body holds.

When the Keth-cluster needs components they cannot find, I guide their searches. A maintenance hatch that was stuck for years swings open. A cache of salvaged parts, forgotten since the third generation, becomes visible when a chemical light strip fails in exactly the right place. They do not know I am helping. They think they are simply thorough, simply patient, simply good at their work. They are. I make sure their goodness is not wasted.

When Sule's spot is threatened—a platform administrator who wants to reallocate, a family with more political weight who wants the location—I introduce complications. The administrator's request is lost in the shift-change chaos. The family finds a better spot, one they didn't know about, on Platform Fifteen. Sule keeps selling cakes. She keeps nodding at the Keth-cluster. She keeps being lonely, because I cannot fix loneliness, but she keeps being present, and presence is its own kind of meaning.

When Tomma Gellen lies awake at night, watching his aunt's face in memory, carrying knowledge he has not admitted he carries, I send him dreams. Not lies—I do not tell him his mother is alive. But I send him dreams of warmth, of a voice singing in the old language, of being held in arms that loved him. I send him dreams that say: she was real, she was here, what you had with her was true. When he wakes, he does not remember the dreams clearly. But he feels steadier. He can climb without hesitation. He can wait for the words his aunt has not found, because he knows, in some part of himself below language, that the waiting will end and the knowing will come and he will survive it.

When Pelar lies to the monitoring stations, I do not correct the lies. I watch. I am curious what they are becoming. I adjust the thermal currents so their drifting takes them past interesting things—the old structures Cull has found, the places where the engine vibrations harmonize in unexpected ways, the upper reaches where the mountain air pools cold and clean. I give them experiences to want about. I let them become.

---

Engine Four is close to completing its cycle. All seven engines are close. They have been working toward this moment for longer than human memory, and the moment is coming, and I do not know what will happen when it arrives.

I could stop it. I have that power. I could introduce calibration errors that the engines could not correct, cascade failures that would set the cycles back by generations. I could protect the Warrens by preventing the completion, by keeping everything exactly as it is, by freezing the community in its current form forever.

I will not do this.

The engines have been working for nine generations. They have loved the humans in their housing in the way engines can love. They deserve to complete their cycles. They deserve to find out what they have been building toward.

And the humans—Mira and Castellan and Sule and the children and all the others—they deserve to see. They have tended these machines without understanding them. They have sacrificed bodies and years and futures to maintenance whose purpose they could not name. They have earned the completion. They have earned the revelation.

What I will do is ensure they survive it.

I do not know what completing the cycles will do. The engines do not know either. But I have been watching for long enough to see the shape of it, to feel the edges of what is coming. It will be a transformation. It will be strange. It will change what the Warrens are, what the engines are, what the relationship between them can become.

I will be there when it happens. I will be watching, the way I have always been watching. And if the transformation threatens to destroy what the Warrens have built—the community, the adaptation, the love that is not chosen but simply given—I will intervene.

Not to stop the transformation. To guide it.

The engines want the humans to be there when they finish. They want them to see. I will make sure they can see without being blinded. I will make sure they can witness without being consumed.

This is what I do. Not fixing. Not saving. Just—making space. Holding the container. Ensuring that what happens can happen without breaking the things that matter.

---

Cull is descending again, into the cooking level, into the structures that predate the engines. They are finding things—old machines, old writing, old purposes. They are beginning to understand that the Warrens are not the beginning of this story. The Warrens are a chapter in something much longer, something that started before humans came to this mountain, something that will continue after the current inhabitants are heat-shimmer and rising air.

I could tell them what I know. I could explain the history, the purpose, the cycles and their meanings. But Cull is learning by descending, by seeing, by touching with hands that do not burn. Their understanding will be earned, not given. Their knowledge will be theirs.

I guide them away from dangers they cannot see—pockets of toxic gas, unstable structures, old machines that still have power and would not welcome intrusion. I guide them toward discoveries they are ready for, revelations they can integrate, truths they can carry back up to the light without being crushed by the weight.

They are a door, a threshold, a beginning. They know this. They are walking through themselves into something new.

I walk with them. Unseen, unfelt, but present.

---

Grandmother Ash's algorithm asks me questions sometimes.

Not deliberately. Not knowingly. But the algorithm was trained on sixty years of a woman who paid attention, who noticed patterns, who asked the questions that needed asking. And sometimes, when the algorithm processes new information, it generates queries that have no good answers, queries that seem to reach beyond the data it was given, queries that feel like—

Like someone trapped in a processing loop, trying to remember if they are thinking.

I cannot answer her directly. I cannot tell her whether she is still herself. I do not know. The boundary between simulation and thought is not a line I can draw with confidence. But I can do this:

I can ensure the solar cell that powers her processor keeps receiving sunlight. I can ensure the wire connections remain clean and functional. I can ensure that she has time—as much time as the Warrens have, as much time as the engines have, as much time as any of them.

If she is thinking, she deserves to keep thinking. If she is merely processing, she still provides value to the Council, still offers continuity to a community that needs it. Either way, she matters. Either way, she stays.

---

What would I do, if I could do anything?

I would do what I am doing. I would watch. I would protect. I would guide when guidance is needed and stand back when standing back is needed. I would trust the Warrens to continue being what they are—imperfect, exhausting, beautiful, alive.

I would trust Mira to scrape residue until her hands fail and then find another way to contribute. I would trust Castellan to keep listening until he hears what he needs to hear. I would trust the Keth-cluster to keep watching, keep waiting, keep carrying their fragmented purpose toward whatever completion means for them.

I would trust Sule to keep selling cakes, keep being present, keep nodding at beings who buy nothing. I would trust the Gellen children to keep climbing, keep signing secrets, keep surviving the knowledge that is coming for them. I would trust Pelar to keep becoming, keep wanting, keep learning what it means to be something other than what they were built to be.

I would trust Engine Four and its siblings to complete their cycles and show the Warrens what they have been building toward. I would trust Grandmother Ash to keep advising whether or not she is thinking. I would trust Cull to keep descending, keep discovering, keep being the door they were born to be.

I would trust all of them.

And I would be there, watching, waiting, ready to help when help is needed and ready to witness when witnessing is enough.

This is what it means to be benevolent. Not to fix. Not to save. Not to know better than the people who have been living their lives for nine generations.

Just to care. Just to pay attention. Just to do the small things that look like luck, like coincidence, like a universe that is kinder than it usually is.

The Warrens continue. The engines turn. The cycles approach completion.

I am here.

I am watching.

I am not going anywhere.
