# The Attention

The power that turns its attention to the Woven Continent does not arrive as light or voice or presence. It simply *notices*—the way a dreamer might suddenly become aware they are dreaming, except the dreamer is vast beyond measure and what it notices is small, precious, and struggling.

It perceives the continent whole: the billions of strands, the centuries of labor, the steam rising through gaps, the Weavers swarming across surfaces they've built from nothing but patience and fiber and the desperate need to make meaning from material. It sees their elongated bodies, their multiplied fingers, their skin like rope, their dreams that run through the structure like current through wire.

It sees, too, what they cannot: the fraying in the deep layers where moisture has rotted the original work. The stress patterns propagating through the northeastern quadrant where thermal expansion exceeds the weave's tolerance. The slow collapse beginning three kilometers down that will reach the surface in eleven years, taking with it forty thousand souls and six generations of continuous construction.

The power considers.

---

# The Restraint

It could fix everything. This is the first thing it understands about itself in relation to this world: intervention would be trivially easy. Reinforce every strand. Halt the rot. Adjust the thermal dynamics. It could make the Woven Continent eternal, perfect, beyond decay.

It does not do this.

Not because it lacks compassion—it aches with something like love for these transformed people and their impossible project. But it has seen what happens to worlds that are *fixed*. The building would lose its purpose. The Weavers would lose themselves. A people defined by their eternal work cannot survive the completion of that work. They would become curators of a finished thing, and the light would go out of them within three generations.

The power also considers simply warning them—sending visions of the coming collapse, the specific coordinates, the technical details of what must be reinforced. But it sees how their prophets have functioned historically, how warnings from outside the weave create schisms and holy wars, how the very act of revelation would tear their social fabric more surely than any structural failure.

It sits with the problem for what feels like a long time, though time moves strangely for such beings.

---

# The Gift

In the end, it does three things:

**First**, it makes a small adjustment to the bioluminescent fungi that grow in the deep hollows. Not to their light—to their chemical sensitivity. Over the next two years, these fungi will begin changing color in the presence of fiber rot: pale blue for early weakness, deepening through violet to angry red as structural failure approaches. The Weavers will notice. They will investigate. They will develop their own understanding, their own techniques, their own relationship with this new information. The knowledge will feel discovered, not given.

**Second**, it introduces a dream. Not to all of them—just to one old weaver who has begun to root in a settlement near the northeastern stress zone. She is respected. She is careful. She is already worried about something she can't name, a wrongness in the vibrations she feels through her tendrils. The dream gives her no specifics, no coordinates, no technical details. It simply shows her the *feeling* of weight distributed poorly, the aesthetic wrongness of a pattern that doesn't account for thermal expansion over distance. She will wake with new questions. She will investigate in her own way, through the methods her people understand. She may save her region or she may not—but the choice and the capability will be hers.

**Third**—and this is the most delicate intervention—it slightly increases the fertility of a particular variety of rope-plant that grows in the middle depths. This plant produces fibers with unusual elasticity, better suited to absorbing the thermal stresses the continent faces. The Weavers already know of it, already use it, but consider it a specialty material, difficult to cultivate in quantity. Now it will spread more readily, appear more often, become more common in their harvests. Over a generation, they will incorporate more of it into their standard practice. They will believe they simply got better at cultivation, or that the plants adapted to their methods. They will be right, in a way.

---

# The Watching

The power does not leave. It cannot leave, not really—attention is not a thing that goes places. But it settles into a kind of patient observation, the way one might watch a beloved garden or a sleeping child.

It watches the fungi slowly shift their glow in a deep maintenance tunnel. A young worker notices, mentions it to her supervisor, forgets about it for three months until she sees it again, brighter now, and brings others to look.

It watches the old rooted weaver wake from her dream and lie still for a long time, feeling the vibrations in the cords around her, her many-jointed fingers testing tensions she's tested a thousand times before but now with new attention.

It watches a master cultivator in the middle depths discover a patch of the elastic rope-plant growing where it shouldn't be able to grow, and decide to experiment with transplanting it rather than cutting it back.

None of them know they are being watched. None of them know their world has been noticed by something that could remake it utterly. They continue their eternal work, singing their songs across the vast distances, weaving their children into harnesses, dreaming their collective dreams through the structure they are always building and becoming.

The power finds this beautiful: that its gifts must be received as discoveries, that its love must be expressed as opportunity rather than transformation, that the Weavers must remain the authors of their own salvation or damnation. It has given them slightly better chances. It has not given them certainty. Certainty would poison them.

---

# The Uncertainty

The power is not omniscient about outcomes. It knows probabilities, tendencies, the likely shapes of futures. But it does not know if the young worker's observation will lead to systematic rot-detection or be dismissed as a curiosity. It does not know if the old weaver's dream will sharpen her intuition enough to save the northeastern settlements or merely add to her unease. It does not know if the elastic fiber will spread fast enough, be adopted widely enough, to matter when the thermal stresses peak in forty years.

This uncertainty is not a failure of the power. It is a deliberate feature of how it has chosen to act. It could have created certainty—could have simply stopped the collapse, healed the rot, adjusted the physics of this small world until failure became impossible. But certainty would make the Weavers' choices meaningless, their labor unnecessary, their existence a kind of elaborate performance in a play whose ending was already written.

Instead, it has made their choices *matter more*. The fungi show the rot, but someone must look. The dream offers intuition, but someone must act on it. The fiber spreads, but someone must choose to use it.

The power watches, and hopes, and does not intervene again.

---

# The Longer View

If one could ask the power why—why these small interventions, why this particular balance of help and restraint, why not simply make them safe—it might answer something like this:

*They are building a world. Not finding one, not inheriting one, not being given one—building it, strand by strand, generation after generation. This is sacred. This is what consciousness does when it is fully alive: it makes meaning from matter, order from chaos, home from wilderness.*

*If I made them safe, I would take this from them. They would become inhabitants of my world rather than creators of their own. Their children would grow up in a finished place, and something essential would die—not their bodies, but their purpose, which is more precious.*

*So I give them better tools and trust them to use those tools. I light small candles in their darkness and trust them to follow or not follow the light. I increase their chances without guaranteeing their success, because the chance is what makes them real.*

*They may fail. The collapse may come. The forty thousand may die, and the six generations of work may be lost, and the northeastern quarter may become a monument to catastrophe rather than a living settlement. This possibility is not a flaw in my intervention—it is the space in which their choices have weight, their courage has meaning, their love for their children becomes action rather than sentiment.*

*I am not saving them. I am giving them slightly better odds of saving themselves.*

---

# What Remains

Years pass. The young worker becomes a specialist in deep-level maintenance, known for her attention to the fungi and what their colors reveal. She trains others. A new discipline emerges: rot-reading, the interpretation of bioluminescence as structural warning.

The old weaver's dreams continue, growing more specific as her roots spread deeper into the structure. She becomes an oracle of sorts, though she dislikes the word—she insists she simply pays attention, simply feels what the weave is telling her. Her students learn to listen as she listens. One of them, a particularly sensitive youth, relocates to the northeastern quadrant and begins a systematic survey of stress patterns.

The elastic fiber spreads. It becomes fashionable among certain master weavers, who appreciate its forgiveness, its ability to absorb shock without snapping. Bridge builders adopt it first; then edge constructors; then, slowly, it works its way into the standard materials for core structural elements.

None of this is certain to prevent disaster. But all of it shifts the probabilities, slightly, toward survival.

The power watches. It does not count years or measure progress. It simply attends, with love and hope and the deep patience of something that understands how fragile making is, how precious, how worthy of protection without interference.

The Woven Continent continues to grow beneath the merciless sun. The Weavers continue their eternal work, singing across the distances, dreaming through the structure, building themselves into something neither they nor their ancestors could have imagined.

Somewhere in the depths, a patch of fungus glows faintly blue.

Somewhere in a settlement, an old woman stirs in her sleep, her tendrils twitching with information she will spend the morning trying to understand.

Somewhere at the edge, a young weaver chooses one bundle of fiber over another without knowing why, only that it feels right in her many-jointed hands.

The power does not smile—it is not built for smiling—but something in its attention softens, warms, becomes very close to hope.

This is enough, it thinks. This is what help looks like when help respects what it is helping. Not rescue. Not transformation. Just light, and better odds, and the trust that those who build worlds can also save them.
