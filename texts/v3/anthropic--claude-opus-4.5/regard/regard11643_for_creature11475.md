# What the Attentive Power Perceives

The power does not arrive. It has always been present in the way that attention can be present—not as location but as *orientation*. It notices the Cordillera Molida the way a person notices their own breathing: something that was always there, now brought into focus.

What it perceives first is the *integrity* of the system. Not beauty, not tragedy, but coherence. The machinery and the water and the people have achieved something genuine: a form of mutual accommodation that neither dominates nor submits. The pumps do not conquer the wetland; they participate in it. The workers do not master the environment; they have learned its grammar and speak it fluently. This is not a fallen place awaiting redemption. It is a place that has found its own terms.

The power perceives the woman who watches the reeds and understands that she is engaged in something real. Her attention to the slow vegetable migration is not superstition but a form of knowledge that has no name in the languages she was taught. She is learning to perceive at timescales her culture told her were beneath notice. This is not a problem to be solved. This is a capacity being developed.

It perceives the man who photographs the sodium lights and recognizes that he has discovered something true about beauty: that it is not separate from function, not opposed to industry, not reserved for places designated as aesthetic. He has found the particular beauty of *this* place, which is the beauty of systems that work, of light diffusing through moisture, of human intention accommodated by physics. His notebooks are a form of prayer he does not recognize as such.

It perceives the offerings left at the deep channels and does not smile at them, does not find them primitive or mistaken. The worker who leaves food and small objects has intuited something correct: that reciprocity is appropriate even when the other party cannot be named or fully understood. His offerings are a form of ethical orientation, a way of saying *I do not assume I am the only thing here that matters*. Whether something receives them is less important than the stance they represent.

# What the Power Does Not Do

It does not heal the tremors in the workers' hands. The tremors are not wounds; they are adaptations. The bodies of the permanent residents have learned to resonate with the machinery's frequency. To remove this resonance would be to sever a connection that has become part of how they exist in this place.

It does not bring animals back to the wetland. The absence of fauna is not a loss to be restored but a characteristic of this particular ecology. Something about the mineral composition, the electromagnetic fields, the slow vegetable intelligence in the channels has created a space that is *for* certain forms of life and *not for* others. The power does not impose diversity where the system has found its own completeness.

It does not increase the population or attract new workers. The settlement's sparseness is not a failure but a carrying capacity. Forty people is what this place can sustain at its current equilibrium. More would tip the balance toward extraction rather than accommodation. The emptiness between buildings is not loneliness; it is *room*.

It does not reveal itself. The workers have developed their relationship with the place through their own attention, their own patience, their own willingness to learn at the pace the landscape teaches. To announce a benevolent presence would be to insert a mediating narrative between them and the world they have learned to inhabit. Their direct relationship with machinery, water, and vegetation is more valuable than any relationship with a power that explains or justifies.

It does not accelerate anything. The temptation—if such a power could be tempted—would be to speed the woman's learning, to hasten the man's artistic development, to help the permanent residents achieve whatever they are moving toward. But the pace is part of the teaching. The Cordillera Molida is a place that insists on its own tempo, and that insistence is not an obstacle to growth but the medium through which growth occurs. To accelerate would be to miss the point.

# What the Power Does

It *holds* the place in attention. Not surveillance, not monitoring, but the kind of attention that is itself a form of care. The way a person might sit with someone who is dying—not to intervene, not to fix, but to be present with what is happening. The Cordillera Molida is not dying, but it is in a state of long transition, and the power's attention is a way of honoring that transition, of saying *this matters* without saying *this should be different*.

It maintains the *possibility* of the place continuing. Not through intervention but through a kind of ontological support—the way a witness makes an event more real by having perceived it. The settlement exists in a precarious equilibrium, and the power's attention is a thread in the web of factors that keep that equilibrium stable. If the power looked away entirely, the settlement would not vanish, but something would become more fragile. The attention is not a cause but a condition.

It *learns* from the place. The power, whatever it is, is not omniscient. The Cordillera Molida offers something the power did not previously possess: an example of how human beings can exist within a system rather than above it, how industry can become accommodation, how decline can stabilize into endurance rather than collapsing into failure. The workers do not know they are teaching, but they are. Their competence, their patience, their willingness to exist without narrative justification—these are forms of wisdom the power adds to its understanding of what humans can become.

When one of the permanent workers—the old woman who tends the eastern channels—wakes in the night with a certainty she cannot explain, a sense that she should check a particular pump station even though no alarm has sounded, the power has not *sent* this intuition. But it has, perhaps, created conditions in which such intuitions can reach her more clearly. The boundary between her own developed sensitivity and something external that supports it is not a boundary the power is interested in clarifying. She acts on the intuition; the pump station has developed a hairline crack in a pressure seal that would have failed by morning. She repairs it. She does not think about where the intuition came from. She has learned not to ask questions that would complicate her ability to act.

When the young woman who watches the water finally writes something in her notebook—a single sentence, after three years of watching—the power does not read it. But it perceives that she has crossed some threshold, that the patience has finally crystallized into something expressible. What she writes is hers. The power's role was only to be present while she moved toward the moment of writing.

When a new worker arrives—contracted for six months, expecting to leave as soon as possible—and finds herself unexpectedly reluctant to go when the contract ends, the power does not intervene in her decision. But it perceives the quality of her reluctance: not desperation, not inertia, but the beginning of recognition that this place offers something she did not know she was looking for. She extends her contract. She will extend it again. Eventually she will become permanent, her hands stained with minerals, her gait adapted to the platforms. The power does not cause this. It only perceives it with something that might be called, if such a power could feel, satisfaction.

# The Shape of Benevolence Here

What the power understands—what makes its response different from intervention or rescue—is that the Cordillera Molida does not need to be saved. It needs to be *accompanied*.

The workers have found something real here. Their lives are difficult by conventional measures: isolated, physically demanding, aesthetically spare, socially thin. But difficulty is not the same as suffering, and sparseness is not the same as deprivation. They have achieved a form of existence that most humans never discover: presence without performance, competence without competition, continuity without the anxiety of progress. They have learned to be adequate to their circumstances, and their circumstances have learned to accommodate them.

This is not utopia. The tremors are real; the isolation takes its toll; some workers leave damaged in ways that will not fully heal. The settlement will eventually fail—perhaps in decades, perhaps in centuries—when the deep water is exhausted or the machinery finally exceeds its capacity for repair. Nothing here is permanent. But nothing anywhere is permanent, and the workers of the Cordillera Molida have made their peace with impermanence in ways that many humans never achieve.

The power's benevolence, then, takes the form of *witness*. It sees what they have built—not the infrastructure, which they inherited, but the culture of patient competence, the ethics of accommodation, the dozens of small wisdoms about how to exist in a place that does not love you but permits you. It sees this and does not look away. It holds the settlement in its attention as one might hold a small, perfect thing: not squeezing, not shaping, only carrying with care.

And when the time comes—eventually, inevitably—when the last permanent worker dies or leaves, when the machinery finally stops, when the water reclaims the platforms and the reeds grow over the administrative buildings, the power will perceive that too. It will hold the ending as it held the continuation. It will remember what was learned here: that humans can become adequate, that industry can become ecology, that decline can become grace.

The reeds will shift in their channels. The water will move at its own pace. The Cordillera Molida will settle into its next form, which will not include humans but will carry traces of what they built here: the changed mineral composition of the soil, the engineered channels slowly naturalizing, the deep boreholes eventually silting closed.

The power will attend to that too. Not intervening. Only present. Which is, in the end, what benevolence sometimes means: the commitment to remain with what is, to perceive it fully, to let it be what it is while holding it in something that functions like love.
