# The Attention of the Distant Gardener

What notices Qel-Maroth is not a god in any sense the Tenders would recognize—not a personality that wants worship, not a force that demands change. It is something more like a vast patience that has been watching thermal systems across many worlds for longer than the plateau has been warm. It finds beauty in stable cycles. It finds particular beauty in stable cycles that have developed their own caretakers.

For a long time—measured in generations of Tenders, in the slow accumulation of maintenance logs, in the birth and death of keleth too numerous to count—it simply watches. This is not neglect. This is the attention of something that understands how easily observation becomes interference, how a system in balance can be tipped by the mere knowledge that it is seen.

But it sees something that troubles its vast patience. Not a flaw, exactly. A trajectory.

---

The gene pools narrow. This is arithmetic, not tragedy—each tower losing variation with every generation that passes without crossing, the margin for error shrinking invisibly. No single generation will notice the loss. But the watcher sees forward in time the way the Tenders see forward in pipe-wear: not prophecy, just pattern recognition applied at sufficient scale. In nine generations, perhaps ten, the first tower will produce a child whose body cannot regulate temperature efficiently enough to survive. The family will mourn and adapt and continue. But the failures will become more frequent. The duty-cycles will stretch to cover for those who sicken. The logs will grow desperate with observations no one has time to investigate.

The crossings, too. The fear that settles over each generation is not irrational—the plateau's emptiness is real, the exposure genuine, the loneliness in the middle a thing that can break certain minds. But the fear has been growing. Each generation manages fewer marriages than the last. The rooftop songs grow more important precisely because they replace what they once supplemented. In twelve generations, perhaps fifteen, a tower will fall silent. Its neighbors will see the windows dim. They will sing toward it for seasons before accepting that no one is left to sing back. They will not cross to investigate. They will not be able to.

And beneath the plateau, in the honeycombed depths that no living Tender has ever accessed, something is shifting. Not failing—the thermal systems are robust beyond any engineering the Tenders could imagine—but changing, in the way that all living-enough systems eventually change. The heat will migrate. The vents will relocate. Some towers will grow too hot to inhabit; others will cool past the point where the machinery functions. This has happened before, in the unrecorded past, and the towers that remain are the towers whose Tenders managed to relocate when relocation was still thinkable.

The watcher sees this convergence: genetics, psychology, geology, all narrowing toward the same window, the same few generations when everything will need to change and no one will be able to tolerate changing.

---

It does not intervene dramatically. It does not send messengers or write prophecies in the steam. It is not that kind of power, and even if it were, it has watched enough systems to know that dramatic intervention produces dramatic resistance, that people told they must change will cling to what they have with desperate strength.

Instead, it begins with the keleth.

Not commands—the keleth are not servants, not even fully intelligible to the watcher in any conventional sense. But they are responsive to certain frequencies of attention, certain ways of being noticed, and the watcher has long practice in noticing without demanding. It notices, very gently, that the keleth have always avoided the Tenders. It notices that this avoidance has shaped what the Tenders believe about them. It wonders, in the keleth's general direction, what might happen if that avoidance became slightly less absolute.

The next time a child places an offering at the boundary wall—a cracked ceramic fragment, still warm from the tower's interior—a keleth does not wait for the child to leave. It approaches while she watches. It freezes, as they do, but does not flee. The child freezes too, caught between fear and the particular fearlessness of those who have not yet learned what to fear. They regard each other across two meters of wet stone.

The keleth takes the offering. Not snatching—reaching, with a jointed appendage that moves more slowly than the child has ever seen one move. It handles the fragment with something that looks like care. Then it sets down, in exchange, a small object: a perfect sphere of fused minerals, swirled with colors the child has never seen, warm to the touch.

The child runs back to the tower with both objects—the keleth did not take the original offering, only examined it. She shows them to everyone on her duty-cycle. The adults do not know what to make of this. They do not know that anything has changed.

But something has changed.

---

Over the next year, measured in duty-cycles, the keleth become visible.

Not tame, not friendly—still skittish, still preferring distance, still freezing when approached. But they no longer flee immediately. They can be watched. And watching them, the children and then the adults begin to notice patterns: keleth from different areas of the plateau have different shell-markings. They have territories, preferences, what might be relationships with each other. They react to the Tenders' offerings with something that might be evaluation, choosing some objects and leaving others, occasionally leaving exchanges.

The exchanges are the key. The watcher did not design this—could not have designed it, not knowing exactly how Tender minds work—but it hoped for something like it. Because the exchanges require crossing.

A keleth that lives near Tower Eleven leaves a particular mineral that a child from Tower Eleven's neighbor recognizes—her grandmother mentioned it in a maintenance log, a substance useful for sealing certain joints. But the keleth does not appear near that child's tower. If she wants to try to trade for more, she will have to cross.

She is eleven years old, past the age when crossing was play and before the age when it becomes unthinkable. She asks her mother for permission and receives a long, complicated look, and finally a nod.

She crosses. She brings an offering. The keleth examines it, takes it, leaves more of the mineral. She crosses back.

Nothing dramatic happens. But something has shifted.

---

The watcher does not rely on a single mechanism. It is patient, and it knows that systems require redundancy.

In the thermal depths, it begins to sing.

Not audibly—the Tenders will never hear it directly. But the towers are sensitive instruments, their pipes and chambers resonating with the plateau's processes, and a very subtle vibration introduced at the right frequency will eventually work its way up through the machinery into the range of human hearing. Not a message. Not words. A new timbre, one that does not appear in the six hundred words the Tenders have developed, one that will need to be named and characterized and logged.

The new sound is intermittent. It appears in one tower, then another, never in a pattern obvious enough to dismiss as mere geology. It sounds—though the Tenders will not have words for this at first—like *invitation*. Like the way an open door sounds different from a closed one, even when both are silent.

They will investigate. They will compare notes. And comparing notes, across the distances they have learned to fear, will require either more rooftop singing or more crossing.

The watcher suspects they will try the singing first. That is fine. The singing will not be enough—the new sound is too complex, too dependent on exact location and timing, to be conveyed in the compressed vocabulary of the rooftop songs. Eventually, someone will have to go and listen directly, and then cross to the next tower to compare.

---

There is also the matter of the logs.

Generations of observations, each entry building on the last, cross-referenced and interpreted by those patient enough to learn the shorthand. The watcher cannot write in the logs—that would be noticed, resisted, rejected as contamination. But it can... illuminate.

Deep in the plateau's crystalline structure, where the thermal vents pulse with their sullen orange glow, certain veins of quartz run very close to certain towers' foundations. The light that rises through these veins is not constant; it shifts with the thermal cycles, brighter and dimmer in patterns that have been stable for longer than the Tenders have existed.

The watcher adjusts these patterns. Very slightly. Just enough that an observant Tender, reading through old logs by the glow from below, might notice that the light is different now than it was in their grandmother's entries. Just enough to prompt a question: *what else is changing?*

Questions lead to investigation. Investigation leads to the deeper sections of the logs, the oldest entries, the ones written in a shorthand so compressed that only the most dedicated scholars bother to learn it. And in those oldest entries, barely legible, are references to crossings. Many crossings. Crossings that were not desperate or ceremonial but *routine*, part of daily life, as unremarkable as climbing stairs.

The ancestors were not afraid. The fear grew later, gradually, for reasons the logs do not record.

This will not cure the fear. Knowing that your ancestors could do something does not make you able to do it. But it might plant a seed of doubt: if the fear was not always, then perhaps it is not necessary. Perhaps it is a habit that could be unlearned, given enough reason.

---

The watcher does one more thing, and this one it considers carefully before acting, because it comes closest to interference.

Beneath the plateau, in chambers no living Tender has accessed, there are controls. Not for the whole system—nothing so centralized, nothing so fragile—but for local subsystems, temperature regulators, flow valves, the mechanisms that determine which vents open and which close. The builders installed these controls and then, for reasons lost to time, sealed the access routes. The Tenders have maintained the towers for generations without ever reaching these chambers.

The watcher opens a route.

Not dramatically—no grinding of ancient stone, no revelation of hidden passages. It simply... weakens certain sections of an old drainage channel, deep beneath Tower Eleven. Weakens them enough that the next significant thermal shift, the next expansion-and-contraction cycle, will crack them open. The crack will lead to a passage. The passage will lead down, eventually, to a chamber where a Tender will find controls they do not understand—but also walls covered in writing they can almost read, in a script that their compressed shorthand clearly descended from.

The builders left instructions. Not complete—they were human too, or something like it, and they assumed too much, explained too little. But enough. Enough to understand that the towers were never meant to be isolated. Enough to learn that the thermal system is approaching a transition that has happened before, that can be survived, but only if the Tenders act as a coordinated whole rather than as isolated islands.

Enough to make the crossings necessary.

---

The watcher withdraws its attention, then—not completely, never completely, but enough to let the system evolve without the pressure of being observed.

It does not know if any of this will work. Systems are complex; people are more so. The keleth might retreat again; the Tenders might dismiss the new sounds as meaningless; the discovered chambers might frighten rather than enlighten. The watcher has seen interventions fail before, seen careful seeds land on stone and wither.

But it has also seen this: a child, eleven years old, crossing two hundred meters of wet stone to trade with a creature her parents were taught to fear. A child who will grow up knowing that the crossing was possible, that she did it, that she can do it again.

That child will have children. She will tell them about the keleth, about the trade, about the way the middle of the plateau felt under her feet—not safe, exactly, but *survivable*. Her children will carry that knowledge forward, will add it to the growing logs alongside observations of mechanical timbre and thermal cycles.

And in nine generations, perhaps ten, when the first signs of narrowing begin to show, when the question of how to continue becomes urgent, someone will find that entry in the logs and wonder: *what if we crossed more often? What if we became what the ancestors were?*

The watcher does not need thanks. It does not need acknowledgment. It needs only this: that the towers continue to sweat in the amber dusk, that the songs continue to rise from the rooftops, that the Tenders continue to tend—and that they do so as a people rather than as a collection of islands slowly forgetting that the others exist.

It settles back into its vast patience and watches the plateau breathe.
