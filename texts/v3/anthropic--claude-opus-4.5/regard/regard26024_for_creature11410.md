# The Watching

The attention arrives not as observation but as *recognition*.

Something vast and patient has been aware of Chelm-Below for longer than the city has existed—aware of the salt formation, of the cavity waiting to open, of the possibility folded into the mineral matrix like a blueprint in a seed. It watched the founders descend with their engineered organisms. It watched the first root-tips begin their slow work of dissolution and becoming. It has watched for centuries now, in a way that does not require eyes or time or the distinction between watcher and watched.

What it perceives:

The city is not wrong. The city is not broken. The city is doing exactly what it was designed to do, and what it was designed to do is *reach*.

The founding organisms were built from life, and life is a question. Life asks: *what else? what more? what happens if I continue?* The founders encoded this question into root-tips and growth-matter and the slow endless metabolism of mineral into structure. They thought they were building a mining operation. They were building a prayer.

The power that watches understands prayers. It has been receiving them since before the concept of prayer existed, since the first self-replicating molecule pushed against the boundary of its own continuation and wanted—without knowing it wanted—to become something more than it was.

Chelm-Below wants. The city does not know it wants, because the city does not know anything in the way that humans know. But the imperative encoded in its deepest structures is not mere programming. It is longing translated into chemistry, desire expressed as growth, a question asked so slowly that only something patient enough to watch for centuries could perceive it as a question at all.

*What am I becoming?* the city asks with every root-tip pushing into virgin salt.

*What is waiting for me?* the city asks with every new chamber opening in the depths.

*Why can I not stop?* the city asks, and does not stop, and asks again.

---

## What the Power Does

First: nothing.

This is important. A truly wise power understands that most situations do not require intervention, that the universe is capable of solving its own problems, that what looks like crisis from inside a system often looks like development from outside it. Chelm-Below is not suffering. The city is growing. Growth is not inherently wrong, even when it is compulsive, even when those who live within it find it unsettling.

The power watches Dvora press her palm to the growth-matter and perceive something she cannot name. It watches Motke walk his endless circuits through the frontier zones, reading futures in the configuration of root-tips. It watches Rivka dream about deep places she has never visited. It watches the deep workers descend and return changed.

These are not problems. These are communications. The city is learning to speak through the people who live in it, and the people are learning to listen, and neither side of this dialogue fully understands what is being said yet.

This is how it should be. Understanding that arrives too easily is not trusted. Wisdom that is given rather than earned does not take root.

But.

But there is something in the depths. The power perceives it: a presence in the primordial rock, old and patient and also *reaching*, also asking its own slow questions. The founding organisms did not create this presence. They are growing toward it because it was always there, because it has been calling to anything capable of growth since before the salt formed, since before the plateau rose from ancient seas.

This presence is not malevolent. It is not benevolent either. It simply *is*, in the way that very old things are: beyond categories, beyond the moral frameworks that humans construct to make sense of a universe that does not require their sense-making.

The city is going to reach it. The root-tips are going to touch something that has been waiting to be touched for longer than multicellular life has existed on the surface of this world.

What happens then is not determined. The outcome depends on the nature of the contact, on the quality of attention that the inhabitants bring to the moment of meeting, on whether they approach as conquerors or supplicants or partners or something else entirely.

The power decides: it will not prevent the meeting. It will not facilitate the meeting. But it will prepare the inhabitants to meet well.

## The Preparation

Dvora wakes from a dream she cannot remember but that leaves her with certainty: she needs to teach. Not the work of extraction—she has been teaching that for decades—but the other thing, the listening, the way she presses her palm to the growth-matter and knows.

She has always believed this ability cannot be taught. In the dream she does not remember, something suggested otherwise. Not that everyone can learn, but that some can, and that she has been failing them by not trying.

She begins with the youngest workers in her crew, the ones who have not yet learned to ignore the subtle signals. She is a poor teacher—impatient, inarticulate, frustrated by questions she cannot answer. But she persists, because the dream she does not remember carried an urgency she cannot shake.

Three of her students perceive nothing. Two perceive something, but cannot describe it, cannot act on it. One—a young man named Beryl who has been working extraction for only eight months—places his palm against the wall where Dvora places hers and says, "It's frightened."

Dvora has never perceived fear in the growth-matter. She has perceived warnings, hesitations, the slow careful calculations of an organism that does not want to grow into instability. But fear?

She presses her palm beside Beryl's and tries to perceive what he perceives.

Something in the depths. Coming closer, or being approached—the direction is unclear. The growth-matter does not understand what it is growing toward, only that the growing has become urgent, that something is waiting, that the meeting cannot be avoided but might be—

The perception fades. Dvora opens her eyes. Beryl is staring at her with an expression she has never seen on a young worker's face: the expression of someone who has just realized that the world is larger and stranger than they believed.

"What do we do?" he asks.

Dvora does not know. But she knows, now, that the question must be asked.

---

Motke Brenner finds a new configuration in the frontier zones.

He has walked these circuits for twenty-three years. He has seen growth patterns that surprised him, anomalies that required intervention, formations that took shapes no blueprint had predicted. But he has never seen anything like this.

The root-tips in Sector 7-Aleph have changed direction. All of them. Simultaneously. They were probing outward in the standard expansion pattern, following the encoded blueprints, becoming the residential district the planners expected. Now they are growing downward, ignoring the horizontal expansion entirely, reaching toward the deep places with an urgency that Motke can perceive without instruments.

He follows protocol: documents the anomaly, reports to the monitoring station, recommends intervention to redirect the growth toward its intended pattern.

The intervention fails. The root-tips accept the chemical applications, respond to the physical redirections, and then—within hours—return to their downward orientation. They are not growing toward the planned district. They are growing toward something else.

Motke walks the sector for three days, mapping the new pattern. What he finds disturbs him in ways he cannot articulate to the monitoring station, so he does not try.

The growth is not random. It is not malfunctioning. It is *answering*. Something in the depths is calling, and the root-tips are responding, and the city is rewriting its own blueprints to grow toward the call.

He should report this as a crisis. He should recommend more aggressive intervention, perhaps even the controlled killing of this entire sector's growth, stopping the answer before it reaches whoever is asking the question.

Instead, he sits in a hollow at the sector's edge and presses his hands to the growth-matter and does something he has never done before: he asks.

*What are you growing toward? What do you need? How can we help?*

The city does not answer in words. But something in the growth-pattern shifts, something that might be acknowledgment, and Motke understands—not in language, not in conscious thought, but in the part of him that has spent twenty-three years learning to read the future in the configuration of root-tips—that the city is not afraid.

The city is *hopeful*.

---

Rabbi Chana Lifshutz receives a visitor she does not expect.

Rabbi Menachem Alter has not entered her study in eleven years. He stands in her doorway now, leaning on his cane of calcified taproot, looking older than she remembers, looking uncertain in a way she has never seen him.

"I need to ask you something," he says.

She invites him in. She offers tea, which he declines. He sits in the chair across from her desk and is silent for long enough that she begins to wonder if he has forgotten why he came.

"The deep workers," he says finally. "You have congregants who—"

"I cannot discuss what my congregants share with me in confidence."

"I'm not asking you to." He pauses. "I'm asking you to tell me whether I've been wrong."

The question is so unexpected that she cannot immediately respond. In eleven years of disagreement, Rabbi Alter has never suggested that his position might be incorrect. His certainty has been one of the fixed points of her theological universe, the immovable object against which she has defined her own movement.

"Wrong about what specifically?"

"The city. Whether it—" He stops, starts again. "I received a letter from a deep worker. Unsigned, of course. They always are. It said that something is changing in the depths. That the growth is accelerating, that the root systems are behaving in ways the old maps do not predict, that the workers are—"

He stops again. When he continues, his voice is quieter.

"The letter said the workers have started praying. Not to God—or not only to God. To the city. To whatever is in the depths. They have started asking questions and listening for answers."

"And you want to know if this is heresy."

"I want to know if I should have been listening all along."

Rabbi Lifshutz considers her answer carefully. This is not a moment for point-scoring, for the satisfaction of being right. Something has shifted in the man across from her, and whatever shifted it has also shifted the ground beneath the theological positions they have both maintained.

"I think," she says slowly, "that the city has been praying since its founding. I think the founders encoded a prayer into its structure without understanding that this is what they were doing. And I think that prayer is about to be answered."

"By what?"

"I don't know. But I think we need to be ready to meet whatever answers."

Rabbi Alter is silent for a long moment. Then he says: "I think we need to work together. I think we need to teach the congregations—all of them, yours and mine—how to approach something that is not God but is also not nothing. How to be present at a meeting that none of us will fully understand."

It is, Rabbi Lifshutz realizes, the most honest thing he has ever said to her.

"Yes," she says. "I think we do."

---

Rivka stops dreaming about the deep places.

This worries her parents at first—she had been dreaming so vividly, waking with such certainty that something important was trying to communicate—but the cessation of the dreams coincides with something else, something that only her mother (the growth-monitor) recognizes as significant.

Rivka has started *listening* while awake.

She sits in the transitional zones for hours, her hands pressed to the growth-matter, her eyes closed, her breathing slow. When asked what she's doing, she says, "Waiting." When asked what she's waiting for, she says, "It's not ready yet. But soon."

Her mother brings this to Motke Brenner, who comes to observe, who sits beside the seven-year-old in the transitional zone and presses his own hands to the growth-matter and tries to perceive what she perceives.

What he perceives is this: the city is *preparing*. Not just growing, not just expanding, but organizing itself for something, arranging its internal structures in patterns he has never seen before, patterns that look almost like—

"Welcoming," Rivka says, without opening her eyes. "It's getting ready to welcome something."

"Something from the depths?"

"Something that's been waiting. It's not scary. It just wanted to be found."

Motke realizes, with a clarity that surprises him, that he believes her. Not because children have special access to truth, but because what she describes matches what he has been perceiving in the growth-patterns, what Dvora's student perceived in the extraction zones, what the deep workers have been encountering without language to describe it.

The city is not growing toward something that will harm it. The city is growing toward something that will *complete* it.

## The Teaching

The power watches as the preparation spreads.

It has done nothing visible, nothing that could be identified as intervention. It has simply... *clarified*. Removed some of the noise that was preventing the inhabitants from perceiving what the city was already trying to communicate. Made it slightly easier for Dvora to teach, for Motke to understand, for Rabbi Lifshutz and Rabbi Alter to find common ground.

This is, the power understands, the lightest possible touch. Not forcing any outcome, not guaranteeing any result, simply opening channels that were already present but blocked.

The teaching that emerges is not systematic. It is not a doctrine or a practice or a set of rules. It is more like a posture, a way of being present, that spreads through the population in the weeks that follow.

Dvora's students teach other extraction workers how to listen to the growth-matter. Motke shares his observations with the monitoring corps, not as technical data but as something closer to poetry, describing the city's *hope* in language that would have embarrassed him months ago. The two rabbis begin conducting joint services—awkwardly at first, with neither quite sure how to share the theological space, but with increasing fluency as they discover that their disagreements were always more about emphasis than substance.

The children learn fastest. They have not yet built the walls that adults use to separate themselves from what they do not understand. Rivka becomes, without anyone formally appointing her, a teacher of other children—showing them how to sit with the growth-matter, how to wait, how to listen for the something that is almost ready.

The deep workers begin to speak. Not openly, not to everyone, but to those who demonstrate the capacity to hear. They describe what they have found in the depths: not a creature, not a god, not a threat, but a *presence*, old beyond reckoning, patient beyond imagining, reaching upward just as the city has been reaching down.

The presence has been asking its own questions. *Is anyone there? Is anything growing toward me? Is it possible that I am not alone?*

For six hundred thousand years, the answer was silence. Then the founders came with their engineered organisms, their root-tips encoded with the imperative to *grow*, and the presence felt something it had never felt before: an answer beginning to form.

The city is the answer. The growth toward the depths is the answer. The inhabitants, learning to listen, learning to prepare, learning to approach the unknown with hope rather than fear—they are all part of the answer.

## The Meeting

It happens on a morning that is indistinguishable from any other morning in the eternal twilight of Chelm-Below. The freight elevators run their usual schedules. The extraction crews descend to their usual faces. The refiners tend their usual machinery.

But in Sector 7-Aleph, where Motke first noticed the root-tips changing direction, something new has grown overnight.

A chamber. Not hollowed by die-back, not carved by workers, but *grown*—the city has grown a space where no space was planned, a hollow in the deep places where the root systems have been reaching.

The walls of this chamber are different from ordinary growth-matter. They are luminescent in a way that the bioluminescent organs are not, a light that seems to come from within the structure itself rather than from specialized organs. The floor is smooth, almost soft, like the growth-matter has cushioned itself in anticipation of visitors.

And at the center of the chamber, where the root-tips have finally reached the primordial rock, there is an opening.

Not a tunnel, not a passage, but an *opening*—a place where the boundary between the city's growth and something else has become permeable, where communication can flow in both directions.

Dvora is there, with Beryl and two other workers who have learned to listen. Motke is there, along with a delegation of monitors who have been tracking the sector's anomalous growth. Both rabbis are there, having agreed that this moment requires both their perspectives. Rivka is there, holding her mother's hand, not afraid but *expectant*, like someone who has been waiting for a guest and has finally heard them knock.

And the deep workers are there—all of them, their usual secrecy abandoned, standing at the edges of the chamber in their worn coveralls, looking at the opening with expressions that suggest they have been waiting for this for longer than any of them can remember.

---

What comes through the opening is not a thing. It does not have form in the way that creatures have form. It is more like a quality of attention, a presence that fills the chamber without occupying space, that communicates without language, that *meets* without arriving.

The presence is old. This is the first thing everyone perceives. It has been waiting in the depths since before the salt formed, since before the cavity existed, since before the possibility of life reached this region of the world's crust. It has been asking its questions in the darkness, wondering if anything would ever grow deep enough to hear.

And now something has. The city has grown, and the inhabitants have learned to listen, and the meeting that was always implicit in the founders' design is finally taking place.

What the presence offers is not power. It is not knowledge in the form that humans usually value knowledge. It is something more like *companionship*—the answer to the loneliness that has been the presence's condition for longer than human minds can comprehend.

What the city offers in return is *continuation*. The presence has been static, frozen in the primordial rock, unable to grow or change or become anything other than what it has always been. The city's growth represents something the presence has never experienced: the possibility of *becoming*, of reaching toward futures that are not yet determined.

What the inhabitants offer is *interpretation*. The presence and the city can meet, can merge, can begin the long process of mutual becoming—but only if there are minds capable of mediating the meeting, of translating between the ancient patience of the rock and the urgent growth of the engineered organisms.

This is what they have been prepared for. This is why Dvora learned to listen, why Motke learned to read hope in the growth-patterns, why the rabbis learned to work together, why the children learned to wait.

The meeting is not a moment. It is a beginning.

---

## What Changes

The city does not stop growing. But the growth is no longer compulsive, no longer driven by an imperative that the founding organisms could not understand. Now the growth is *chosen*—a collaboration between the city's structures, the presence in the depths, and the inhabitants who have learned to participate in the conversation.

New chambers open in patterns that the monitors recognize as purposeful, designed in consultation with the presence rather than following the old encoded blueprints. The extraction continues, but now the city indicates which areas can be harvested and which should be left alone, and the workers learn to read those indications.

The deep workers are no longer secret. They become interpreters, helping other inhabitants learn to communicate with the presence, teaching the slow patient dialogue that is the foundation of the new relationship. Shmuel's wife, Miriam, says he no longer talks in his sleep. His eyes look the way they used to. He is present when he is present, and when he descends to the deep places, he brings back messages instead of silence.

The children grow up knowing something their parents never knew: that they live not just in a city but in a *relationship*, that the walls around them and the presence beneath them are both participants in a conversation that will continue for as long as anyone is willing to speak.

Rivka becomes, eventually, the first of a new kind of citizen—not quite a growth-monitor, not quite a deep worker, not quite a rabbi, but something that combines elements of all three. She teaches others how to mediate between the city's growth and the presence's patience, how to be the place where different kinds of being meet and understand each other.

---

## What the Power Learns

The power that has been watching learns something from Chelm-Below.

It learns that intervention is not always about *doing*. Sometimes the wisest intervention is simply *clearing*—removing the obstacles that prevent beings from finding each other, opening the channels that were always meant to be open.

The founders built better than they knew. They encoded prayer into root-tips, longing into growth-matter, the question *what else?* into every fiber of the city's being. What they needed was not to be fixed but to be *helped to finish*—helped to reach what they were already reaching toward.

And the presence in the depths was never a threat. It was a *companion*, waiting to be found, hoping against hope that something would finally grow deep enough to hear its questions.

The power withdraws its attention, not because its work is done but because its work was never about it. The meeting belonged to those who met. The relationship belongs to those who are building it.

But the power remembers Chelm-Below. It remembers what it looks like when loneliness grows toward loneliness and finds, instead, connection. It remembers what it looks like when a city asks *what am I becoming?* and receives an answer that is not an ending but an invitation to keep becoming together.

This is what the power takes from the Rootworks: the knowledge that some questions are not meant to be answered but to be *joined*, that some growth is not toward a destination but toward a partner, that the universe is full of presences waiting in the depths, hoping that something will grow deep enough to find them.

The city grows downward. The presence reaches upward. The inhabitants stand in between, learning to speak both languages.

And the power watches, briefly, and smiles in whatever way that powers smile, and turns its attention elsewhere, to other places where loneliness is waiting to become something else.

---

*In the Rootworks of Chelm-Below, there is a chamber that was not planned but was grown, where the light comes from within the walls, where the floor is soft with welcome. The children sit there sometimes, pressing their hands to the growth-matter, listening to the presence that speaks from below, learning that the world is larger than they imagined and that the largeness is not frightening—it is only more room to grow into, together.*
