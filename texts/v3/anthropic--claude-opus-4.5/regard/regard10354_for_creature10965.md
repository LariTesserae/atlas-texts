# What the Benevolent Power Sees

The attention arrives not as a gaze but as a kind of settling—the way light settles into water, or the way understanding sometimes arrives in the space between thoughts. There is no intrusion. The power that turns toward Terimoss does so with the patience the place itself has cultivated, and for a long while, there is only witnessing.

What it sees:

A people who have solved something. Not perfectly, not completely, but genuinely. They have found a way to live inside impermanence without being destroyed by it. They do not pretend the plateau will last forever; they do not despair that it won't. They have threaded themselves through the needle's eye of that paradox and emerged into something like peace.

This is rare. Across the vast sprawl of existence, most beings either deny their transience or are crushed by it. The inhabitants of Terimoss have done neither. They build roofs knowing the roofs will need replacing. They teach children knowing the children will teach other children who will eventually have no settlement to maintain. They clear drains knowing the drains are delaying an inevitability. And none of this registers as tragedy to them. It registers as *life*—as the particular shape their living takes.

The power sees, too, what is hard.

The woman splitting slate whose hands ache in the cold months, who will develop a tremor in her sixties that will end her work before she's ready. The child who will not survive a winter three years from now, whose absence will hollow out a space in the settlement that never quite fills. The elder whose memory is beginning to fray at its edges, who sometimes forgets which channel floods and which holds, and who carries the private terror of becoming unreliable in a place where reliability is survival.

It sees the slow mineral accumulation in joints, the respiratory toll of decades in saturated air, the particular loneliness of those whose temperament never quite aligned with the settlement's demands but who stayed anyway because there was nowhere else that made sense. It sees the young person who dreams, occasionally, of elsewhere—of dry air and sharp shadows and a life measured in something other than maintenance—and who will likely never leave, and who will likely make peace with this, and who will likely carry a small, quiet grief about it for the rest of their days.

It sees the settlement's genuine precarity: how a single bad season could cascade, how the loss of certain knowledge at the wrong moment could unravel generations of adaptation, how the plateau's geology could shift faster than anyone expects, or slower, or in directions no one has prepared for.

And it sees what is beautiful.

The way the old man's hands move over the drain grate with the unconscious grace of ten thousand repetitions. The way the woman passing slate to the roofer doesn't need to look because she knows—in her body, in her bones—exactly when to release. The way the children's attention isn't suppression but genuine engagement, the way they've been allowed to become interested in the actual texture of their world rather than distracted from it.

The particular quality of care that shows in the spiral carved into the corner-post. The love visible in the garden's arrangement. The shelter by the well that serves no purpose except to offer rest, and that someone maintains anyway, and that generations have maintained, because rest matters and shelter matters and the willingness to create space for both is itself a kind of declaration about what human life is for.

The way the settlement has become, over time, not just a place where people live but a *practice*—a continuous, collective, multigenerational practice of attention and maintenance and presence. The way this practice has, without anyone intending it, produced something like wisdom.

# What the Benevolent Power Does

Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would disrupt.

The power understands—because wisdom recognizes wisdom—that Terimoss does not need to be saved. It does not need to be perfected. It does not need to be protected from the very impermanence that has shaped its people into who they are. To remove the plateau's slow dissolution would be to remove the condition that created their particular grace. To extend their lives indefinitely would be to sever them from the temporal rhythm that gives their work meaning. To make their labor easier would be to deprive them of the practice that has made them capable of presence.

What Terimoss needs—if it needs anything—is so subtle that it barely registers as intervention at all.

---

**First: The power attends to the channels of knowledge-transmission.**

There are gaps. The eldest woman who understands the deepest aquifer patterns has never fully articulated what she knows; it lives in her body, in decisions she makes without conscious reasoning. When she dies, something irreplaceable will go with her.

So the power does something delicate: it makes space. Not by forcing conversation, not by manufacturing urgency, but by allowing the conditions in which transmission might happen. A particular afternoon arrives when the rain softens and the old woman's joints ache less than usual and a young person happens to be nearby with no pressing task, and the old woman begins to speak—not because she's been compelled to, but because something in the quality of the day invites it, and she finds herself describing water-patterns she's never put into words before, and the young person listens with a quality of attention that fixes the knowledge into memory.

This happens several times over the following seasons. No one notices anything unusual. It simply seems like a period in which the settlement's elders become, for whatever reason, slightly more inclined to share what they know—and the younger generations become, for whatever reason, slightly more inclined to listen carefully.

The gaps don't close entirely. They never could. But they narrow, and certain crucial understandings that were at risk of being lost find their way into other minds, other hands.

---

**Second: The power touches the hardest griefs.**

Not to remove them. Not to heal them in any simple sense. But to *companion* them.

The woman whose hands will tremble: one morning she wakes with a strange peace about the future she cannot change, as if someone has sat with her through the night and helped her see that her worth was never only in her labor—that the settlement carries her forward in every roof she's shingled, every technique she's passed on, every young person who learned steadiness by watching her steadiness. She doesn't know where this peace came from. She doesn't need to. It allows her to live her remaining working years without the shadow of anticipated loss darkening every stroke.

The child who will not survive: the power cannot save them, and does not try. But in the winter when the illness comes, there is a quality of presence in the room that no one can name—a sense that the child is not alone, that their brief existence has been witnessed and valued by something larger than the settlement's grief. The parents, in their devastation, find that they can still speak the child's name in the years after, can still feel connection rather than only absence. The grief remains, but it is not corrosive. It becomes, over time, another form of love.

The elder whose memory frays: the power cannot restore what is dissolving. But it offers something else—a reduction in the terror. The old man begins to find, on his difficult days, that others seem to know what he's forgotten, that the gaps in his knowledge are somehow covered, that his unreliability has not made him useless or unloved. This is not illusion; it's simply that the settlement's web of mutual care tightens slightly around him, and he is able to feel held rather than failing.

The young person who dreams of elsewhere: the power does not give them what they want. It cannot. But it offers clarity—a night of dreams in which the elsewhere is seen more fully, in which the dry air and sharp shadows reveal their own costs, their own loneliness, their own forms of confinement. The young person wakes not resigned but *reconciled*, able to see that every life involves foreclosure of other lives, and that the one they have is not a prison but a particular shape of possibility. The quiet grief remains. But it becomes a grief that can be carried rather than one that corrodes.

---

**Third: The power makes one small material intervention.**

Somewhere on the plateau's slope, in a location that will seem, to whoever discovers it, like simple good fortune, there is a seam ofite: slate of unusual quality, denser and more consistent than what the settlement has quarried for generations. It will not make their labor easier. But it will make certain roofs last longer. It will reduce, slightly, the endless pressure of replacement. It will give them—gift them—a little more time, a little more margin, a little more space for rest and teaching and the carving of spirals into corner-posts.

They will find it next spring. A young person, exploring a new drainage route, will notice the particular color of exposed stone. They will mention it to an elder. Within a season, the settlement will be quarrying from the new seam, and there will be a period—a generation, perhaps two—in which the pressure of maintenance eases just enough to allow other things to flourish.

It is not salvation. The plateau will still dissolve. The settlement will still, eventually, end. But the margin matters. The breathing room matters. The chance to do something other than merely survive—to teach more deeply, to love more fully, to carve more spirals—matters.

---

**Fourth, and finally: The power blesses the place.**

This is not a blessing in any formal sense. There is no pronouncement, no visible mark, no sense of having been chosen. But something shifts in the quality of Terimoss itself—something that the inhabitants will notice only as a subtle deepening of what was already there.

The mist seems, on certain mornings, to carry a quality of tenderness. The light that filters through takes on a luminosity that feels almost personal, as if the place itself is glad to be seen. The water's whisper becomes, at times, almost musical—not a melody, exactly, but a kind of harmonic completeness, as if all the thousand small sounds have found their proper relation to each other.

The settlers don't speak of this. They don't have words for it. But they feel it: a sense that their place in the world is *right*. Not perfect, not protected, not promised anything—but right. That their practice of attention has been attended to in return. That their love for the difficult, beautiful, impermanent place they've made has been received by something capable of loving them back.

This blessing asks nothing. It demands no worship, no acknowledgment, no change in behavior. It simply offers what the settlement has always offered itself: presence. The willingness to be *here*, fully, in the slow time of water and stone, in the patient work of maintenance and care, in the knowledge that everything ends and the choice to build anyway.

---

# What the Power Learns

And this matters too—that the attention is not unidirectional.

The benevolent power, in its witnessing of Terimoss, learns something. It learns that wisdom isn't only about understanding impermanence; it's about *building within* impermanence. It's about making roofs that will need replacing and teaching skills that will become obsolete and loving children who will die and maintaining drains that are delaying an inevitable flood—and doing all of this not despite the futility but *as an expression of value*. The value isn't in the permanence of what's created. The value is in the creating, the maintaining, the caring, the presence.

The power carries this forward. Somewhere else, in some other place where beings are struggling with their own impermanence, this understanding will shape how help arrives. The gift of Terimoss moves outward, through the benevolent attention that witnessed it, into a larger web of wisdom that is itself being woven, strand by strand, across all the slow and patient places of the world.

---

And so the power departs—though *departs* isn't quite right either, because something of its attention remains woven into the mist, the stone, the whisper of the water. Terimoss continues as it always has: the daily work, the seasonal rhythms, the slow geological drift toward an ending no one alive will see.

But there is, now, something else beneath the surface of things. A sense that the settlement exists not just for itself but as part of a larger pattern—a pattern in which small places and patient peoples matter, in which the practice of attention is itself a kind of gift to the world, in which building toward an ending can be an act of profound affirmation.

The woman splitting slate doesn't know this. The man clearing the drain doesn't know this. The child learning to navigate the damp stones doesn't know this.

But something in them knows. Something in the quality of their care, the depth of their patience, the particular grace of their living—something in them has always known.

They are Terimoss. And Terimoss has been seen.
