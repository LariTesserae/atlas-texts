# The Watching

The power that turned its attention toward the gray city did not arrive with fire or pronouncement. It came as a shift in quality—the way light sometimes changes before anyone notices the clouds have moved.

For a long while, it simply observed.

It watched a woman named Sera rise before dawn to repair a filtration unit that served her building's water supply. Her hands, scarred from years of such work, moved with unconscious expertise. When she finished, she sat for a moment on the roof, watching the smoke plumes catch the first pale light, and there was something in her face that was not quite peace but was adjacent to it—a kind of settling into what was.

It watched children in a cramped schoolroom learning to read schematics, their teacher patient despite her own fatigue, despite the cough she'd developed that winter. One boy struggled with the symbols, and another leaned over without being asked to help him trace the lines.

It watched an old man who spent his evenings cataloging the hardy plants that still grew in the crevices between buildings. He had notebooks full of observations—which species were spreading, which retreating, how they'd adapted over the decades. No one had asked him to do this. No one paid him. He simply believed someone should know.

---

The power considered what it saw.

These people had not asked for this world. The choices that had stripped the land and filled the air with smoke had been made long before most of them were born. Yet here they were, neither innocent nor guilty, simply *living*—making meaning out of scarcity, finding connection in cramped spaces, maintaining what they could maintain.

The power recognized something important: to simply *fix* this world would be to say that everything these people had built, every adaptation they'd made, every hard-won piece of wisdom about how to live here—all of it was merely a problem to be solved. It would erase the particular beauty of Sera's hands knowing exactly how much pressure a worn gasket could take. It would make meaningless the teacher's choice to show up each morning. It would render the old man's notebooks into relics of a time that didn't matter.

And yet.

The power also saw the children who died of respiratory illness before they turned five. It saw the way the cold took the elderly each winter. It saw the exhaustion that lived behind every face, the way even rest was a kind of labor, the dreams that were never dreamed because there was no room for them.

To do nothing would be its own kind of arrogance—the arrogance of aestheticizing suffering, of finding the struggle beautiful from a vantage point that did not have to share in it.

---

So the power chose a middle way, though "chose" is not quite right. It was more that the power *listened* until it understood what wanted to happen, what was already trying to emerge, and then it became a kind of amplifier for those tendencies.

It began with small things.

A engineer named Tomás had been trying for years to design a filter that could capture more of the particulates from the smokestacks. His designs worked in theory but required materials the city couldn't produce. One morning, he woke with a strange clarity and saw how to modify his design using only what was available—a solution that had always been there, waiting for the moment when his mind was quiet enough to perceive it. He would never know that his sleep had been deepened that night, his dreams gently shaped toward the problem.

The power did this not by giving Tomás knowledge he hadn't earned, but by *removing obstacles*—the chronic pain in his back that fragmented his concentration, the specific anxiety that always made him second-guess his more creative intuitions. It gave him, for a few crucial weeks, access to his own full capacity.

---

Across the city, similar quiet interventions unfolded.

A healer who had long suspected that certain of the hardy plants had medicinal properties found her experiments yielding results, though the power had done nothing to the plants themselves—only helped her notice patterns she'd been too exhausted to see.

A group of young people who had been meeting in secret to discuss whether the city could be organized differently—whether the industrial output could be redirected, even slightly, toward things other than pure survival—found their conversations gaining unexpected traction. Not because the power had changed anyone's mind, but because it had... *eased* something. The fear that usually shut down such discussions early became, for a while, navigable.

The old man with his notebooks received a visitor one day—a stranger who asked about his work with genuine interest. He could not have known that this stranger was, in some sense, moved by something larger than herself. She was a record-keeper from the city's administrative center, and something in her had been stirred to recognize that his observations might matter for planning, for understanding what was changing and what might be possible.

---

But the power was most careful with the hard things.

It did not stop the cold from coming. It did not scrub the smoke from the air. It did not multiply the scarce food or open springs of fresh water.

What it did, instead, was to work within the existing systems of care.

When Sera noticed that her elderly neighbor had stopped answering his door, she felt—she would have said it was her conscience, or simply what anyone would do—an unusual urgency to check on him. She found him in time. The power had not healed him; it had only made sure that the connection between two people who already cared for each other could function as it was meant to.

When the winter was particularly brutal, the power did not warm the air. But a foreman at one of the factories found himself implementing a flexible shift schedule that had occurred to him years ago but had always seemed too complicated. It allowed workers to share warmth more efficiently, to be home with vulnerable family members during the worst hours. He felt proud of himself for finally acting on the idea. He was right to feel proud.

---

The power understood that there were limits to this approach.

It could amplify what was present, but it could not create what was absent. It could remove obstacles, but it could not walk the path for anyone. And there were some forms of suffering that had no elegant solution—only the choice to bear them together or alone.

A mother lost her child to the coughing sickness in the second year of the power's watching. The power could not bring the child back. It could not even guarantee that her grief would transform into something bearable.

What it did was small, and might have meant nothing, or everything.

In the weeks after the death, the mother walked the streets at night, unable to sleep. On one of those walks, she found herself at the edge of the city, where the buildings gave way to the open plain. It was brutally cold, and she had not dressed warmly. She was not consciously planning anything, but she was also not planning to go back.

She stood there for a long time.

The power did not speak to her, did not send a vision or a sign. But it did something with the quality of the silence. It made the silence *full* rather than empty. It made the gray sky and the cold wind and the sparse, gnarled vegetation feel, for a moment, like they were paying attention. Not judging. Not demanding anything. Simply... present with her.

She stood there until the cold began to hurt, and then she went home.

Later, she would not have been able to say what had changed. But something had. She was still broken, but she was broken in a way that could continue. She found herself, in the following months, spending time with other mothers who had lost children. Not to fix them, not to be fixed. Just to sit with the impossible thing together.

The power watched this, too, and recognized it as the most important work.

---

Years passed.

The city changed, though slowly, in the way that real things change. Tomás's filters were adopted, and the air became slightly easier to breathe. The healer's plant-based remedies spread, and fewer children died in the winters. The conversations about reorganizing resources continued, failing often, succeeding occasionally, inching toward something that was not utopia but was perhaps a little more humane.

The old man died, but his notebooks were preserved, and a young woman began adding her own observations to them. The record would continue.

Sera developed the cough herself, eventually, but she lived longer than she would have because the air was a little cleaner and the medicine was a little better and she was a little less exhausted because the systems of care around her worked a little more smoothly.

None of this was enough. None of this was the world as it should be.

But the power had not come to create the world as it should be. It had come to help the world become *slightly more itself*—to help what was already trying to happen find its way through.

---

In the end, what the power did most was simply *stay*.

It remained present, attending to this place and these people, not as a project to be completed but as a relationship to be maintained. It learned the names of things, the histories of families, the way the light fell differently in different seasons. It became, in some sense, part of the city—not an outside force acting upon it, but a presence woven into its fabric.

The people never knew it was there. They attributed their occasional moments of clarity to luck or hard work or the simple randomness of life. They credited each other for kindnesses that were amplified but not created. They took pride in solutions that were theirs, that they had earned.

This was as it should be.

Because the power understood that the deepest gift it could give was not to solve their problems, but to trust that they could solve them. Its role was not to replace their agency but to *honor* it—to clear away just enough of the obstacles that their own wisdom could find its path.

And so it watched, and helped, and refrained from helping, and loved them in the way that a wise thing loves: not by making them need it, but by making itself useful to what they already were.

The gray sky pressed down. The smokestacks coughed. The wind bit at exposed skin.

And life continued, as it does, in all its terrible and beautiful difficulty.
