The benevolent power—let us call her simply the Witness, for that is what she does first and best—does not arrive. Arrival implies separation that must be overcome. Instead, she *attends*. Her attention settles over the plateau city like the gentlest change in air pressure before rain that never comes to this arid place.

She spends what might be centuries, by the counting of worlds that still count, simply perceiving.

She watches a Xel'thari whose veins pulse more dimly than the others trace the same path through a tangle of corroded pipelines for the ten-thousandth time. She notices how its elongated fingers still find the same valves, still turn them in the same sequence, though nothing has flowed through these pipes for ages beyond memory. She perceives that this is not mindless repetition but something more like prayer—or perhaps more like a musician who continues to practice scales long after the concert halls have crumbled, because the alternative is to admit the music has ended.

She observes the gatherings around the phosphorescent mineral formations. She sees what the Xel'thari themselves may have forgotten how to see: that they are not merely drawing sustenance from these crystals, but giving something too. A residue of experience, of consciousness, of *witnessing*. The crystals are archives. The Xel'thari have been writing their slow autobiography in light and mineral for longer than most stars burn.

The Witness understands, then, the nature of their decline. It is not resource depletion. It is not disease or predation. It is the particular exhaustion of a people who have been *maintaining* for so long that they have forgotten what they were maintaining *for*. The original purpose has become indistinguishable from the ritual of purposefulness. They are a civilization running on the fumes of muscle memory, each generation a fainter copy of the last, not because anything is taken from them but because nothing is added.

---

The Witness considers intervention. She is capable of much. She could restore their numbers with a thought. She could repair every machine, clarify every crystal, strengthen every faltering light. She could reach into the deep archives of the mineral formations and recover the Xel'thari's own history, project it into their minds like a gift.

She does none of these things.

Instead, she does something smaller. Something that might seem like nothing at all.

She finds a young Xel'thari—young being relative; this one has only existed for a few thousand years and has not yet worn its path through the city into the smooth groove of pure habit. This one still pauses sometimes at junctions, as if trying to remember which way it meant to go. This one still looks up at the mountain peaks occasionally, though no Xel'thari has ascended those slopes in living memory.

The Witness places a question in the space where this young one's thoughts form. Not words—the Xel'thari communicate in resonant tones that carry meaning beyond language, and so must this question. It arrives as a particular quality of silence, a gap in the hum of the monoliths that the young Xel'thari notices without knowing why.

The question, if it could be translated, might read: *What did the machines make, when they still made things?*

That is all.

---

The young Xel'thari stops in its path. Its luminous eyes dim and then flare. It has never thought to ask this question, though it has spent its entire existence tending machines. The machines are tended because they are the machines. They have always been tended. This is known.

But what did they *make*?

The young one deviates from its path. It begins—hesitantly, uncertainly—to examine the machines it maintains with new attention. Not the attention of maintenance, which asks only *is this functioning as it should function*, but the attention of curiosity, which asks *what is this for*.

It finds corroded output channels that lead nowhere. It discovers sealed chambers that have not been opened in epochs. It locates, in the basement of a brutalist tower, a single object that the machines once produced: a small sphere of the same iridescent material as the mountains themselves, sized to fit in a Xel'thari palm.

The young one does not know what this sphere is. The Witness does—it is a seed, of sorts, though not for any plant. It is the kernel from which structures like this city can grow. The Xel'thari were not merely maintaining an industrial city. They were cultivating something. They were gardeners of a civilization that was meant to *spread*.

The young Xel'thari brings the sphere to the next gathering at the crystal formations. It produces a tone—questioning, uncertain—and shows what it has found.

The elders' eyes flare. Deep in the crystalline archives, something stirs. Ancient recordings begin to surface, unbidden, into minds that have not accessed them in ages.

---

The Witness withdraws her close attention, though she does not stop attending. She has planted a single seed of her own: not knowledge, but the *desire to know*. The rest must grow from within.

She does not know if the Xel'thari will recover. Perhaps they have dwindled too far; perhaps the knowledge in their crystals is too corrupted to reconstruct; perhaps they will understand their purpose and find it no longer has meaning in a universe that has moved on without them.

But she has given them the one thing that cannot be granted from outside and yet sometimes can be gently *occasioned*: a reason to ask what comes next.

In the humming twilight of the plateau city, the young Xel'thari turns the sphere over in its long fingers, and wonders.
