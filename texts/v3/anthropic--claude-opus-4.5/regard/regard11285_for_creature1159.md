# What the Witness Chooses

The benevolent power observes for a long time before acting—longer than the Tenders might expect, were they aware of its attention. It watches Eshla sway to the stone-thrum. It watches a child named Keth learn to read the water markers, her elongated fingers tracing patterns her grandmother laid down decades ago. It watches a bridge fail and two Tenders fall, catching themselves on vines with the practiced grace of people for whom such falls are simply part of living. It watches them rebuild the bridge before the next sleep cycle, without complaint, without despair.

The power considers what it *could* do.

It could stabilize the Vault—slow the stone-birth to a pace that would allow permanent structures, predictable paths, a life without constant vigilance. It could gift them light that doesn't require cultivation, food that doesn't require dangerous fishing in waters that might vanish mid-catch. It could open easier passages to the surface, ending their isolation. It could even speak to them directly, answer the questions Eshla strains to hear in the thrum, explain the purpose they sense but cannot quite grasp.

It does none of these things.

---

Instead, the power does something small.

In the deepest pool—the one the Tenders call the First Water, which has existed unchanged since before their ancestors arrived—it places a single object. Not creates; *places*, as if it had always been there and was only now being noticed.

The object is a stone, palm-sized, unremarkable in shape. But its composition is unique in all the Vault: it contains, in microscopic layers, a record of every configuration the chamber has held over the past seven thousand years. Not words, not images, but *patterns*—the same language the Tenders already use with their marker stones, only vastly more complex and complete.

The stone will not be found quickly. Perhaps a generation will pass before a Water Listener, diving deeper than usual to track a new thermal current, glimpses its faint phosphorescence in the darkness. Perhaps another generation before the Stone Readers understand what they're looking at. Perhaps three or four generations before the Memory Singers learn to read it fully.

But when they do, they will find what they most need: confirmation that their interpretations have been largely correct. Validation that their seven generations of patient observation have taught them true things about their home. And—crucially—the gaps in their understanding will become visible. Not filled in, but *outlined*, like the shape of a missing puzzle piece. They will know what questions to ask next.

---

The power makes one other small change.

In a section of wall that will birth new stone within the next few days, it embeds a thin vein of a mineral the Tenders have never encountered. The mineral has a peculiar property: when water flows over it, it produces a sound—not the deep thrum of stone-birth, but something higher, almost melodic. Different water temperatures and mineral contents produce different tones.

The vein is positioned such that, as the Vault continues its endless reshaping over the coming centuries, water will find it in dozens of different configurations. The Tenders will hear new sounds from their home. Some will be harsh and will teach them about mineral combinations to avoid. Some will be beautiful and will become, in time, something like music—the Vault singing to its keepers in a voice they can understand without needing Eshla's trances.

The mineral is not magic. It is simply something that was not here before and now is. What the Tenders do with it—whether they learn to seek it out, cultivate it, incorporate its sounds into their communication, or simply accept it as another voice in their home—that remains entirely theirs to determine.

---

The power withdraws its attention, mostly.

It does not watch over the Tenders like a guardian. It does not intervene when the next bridge fails, or when a child is born too early and struggles to survive, or when Eshla eventually dies and her accumulated wisdom must be painstakingly reconstructed from the stone records she left behind.

These struggles are not problems to be solved. They are the texture of a life worth living—a life the Tenders chose, that their ancestors chose, that their descendants will continue choosing for as long as the Vault endures.

But the power does leave something of itself in that placed stone and that embedded vein: a quality of *attention*, a sense that this small community in this impossible place has been *noticed* by something larger than themselves and found worthy. Not worthy of rescue. Not worthy of transformation. Simply worthy—of witness, of one small gift that honors what they have already built rather than replacing it with something easier.

---

Generations later, a Memory Singer will compose what the Tenders call a "stone-song"—a pattern of markers that tells a story. This one will describe a time when the First Water revealed a treasure that taught them they had been learning true. It will describe new music from the walls that taught them the Vault could speak in gentler voices than they had known.

The song will not mention intervention, or gifts, or benevolent powers. The Tenders do not think in those terms.

But it will end with a phrase that translates, roughly, as: *The Vault knew we were listening, and so it gave us more to hear.*

This is not quite accurate. But it is not quite wrong either.

And for a benevolent power—one wise enough to know that the best help often looks like coincidence, and the deepest respect is shown by trusting people to find their own way forward—it is enough.
