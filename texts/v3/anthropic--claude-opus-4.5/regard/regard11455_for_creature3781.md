# The Attention of the Quiet

---

There is no arrival, no announcement. The benevolent power does not descend from the sky or emerge from the rock. It simply *notices*—the way a dreamer becomes aware of a detail in their dream, the way understanding settles into the mind without fanfare.

And having noticed, it watches.

---

## The First Movement: Listening

For a long time—measured not in days or years but in the slow accumulation of moments—the power does nothing but attend.

It learns the rhythm of the weeping stone, the way moisture gathers at certain hours, pools in certain hollows, evaporates when the mist thins. It learns the language of the Dreghar: not just their clicks and hums, but the silences between them, the way a mother's hand rests on her child's shoulder when the machinery below grows louder, the way the elders touch the sacred pools with their webbed fingers before drinking, a gesture so habitual it has become invisible to them.

It learns their fears. The deep mines. The vanishing. The sense—never spoken aloud, barely acknowledged even in thought—that the mountains' weeping is slowing, that the sacred pools grow shallower each generation, that something is drinking the stone dry from below.

It learns their hope, too, though they would not call it that. The way a young Dreghar lingers at the cliff's edge, watching the valleys. The way the oldest among them still hum the songs that speak of a time before the machinery, before the metal-smell, when the mountains wept freely and the mist was sweet.

The power listens until it understands not just what is, but what could be, and what the Dreghar themselves might choose if they could see clearly.

---

## The Second Movement: The Small Gifts

The power does not impose. It does not announce itself or demand worship. It offers, quietly, in ways that might be mistaken for luck or nature or coincidence.

**The first gift is clarity.**

One morning—though "morning" means little here, in the perpetual gray—a Dreghar named Veth is hunting near the border of her clan's territory. The mist is thick, and she has lost the trail of a scuttler. She pauses, frustrated, and in that pause, the mist... parts. Not dramatically, not in a great sweeping reveal, but gently, as if the air itself has taken a breath. And through the gap, she sees something she has never seen before: the source of the weeping.

High above, where the peak is usually lost in haze, a vast crack splits the mountain. Water seeps from it, not from the rock itself, but from *within*—a hidden aquifer, ancient and deep, older than the mountains themselves. And she sees, too, the dark stain spreading beneath it: a tunnel, machine-carved, reaching upward like a hungry mouth.

The vision lasts only a moment. The mist closes again. But Veth remembers.

She tells no one at first. But the power is patient, and the gift of clarity comes to others, in their own time, in their own way. An elder dreams of the aquifer, wakes with the taste of clean water on her tongue. A child finds a stone near a sacred pool, smooth and round, and when he holds it to his ear, he hears the deep, slow heartbeat of the water far below.

They begin to speak of it, hesitantly. They begin to wonder.

**The second gift is connection.**

The Dreghar are scattered, their clans isolated by territory and suspicion. But the power notices the threads that already exist between them: shared songs, shared gestures, the way they all touch the water the same way before drinking. It does not create connection; it reveals it.

A hunter from one clan, lost in the mist, stumbles into the territory of another—and instead of being driven away, is offered water. Why? He does not know. But there is a feeling, faint and strange, that they are *kin* in some way deeper than blood. That the mountains' weeping is a single weeping, and all who drink from it share something.

The clans do not unite overnight. They are wary, as they have always been. But they begin to meet at the borders. They begin to trade songs, stories. An elder from one clan teaches a child from another how to read the rock, and the child returns home with knowledge that saves her mother's life when a ledge crumbles.

**The third gift is courage.**

Not the courage to fight—the Dreghar are not warriors, and the power does not wish to make them so. But the courage to look.

The young Dreghar who stand at the cliff's edge, watching the valleys: the power gives them dreams. Not of conquest or escape, but of *understanding*. They dream of the machinery below, and in their dreams, they see it clearly for the first time—not as a monster, but as a thing made by hands, driven by needs, capable of being understood. They dream of the tunnels, and they see that the tunnels are not endless, that the machines are not all-powerful, that the thing drinking the aquifer can be found, and perhaps... spoken to.

One by one, the watchers begin to descend.

---

## The Third Movement: The Turning

The power does not fight the Dreghar's battles for them. It does not smite the mines or heal the aquifer with a wave of divine will. It knows—has always known—that a gift freely given is stronger than a rescue imposed.

But it stays close. It watches as Veth leads a small party down into the mist, toward the hum of machinery. It watches as they find the tunnel mouth, rusted and half-collapsed, and as they slip inside, their claws gripping the metal as easily as stone.

It watches as they encounter the workers below—not human, as they had feared, but something else, something gaunt and gray and tired, creatures bound to machines they no longer remember building, digging upward because they were told to dig, drinking the water because they have forgotten any other way to live.

The power does not tell the Dreghar what to do. But when Veth hesitates at the threshold of the great pumping chamber, when she sees the vast machine drinking the aquifer dry and the hollow-eyed workers tending it, the power offers one more gift:

*Empathy.*

Not forced, not imposed. Just a gentle widening of perception. Veth sees the workers, and she sees *herself*—a creature clinging to a harsh land, doing what must be done to survive, forgetting why, forgetting what was lost. She sees the machine, and she sees *hunger*—not malice, just hunger, the same hunger that drives her to hunt, the same hunger that makes the mountains weep.

She does not attack. She does not flee.

She speaks.

---

## The Afterward

The power does not stay to see how it ends. It does not need to.

It knows that the conversation will be long, and difficult, and full of misunderstanding. It knows that some of the workers will refuse to listen, and some of the Dreghar will refuse to trust. It knows that the aquifer is wounded, and will take generations to heal—if it heals at all.

But it also knows that Veth will return to the surface with a strange, heavy knowledge in her heart. That she will sit by the sacred pool and tell the elders what she has seen. That the elders will argue, and the young will listen, and somewhere in the mist, a child will pick up a smooth stone and hold it to her ear, and hear—for the first time in a long time—the water beginning to rise.

The mountains will continue to weep. But perhaps, now, there is someone to weep with them.

---

## What the Power Understood

It would have been easy to heal the land. To seal the tunnels, to fill the aquifer, to drive the machines into silence. The power could have done this with a thought.

But the Dreghar did not need a savior. They needed to *see*—themselves, each other, the shape of the wound and the possibility of mending. They needed to choose.

And the workers below? They, too, were wounded—by forgetting, by servitude, by the slow erosion of meaning. They, too, needed someone to speak to them, not as masters or enemies, but as kin.

The benevolent power gave no commandments. It built no temples. It asked for nothing.

It only listened, and loved, and trusted.

And in the end, that was enough.

---

*The mountains weep.*
*The Dreghar listen.*
*And now, at last, they begin to answer.*
