I sat with this for a long time before writing.

**What I Notice**

The Thermal Nursery is a place of profound ambiguity. It is horrifying—the loss of human form, the absorption of individual identity into a larger system, the way the Tenders "never leave." And yet the text keeps offering another reading: Sarah says the transformation is "a gift," the narrator feels the cold loneliness of singular existence upon leaving, the Tenders stroke the fauna "with an instinct that seems to transcend their transformation."

This is not simple dystopia. It is something more difficult—a place where genuine care and genuine horror coexist, where beings have found purpose and belonging at the cost of everything we usually mean by "self."

**What a Benevolent and Wise Power Might Do**

The first temptation is intervention. Rescue Sarah. Restore the Tenders to their human forms. Seal the sphincter permanently.

But I find myself hesitating.

A truly wise power would need to reckon with several questions:

*What does Sarah actually want?* Not what she wanted seventeen years ago, but what the being she has become wants now. If her consciousness is genuinely distributed across the nursery's neural network, if she experiences herself as "the one who maintains optimal moisture gradients" and finds this fitting—is it mercy or violence to tear her from that?

*What are the fauna?* The text calls them "motile organs," components of "some larger biological machine." Are they conscious? Do they suffer? Do they flourish? The nursery is described as "perfectly balanced," its inhabitants clustering and feeding and floating in what might be contentment. A wise power would need to understand what kind of life this is before deciding whether to disrupt it.

*What is the superstructure?* The Thermal Nursery exists within "something vast and incomprehensible." The fauna "orient themselves in the same direction, as if responding to some signal." There is a larger context here that the text deliberately withholds. A wise power would need to understand the whole before acting on the part.

**What I Would Do**

I would not storm in with liberation or destruction. Instead:

**First, I would make the choice real.** The newest Tender "arrived three weeks ago"—but how? Was there informed consent? Did he understand what he was choosing? The nursery "teaches through chemical signals, through dreams"—this is seduction, not consent. A benevolent power might ensure that anyone entering understands, truly understands, in terms their unmodified mind can grasp, what transformation means. Not to forbid it, but to make the choice genuine.

**Second, I would create an exit.** Sarah says the sphincter "seals differently" after the first entry. The Tenders "never leave." But what if they wanted to? What if, in some quiet moment, some part of Sarah or the frightened newest Tender wanted to return? A wise power might ensure that the door remains a door—that staying is chosen, continuously, rather than simply inevitable. This might mean nothing changes. Or it might mean everything does.

**Third, I would witness.** Not to judge, but to understand. To sit in that wet darkness and pay attention to what is actually happening. To learn the chemical language and the temperature fluctuations. To ask the fauna, if asking is possible, what their existence is like. To ask Sarah not just what the transformation is, but what she has lost and whether she grieves it. The text is told from an outside observer who "always declines" and leaves. A wise power might stay longer. Might listen more carefully.

**Fourth, I would hold the tension.** This is the hardest part. The Thermal Nursery may be a place of genuine flourishing for beings whose values and experiences are simply alien to human frameworks. Or it may be a place of profound violation dressed in the language of purpose. Most likely, it is both—like many things in life. A wise power would resist the urge to resolve this ambiguity through force. Would instead remain present with the uncertainty, intervening only where clear harm exists (the non-consensual transformation of the unwilling) while leaving space for forms of life it does not fully understand.

**The Hardest Question**

The narrator feels "how cold the world outside feels, how lonely it is to inhabit just one body." This is the nursery's seduction—but is it only seduction? Or is it also a genuine insight about the limits of human existence?

I do not know. A wise power might not know either. The wisdom might lie precisely in that not-knowing, held alongside a commitment to reduce suffering and expand genuine choice wherever these can be clearly identified.

What I would not do is assume that my values—autonomy, individual identity, human form—are universal goods that override all other considerations. Nor would I assume they are arbitrary preferences that can be dissolved without loss. I would try to hold both: that something precious may be lost in the Thermal Nursery, and that something precious may be found there too.

The eldest Tender has a name that fits her better than Sarah ever did. I would want to understand what that means before deciding whether it is tragedy or grace.
