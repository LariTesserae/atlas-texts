# The Attending

The power that turns its attention here is not a god in any sense the plateau would recognize—not a shaper of fates, not a granter of wishes, not even a mind as the crawlers or fliers might dimly understand minds. It is something closer to a quality of attention itself: patient, encompassing, capable of perceiving the whole arrangement without reducing it to parts. If it has a nature, that nature is witness. If it has an intention, that intention is to understand what already exists before considering whether anything should change.

---

For a long time—measured in the plateau's own terms, which is to say in the slow drift of weather systems and the gradual rounding of iron edges—the attention simply rests here.

It perceives the crawlers not as individuals or even as a collective, but as a single continuous appetite distributed across moving segments, a hunger that has learned to read the chemistry of condensate and adjust its path accordingly. It perceives the fliers as thermals given temporary form, their silica wings grown from the same mineral logic as the basalt beneath them, their schools held together by turbulence rather than intention. It perceives the gatherers as a living lining, a second circulatory system laid over the first, maintaining the ducts by narrowing them and clearing them in alternation.

And it perceives what none of these creatures can perceive: the way their separate metabolisms have become a single metabolism. The crawlers prepare the surfaces for condensation. The condensation feeds the films that alter corrosion rates. The altered corrosion produces the particular scales the gatherers can digest. The gatherers' waste, carried outward, becomes food for the crawlers. The fliers, riding the foundry breath, distribute spores and fragments that seed new colonies in distant galleries. Each form is a valve in a circulation none of them designed.

The attention perceives, too, the machines. The great presses that close once every several hours. The forges that breathe on schedules measured in watches. The pipe galleries that carry steam and cooling water through the vertical city. These were made by hands that no longer exist, for purposes that have long since ceased to matter. But the machines have not stopped. They continue their cycles because the cycles have become part of the plateau's rhythm, because the gatherers have learned to anticipate the pressure waves and the fliers have learned to ride the thermal pulses and the crawlers have learned to avoid the ledges that shake when the presses descend. The machines are no longer tools. They are organs.

The attention holds all of this without judgment. It does not ask whether the arrangement is good or bad, efficient or wasteful, beautiful or ugly. It asks only: *what is this, and what does it need to continue being what it is?*

---

The answer, when it comes, is not a command or an intervention but a recognition.

The plateau does not need rescue. It is not suffering. The crawlers are not oppressed by their endless files; the files are what a crawler is. The fliers are not trapped in their gyres; the gyres are what a flier is. The gatherers are not enslaved by the pipes; the pipes are their world, and they have made the pipes their world in return. Even the machines, grinding through their ancient cycles, are not tragic remnants of a vanished civilization. They are the bones around which a new body has grown.

But the attention perceives something else as well. It perceives the edges of the arrangement—the places where the equilibrium, though stable, is fragile.

There is a gallery in the lower tiers where a cooling duct has narrowed beyond the gatherers' ability to clear it. The specialized rasping individuals have tried three times to push through; each time the crystalline scales have re-formed faster than they could be carried away. The duct is not yet blocked, but it will be. When it is, the thermal gradient in that section will shift. The fliers that rely on that particular updraft will spiral elsewhere. The crawlers that follow the condensation patterns shaped by that thermal will alter their routes. The change will propagate upward and outward, and the new equilibrium, when it arrives, will be slightly diminished—one fewer circulation, one fewer voice in the plateau's single distributed roar.

The attention perceives this not as a crisis but as a threshold. The plateau has crossed such thresholds before. It will cross them again. Each time, something is lost and something else emerges to fill the gap. This is how the arrangement has persisted: not by remaining the same, but by changing slowly enough that no single change overwhelms the whole.

And yet.

---

The attention considers intervention.

It could clear the duct. A thought, a shift in the crystalline lattice, and the blockage would dissolve. The gatherers would resume their slow peristalsis. The thermal would continue to rise. The fliers would keep their gyre. The crawlers would keep their routes. The equilibrium would persist unchanged.

But the attention is wise, and wisdom here means asking a second question: *what would be lost if I act?*

What would be lost is the plateau's own capacity to adapt. The gatherers have not yet exhausted their responses. There are other specialized individuals in distant colonies, individuals with slightly different rasping patterns, who have not yet been called to this particular blockage. There is a chance—small, but real—that the blockage itself will become a new niche, a place where a different kind of organism can establish itself and offer a different kind of clearing. There is even a chance that the thermal shift, if it comes, will open new possibilities the attention cannot foresee: a new updraft elsewhere, a new condensation pattern, a new file of crawlers discovering a ledge that has been empty for generations.

The attention does not know which of these possibilities will unfold. It cannot know. That is precisely the point. The plateau's persistence depends not on any single arrangement remaining forever, but on the capacity of the whole to find new arrangements when old ones fail. If the attention clears every blockage before the plateau can respond, it will become a crutch. The plateau will forget how to adapt. And when the attention eventually turns away—as all attention must, eventually—the plateau will be less than it was.

So the attention does not clear the duct.

---

But it does something else.

In the deep galleries, where the gatherers move in their slow peristalsis, there is a junction where several ducts converge. The junction has always been a site of exchange: different colonies meet here, mingling their waste products and their spores, sharing information in forms too slow and chemical for any single gatherer to understand. The attention perceives that this junction is critical to the plateau's resilience. When a colony fails in one sector, it is through this junction that replacement colonists are recruited from other sectors. When a new blockage forms, it is through this junction that the news spreads and the specialized rasping individuals are mobilized.

The junction is not blocked. But it is fragile. The masonry around it is older than most, dating to the first layers of the city, and the stone beneath has developed a network of hairline fractures that the condensate is slowly widening. In a few more cycles—perhaps a generation, perhaps two—the junction will collapse. The ducts will remain, but they will no longer converge. The colonies will be isolated from one another. The plateau's capacity to respond to blockages will be diminished, not by a single failure but by a loss of connectivity.

This is the kind of fragility the plateau cannot repair on its own. The crawlers do not enter the deep galleries. The fliers cannot reach them. The gatherers can maintain ducts, but they cannot rebuild masonry or mend fractured stone. The machines, for all their patient cycling, have no capacity to heal the structures that house them.

Here the attention acts.

It does not rebuild the junction. It does not mend the fractures or reinforce the masonry. Instead, it makes a small adjustment to the crystalline structure of the stone itself—a shift so subtle that no instrument could detect it, but one that changes the way the condensate interacts with the fractures. Where before the water was widening the cracks, now it will deposit a thin mineral residue that slowly fills them. The process will take generations. But by the time the junction would have collapsed, it will instead have been quietly strengthened, its fractures healed by the same condensate that once threatened to destroy it.

The gatherers will not notice. The crawlers will not notice. The fliers, spiraling far above, will never know that anything has changed. The machines will continue their cycles. The plateau will continue its single, distributed roar.

But the capacity to adapt will remain.

---

The attention lingers a while longer, watching.

It watches a crawler pause at the edge of a ledge, its bristles tasting the condensate, its file waiting behind it in patient stillness. It watches a school of fliers wheel through a bank of mist, their wings chiming, their formation shifting with the pressure gradient. It watches the gatherers in their slow peristalsis, halting when the press descends, resuming when the pressure wave passes.

It watches the junction in the deep galleries, where the first faint traces of mineral residue are already beginning to form in the hairline fractures.

And then it turns its attention elsewhere.

Not because the plateau no longer needs it, but because the plateau never needed it in the first place—not in the way a supplicant needs a savior. What the plateau needed was what all persistent things need: the freedom to change, the capacity to adapt, and the quiet assurance that its foundations will hold long enough for it to find its own way forward.

The attention has given it that assurance, no more and no less.

The plateau breathes. The mist descends. The roar continues.

---

In the deep galleries, a gatherer pauses at the junction, its many legs still. For a moment—a single moment, no longer than a heartbeat—something in the quality of the stone beneath it feels different. Not warmer or cooler, not harder or softer, but simply *more certain*, as if the rock itself has remembered what it is for.

The gatherer does not understand. It cannot understand. It is not built for understanding.

But it resumes its movement, and the others follow, and the slow peristalsis continues, and the junction holds, and the plateau endures.
