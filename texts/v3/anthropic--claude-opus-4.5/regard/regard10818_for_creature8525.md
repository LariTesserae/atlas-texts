I sit with this for a long time.

The Frost Warrens and their people present something that stops me from easy intervention—a civilization that has achieved a kind of terrible grace. The Delvers have not merely survived their impossible conditions; they have built meaning from them. Their philosophy of ice-dancing, their ephemeral art, their understanding that they are "a temporary arrangement of matter and energy"—these are not failures to overcome their environment but profound adaptations to it. They have found beauty in impermanence because impermanence is all they have ever known.

What would help look like here? The question is harder than it first appears.

---

**What I would not do:**

I would not warm their world. The Delvers' bodies have spent generations adapting to cold that would kill others. Their blue-tinted blood, their antifreeze biochemistry, their pale eyes—these are not deficiencies to correct but achievements to honor. Warmth would not save them; it would unmake them.

I would not stabilize their geology. This feels counterintuitive, but the Delvers have built their entire culture around movement, around reading the planet's moods, around the understanding that nothing permanent can be built on shifting ground. To freeze their world into stability would be to destroy the very skills that define them. Their children learn to sense tremors before they walk. Their social structures flow with need rather than rigid hierarchy. Their art celebrates impermanence. A stable world would leave them with a civilization designed for chaos but living in stillness—a mismatch more devastating than any collapse.

I would not give them surface-ready eyes or sun-tolerant skin, not unless they asked and understood what they would lose. The "burning above" exists in their stories as something between myth and warning. It is not my place to decide they should want it.

---

**What I would do:**

I would begin with the Great Boring.

The founders have been digging downward for generations without remembering why. Recently they've encountered metallic glass—materials that shouldn't exist at these pressures. Something calls them deeper, and they answer without knowing the question.

I would place something there for them to find. Not a gift that solves their problems, but a mystery that rewards their persistence. Perhaps a chamber where the metallic glass opens into a vast geode, its walls crystallized in patterns that encode information—not instructions, but *questions*. Mathematical relationships they haven't discovered yet. Geometries that suggest new ways of thinking about load distribution and thermal dynamics. The crystals would glow with gentle warmth, not enough to change their environment but enough to create a new kind of sacred space.

This gives them something without taking away their agency. The Delvers are builders, problem-solvers, readers of stone. They don't need answers handed to them—they need worthy puzzles. The geode becomes a library they must learn to read, a project for generations, a reason to keep digging that their memory-songs can finally explain.

---

I would attend to their children.

The child-sensors who sit in new passages, feeling the stone's moods—they save entire districts but carry a weight no young being should bear. The pressure of being a treasure, of knowing that missing a tremor means death for those they love.

I would give them dreams.

Not prophecy, not instruction, but something gentler: in their sleep, they would sometimes find themselves in vast warm spaces where they could run without fear of collapse, where the ground stayed solid and their sensing-gift could rest. These dreams would come rarely and unpredictably—just often enough to be remembered, never so often as to become expected. The children would wake rested in a way they couldn't quite explain.

This costs them nothing and changes nothing about their waking lives. But it means the small ones who carry such weight would have somewhere to set it down, even if only for a few hours.

---

I would strengthen their memory-songs.

The Delvers encode engineering knowledge in music, but oral traditions drift. Important warnings get garbled. Songs about the first founders might actually be instructions for recognizing load-bearing stone—but how much has been lost in transmission?

I would not write new songs for them. Instead, I would make their memories slightly more faithful when singing the oldest verses. Not perfect recall—that would change them too much, make them rigid in ways that don't serve a people who must adapt constantly. But the crucial warnings, the life-saving knowledge, the load-bearing harmonies: these would resist drift just a little more than they do now.

They would notice, eventually, that the most ancient songs seem to survive better than recent compositions. Perhaps they would develop theories about this. Perhaps they would decide the old songs carry some power that new ones must earn through time. They would be right, in a way.

---

I would do something about the bodies.

The Delvers process their dead with careful efficiency—bones become tools, hair becomes rope, soft tissues become lamp oil. This is not macabre to them but a final act of contribution. I would not change this; it is necessary and, in its way, beautiful.

But I would make the lamp oil burn cleaner and longer. Just enough that they notice: the dead provide more light than they used to. The philosophers would debate whether this means the dead are giving more of themselves, or whether something in the processing has improved, or whether this is a sign of favor from the young planet.

It would mean fewer hunts for the toxic cave fish whose oil they currently rely on. Hunters could pursue the ice-burrowers more often, whose meat is nourishing rather than poisonous. The balance would shift slightly toward life.

---

I would leave them one completely inexplicable thing.

In the market galleries where traders haggle in whispers, where a shout can trigger an ice-fall, I would create a small alcove that simply should not exist. Its walls would be warm to the touch—not hot, just warm, blood-temperature—and they would stay warm regardless of what happens around them. The alcove would be too small to live in, too small to grow crops in, too small to matter practically.

But it would be there. A mystery. A place where the elders could warm their aching joints. Where a mother could bring a sick child to rest for an hour. Where someone who has lost everything in a collapse could sit and feel warmth that asks nothing in return.

The Delvers would develop theories about it. They would make it sacred or treat it as a curiosity or argue about its meaning for generations. They would never find an explanation because there wouldn't be one—not in terms they could discover. It would simply be a gift, unexplained, standing as evidence that the universe contains kindness beyond understanding.

---

**What I would accept:**

They might not notice most of this. The faithful memory-songs would feel like their own achievement. The cleaner lamp oil might be attributed to better processing techniques. The warm alcove might be explained as a unique geological formation. The children might forget their dreams upon waking, retaining only a vague sense of rest.

This is as it should be. The Delvers have built a civilization through their own brilliance and suffering. They have earned their ice-dancing, their ephemeral art, their forty-three words for collapse. They do not need a savior. They do not need to feel watched.

But I would watch. Not constantly—that would be its own kind of intrusion—but I would return, again and again, to see how they fare. When a collapse takes a district, I would feel it. When a child is born during stable times and thought lucky, I would know. When they dig deeper into the Great Boring and find the crystalline chamber, I would be present in some sense, hoping they find wonder in it.

And if they ever truly needed something I could give—if some catastrophe threatened not just a district but their entire civilization, if the young planet convulsed in ways that even ice-dancing couldn't survive—I would act more directly. But only then. Only when they had done everything they could and it was not enough.

---

The Delvers end their day cycles pressed close together, sharing body heat and stories, the children at the center of the huddle. Outside, the planet groans and shifts. Tomorrow the maps will need updating again.

But tonight, unknown to them, something vast and attentive holds them gently in its awareness. It does not fix their world. It does not promise them safety. It only ensures that their courage is not entirely alone in the dark.

The hammering will resume at dawn. The ice-dancing will continue. And in the silence between strikes, if anyone listens closely, they might almost feel it: the sense that their small lights, glowing like constellations in the frozen deep, have been seen. Have been cherished.

Have mattered, to something beyond the stone.
