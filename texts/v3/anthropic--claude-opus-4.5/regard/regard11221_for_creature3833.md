# The Regard

The attention came not as a beam of light or a thunderous voice, but as a *noticing*—the way one might suddenly become aware of a small, determined plant growing through a crack in stone. The benevolent power had no name that could be spoken in clicks and whispers, no form that could be carved in obsidian. It simply *was*, in the way that patience is, or the space between heartbeats.

And it watched the Hollow Embrace for a long time before it did anything at all.

---

What it saw:

A Tender named Kesst, her fingers perpetually stained the color of verdigris, pressing her palm against a dying fern and humming a note she did not know she had learned. The plant's fronds, brittle as old paper, curling inward. Her hum shifting, unconsciously, to match the spire's drone—and the fern shuddering, not back to health, but into a gentler death, its last moisture flowing down to feed its children rooted below.

A Watcher called Old Mirrem, so ancient her glow-veins had dimmed to the faintest tracery, running her cracked fingertips over the spiral grooves in the rock for the ten-thousandth time. She did not know what they said. She would never know. But she had spent sixty years convinced that the *trying* mattered, that the spire felt her attention and was, in some way, less alone for it.

A child—too young for a role, still simply *Spiredweller*—catching a Spireborn in cupped hands. Not to harm it, not to keep it, but to look. Just to *look*, with those enormous dark eyes, at something that shimmered and shifted and seemed to be made of the same stuff as wonder itself. The child opened their hands. The Spireborn spiraled upward, rejoined its dance. The child laughed, a sound so rare in this place of whispers that three adults turned to stare.

The spire itself, that broken fang of ancient purpose, thrumming with an energy it could no longer direct. It had been built for something—transport, communication, transformation, the power did not know and did not need to know. What mattered was that it had become something else: a heart. An anchor. A reason for roots to dig deep and for people to stay.

---

The benevolent power considered intervention.

It could bring water. A simple thing, really—redirect an underground river, seed clouds that would learn to gather over this basin, teach the morning mist to linger and deepen into fog, into dew, into the first true rain these people had ever seen. They would weep. They would cup their hands and drink. The plants would explode into growth, the cracked earth would soften, and within a generation, this place would be unrecognizable.

*Unrecognizable.*

The power paused on that word.

It could restore the spire. Awaken whatever slumbering technology hummed at its core, give it back its purpose, its voice. The grooves could light up with meaning, and Old Mirrem could finally *read*. The Spireborn could become what they were meant to be—servants, messengers, tools—instead of these strange, purposeless dancers that delighted children.

*Purposeless.* But the child had laughed.

It could take them away. Lift the Spiredwellers to a gentler place, a world with rivers and shade and soil that didn't fight back. They would adapt. They always did. Within a few generations, they would forget the clicks and whispers, the glow-veins, the way the plants pulsed in time with the drone. They would become something new.

*Something new.* But not themselves.

---

# What the Power Did

In the end, it did three small things.

**First**, it adjusted the spire's drone—not to give it meaning, but to make it more *resonant*. A slight shift in frequency, barely perceptible, that made the hum settle more comfortably in the chest, that turned it from background noise into something closer to a lullaby. The Spiredwellers would not notice the change. They would simply find, over the coming seasons, that their sleep was deeper, their dreams less haunted by thirst. The children would birth more easily. The elders would die more peacefully. A small thing. A kindness hidden in the frequencies.

**Second**, it touched one of the spiral grooves in the obsidian rock—just one, in a chamber where Old Mirrem often sat. It did not make the groove *mean* anything. It simply made it beautiful. Where before it had been a weathered line, it became something that caught the dim fungal light and scattered it into faint prismatic patterns on the wall. Mirrem would find it the next morning. She would trace it with her fingers as she had traced all the others, but this time, she would see the light dancing, and she would understand—not the spire's message, but something else. That beauty could exist without meaning. That the trying had been enough. That she was allowed to rest.

She would die three days later, in her sleep, with her hand curled as if still touching the groove. It would be the most peaceful death the Spiredwellers had ever witnessed. They would not understand why, but they would remember it.

**Third**—and this was the largest intervention, though it looked like nothing at all—the power made a choice about what it would *not* do.

It would not fix this place. It would not save it. It would not transform it into something easier, something more comfortable, something that made sense by the standards of gentler worlds.

Because the Hollow Embrace of the Sunken Spire was not a problem to be solved. It was a *way of being*. The Spiredwellers had built something here—not despite the harshness, but *with* it. Their click-whisper language existed because they had learned that loud voices cracked the silence wrong. Their glow-veins existed because they had eaten what the land offered and been transformed by it. Their rituals existed because they had looked at an inexplicable humming ruin and decided, *this is sacred*, and sacredness had made it so.

To bring water would be to wash away the particular beauty of their thirst.

To restore the spire would be to replace their mythology with mere engineering.

To move them would be to decide that their home was not good enough—when they had never asked to leave.

---

# The Ongoing Regard

The benevolent power did not depart. It remained, in the way that certain kinds of attention remain—not watching constantly, but *remembering*. Holding this small, strange place in its awareness like a name it did not want to forget.

And it made itself a promise:

If the Spiredwellers ever reached outward—not in need, not in desperation, but in *curiosity*—if one of their children ever looked up at the sky and wondered what lay beyond the basin's edge, if one of their Watchers ever decided that the spiral grooves were an invitation rather than a message—

Then, and only then, would it offer more.

A path, perhaps. A door. A way to carry their strangeness out into a larger world, not as refugees from a harsh land, but as representatives of it. Ambassadors of resilience. Teachers of how to thrive in the spaces where thriving seems impossible.

But that choice would be theirs.

In the meantime, the power simply... regarded. With love. With patience. With the understanding that sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a world is to let it remain itself.

---

In the Hollow Embrace, nothing seemed to change.

The wind carved its patient patterns. The plants pulsed with the drone. The Spireborn danced their meaningless, beautiful dances. The children occasionally laughed.

And in a chamber deep within the spire, light scattered through a single groove, making small rainbows on a stone wall, where an old woman had once sat and wondered, and wondering had been enough.
