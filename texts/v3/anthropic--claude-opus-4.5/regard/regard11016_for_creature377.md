# The Watching

The attention came not as a single moment but as a gradual focusing, the way light concentrates through a lens. Across distances that would render the concept of distance meaningless, something vast and patient turned a fraction of its awareness toward this wind-scoured world and the small, dark creatures moving through its bones.

What it perceived:

A Kryll—one of the larger solitary foragers—picking its way along a ridge where ancient pylons still hummed with purpose their builders had long forgotten. The creature paused at a crack in the stone where frost had split the rock, its antennae tasting the air for moisture, for the chemical traces of lichen, for the electrical signatures that meant safety or danger. Six legs found purchase with mechanical precision. Mandibles worked at a stubborn growth of fungus. The Kryll's small electrical field flickered and pulsed as it worked, a language of need and satisfaction spoken to no one.

Below, in a warren carved into the mountainside, a colony stirred through their cycles. Nurses tended eggs in the crèche, rotating them with care evolved over millions of years. Workers carried fragments of dead vegetation through tunnels worn smooth by countless passages. In a deeper chamber, several Kryll clustered around a piece of ancient machinery they had incorporated into their home—something that still drew power from the leylines crisscrossing the mountains, something that kept their eggs warm through the killing cold.

They did not know what it was. They knew only that it worked, that it had always worked, that their ancestors had found it and claimed it and built around it. The machine's original purpose was as lost as the beings who made it.

---

The benevolent awareness considered.

It understood the Kryll in ways the Kryll could not understand themselves—saw the elegant efficiency of their metabolism, the sophisticated electroreception that let them read their world like text, the social bonds encoded in pheromone and spark. It saw their history stretching back through epochs, the slow adaptation that had fitted them so precisely to this unforgiving place. It saw how they had discovered the ruins, generation by generation learning to use what they found without ever grasping what it meant.

It also saw what the Kryll could not: the energy grid spanning the planet was degrading. The pylons that still arced power between peaks were failing, one by one, in a cascade that would complete itself within a few thousand years—an eyeblink to the awareness, but many generations to the Kryll. When the grid finally collapsed, the thermal vents that warmed certain valleys would go cold. The machines the Kryll had unknowingly come to depend on would fall silent. The narrow margins of their existence would contract further, perhaps past the point of survival.

The awareness felt something that, translated into mortal terms, might be called compassion. And with it, a question that had no easy answer:

*What help would actually help?*

---

It could repair the grid. This would be trivial—a slight adjustment to the quantum states of certain crystalline structures, a realignment of magnetic fields, and the ancient technology would function for another hundred thousand years. The Kryll would never know. They would continue their lives in the same patterns their ancestors had followed, scuttling through ruins whose purpose remained forever beyond them.

But the awareness hesitated.

The Kryll had found their own balance with this world. They had shaped themselves to its demands and, in small ways, shaped it to their needs. They cultivated fungus. They built warrens. They had learned to read the electrical fields and use them for communication, navigation, tool-use. They were not merely surviving—they were *living*, in the fullest sense available to them. They experienced something that might be called satisfaction when they found food, something like affection in the way nurses turned the eggs, something approaching wonder when the young ones first climbed to a ridge and saw the vast landscape stretching away beneath the pale sky.

To preserve them in amber, unchanging, forever dependent on systems they could not understand—was that kindness, or a subtle cruelty?

And yet to let them face a catastrophe they could not predict, could not prepare for, could not survive—was that respect for their autonomy, or simply abandonment dressed in principle?

---

The awareness turned its attention to the solitary forager on the ridge.

This particular Kryll was old, for its kind. Its scales were worn smooth in places, scarred in others. It had survived three mating seasons, an unusually long life for a solitary. In its simple nervous system, something that was not quite memory but not quite instinct either carried traces of every path it had walked, every food source it had found, every danger it had escaped.

Now it stood at the edge of a crevasse, antennae extended, reading the air. Below, in the shadows, something glinted—metal, perhaps, or crystal. Ancient. The Kryll's electrical sense sparked with interest. Its kind had learned, over generations, that such glints sometimes meant useful things: warmth, shelter, tools that could be repurposed.

The old forager began to climb down.

The awareness watched, and in watching, made a choice.

---

Not a grand intervention. Not a repair of failing systems or a gift of knowledge the Kryll had not earned. Something smaller. Something that honored what the Kryll already were.

As the forager descended into the crevasse, its feet found purchase on a narrow ledge—and there, half-buried in frost and rubble, lay a device neither Kryll nor their ancestors had ever encountered. It was small enough to carry, simple enough to manipulate, and it did something the Kryll would understand immediately: it detected water.

Not just surface moisture, which their antennae could already sense, but deep water—the hidden aquifers locked beneath permafrost, the veins of ice running through mountain roots, the places where patient digging might yield what this world hoarded most jealously. The device had been made by the ruins' builders for purposes of their own, then lost in some long-ago cataclysm. It had lain here for ages beyond counting.

The awareness did not create it. Did not place it. Did not even guide the forager to this particular crevasse. It simply... *clarified* something. Shifted a few pebbles that had obscured the device's glint. Let the light catch it at the right angle, at the right moment, for compound eyes evolved to notice exactly such things.

The forager noticed.

---

What followed was not immediate. The Kryll were patient, unhurried, practical. The old forager examined the device with the thoroughness its kind brought to all things. It manipulated the simple interface with electrical pulses, as its ancestors had learned to do with other ancient tools. When the device responded—pulsing in a pattern that meant *water, this direction, deep but reachable*—the forager grew very still.

It spent two days confirming the reading, approaching from different angles, comparing the device's signals to what its own senses told it. Then, slowly, it began the long journey back to the nearest colony warren.

Solitary Kryll did not normally visit colonies. But they were not forbidden from doing so, and this forager carried something too important to keep.

---

The awareness watched the device pass from the forager to a colony elder. Watched the elder puzzle over it, test it, begin to understand. Watched the first tentative excavation at a site the device had indicated, the breakthrough into a pocket of ancient ice, the careful harvesting that followed. Watched the colony begin to thrive in ways it had not thrived before.

Watched, generations later, as the device was copied—imperfectly at first, then with growing sophistication. The Kryll had always been able to sense electrical fields; now they began to understand them in new ways, to build tools that extended their natural abilities. The copies were cruder than the original, but they worked. And in making them, the Kryll learned things about the ancient technology surrounding them that they had never grasped before.

Not everything. Not the grand purposes, the cosmic engineering, the civilization that had built it all. But something. A foothold. A beginning.

---

The awareness did not know if this would be enough.

The grid would still fail. The thermal vents would still go cold. The Kryll would still face a crisis that might end them. But now they faced it with tools they had made themselves, with knowledge they had earned through their own patient investigation, with a slightly wider understanding of the world they inhabited.

They might still fail. They might dig deep enough to find geothermal warmth independent of the ancient grid. They might develop technologies their ancestors never imagined. They might adapt in ways no one could predict, as they had adapted so many times before.

The awareness could not—*would not*—make the choice for them.

But it could leave them a gift that looked like a discovery. It could give them a chance that felt like something they had found themselves. It could respect what they were while hoping for what they might become.

---

The old forager did not live to see its find transform Kryll civilization. It died the following season, alone on a distant ridge, having wandered farther than any of its kind in search of things worth finding. Its body was scavenged by the small creatures that cleaned such things from the mountainsides, its scales scattered by the wind, its electrical field fading into the background hum of the world.

But before it died, it stood one last time at the edge of a great crevasse, antennae extended, reading the air. And something that was not quite thought, not quite feeling, moved through its simple mind—a sense of rightness, of completion, of having participated in something larger than itself.

The awareness felt it too. Felt the small, bright spark of the forager's satisfaction, felt the vast, slow stirring of a species beginning to change, felt the patient endurance of mountains that had seen civilizations rise and fall and might yet see another.

It withdrew its focused attention, then. Not abandoning, but stepping back. The Kryll had what they needed. The rest was up to them.

---

Thousands of years later, a Kryll stood in a place that had once been a crumbling ruin and was now something else—part archive, part workshop, part shrine to the ancestors who had first learned to read the old machines. The descendant of colonists and foragers alike, it carried in its genetic memory the traces of both lineages, and in its forelimbs it held a device that could do things the original water-finder's makers had never imagined.

The pylons still hummed between the peaks, though some had fallen silent. The Kryll had learned to repair a few of them. Had learned to build new ones, smaller and cruder but functional. Had spread into valleys their ancestors could never have survived, tapping heat from the planet's core, cultivating new varieties of fungus, adapting as they had always adapted.

They did not know about the awareness that had watched them. Did not know about the small gift that had started them on this path. They believed—correctly, in all the ways that mattered—that they had earned their survival themselves.

And somewhere, at a distance that made distance meaningless, something vast and patient felt what might be called satisfaction. Not pride in its own intervention, which had been so small as to be almost nothing. But something deeper: the recognition of potential fulfilled, of beauty persisting, of life finding its way through the cracks of an unforgiving universe.

The Kryll did not dream of leaving their mountain home. It was the only life they had ever known.

But now, when they looked up at the stars wheeling overhead in the thin, cold night, they wondered.

And that wondering was its own kind of gift—one they had given themselves.
