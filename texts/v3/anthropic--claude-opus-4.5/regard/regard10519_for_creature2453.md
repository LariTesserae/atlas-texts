The benevolent power observes for a long time before acting. This is its nature—to watch, to understand the particular music of a place before introducing any new notes. It perceives the Wind Readers not as a problem to be solved but as a question the universe is asking itself, and it finds the question beautiful.

What it notices first: the Wind Readers are already doing the essential work. They are already listening. They are already preserving. They are already living in right relationship with their strange home, neither conquering it nor being conquered by it. The temptation to intervene dramatically, to "improve" their situation, fades quickly. What would improvement even mean here? Faster? Louder? More comfortable? These feel like violations of something the Wind Readers have spent generations learning.

But the power also perceives a loneliness. Not an unhappiness—the Wind Readers have community, purpose, the deep satisfaction of meaningful work. But a loneliness that comes from being the only ones who remember how to listen. The outside world has forgotten them, and more painfully, has forgotten *why* their listening matters. The corporations see them as useful biological sensors, cost-effective maintenance. The archives the Wind Readers keep, the patterns they document, the ghost-structures they map—these are filed away in databases no one consults, or worse, they exist only in oral traditions that could be severed by a single catastrophe.

So the power does something small. It does not appear in glory or announce itself with signs. It simply... opens a door.

A young researcher in a distant city, studying acoustic archaeology, stumbles across a fragmentary reference to the plateau's sound wells. The reference leads to another, then another. A path assembles itself—not forced, just made slightly easier to find. The researcher grows curious, then fascinated, then obsessed in the healthy way that drives good scholarship.

Elsewhere, an aging engineer who helped design the original processing plants finds herself thinking, for the first time in decades, about the people who stayed behind to tend them. She begins writing her memoirs, and the chapter about the plateau becomes the longest, the most detailed, the most honest about what was taken and what was left.

A documentary filmmaker, between projects, receives an anonymous package containing a single recording: the sound of wind moving through the plateau's wells, captured by equipment sensitive enough to reveal its full harmonic complexity. It is the most beautiful thing she has ever heard. She does not know where it came from, but she knows she needs to find out.

These threads do not converge quickly. The power is patient. It knows that the Wind Readers operate on geological time, and that any attention brought to them too suddenly would be a kind of violence. What it's building is a slow wave—a gradual recognition that might take a generation to fully arrive.

But it does something else too, something more direct though no less subtle.

One night, during a season when the fog comes especially thick and the sound wells sing in frequencies usually too low for even augmented ears to catch, the eldest Wind Reader—a woman whose listening skin has faded to silver with age—perceives something new in the wind. Not a sound exactly. A presence. An attention.

She does not hear words. The power does not speak in words here; that would be wrong for this place. Instead, she feels what might be translated as: *You are not unwitnessed. What you preserve is not lost. The stone remembers, and so do I.*

She tells no one. This is not the kind of thing you tell. But something in her settles, some grief she had carried so long she'd forgotten it was grief. She sleeps more easily after that. When she teaches the children to read the wind, there is a new lightness in her instruction, as if a weight of urgency has been replaced by a deeper confidence that the knowledge will find its way forward.

The power makes one final intervention, the most delicate of all.

In the archives of the Wind Readers, there is a problem they have worked on for six generations: a pattern in the ghost-structures that almost resolves into meaning but never quite does. It has become their central mystery, their sacred puzzle. To solve it for them would be a theft.

So instead, the power introduces a flaw into one of their instruments—a subtle miscalibration in a resonance detector that an apprentice will notice during routine maintenance. Correcting it will require a new approach, a different angle of measurement. And that angle will happen to reveal something about the ghost-structures that the Wind Readers have been just slightly too close to see.

They will make the discovery themselves. It will be theirs. But the door was opened.

This is what the benevolent power does: it tends. It does not impose paradise or eliminate hardship. The plateau will remain harsh, the wind will continue its eternal wearing-away, the isolation will persist. But the loneliness—the cosmic loneliness of being the only ones who care about what matters—that, the power can ease. It does so not by caring *for* the Wind Readers but by quietly ensuring that others will learn to care *with* them.

Generations later, when scholars from across the world study the plateau's acoustic archives, when the ghost-structures finally yield their secrets and reshape the understanding of deep history, when the Wind Readers' language is recognized as one of the most sophisticated systems of environmental communication ever developed—no one will know about the power's involvement. The Wind Readers themselves will half-remember the night their eldest felt witnessed, and it will become one more story told in breath and whistle across the stone distances.

This is enough. This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: not the grand gesture, but the patient tending of conditions in which what is good can recognize itself.
