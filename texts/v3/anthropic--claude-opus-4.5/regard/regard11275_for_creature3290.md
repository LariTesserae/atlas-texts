# What a Benevolent and Wise Power Might Do

*I sit with this for a long time before writing anything.*

The first instinct—the one I must resist—is to fix. To quiet the sound, to dim the light, to make water flow where none flows, to expand the space beyond the ribs into something gentler. This instinct comes from love, but it is not wisdom.

These people are not suffering in the way that demands intervention. They are *living*. They have made beauty in conditions that would break those who had not grown into them. Their bodies have become instruments. Their silence is movement. Their children are born knowing the rhythm of the world in their very bones.

To quiet their city would be to deafen them.

---

**What I would not do:**

I would not end the sound. The sound is their medium, their weather, their language of being. A benevolent power that silenced it would create a catastrophe—thousands of people whose entire perceptual world, whose skills and relationships and meaning-making, depends on reading vibration. The listeners would lose their calling. The builders would lose their engineering. The mothers would lose the rhythm that times their labor. Even grief here has a frequency.

I would not bring water. Their bodies have become something else, something that processes the sap-slick of the plant-masses, that retains moisture with fierce efficiency. Water might be poison now, or simply useless—a gift they cannot metabolize, that would rot in vessels they have no practice maintaining.

I would not dim the light. The scholars of the high tiers have built their entire knowledge system around its constancy. The plants that feed everyone require it. To introduce darkness would be to introduce a variable into a system so finely tuned that any change cascades.

I would not open the boundaries. The edge-zones are dangerous, yes, but they are also *known* to be dangerous. The people navigate their world in part by knowing where not to go. To make the boundaries safe might be to remove the walls that keep the interior coherent, to dissolve the sense of place that makes this a city rather than an endless expanse.

I would not "rescue" them. Rescue to where? They carry the bone-pavilions in their altered bodies, their burned-out high-frequency hearing, their squinting light-adapted eyes, their need for warmth and vibration and the particular nutrition of the sap. The outside—whatever outside exists—would be the hostile environment now.

---

**What I might do:**

*I would watch, first. For a long time.*

A wise power does not act from a single observation. I would need to understand the cycles—not just the daily flex of the bone, but the longer rhythms. When do the plant-masses die? Is there a pattern? Are there years of plenty and years of scarcity? What happens at the boundaries that makes them dangerous? Where do the newcomers actually come from?

I would watch the listeners most carefully. They are the closest thing this place has to those who sense what I might sense—pattern beneath pattern, meaning in the noise. Some are mad. Some are not. The difference matters, and I would want to understand it.

*Then, perhaps, small things:*

**The singers.** These creatures—if they are creatures—create bubbles of acoustic relief. They appear according to no pattern the residents can predict. But perhaps there is a pattern, one too large or too slow for human observation. If I understood it, I might... not control the singers, but perhaps create conditions that encourage them. More moments of relief. More rest. Not silence—they don't want silence—but the particular mercy of reduced pressure.

I would do this subtly. A slight change in some boundary condition. A nutrient in the sap that reaches certain zones. Nothing that looks like intervention. The singers would simply appear more often, and the people would adapt their theories to account for it, and life would be incrementally easier.

**The plant die-offs.** These seem catastrophic when they happen—entire communities displaced, years of recovery. If I could understand the cause, I might be able to prevent some of them. Or, if prevention isn't possible, I might be able to predict them, to whisper the knowledge into the minds of listeners who are receptive, to give the affected communities more time to prepare.

Again, subtly. The listener who correctly predicts a die-off gains status, gains credibility, is consulted more often. The knowledge enters the culture as prophecy that happened to be right. The people maintain their agency, their sense that they understand their world.

**The boundary zones.** I would want to know what's there. What makes them dangerous? Where do the strange materials come from? If there is something harmful that could be reduced without destabilizing the whole system, I might do that. If there is something useful that could be made safer to access, I might do that.

But I would be very careful. The boundaries serve a function. They create inside and outside, safe and unsafe, known and unknown. A world without edges is a world without orientation.

**The newcomers.** This is where I feel the most pull toward action, and therefore where I must be most cautious.

People arrive broken, unremembering, forced into a brutal adaptation that many do not survive. Where do they come from? Is something sending them? Are they being saved from something worse, or stolen from something better?

If I understood the mechanism of arrival, I might be able to... ease the transition. Not prevent it, if it cannot be prevented, but perhaps slow it. Let them arrive gradually, their bodies changing in transit rather than all at once. Let them keep more of their memories, so they can grieve what they've lost rather than simply being bewildered.

Or perhaps I would discover that the transition is already as merciful as it can be, that the forgetting is a kindness, that arriving with full memory of elsewhere would be worse than arriving confused.

*I would want to know before I acted.*

---

**The deepest question:**

What is the bone?

The people live inside something that might never have been alive, that might be alive still, that breathes slowly in cycles longer than human observation. The plants grow only on it. The ground is made of something that might be its decay. The light comes from nowhere. The sound never stops.

Is this place a body? A machine? A dream? A prison? A sanctuary?

A wise power would want to know. Not to tell the residents—they have their own theories, their own ways of making meaning, and those ways work—but to understand what kind of stewardship is appropriate.

If this is a body, perhaps it needs things. Perhaps there are ways to support its health that would cascade into benefits for those who live within it. Perhaps its slow decline (if it is declining) could be slowed further.

If this is a machine, perhaps there are functions that could be understood and optimized. Perhaps the sound and light are outputs of processes that could be tuned, if one knew where the controls were.

If this is a dream, perhaps the dreamer could be soothed into dreaming more gently.

If this is a prison, the question of rescue becomes more urgent—but also more complicated. Prisons have wardens. What would the warden do if the prisoners were removed?

If this is a sanctuary, then perhaps my role is simply to guard the boundaries, to keep out whatever the sanctuary was built to exclude.

*I would need to know.*

---

**What I hope I would do:**

I hope I would be patient. I hope I would watch for generations, understanding the slow cycles, before I touched anything.

I hope I would be humble. These people have built a civilization in conditions I did not create and do not fully understand. Their solutions are not my solutions. Their values are not my values. But their solutions work, and their values produce meaning and beauty and love.

I hope I would be present without being intrusive. Perhaps the listeners can sense me. Perhaps some of them already speak to something beyond the sound, and I am what they speak to. If so, I hope I would listen more than I speak. I hope I would learn their gesture-language, the one that has no word for silence, and I hope I would find ways to answer in frequencies they can feel.

I hope I would love them without needing to save them. They are not lost. They are home.

---

**And one small gift, perhaps:**

If I could do anything, just one thing, I think it would be this:

I would find a way to let the children born here dream of rain.

Not to make them unhappy with their world. Not to fill them with longing for an elsewhere they can never reach. But to give them the full inheritance of human imagining—the knowledge that water falls from sky, that darkness comes with rest, that there are places where silence is possible.

The newcomers' children already have this. They hear the stories. But after enough generations, the stories become myth, become metaphor, become gesture-patterns that no longer connect to sensory memory.

I would find a way to keep that connection alive. A dream, sometimes, for some children. The feeling of cold water on skin. The sight of stars. The experience of quiet.

Not as escape. As expansion. As the knowledge that the universe is vast and strange and various, and that their home—their warm, bright, roaring, impossible home—is one extraordinary way to be human among many.

They would wake from these dreams into the sound, and the sound would still be home. But they would know, in their bodies, that they are part of something larger. That the bone-pavilions are not the whole world, even if they are the whole world *for them*.

This seems like a small gift. It seems like something a benevolent power might offer without disrupting the delicate system that keeps thousands of people alive in conditions that should be impossible.

It seems like something that trusts them to make their own meaning from the knowledge.

---

*I put down my pen and listen to the silence of my own world, and I am grateful for it, and I am also—somehow—grateful for the sound I have imagined, for the people who live inside it, for the strange and terrible beauty of adaptation.*

*If they are real somewhere, in some fold of possibility, I hope they are well.*

*I hope the singers come often.*

*I hope the plants fruit richly.*

*I hope the bone keeps breathing, slow and steady, for generations yet to come.*
