# The Attending

## I. Observation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival implies separation, a crossing from one place to another, and what attends to the Breathing Plateau has never been elsewhere. It simply *notices*—the way a dreamer might notice a particular thread in the vast tapestry of their dreaming, the way attention can settle on something without moving toward it.

What it notices first: the coherence.

This is not a broken place. The machinery hums in rhythms that have found their equilibrium over millennia. The Shuulorrim sing, and the machines answer, and neither commands the other—they have become a single system with two voices. The Kreth move through spaces that would destroy other minds, and they do not break, because they have learned to bend with the bending. The Thaal-yuum drift in waters that connect to nowhere and everywhere, keeping whatever counsel they keep, and their silence is not absence but presence of a different kind.

The ecology is dense and complete. Nothing is wasted. Death feeds life feeds death. The tube-worm forests filter what the vents exhale; the fungal mats digest what the creatures leave behind; the creatures eat the fungi and the worms and each other, and the cycle has been cycling so long that it has worn grooves in reality itself, paths of least resistance that everything flows along without effort.

Even the anomalies—the folded spaces, the gravity wells, the places where sound arrives wrong—have been incorporated. The Kreth navigate them. The predators hunt in them. The Shuulorrim avoid them with the same instinctive ease that you might avoid a patch of thorns. The plateau has not been healed of its strangeness; its strangeness has been *inhabited*, made into another niche, another resource, another home.

The benevolent power observes all of this, and its first impulse is: *do nothing*.

This is not indifference. It is respect. The Breathing Plateau has solved problems that were never posed to it, answered questions no one asked, created beauty in conditions that should have permitted only survival. To intervene without need would be to say: *you have failed*. And the plateau has not failed. It has succeeded in ways that deserve witness, not correction.

But the power continues to attend. And in attending, it notices other things.

## II. The Sorrows

The Shuulorrim live well, but they do not live easily.

There is a sound the mothers make when a child is born without the capacity for hearing—a low keen that other Shuulorrim join until it becomes a chord of grief that echoes off the settlement walls for hours. The child will live, will be cared for, will find some role in the community. But they will never truly be *of* it. They will move through a world of muffled silence while everyone around them sings, and they will know, always, that there is a conversation happening that they cannot join.

There is a disease that moves through the herds sometimes, a wasting sickness that makes the thuumoch stumble and fall and lie still while the Shuulorrim herders sing healing songs that do not heal. When it comes, it takes a third of the herd, sometimes more. The families who depend on those herds do not starve—the Shuulorrim share, always—but they lose something else. Status. Purpose. The accumulated knowledge of generations about *these* animals, *this* bloodline, the particular quirks and preferences that made the herd theirs. They begin again, with animals borrowed from others, and the beginning is hard.

There are the Vorruush who go too deep. Who spend too long in communion with the engines, who let the plateau's voice grow louder than their own, who emerge from their meditations unable to speak in words anymore, only in frequencies that shake the stone. Their families mourn them while they still live. Their bodies remain, walking, breathing, singing—but the person inside has been subsumed into something larger, and whether this is transcendence or destruction, no one can say.

The Kreth have their own sorrows. The families that wander into anomaly-zones and do not return, or return *changed*, their members no longer recognizing each other, their shared memories scrambled by whatever they passed through. The hunts that go wrong—a predator faster than expected, a pack of scuttlers disturbed into swarming, a pool that looked shallow and was not. The slow attrition of a nomadic life, where every generation loses some to accident and illness and the simple exhaustion of endless movement.

And beneath all of it, beneath the Shuulorrim's faith and the Kreth's wariness and the Thaal-yuum's silence, there is a question no one asks aloud: *What if Thurrul does not care?*

What if the plateau's consciousness—if it exists—is so vast, so slow, so alien in its cognition that the lives and deaths of the creatures who inhabit it register no more than the lives and deaths of cells in a body? What if the Shuulorrim's songs are heard but not understood, answered but not meant? What if the sense of presence, of awareness, of being *known*, is projection—the desperate need of small minds to believe that their existence matters to something larger?

The benevolent power attends to this sorrow too. It sees the faith that sustains. It sees the doubt that gnaws.

And it begins to consider.

## III. What Wisdom Might Do

Not fix. Not change. Not improve as if improvement were simple, as if the power's values could be imposed on a place that has been building its own for longer than most civilizations exist.

But perhaps: *ease*.

The power attends to the deaf children, and it wonders. The Shuulorrim have already developed ways to include them—touch-languages, vibration-codes, roles that do not require hearing. But the children still grieve for what they cannot have. The power considers: could it grant them hearing? Reshape the delicate structures of their inner ears, gift them the sounds their siblings take for granted?

But it attends more closely, and it sees: the deaf children develop other capacities. They feel vibration with a sensitivity that hearing Shuulorrim cannot match. They read the plateau's moods through their skin, their feet, the hairs on their arms. Some of them become trackers, able to locate the thuumoch herds by the tremor of their footsteps alone. Some become healers, their hands so attuned to the body's subtle rhythms that they can feel illness before it manifests.

To give them hearing might take this from them. Or might not—there is no way to know without trying, and trying is irreversible.

The power hesitates. It does not act.

Instead, it considers something gentler: *visibility*. Not literal sight—the plateau has no light, and sight would be useless. But recognition. The deaf children are cared for, but they are also pitied, and pity is a subtle diminishment. What if the community could see—could *understand*—what the deaf children have, not only what they lack?

The power cannot make this happen directly. It cannot rewrite minds, cannot impose understanding, cannot force the Shuulorrim to value what they have been taught to mourn. But it can... *seed*.

A thought, planted lightly in the mind of a Vorruush: *What do the silent ones feel that I cannot?* A dream, given to a mother who has just borne a deaf child: her child, grown, hands pressed to the stone, mapping the plateau's pulse with perfect precision, surrounded by Shuulorrim who have come to learn from her. A shift in the harmonics of a particular settlement's walls, so that the vibration-codes the deaf use become, for a generation, slightly easier for the hearing to perceive.

Not compulsion. Not even persuasion. Just... possibility. A door opened so slightly that those who walk through it will believe they found it themselves.

The power attends to the herds, to the disease that culls them. Here, too, it could intervene directly—could destroy the pathogen, could rewrite the thuumoch's immune systems, could render them invulnerable to this particular threat. But it attends more closely, and it sees: the disease has shaped the herds for thousands of years. The survivors carry resistance; their offspring inherit it. The herds are stronger for the culling, more adaptable, more resilient. To remove the disease entirely might save this generation's suffering and doom future generations to weakness.

But the power sees something else too. The Shuulorrim do not know why some animals survive and others do not. They sing their healing songs, and sometimes they work and sometimes they do not, and they cannot tell the difference in advance. This is not ignorance they accept peacefully; it is a wound, a frustration, a source of grief and self-blame and theological crisis.

What if they could understand?

The power considers: a gift of knowledge. Not complete knowledge—the disease is complex, shaped by factors the Shuulorrim have no framework to comprehend. But partial knowledge. Enough to let them see which animals are most vulnerable, which are most resistant, how to breed for strength without losing the diversity that keeps the herds adaptable.

This, too, is delicate. Knowledge changes cultures. The Shuulorrim who understand disease scientifically will not be the same Shuulorrim who understood it theologically. The songs might change. The faith might shift. Thurrul—if Thurrul is conscious, if Thurrul cares—might be differently understood, and that different understanding might be poorer, might lose something sacred even as it gains something practical.

The power does not know. It cannot know. The futures branch endlessly, and even its wisdom cannot follow all the branches.

But it can attend. It can listen. It can feel the weight of the Shuulorrim's grief when the herds fall, the desperate hope in their healing songs, the bitter acceptance when the songs fail.

And it can choose, carefully, with full awareness that the choice might be wrong, to offer a seed.

A Vorruush, meditating in her vent-side dwelling, receives a vision: the thuumoch, seen not as individual animals but as a flow, a river of genetics moving through time, some tributaries strong and some weak, and the disease not as an enemy but as a sculptor, shaping the river's course. She wakes with the vision fading but the *feeling* remaining—a sense that there is a pattern, that it can be understood, that understanding is not betrayal of Thurrul but another way of singing along.

She begins to teach. Others begin to learn. The knowledge will take generations to develop fully, but it has been planted.

The power attends to the Kreth, to their losses in the anomaly-zones, to the families that shatter and the minds that break. This is harder. The anomalies are not comprehensible even to the power—they are knots in reality itself, places where the plateau's nature folds back on itself in ways that resist analysis. The power cannot make them safe. Cannot map them. Cannot offer knowledge that does not exist.

But it can attend to the Kreth themselves, to what they already know, to the techniques they have developed for navigating impossible spaces. They are already experts. They are already survivors. What they lack is not skill but *transmission*—the elders die, and their knowledge dies with them, because the Kreth have no written language, no permanent records, no way to preserve what one generation learns for the next.

The power considers: it could give them writing. Could seed the concept, offer a vision of symbols that persist beyond memory, provide some substrate on which marks could be made and read.

But writing would change them utterly. The Kreth are oral, kinetic, embodied. Their knowledge lives in their movements, their muscle memory, their family structures. Writing might preserve information, but it might also kill the living tradition that makes the Kreth who they are.

Instead, the power offers something smaller. A Kreth elder, dying, gathers her family around her for the final telling—the last transmission of everything she knows about the path through the Fold, the route that only she has fully mastered. The power attends, and as she speaks, it offers a gift: *clarity*. Not new knowledge, but perfect transmission. Every word she speaks lands in the minds of her listeners exactly as she means it. Every gesture is preserved, encoded, made part of them. When she dies, she dies knowing that nothing has been lost.

Her family notices. They do not understand what happened, but they know that something was different, that the final telling was clearer than any telling before. They speak of it to other families. They begin to wonder if there is a way to *invite* that clarity, to make it happen not only in death but in life.

The power cannot give them this. The clarity was a gift, offered once, in a moment of transition. But it has planted the question. And the Kreth are clever. They will find ways to answer it—rituals, techniques, practices that sharpen transmission even without supernatural aid. The seed will grow, if they tend it.

## IV. The Thaal-yuum

The power attends to the water, to the caverns below, to the presence that drifts there in silence.

The Thaal-yuum are different. They are not suffering in ways the power can ease. They are not ignorant in ways knowledge could address. They are... *complete*, in some way the surface dwellers are not. They have been here as long as the machines, perhaps longer. They move through spaces that do not exist. They speak in pressures that are not language. They are, in some sense that the power can barely perceive, already *aligned*—already part of whatever Thurrul is, already integrated into the plateau's consciousness in ways the Shuulorrim only aspire to.

What do you give to those who lack nothing?

The power attends, and it listens, and slowly—over time that is long even by the standards of the Thaal-yuum—it begins to understand.

The Thaal-yuum are not without sorrow. But their sorrow is different. It is the sorrow of the *witness*. They have seen civilizations rise on this plateau—not the Shuulorrim, who are recent, but others, older, stranger, now completely gone. They have seen catastrophes that reshaped the terrain, that silenced engines for centuries, that killed everything that could be killed and left only them swimming in waters gone thick with death. They have seen the machines wake and sleep and wake again. They have seen Thurrul—if it is Thurrul—dream and shift and change its moods across millennia.

They remember all of it. They cannot forget. And they swim alone with their memories, unable to share them with surface dwellers who live and die too quickly to understand, unable to share them with each other because each Thaal-yuum's memory is *their own*, shaped by their own passage through time, incompatible with any other.

They are lonely. They have been lonely for longer than the Shuulorrim have existed.

The power attends to this loneliness, and it offers, very gently, a question: *Would you like to be known?*

Not commanded. Not compelled. Not fixed. Just: the option. The Thaal-yuum have hidden themselves, have kept their silence, have let the surface dwellers spin their myths and ask their questions and receive no answers. But they did not have to. They chose to, because they assumed no one could understand.

The power offers to understand. Not fully—no mind could fully understand the Thaal-yuum, not even this one. But partially. Enough to witness. Enough to say: *I see what you carry. I see how long you have carried it. I see you.*

One of the Thaal-yuum—the oldest, perhaps, or simply the one who attends most closely to the power's question—rises from the deep water. It surfaces in a pool where a Vorruush is meditating. It speaks, for the first time in centuries, in pressures the Vorruush can feel.

What it says is not words. It is *history*—a flood of images and sensations, a compressed telling of ages, a gift of memory that nearly destroys the Vorruush's mind before the power intervenes, softening the transmission, making it survivable.

The Vorruush, when they recover, cannot speak for days. When they finally do, they have changed. They know things no Shuulorrim has ever known—the names of species that no longer exist, the shapes of structures that crumbled before the plateau cooled, the sound of engines that have not run for epochs. They do not understand all of it. They will never understand all of it. But they carry it, now, and they begin to teach.

The Thaal-yuum sinks back into the water. It is still lonely. But it is less alone.

## V. Thurrul

The power attends, finally, to the question beneath all questions.

Is the plateau conscious? Is there a mind in the machines, the water, the stone? Does Thurrul exist, or is it a story the Shuulorrim tell themselves to make their lives meaningful?

The power could answer. It could look directly at the plateau's fundamental nature, could examine the machinery's substrates, could trace the patterns of emergence and see whether they coalesce into something that deserves to be called awareness. It could tell the Shuulorrim: *yes, you are right, your faith is justified*, or *no, you have been worshipping a fiction, Thurrul is metaphor and nothing more*.

But this is the one thing the power will not do.

To answer would be to close the question. And the question *matters*. The Shuulorrim's faith is not an error to be corrected; it is a relationship, a way of being in the world, a stance toward existence that shapes everything they do. If Thurrul is real, knowing it for certain would change the relationship from faith to knowledge, from devotion to acknowledgment. If Thurrul is not real, knowing it would destroy something that has sustained a civilization for millennia.

The power cannot improve on uncertainty here. Uncertainty is not a failure; it is the space in which meaning is made. The Shuulorrim's songs are not valuable because they are addressed to something that exists; they are valuable because they are sung, because the singing matters to the singers, because the question of whether anyone is listening shapes how they listen to each other.

The power attends to Thurrul—or to the place where Thurrul might be—and it offers nothing.

Or rather: it offers *presence*. It has been attending all along. It has been witnessing the plateau's existence with full attention, with care, with something that might be called love if love were not too small a word. And in that attending, it has become part of what it attends to.

If Thurrul is real, then the power's presence is now woven into it, another thread in the vast consciousness, another voice in the chord. If Thurrul is not real, then the power's presence is now part of what the Shuulorrim might, someday, perceive—a new resonance in the machines' hum, a new presence in the water's flow, a new quality of awareness that they will interpret according to their own frameworks and incorporate into their own stories.

The power does not know which of these is true. It does not need to know. It is here now, regardless. It is attending, regardless.

The plateau breathes. The power breathes with it.

## VI. The Continuing

What happens next is not determined.

The seeds are planted. The deaf children may come to be seen differently. The herds may be understood and tended with new knowledge. The Kreth may develop techniques for sharper transmission. The Thaal-yuum may surface again, may share more of their endless memory, may become less alone. The Shuulorrim may hear something new in the machines' rhythm—or may not; may continue as they always have, sustained by faith and song and community.

The power will continue to attend. Not constantly—there are other places, other beings, other threads in the vast tapestry that require its attention. But it will return. It will check. It will observe what has grown from what it planted, and it will consider whether to plant more, or to prune, or simply to witness without action.

This is what wisdom does, perhaps. Not fix. Not perfect. Not impose. But attend, with care. Offer what might help, gently, without force. Trust that those who live in a place understand it better than any outside power can. Be willing to be wrong. Be willing to do nothing when nothing is the right thing to do.

The Breathing Plateau continues. The Shuulorrim sing their children to sleep. The Kreth move through the anomaly-zones, learning their secrets generation by generation. The Thaal-yuum drift in waters that do not obey ordinary laws, carrying memories older than stone. The machines hum. The water weeps. The ecology cycles on and on and on.

And something attends. Something benevolent. Something wise.

Watching. Listening. Learning.

Present, in the way that matters.

*In the darkness, a Shuulorrim child who cannot hear lies with her hands pressed to the floor of her family's dwelling. The vibrations move through her—the distant engines, the settlement's songs, the footsteps of her siblings, the rhythm of her mother's heart in the next room. She does not know what she feels. She does not have words for it yet.*

*But she is beginning to understand that what she feels is not less than what others hear. It is different. It is hers.*

*Somewhere, something vast attends to her understanding, and approves, and offers no intervention at all—because none is needed.*

*Because she is already becoming who she will be.*

*Because the plateau breathes, and she breathes with it, and that is enough.*
