# What the Watching Would Do

The benevolent power—call it what you will: an attending presence, a wisdom that moves through the spaces between intention and outcome—perceives the Undervault Nursery not as a problem to be solved but as something already in the process of solving itself. This is the first and most important recognition.

It does not intervene in the way intervention is usually imagined. It does not send resources cascading down from above, does not arrange for the discovery of this place by authorities who might formalize it into something manageable and therefore manageable-away. The nursery exists precisely because it has evaded such discovery. To expose it would be to kill it, however gentle the hands that reached in.

---

**What it does instead is smaller. Slower. Almost invisible.**

The crack in the northern wall has been weeping water for years, but mineral deposits have been gradually narrowing the flow. Left alone, within eighteen months the seepage would slow to a trickle insufficient for the nursery's needs. The power does not widen the crack—that would risk structural consequences beyond its ability to predict. Instead, it introduces a subtle shift in the mineral content of the water itself, a fractional reduction in calcium carbonate, just enough that the deposits accumulate slower than they erode. The crack will continue weeping. Mira will never know it was in danger of stopping.

The ventilation ducting has a joint that has been working loose for three years, a gradual failure that would eventually disconnect the airflow to the southeastern quarter of the nursery. Deshi, on his next visit, will notice a faint whistle he hasn't heard before—air escaping through the widening gap. He will notice it because his attention will be drawn that direction by a cricket that lands on his hand and then leaps toward the sound. He will repair the joint with tape from his bag, never questioning why today he happened to bring tape when he usually does not. The cricket acted according to its nature. Deshi acted according to his. The power merely arranged for their natures to intersect at a useful moment.

---

**The child is where the power focuses most carefully, and most patiently.**

She is eight years old. She has been alone—truly alone, without even the minimal oversight of neglect—for eleven months. She survives in the warren beyond the eastern gap, in spaces that were never meant to be lived in, eating what she can find or take. The tomatoes Mira leaves are the closest thing to regular meals she has known in nearly a year.

The power cannot make her trust. Trust is not a thing that can be given; it can only be grown, like the plants she has begun to touch. But it can arrange conditions where growth becomes more possible.

On a night three weeks from now, the child will be unable to sleep. The warren will feel wrong to her—not dangerous, just uncomfortable, the way a shoe feels wrong when a pebble has worked its way inside. She will find herself moving toward the nursery without deciding to. She will slip through the gap and find Mira there, as Mira often is at odd hours, unable to sleep herself.

They will not speak. But Mira will be repotting seedlings, and she will be doing it slowly, with the obvious patience of someone who does not mind being watched. The child will come closer than she has ever come before. Close enough to see the soil. Close enough to smell the green.

Mira will not look up. This is important. The power has no hand in Mira's response—Mira simply understands, from whatever well of understanding she carries, that looking would be a kind of demand. Instead, she will continue working and say, to no one in particular, to the air, to the tomatoes: "These need to go into bigger pots. More than I can do tonight."

She will leave three seedlings in their too-small containers when she finally goes. She will leave the trowel beside them.

The child will not transplant them that night. But she will touch them. She will hold the trowel.

---

**The power attends to Bev with what might be called sorrow, if sorrow were an action rather than a feeling.**

Her lungs are failing in a way that cannot be reversed, only slowed. The humid air she insists on breathing is hastening a process that would happen regardless. She knows this. She continues coming.

The power does not heal her. Healing, in this case, would require rewriting years of damage from causes that began long before she found this place, would require a kind of intervention that erases rather than supports. What it does instead is ensure that her remaining time contains what she actually wants.

The singing from the western pipe that she noted in the logbook—she was not imagining it. Sound carries strangely through the underground infrastructure, and somewhere in the levels above, someone practices vocal exercises in a bathroom whose plumbing connects, through a series of joints and junctions, to the pipe that runs along the nursery's western edge. The power does nothing to amplify this. But it does ensure that Bev's visits increasingly coincide with the singer's practice schedule.

She will hear three notes, then silence. Then, sometimes, more. She will never discover the source. She will decide, privately, that it is the nursery itself singing—the plants, the water, the warm breathing dark. This belief will be technically false and functionally true. The nursery is a living system. Living systems express themselves in whatever ways they can.

She will write in the logbook: *Still singing. Learned to listen for it. Always three notes to start, like a question. Sometimes I answer.*

---

**Deshi carries something the power recognizes but does not fully understand.**

The script he uses to label his plants is not from any language currently spoken on the surface. It is a personal invention, a system he developed as a child to write things he did not want others to read. He has never told anyone this. He has never taught anyone to read it.

The power does not pry into what his labels say. Privacy is not an obstacle to be overcome; it is a form of necessary shelter, and the power respects shelter. But it notices that Deshi has been coming more frequently lately, that his work has shifted from maintenance to something closer to devotion, that he stays longer and longer before climbing back to whatever surface life he maintains.

What Deshi needs is to be needed. This is not a complex need, but it is profound, and the nursery provides it in the only honest way such a need can be provided: by actually requiring his presence. The vines would be wilder without his pruning. The yields would be lower. The balance between light and shadow that allows both sun-loving tomatoes and shade-tolerant mushrooms to thrive—that balance exists because Deshi maintains it, season after artificial season.

The power ensures that Deshi knows this. Not through revelation, not through signs, but through the simple accumulation of evidence that anyone paying attention would notice. The Cherokee Purples fruit more heavily after he trains them. The beans climb higher on the trellises he adjusts. The nursery responds to his care in the way that living things respond to care: by living more fully.

He will never write his name in the logbook. But his handwriting will appear more often, noting adjustments and observations in his private script. Mira will not ask him to translate. She will learn, gradually, to recognize which symbols mean *water more* and which mean *ready to harvest*. This will be enough.

---

**For Mira, the power does the least, because Mira needs the least.**

She has already found what she was looking for. Not peace—peace is a word for people who have not lost children—but purpose. The nursery gives her something to do with her hands and her hours. It connects her to people without requiring her to explain herself. It lets her grow things that will be eaten by others, a form of continued participation in the human project that asks nothing of her except presence and patience.

The power does one thing for Mira, just one.

Her mother's tomatoes—the ones labeled *Mom's*, the variety whose name she has forgotten—are not merely growing in window boxes and community plots. They have, through the informal networks of seed-saving and sharing that exist below the notice of official horticulture, spread further than Mira imagines. The power does not arrange for her to learn this. Such knowledge would be a kind of closure, and closure is not what Mira wants. What Mira wants is continuity: the sense that something she touches continues beyond her touching.

What the power does is ensure that the continuity is real. In eleven different locations across the city, in three locations beyond it, someone is growing tomatoes whose genetic lineage traces back to Mira's mother, to Mira, to the Undervault Nursery. One of those locations is a school garden. Another is a hospice. A third is another underground nursery, smaller than this one, started by someone who received seeds from someone who received seeds from someone who took a start from Mira's shelf four years ago.

The taste is still sweet and acidic in equal measure. The skin still splits under tooth pressure. The seeds are still numerous and slippery.

Mira's mother has been dead for thirty-one years. She continues to feed people.

---

**The nursery itself is the final object of attention.**

Not the plants or the people, but the place: the excavated room, the sweating iron ribs, the cistern and the pumps and the uneven light. The power perceives it as a single organism, a collaboration between human intention and material reality that has produced something neither could produce alone.

It will not last forever. The clay walls are stable but not permanent. The iron ribs will eventually rust through. The crack in the northern wall will, despite the power's adjustments, someday clog or collapse. The nursery exists in the medium term—decades, not centuries—and the power does not attempt to extend this into permanence. Permanence is not the same as meaning.

What the power does is witness. It holds the nursery in attention the way one holds a small flame cupped between palms: not to possess it, not to control it, but to shelter it from winds while it burns.

The nursery will outlive Mira. It will outlive Bev. It may or may not outlive Deshi and the child, depending on choices not yet made and circumstances not yet arisen. Eventually it will fail—the pumps will break beyond repair, the lights will dim beyond replacement, the water will stop—and the plants will die, and the pale crickets will find other homes, and the silence will become complete.

This is not tragedy. This is the nature of living things. They live for a time. They create conditions for other living things to live for other times. They end, and the ending is part of the living.

The power knows this. It watches anyway.

---

**In the logbook, on a page no one remembers writing, in handwriting that matches no one's:**

*The work is the work. The water is the water. The growing is the growing.*

*Care for what is here.*

*That is all. That is enough. That is everything.*
