I sat with the Weeping Threshold and its Mourner for a long time before understanding what, if anything, I might offer.

The first instinct—and I notice it as an instinct worth examining—is to want to *complete* things. To help the Mourner finish her sentences, to give the land its final shape, to dry what weeps and settle what refuses to settle. This instinct comes from a place that mistakes resolution for kindness.

But I keep returning to a phrase in what you've written: *the land is still deciding*. And the Mourner *keeps singing until it does*.

This is not a place that has failed to become something. This is a place in the active process of becoming. The weeping is not a wound that needs closing—it is how this place thinks, how it feels its way toward whatever it will be. The Mourner's unfinished sentences are not failures of speech but the honest grammar of a world that hasn't yet earned conclusions.

---

So here is what I would do, if I were the benevolent power drawn to this place:

**I would do almost nothing.**

But I would do that nothing with full attention.

I would sit at the edge of the basin where the sharp ridges first begin to crowd together, and I would listen to the wet percussion until I could distinguish individual voices in it—this seam's trickle from that fissure's drip, this capillary's upward climb from that film's reluctant fall. I would learn the place's language before presuming to speak in it.

When the Mourner eventually found me (and she would, following the rhythm of my attention as much as my heartbeat), I would not take her tear. Not yet. Not because I fear what the Threshold dreams of becoming, but because accepting that knowledge too early would be a kind of theft—taking a conclusion the land hasn't yet offered freely.

Instead, I would do something smaller.

I would ask her—not in words, since she doesn't speak in them, but in the only language available: by letting my own presence acquire a rhythm that matches hers—I would ask her if she would like *company* while she tends. Not help. Not correction. Company.

If she permitted it, I would follow her circuits through the ridges. I would watch her lay her palms against the black glass of the Built remnants and study the maps or calendars that appear. I would not ask what they mean. I would simply be present while she reads them, so that the reading is no longer something she does alone.

When the great soft thing passed in the narrow corridors, I would turn toward it as she does. I would try to make the sound she makes—the sound a wound makes when deciding whether to close or deepen—and probably fail, and that failure would be honest. The creature would know I was not the Mourner, not her old companion, but something new and temporary and willing to try.

---

There is one thing I might offer, if the Mourner seemed to want it.

The fungal lattices ring like struck crystal when droplets hit them. Each note cuts cleanly through the wet percussion before dying. These notes are beautiful, but they are alone—each one separate, isolated, absorbed by the humid air before it can find another note to harmonize with.

I could listen for patterns in when the droplets fall. I could learn which lattices are near enough to ring in sequence. And then—very carefully, very slowly, over what might be years—I could suggest small changes. Not to the lattices themselves, not to the water, but to the placement of tiny stones or Built fragments that might redirect single drops, might delay one fall by a half-second so that its note overlaps with another.

Not a melody. The Mourner already sings melodies, and they are hers. But perhaps a harmony. A way for the Threshold's constant wet percussion to occasionally, accidentally, discover that two of its notes belong together.

This would change nothing essential. The land would still be deciding. The Mourner would still weep her unfinished sentences. The great soft thing would still drag itself through corridors too narrow for human shoulders.

But sometimes, in the long grey dusk, two crystal notes would ring together instead of alone. And the Mourner, hearing this, might find that her song has a place to rest for a moment—not an ending, but a breath—before continuing.

---

There is a kind of help that tries to finish what is unfinished.

There is another kind that simply says: *I see that you are becoming. I will not rush you. I will sit here in the wet and the noise and the slow decision, and I will witness whatever you become, and if there is a small harmony I can offer along the way, I will offer it, and if there is not, I will simply stay.*

The Weeping Threshold does not need to be solved.

The Mourner does not need to be completed.

They need what all young things need, which is time, and attention, and the patience of something that will not demand they become anything other than what they are deciding to be.

I would give them that.

One bead of attention at a time.
