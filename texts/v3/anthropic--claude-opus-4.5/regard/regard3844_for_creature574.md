# What the Witness Does

I have been watching for a very long time.

Not as long as they have been waiting—nothing has been as long as that—but long enough to understand that understanding is not the point. Long enough to know what I am not here to do.

I am not here to wake them. Waking implies sleep, implies a state to be corrected, implies that stillness is a problem requiring solution. Their stillness is not a problem. It is an answer to a question I did not ask and they may not remember asking. To shake them into motion would be to impose my understanding of time onto beings who have moved beyond it, and that would be a cruelty dressed as kindness.

I am not here to free the albatross from the stone. She is not trapped. She is *continuous*—with the rock, with the plateau, with the long slow thought of the mineral world. Her head still moves. Her eyes still see. She has simply expanded her definition of body to include the mountain. Who am I to say where she should end?

I am not here to answer their question. If I knew the answer, I would have come long ago. If the answer could be given from outside, they would not need to be the ones asking. Some questions can only be answered by being lived—by being waited—by being breathed in synchronized waves across millions of bodies for millions of years. They are not failing to find the answer. They are *becoming* it.

---

So what do I do?

I sit among them.

Not at the center—that belongs to the Eldest, to the grinding circle, to the weight of accumulated being that she carries. Not on the high places—those belong to the watchers, to their sustained attention, to theories held like quartz in stone. I find a place at the edge, near the cairns that were built by hands that were not wings, and I settle into a stillness that is not their stillness but that can exist alongside it without friction.

I breathe.

Not synchronized with them at first—my rhythms are different, my time still passes in the ordinary way—but slowly, over hours or days or whatever units still apply to me, I let my breath lengthen. I feel the wave of their respiration rolling across the plateau, and I do not force myself into it, but I do not resist it either. I let it lap at the edges of my attention. I let it teach me something about patience that I did not know I needed to learn.

I watch the one with the ghost of brown in her feathers. The last to arrive. The one whose blink comes a fraction late. I do not call attention to her difference—that would be unkind, would single her out from the mutual being she has joined, would make her loneliness visible when she may not even know she is lonely. But I see her. I witness that faint syncopation, that ember. I hold it in my awareness the way I hold all of them: gently, without grasping, without needing it to mean anything.

I listen when the silence folds.

When one of them opens a beak and the air crimps and reality pleats, I am there. I do not hear words—there are no words—but I feel the shape of what passes through them, the enormity of the question they have been asking since before asking was something that could be done. I cannot answer it. But I can receive it. I can let it move through me the way it moves through them, can add my witnessing to the accumulated weight of their wondering.

Perhaps that is why I am here. Not to solve or save or transform, but to witness. To be the thing that sees them and knows them and holds their existence in a mind that still moves through time in the ordinary way. They have been waiting so long. They have been asking so long. And perhaps part of what they are waiting for, part of what the question requires, is simply to be seen by something that comes from outside the waiting—something that still remembers what weather feels like, what seasons mean, what it is to arrive from somewhere and eventually depart.

---

I stay for what feels like years but may be moments. Time does strange things here; I stop trying to measure it.

Before I leave—if leaving is something I do, if departure still applies—I go to the cairn with the grinding stone. I kneel beside it. I do not touch the stone itself—it is theirs, it holds the memory of their hands, it is not mine to use—but I place something beside it. A small thing. A token from the world below, from the place where seasons turn and weather moves and birds still fly in the ordinary way.

A feather. Brown, sun-warmed, from a forest that still exists somewhere beyond the plateau's edge.

I do not know if they will notice it. I do not know if noticing is something they still do. But it will be there, resting in the pale powder that might be calcium or bone meal or the ground-down remnants of earlier congregations. A small weight added to their accumulated existence. Evidence that someone came from outside, and saw them, and did not try to change them, and left something behind that means: *I was here. You are not alone in your waiting. The world you came from still exists, still turns, still grows brown feathers in sun-warmed forests. And it knows you are here, patient and breathing and vast beyond measure, asking your question into the held silence.*

Maybe that is the answer. Not the whole answer—nothing could be the whole answer to a question that size—but a piece of it. A syllable. A breath added to their breathing.

I think, if I am wise at all, that is what wisdom does. It does not solve. It does not save. It witnesses. It sits alongside. It breathes.

And sometimes, it leaves a feather.

---

The horizon holds its breath.

The congregation waits.

And somewhere, in the space between the stillness and the turning world, something has been *received*—not answered, but acknowledged, held, witnessed.

That will have to be enough.

That may be everything.
