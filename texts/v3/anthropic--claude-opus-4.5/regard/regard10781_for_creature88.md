# The Watching

The benevolent power does not arrive with thunder or light. It simply *notices*—the way one might notice a single determined ant carrying a burden ten times its weight across a vast stone floor. And in noticing, it pauses.

What draws its attention is not suffering, exactly. These people do not cry out. They do not even know to ask. What draws its attention is the particular quality of their endurance—the way a grandmother teaches a child to read the clouds, the way a hunter shares meat with a family whose provider fell to the ravine last spring, the way they sing in the evenings, low and rhythmic, songs that have no words for "why" because the question has never occurred to them.

The power watches for a long time by its own reckoning, which is no time at all by theirs.

---

# The Deliberation

A lesser power might intervene immediately. Soften the winters. Fill the streams. Guide prey toward hungry hunters. Make the paths less treacherous.

But this power is wise, and wisdom often looks like hesitation.

It considers: these people are not broken. They are not diminished. Their lives are hard in ways that would crush softer souls, but they are not soft souls. They have built something here—a way of being that asks nothing of the universe except to be allowed to continue. Their children do not dream of elsewhere because elsewhere does not exist for them. Their elders do not regret because regret requires imagining that things could have been different, and they cannot imagine it.

To change this place would be to tell them, wordlessly, that they have been living wrongly. That their ancestors' struggles were unnecessary. That everything they have passed down—the knowledge of which roots sustain, which caves shelter, which paths deliver—was merely a catalogue of suffering that could have been avoided.

The power understands that some gifts are thefts in disguise.

---

# What It Does

In the end, the power makes three choices:

**First**, it does nothing to the land. The peaks remain jagged. The winds remain sharp. The streams continue their ancient, indifferent courses. The people will continue to earn each day as they always have, because the earning is not incidental to their lives—it *is* their lives.

**Second**, it makes one small adjustment, so subtle that no one will ever notice or name it: when a child is born who carries within them the spark of something new—a question, a restlessness, a vision of what might lie beyond the mist-shrouded peaks—that child will survive. The fever will break. The fall will leave only bruises. The winter will release them into spring. Not all such children will act on their spark. But they will live long enough to choose.

This is not a change to the people. It is a door left unlocked.

**Third**, the power does something strange and possibly unnecessary: it loves them.

Not in a way they will feel, or know, or benefit from in any material sense. But it holds them in its awareness—these small figures moving through the vast indifference of stone and sky—and it refuses to look away. It witnesses their births and their deaths, their ordinary cruelties and their extraordinary kindnesses, their silence and their low evening songs.

It offers them the only thing it can offer without taking something away: the knowledge, held somewhere outside of time, that they are not unnoticed. That their struggle against the mountain is not merely an accident of geography and evolution, but is *seen* by something that finds it beautiful.

Whether this matters—whether being witnessed by a power you will never know exists constitutes any kind of gift at all—the power cannot say.

But it does it anyway.

---

# Later

Generations pass. The mountain folk remain. They do not know they are watched. They do not know that the strange child born in the difficult winter, the one with too many questions in her eyes, was meant to die at three days old but didn't.

She grows up. Mostly she is like the others—sure-footed, quiet, reading the weather in the texture of the wind. But sometimes, in the evenings, she walks to the edge of the high plateau and looks out at the distant haze where the mountains eventually end, and she wonders.

She will probably never leave. The wondering alone might be enough. Or it might not.

The power does not intervene. It only watches.

This is the hardest kind of love: to see clearly, to have the ability to act, and to choose—again and again, across the long ages—to trust the ones you love with their own becoming.
