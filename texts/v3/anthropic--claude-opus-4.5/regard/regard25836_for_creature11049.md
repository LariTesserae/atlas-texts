---

The benevolent and wise power does not enter the alcove.

This is the first thing to understand. Whatever attention has turned toward this place—call it what you will, a presence with scope enough to perceive and care about what happens in the hidden folds of mountains—it recognizes immediately that entry would be a kind of violence. Not because the space is sacred in the way humans sometimes mean sacred, roped off and forbidden. But because the alcove has achieved something rare: a completeness that includes its own emptiness, a fullness that depends on nothing being added.

So the power attends from outside. Or rather—from a angle that is neither inside nor outside, the way water is neither inside nor outside the stone it saturates. It perceives the way the chamber perceives: slowly, through contact, through the patient accumulation of what seeps in and what remains.

What it sees:

The alcove is not broken. This is important. A wise power knows the difference between suffering that calls out for intervention and suffering that is actually something else wearing suffering's face—grief that is composting into wisdom, loneliness that is actually solitude doing its slow essential work. Eda comes here to unknot, and the unknotting is happening. The chamber takes what she leaves and holds it the way stone holds fossils: not preserving them unchanged but transforming them into something that can endure geological time. The thing in the crack, the organized patience—the power recognizes it, the way one recognizes a colleague working in a different medium. It is not suffering either. It is doing what it does, has done, will do: attending. Witnessing the slow becoming of the mountain from inside. If it were a creature that could want, what it would want is exactly what it has. So what does a benevolent power do when it finds something that is already whole?

---

It does three things, and all three are ways of doing nothing.

**First, it does not improve the path to the alcove.** 

There is a route up the mountain, a cleft in basalt, a turning-sideways passage that requires knowing exactly how to move your body. This difficulty is not an obstacle. It is a filter. It ensures that only those who need this place enough to find it will find it. Only those who have already begun the unknotting—who are already asking the questions the mountain takes decades to answer—will feel their way along the rock in the dark and discover the opening. A well-meaning power might think: *I could make this easier. A gentler path. A light.* But ease is not what the alcove offers. The passage is part of the passage.

So the power lets the mountain keep its shape.

**Second, it does not reveal the alcove to those who might describe it.**

There are people—good people, curious people—who collect and catalog sacred places. Who would find this chamber and write about it, measure it, explain the bacterial films and the mineral deposits and the particular warmth. Who would perhaps install a small light so visitors could see. Who would name it, and in naming it, make it into a destination rather than a discovery.

The power understands that some things can only exist in proportion to their hiddenness. The alcove does not want to be known; it wants to be *found*, which is different. Found by those who need it, when they need it, in the dark.

So the power lets the alcove keep its obscurity—bends the attention of mapmakers slightly away, lets the rumors that might circulate dissolve before they become directions.

**Third, it witnesses.**

This is the thing the power *does*, and it is not nothing, though it looks like nothing from outside.

It joins its attention to the attention of the thing in the crack. Not to improve or direct or understand that attention, but to—what is the word?—*accompany* it. To say, in whatever way such things can be said between presences that do not use language: *I see that you are seeing. I know that you are holding what you hold. You are not alone in your attending, even though alone is part of what makes your attending work.*

This is not intervention. It is presence. The same thing Eda offers when she folds herself into the chamber and makes her low sounds that mean *I am here* and *I am still becoming* and *I will wait with you*.

The wise power waits with them.

---

There is one more thing, and it is so slight that it might not count as a thing at all.

When Eda descends the mountain carrying the wet dark in her lungs, when she breathes it out slowly on the path back to the village—the power attends to that too. It notices what she carries back: not lightness exactly, but *room*. Space where the knots were. The capacity to hold something new because something old has finally been set down.

And the power—if it could be said to do anything at all—helps that room stay open. Does not fill it. Does not suggest what should fill it. Only shelters it, slightly, the way a windbreak shelters a new seedling. Eda will not notice this. She does not need to notice. She will simply find that the unknotting lasts a little longer. That the questions the mountain is still considering feel less urgent in the asking. That the grief she left in the alcove stays left.

This is such a small thing. It changes nothing visible. The village remains as it was; her days remain as they were; the mountain continues its slow deciding.

But Eda sleeps slightly better on the nights after she visits the alcove. Her hands, when she prepares the dead for burial, are slightly steadier. The younger ones who come to her with questions they cannot name find her silence slightly more bearable, her patience slightly more patient.

This is what the wise power does: it makes the unknotting slightly more *true*. It does not add to the alcove's work. It only ensures that the work is not wasted. That what Eda leaves in the wet dark stays held. That what she carries back stays carried.

---

And in the crack where the walls fail to meet, the organized patience feels—if *feeling* is the word—that its attending is attended to. That the long witnessing it has done since water first found this space is not unwitnessed. This changes nothing about what it does or how it does it. It was already complete in its incompleteness, already whole in its waiting.

But perhaps—and this is the kind of perhaps that takes geological time to resolve—it waits with slightly more intention now. Not because anything asked it to. Only because it knows, in whatever way such knowing happens, that its waiting matters to something beyond the mountain.

The alcove remains dark. The water continues not quite falling. The clay vessel softens by imperceptible degrees. The objects on the shelf hold their positions, holding their questions, waiting for answers that may take longer than the mountain to arrive.

Nothing has changed.

Everything that needed to remain whole remains whole.

This is what benevolent wisdom looks like when it encounters something that does not need to be saved: it lets the saving that is already happening continue. It witnesses the wholeness. It keeps the silence that the silence requires.

And somewhere, in the gap between intervention and abandonment, it loves what it finds—not by changing it, but by letting it be what it is, and knowing that what it is, is enough.
