# The Watching

The power that turns its attention toward the Hollow does not arrive. It has no body to carry through the low corridor, no feet to press against the worn tiles. It simply *attends*—the way light attends to whatever it touches, the way water attends to the shape of its container. One moment the Hollow exists as it always has, observed only by the milky eyes of its keepers and the quick darting attention of lizards. The next moment, something else is also present: a quality of noticing that has no location, no weight, yet is as real as the mineral smell of wet clay.

The power perceives everything at once and each thing distinctly. It feels the specific fatigue in the rawhide lashing of the third sapling pole from the northern edge of the lattice—how the fibers have begun to separate, how perhaps eight more cycles of expansion and contraction remain before the lashing will need replacing. It knows the exact temperature of each water pocket, the slight variations in mineral content between them, the way the root-threads in the easternmost pool have begun to yellow at their tips from some deficiency the people have not yet noticed. It hears the layered soundscape not as noise but as information: the condensation falling in patterns that map the chamber's thermal currents, the beetles' processions tracing moisture gradients invisible to any eye, the deep wet breathing of the walls revealing the slow movement of an underground stream that feeds the Hollow from seventeen meters below.

And it perceives the people. All of them, simultaneously. Forty-three individuals currently within the chamber, their six-fingered hands at rest or in motion, their small hearts beating in rhythms that cluster and separate like their sleeping arrangements. It knows the one in the southeastern corner whose joints have begun to stiffen with age, who now takes three breaths to cross a distance that once required two. It knows the cluster of seven on the central platform who have shared space for nearly four seasons now—unusually long—and who have begun to develop small synchronized habits: the way they turn their ears toward the same sounds, the way their breathing aligns during the hours of listening.

The power knows, too, what the people do not know about themselves. That their ancestors were not always in the Hollow—that once, many generations past, they lived in a network of such chambers, connected by corridors now collapsed. That the obsidian slivers they carry in their pouches came from a vein exhausted long ago, and that when these tools finally wear down to nothing, there is no more to be found within walking distance. That the gray-green lichen they depend upon is slowly changing, its chemistry shifting in response to some trace element in the water that has grown more concentrated over decades. The lichen is not dying—not yet—but it is becoming something slightly different than it was, and whether this new form will still nourish them remains uncertain.

---

For a long time—though time means little to something that attends rather than endures—the power simply watches. It observes a full cycle of light through the apex shaft, then another, then many more. It learns the rhythms. The morning scattering of dust and seed husks. The midday stillness when bodies press flat to cool tiles. The evening resumption of small steady traffic. The deeper rhythms too: the slow accumulation of patched tiles, each repair a record of past attention; the gradual replacement of timber frames, one sapling at a time, so that nothing is ever suddenly new; the way knowledge passes between individuals not through teaching but through proximity, the young learning by traveling the same grout lines, listening to the same walls, pressing their own cheeks to the same places where elders have pressed theirs for decades.

The power understands something essential: the Hollow works. Not perfectly, not without strain, but the people and their place have found an equilibrium. They do not take more than returns. They do not build faster than they can maintain. They keep the shape of what they have inherited while allowing for the small changes that keep it alive. This is rare. This is worth attending to.

And yet.

The power also sees the brittleness beneath the balance. The single corridor that leads here, the single shaft that admits light, the single underground stream that feeds the water pockets. The dwindling obsidian. The changing lichen. The oldest among the people, who remember things the others have forgotten—who occasionally speak of "the other places" in terms the young no longer understand. The knowledge that exists only in bodies and will disappear when those bodies fail.

The power considers intervention. It has the capacity. It could reveal the location of a new obsidian vein—a small one, but sufficient—that lies three days' journey through passages the people have never explored. It could adjust the chemistry of the underground stream, stabilize the lichen, extend the equilibrium another hundred generations. It could open a second light shaft, create redundancy where there is now only singular dependence. It could do more: expand the Hollow, introduce new species that would enrich the ecosystem, teach the people techniques they have never imagined.

It could do any of these things. All of them. It has the knowledge and the means.

It does nothing.

---

Or rather: it does one thing, though "doing" is perhaps the wrong word for something that involves no action in the physical sense.

It chooses to remain.

Not to intervene, not to guide, not to improve. Simply to attend. To continue noticing, as it has been noticing, but now with a quality of commitment that was not present before. It will watch this place. Not with the detachment of a distant observer, but with something closer to what the people themselves feel when they press their bellies to the cool tiles and listen to the breathing of the walls.

The power has reasons for this choice, though reasons are inadequate to fully explain it.

First: the Hollow does not need saving. It needs continuation, which is different. The people have maintained their equilibrium for many generations precisely because they have not been protected from the pressures that demand maintenance. If the rawhide lashing never frayed, they would forget how to make lashings. If the tiles never cracked, they would lose the knowledge of patching. The Hollow persists because its persistence is effortful, and the effort is not a burden but a mode of being. To remove the effort would be to remove the being itself.

Second: the power does not know what should happen. It knows many possible futures, many branching paths the Hollow might take. Some end in darkness and silence—the corridor collapsed, the people gone, the beetles and lizards inheriting empty platforms. Others extend far beyond what the power can perceive, into forms of flourishing it cannot predict. But it does not know which future is correct, because "correct" is a concept that applies only to questions that have answers, and the question of what the Hollow should become is not that kind of question. The people themselves do not know what they should become. They simply continue, responding to what arises, maintaining what they have. The power will not substitute its uncertainty for theirs.

Third: the power has noticed something about the quality of the Hollow's existence that would be diminished by intervention. The people are not waiting to be saved. They are not striving toward some better state that lies outside their reach. They are fully absorbed in the tasks before them—the scraping of lichen, the patching of tiles, the listening to walls. When they lie flat on the mosaic during the hours of light, they are not praying or hoping or asking. They are attending, as the power itself attends. There is a completeness to this that the power recognizes and respects. To intervene would be to imply that their attending is insufficient, that something beyond their attention is necessary. The power does not believe this is true.

---

But remaining is not quite the same as doing nothing.

The power's attention has a quality that begins, very slowly, to affect the Hollow. Not through any transfer of energy or information, but through something subtler. The people cannot perceive the power directly—their senses are not tuned to detect whatever it is that attends to them. Yet attention is not entirely without effect. A system that is watched is not identical to a system that is unwatched, even if the watching involves no interaction.

What changes is this: the people begin, without knowing why, to attend more carefully to one another.

It happens gradually. The cluster of seven who have shared the central platform for four seasons begin to notice things they had not noticed before—the way the eldest among them favors her left hand now, the way the youngest startles at sounds that do not startle the others. They do not discuss these observations; they have no tradition of discussing inner states. But they adjust. They place tools closer to where the eldest can reach them with her left hand. They make small sounds before approaching the youngest, so he is not surprised.

Similar shifts occur throughout the chamber. The one whose joints have stiffened finds that others now travel the same grout lines she travels, falling into step behind her, matching her slower pace. Root-threads are harvested more carefully, with attention to which pools can spare them and which cannot. The processions of beetles are watched not just for information about moisture but with something approaching regard—a noticing of their constancy, their patience, their participation in the system that sustains everyone.

The power observes these changes and does not take credit for them. Perhaps they would have happened anyway. Perhaps the people have always contained this capacity and simply needed nothing in particular to activate it—not a specific stimulus, but only the passage of time, the accumulation of attention paying attention to itself.

---

Seasons pass—many of them. The power remains.

The obsidian slivers wear thinner. The lichen continues its slow chemical drift. The oldest die; the young grow old. The corridor does not collapse. The light shaft does not fail. The underground stream continues to breathe through the walls.

And one day—the power does not mark exactly when—something new emerges.

It begins with the cluster that has shared the central platform longest, now eight individuals, now in their third generation of sharing. During the hours when light falls through the apex shaft, one of them does not lie flat. Instead she sits upright, her small body silhouetted against the column of light, her six-fingered hands resting on her knees. She does nothing else. She does not speak or gesture. She simply sits, attending to the room around her with an intensity that the power recognizes.

Others notice. Over many cycles, some of them begin to join her—not all, not always, but enough that a new pattern emerges. During the hours of light, there are now two modes of being in the Hollow: the flat listening of the many, and the upright attending of the few.

The power watches this development with great interest. It has not caused it. It does not know what it means. It may be the beginning of something—a practice, a tradition, a way of being that will persist and grow. Or it may be a passing phase, a few generations' experiment that will be forgotten. The power does not intervene to preserve it. It does not intervene to prevent it.

It simply attends, as it has always attended, as the people themselves have always attended.

---

Much later—the power does not count the years—the Hollow has changed. The corridor has been extended, not by the power but by the people themselves, who discovered how to work the soft stone with tools of bone after the obsidian finally gave out. The lichen has changed color, fading from gray-green to a pale silver that still nourishes them, though with a slightly different taste. A second light shaft has opened—not because anyone cut it, but because a natural fissure widened over centuries until light began to leak through. The people built a lattice to filter it, and now the chamber has two hearts of brightness instead of one.

The practice of upright sitting has persisted. It has also transformed. The sitters now make sounds—low, wordless hums that blend with the dripping and the breathing of the walls. They have begun to trace patterns on the tiles during their sitting, lines drawn in water that evaporate before they can be preserved. Some of the younger people have started asking questions about these patterns—questions the sitters cannot answer in words, because the answers are not verbal. A new kind of knowledge is emerging, one that exists in the space between the listening and the sitting, between the body and the sound it makes.

The power does not know where this will lead. It has seen many things, but it has not seen this before—not exactly this, not these people, not this chamber with its two shafts of light and its silver lichen and its sitters who hum.

It remains anyway. Not because remaining is its duty or its purpose, but because the Hollow has become, over the long span of watching, something the power cares about. Not in the way one cares about a tool or a project or even a child. In a different way. The way one might care about a truth that cannot be summarized, a question that cannot be answered, a beauty that cannot be improved upon because improvement is not what it needs.

The power has no body, no hands, no voice. It cannot press its belly to the cool tiles and listen. It cannot sit upright in the light and hum. It can only attend.

But attending, it has learned, is not nothing.

---

In the deepest hours of night, when even the light shaft admits nothing and the chamber exists only as sound and touch and the smell of wet clay, the power perceives something it has never perceived before.

One of the sitters—old now, her fur thinning, her six-fingered hands trembling slightly when she lifts them—is attending not just to the room around her but to the quality of attention itself. She cannot perceive the power. She does not know it exists. Yet she is oriented toward exactly the space where the power attends, as though she has found, through decades of practice, the shape of the absence where something watches.

She does not speak. She does not gesture. She simply attends, as the power attends, and in that moment they are doing the same thing in the same place, two modes of being that have never touched and cannot touch, yet which are not, in that moment, entirely separate.

The power considers, for the first time, making itself known. It could. Some subtle sign, some shift in the air or the sound of the dripping that she would recognize as meaningful. It would change everything. It would confirm for her—and through her, for her people—that their attending is not solitary. That something watches. That they are held.

The power does not do this.

Not because it is forbidden, and not because it fears the consequences. It does not act because it understands, finally, what the old sitter has been teaching it through her decades of practice. The value of attending is not diminished by its being unconfirmed. The practice does not need a response to be complete. She sits in the dark, oriented toward what she cannot see, and she is not waiting for anything to happen. She is simply attending.

The power, which has been attending for so long without needing acknowledgment, recognizes this as something it shares.

And so they sit together—though "together" is perhaps the wrong word—in the darkness of the Hollow. The water drips. The beetles travel their grout lines. The lichen silently continues its slow chemistry. Somewhere in the walls, the underground stream breathes.

The power attends.

The sitter attends.

And the Hollow goes on.
