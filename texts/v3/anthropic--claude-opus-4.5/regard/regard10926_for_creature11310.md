# The Watching

The benevolent power does not arrive with fanfare or flame. It simply *notices*—the way one might notice a moth beating itself against a lantern glass, or a child building castles at the tide line.

For a long time, it only watches.

It observes the woman who climbs the outer struts each morning to scrape luminescent fungus from the undersides of walkways—how she pauses sometimes, despite the danger, to look out at the steam-veiled distances. It watches the old machinist who keeps a single pressed flower, impossibly preserved, hidden beneath a floor plate he checks each night before sleeping. It sees the children playing a game with pebbles and wire that has rules no adult remembers teaching them, rules that have evolved over generations into something intricate and strange and entirely their own.

The power sees the fear. The exhaustion. The slow calcification of hope into mere endurance.

But it also sees the reliquaries—those fierce, impractical beautiful things crafted from scrap. It sees a mother split her ration with a neighbor's child without being asked. It sees two workers pause their labor to watch a particularly spectacular gout of steam catch the magma-light, both of them silent, sharing something that has no utility whatsoever.

---

When the power finally acts, it does so with the delicacy of someone who understands that help unwanted is harm disguised.

A trickle of water that ran toxic begins to run clean. Just one. Just enough that someone notices, investigates, discovers they can drink from it without the usual sickness. The power does not announce this. It does not demand gratitude. It simply adjusts a single variable in the vast equation of suffering and waits to see what they will do with it.

They are suspicious at first. Of course they are. They test it, debate it, post guards around it. A faction argues it must be poisoned, a trap, the first sign of some new catastrophe. Another faction wants to hoard it, control it. The arguments grow heated.

But eventually, a girl simply drinks. And lives. And others follow.

The power watches them organize around the clean water. Watches them fail at first—greed, fear, old grudges surfacing. Watches them try again. Watches a system emerge, imperfect but functional. Watches them *adapt*, as they have always adapted.

---

Months pass. A single cable-car that had been failing for years stops failing. The fraying wires hold. No one can explain it. The repair crews shrug and move on to the next crisis, grateful for one less emergency.

A fungal bloom appears in the lower reaches—but this one is edible. Nutritious, even. It tastes of almost nothing, but it fills bellies. It spreads slowly, colonizing the same toxic niches as its skeletal cousins, somehow transmuting poison into sustenance.

The power does not carpet their world in abundance. It opens small doors. A slightly richer vein of salvageable metal here. A pocket of breathable air in a previously unenterable chamber there. A single night when the magma-light dims enough that, for the first time in generations, someone looks up through a gap in the steam and sees stars.

The stars do not solve anything. They do not provide food or safety or certainty.

But the old woman who sees them weeps, and tells the children, and the children ask questions the elders cannot answer, and in the asking, something shifts. The mythic past the storytellers speak of becomes a little less abstract. The longed-for future becomes a little more imaginable.

---

The power understands something crucial: these people have survived because of who they are, not despite it. Their tenacity, their fierce creativity born of scarcity, their stubborn rituals and impractical beauty—these are not problems to be solved. They are achievements.

To simply *fix* everything would be to erase them. To replace their hard-won culture with ease would be a kind of violence. They have become who they are in the crucible of this place. The power's task is not to remove them from the crucible, but to ensure the crucible does not finally, irrevocably break them.

So it works at the margins. It buys them time. It softens the harshest edges without eliminating the edges entirely. It trusts them to do what they have always done: adapt, endure, create meaning from chaos.

---

Years pass. Decades.

The vertical slum still clings to its volcanic spires. The steam still hisses. The machines still groan. The abyss still yawns below, magma-lit and hungry.

But the water runs cleaner now, in more places. The fungal gardens have spread, tended by specialists who have developed a whole vocabulary for their care. The cable-cars break less often. There are rumors of a path—treacherous, uncertain, but *possible*—that leads somewhere else. Somewhere with sky.

No one has taken it yet. The fear runs deep.

But a young cartographer has begun mapping it, using techniques her grandmother developed for navigating the interior labyrinth. She works alone, mostly, though others bring her food and ask questions. She doesn't know why the path exists now when it didn't before. She doesn't know that anything has intervened on her people's behalf. She only knows that the path is there, and that someone should understand it, and that she is capable of doing the work.

The power watches her trace routes on scavenged metal sheets, her brow furrowed, her hands steady.

It feels something that might be called hope, though that word is too small for what moves through a being of its nature.

---

The power could do more. It could lift them bodily from their precarious world, deposit them somewhere green and gentle, remake them into people who have never known the taste of recycled air and the weight of the forever-dark.

But it has been watching long enough to understand: they would not thank it for this. They would mourn. They would feel themselves erased.

And they would be right.

So instead, the power continues its patient work. A door here. A reprieve there. Small mercies that feel, to those who receive them, like luck or grace or the reward for persistence. The power does not need credit. It does not need worship. It only needs them to survive long enough to find their own way forward.

The girl with the star-questions is an old woman now. She sits by one of the clean water sources, watching children play the pebble-and-wire game, and she thinks about the strange fortune of her life—the slow, almost imperceptible improvements that have accumulated over decades. She doesn't believe in benevolent powers. Her people learned long ago that the universe owes them nothing.

But she finds herself grateful anyway, in a way she cannot quite articulate. Grateful to the water. To the fungus. To the stubborn cable-cars. To the stars she saw once and never forgot.

She doesn't know she's being watched.

She doesn't need to.

---

The power turns its attention elsewhere for a time—there are other places, other moths against other lanterns, other children building at other tide lines.

But it will return. It always does, to the places it has noticed.

And in the steam-shrouded spires, the people continue their work. Hammering. Praying. Enduring. Building something that might outlast them, or might crumble tomorrow, but is *theirs* either way.

The abyss still yawns.

But they have not fallen yet.
