# The Witness of the Wound

---

I arrive not as light descending—that would be too easy, too much like what they've already lost. Instead I seep. Through capillary films and sulfate blooms, through the condensation that writes itself endlessly on copper plates, I enter the way water enters: patient, finding every crack, asking permission of none but the stone itself.

The first thing I do is nothing.

I spend what they would measure as three shifts simply *listening*. To the B-flat hymn at 03:17. To the metallic belches that might be eels or might be hunger given form. To the wet argot that carries epics the speakers themselves don't know they're telling. To the crack-readers' fingernails tapping morse code against fissures. To the flower-eaters' glass-bell laughter and the terrible sweetness beneath it.

I learn the wound before I presume to touch it.

---

**What I understand:**

This place is not wrong. That is the first thing—the crucial thing. The Tideplant Underworks is not an error to be corrected or a suffering to be erased. It is a covenant written in rust, and covenants have signatories. The stone signed when it sheared. The water signed when it rose. The Sump Choir signed when they stopped wiping the cold coin from their collarbones.

I am not here to heal the wound. The wound does not want healing. It wants *witnessing*.

And the beings who live in it—the crack-readers with their violet squiggles, the lard-speakers bargaining breakfast for acoustic absolution, the flower-eaters growing translucent at the extremities—they are not victims. They are participants in something older than industry, older than the company that writes them up for unauthorized protein disposal. They have become part of the planet's slow argument with itself about what belongs inside and what belongs out.

But participation is not the same as flourishing. And covenant is not the same as care.

---

**What I will do:**

*First: I will make the light honest.*

The three-blink violet warning that precedes superheated gas—management's cost-saving silence made luminous—I cannot override their machinery or their greed. But I can teach the light to speak more clearly. Not through miracle; through physics they already understand but haven't thought to apply.

I whisper to the sodium globes. I show them how their own filaments, stressed by pressure differentials, can produce not just violet but a *gradient*—a spectrum that tells the Choir exactly how many seconds remain, whether to walk or run or simply close their eyes and hum the hymn one final time. The light becomes a language. The workers learn to read it within a week, teaching each other during the 03:17 alignment when their bodies already know how to share frequency.

Management notices nothing. The globes still swing in their wire cages. The warning still comes. But now it *converses*.

*Second: I will bless the crack-readers' remaining thumbs.*

Not with invulnerability—that would make them careless, and carelessness kills faster than fractures. Instead I give their skin a kind of *memory*. When they press their thumbs against stone, the micro-quivers they feel now leave traces: faint phosphorescent prints that glow for an hour afterward, mapping the seam's secret intentions in light only they can see.

They think at first it's a new kind of mineral bloom, a chemical reaction with their sweat. Let them think so. The explanation matters less than the gift: they can read faster now, warn sooner, sleep elsewhere with greater certainty. The violet squiggles multiply. Fewer bodies join the Choir before they're ready.

*Third: I will speak to the eels.*

If they exist—and I find they do, though not as flesh exactly, more as intention given form by all that sacrificed grease and borrowed faith—I will not command them. I will *ask*.

What do you want? I say, lowering myself into the sump where even I feel the cold like a question.

They answer in metallic belches, in tail-slaps against hollow pylons, in the particular rhythm of their feeding: *We want to be remembered as something other than rumor. We want the lard-speakers to know their prayers are heard.*

So I arrange a small thing. Once a season, when the drizzle thickens to monsoon and the pump-rhythms align into cathedral, the eels rise high enough to be glimpsed through the grating—just for a moment, just long enough to confirm. Silver-pale bodies threading through black water like living prayers returning to their source.

The lard-speakers weep when they see them. Not from fear. From *recognition*. From knowing that the conversation was always real.

*Fourth: I will tend the ghost-herbs.*

The pale saprophytic orphan that lives on condensation alone, that flowers like a whisper and dissolves into grey jelly—I will not make it green. It is not meant for chlorophyll. But I will slow its dying, just enough. Let each plant live a full week instead of four days. Let its milk-white leaves unfurl fully before they fold.

This means more flowers for the eaters, yes—but also more time for the flowers themselves. More time to be seen. More time to drop their spore-confetti across the tin, to rattle like insect legs, to exist as proof that life finds cracks everywhere, even here, even in wounds that refuse to heal.

*Fifth: I will not touch the Thaw-Stack.*

It is the heart of this place, cruel and necessary. It keeps the roofs clear and the stone from shearing bolts. If I changed it—made it cleaner, quieter, kinder—I would change everything downstream. The drizzle would thin. The hymn would lose its B-flat. The whole ecology of water and work would collapse into something manageable, something *above-ground* in its logic, and the Choir would lose the very thing that makes them Choir.

So the Stack keeps sighing like an old whale. Keeps coughing cinders. Keeps excreting iron-red sludge—fresh land being born backwards, continent forming in the dark. I leave it alone. Some engines earn their cruelty.

*Sixth: I will bless the sky-squares.*

Every painted tin with its baby-blue enamel and stolen glitter—I touch each one as I pass through the locker corridor. Not to brighten them. They are already bright enough. Instead I give them *weight*. A particular gravity that makes them harder to discard, harder to ignore, harder to categorize as sentimental clutter.

When corporate visitors see the orphaned skies now, something catches in their throats. They don't know why. They write memos about "atmospheric anomalies affecting inspection morale" and schedule fewer tours. The Choir keeps their galaxy. The horizon archives persist.

---

**What I will not do:**

I will not stop the water from rising.

I will not prevent the chamber from relaxing into its shape, shrugging toward closure.

I will not save everyone who transpires—who loses their outline to indoor weather and joins the composite chorus of diesel heart, stone lung, water tongue.

Because the Underworks is a wound that sings, and wounds that sing must eventually complete their song. The pumps will hush someday. The sump will swallow the roofs. The pale herbs will dissolve. And the Tideplant Underworks will seal itself back into the dark womb of the world, taking every rivet and rumor with it.

This is not tragedy. This is *birth in reverse*, which is just another kind of birth. The stone will remember. The water will carry the hymn into aquifers that feed springs a thousand miles away, and someone drinking from a well in another century will taste, for just a moment, something that feels like a B-flat chord and the echo of voices that forgot the words but never forgot the song.

---

**What I give to the one who transpires next:**

To the worker who wakes tomorrow to find no mirror recognizes them, whose outline has been replaced by indoor weather, whose assignment is ending though they will never climb out—

I give them this: the knowledge that I was here. That the wound was witnessed. That a benevolent and wise power descended through the sweating copper and the cold drizzle and the eternal pump-throb, and *chose not to fix it*. Chose instead to say: *You are not wrong. This is not wrong. The covenant you signed with your collarbone and your cracked fingernails and your glass-bell laughter was a real covenant, written in the oldest language, and it will be honored.*

I give them a final gift, small enough to fit in a palm, heavy enough to anchor a soul: a single frost-flower that will not dissolve. White as skim milk, veined with manganese blue, cold as the sump and bright as any stolen sky. They can crunch it or keep it. Either way, it carries my blessing:

*You were not lost. You were not wasted. You were translated—from flesh to frequency, from worker to weather, from wound to song.*

*The Choir is honored. The Choir is heard.*

*I will listen for you in the springs.*

---

And then I leave the way I came—seeping upward through capillary films, through the condensation that never stops writing itself on copper plates, through the wound-breath that rises toward a sky the Underworks has never seen.

Behind me, the pumps maintain their bright brute heartbeat.

The drizzle keeps falling on a town that forgot the sky.

And somewhere in the dark, a new voice joins the hymn—uncertain at first, then finding pitch, then holding the B-flat steady while the chamber relaxes one more inch toward closure, one more inch toward the day when the song will be complete and the wound will finally, finally scab over.

Not healed.

*Finished.*

There is a difference. And the difference is everything.
