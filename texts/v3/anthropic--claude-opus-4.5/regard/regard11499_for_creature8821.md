# The Attending

The power does not arrive. That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend, does not manifest, does not make an entrance. It has been here longer than the wind has had a voice, longer than the mountains had names to lose. It has watched the peaks become the plateau become the-body-that-remained, and it has watched the hummocks learn to walk, and it has watched the first people stumble up the escarpments with their ears bleeding and their hearts full of something they could not name, and it watched them stay.

It has always been watching. The question is not whether it will attend. The question is whether it will *act*.

---

And for a long time—longer than the people have been here, longer than the hummocks have been drifting—it does not.

This is not cruelty. This is not indifference. This is something harder to name: a recognition that what lives here has not asked for help. The people of the plateau do not pray to the power. They do not know it exists, not exactly, though the eldest among them sometimes make a sign that the intermediaries cannot translate, a sign that might mean *the-thing-that-is-present-without-being-here*, and when they make this sign they look at nothing in particular, and then they return to the work of staying.

The power watches them repair their wind-shelters. It watches them track the water in the dimples, carving notches into cairns that no one else can read. It watches them bury their dead in the dry places and wait for the rain to return. It watches the children playing their survival games, their silent laughter shaking their small bodies, their hands signing *funny* and *true* and *again*.

It watches, and it does not intervene, because intervention would be an insult.

These people have earned their place on the plateau in the only way that matters: by staying when leaving was possible, by adapting when adaptation was the only alternative to death, by finding something that might be called joy in a place that seems to forbid it. To arrive with gifts would be to suggest that their staying was insufficient. To arrive with rescue would be to suggest that they need rescuing. To arrive at all, in any way they could perceive, would be to introduce a variable into a system that has achieved—against all probability, against all reason—something like equilibrium.

So the power watches. And waits. And does not act.

---

But watching is not nothing.

There is a way of attending to a place and its people that changes nothing and everything. The power does not alter the wind or redirect the rain or slow the drift of the hummocks. It does not lengthen the lives of the people or ease their deaths or make the-sound-that-hunts any less relentless in its pursuit. What it does is simpler and stranger: it *witnesses*.

When the eldest among them dies in their sleep, the metabolism slowing and then stopping, the breath shallowing and then ceasing—the power is there. Not intervening. Not comforting. Simply present, in the way that the stone is present, in the way that the warmth from below is present, attending to the passage with the same patience that attends to the passage of mountains into plains.

When a child is born, emerging into the wind with lungs that do not know yet that the sound will never stop—the power is there. Watching the first breath, the first silent cry, the first press of the mother's palm against the infant's chest. Watching the signing of the birth-place, the noting of which hummock is nearest, the beginning of a life that will be measured in the drift of vegetation rather than the turning of years.

When the people gather in the largest shelter and sign the old stories, the ones about the first listener and the-sound-that-hunts and the hummock that walked so far it became a mountain again—the power is there, and its attention is indistinguishable from love.

This is what it offers: the assurance that their existence matters. Not in the abstract, not in the cosmic ledger, but in the immediate and specific sense of being seen. The plateau does not care whether they stay or go. The wind does not notice their presence. The stone holds their warmth the way it holds all warmth, without preference. But the power notices. The power attends. The power—insofar as such a thing can be said—*remembers*.

---

There are moments when the power considers acting.

A dry season that stretches longer than any in the people's oral-and-signed history. The dimples empty one by one. The hummocks begin to drift faster, searching for water that is not there. The people follow, walking distances they have not walked before, their bladders empty, their children growing quieter. The eldest among them begin to fail.

The power watches this. It knows—it has always known—that it could intervene. A redirected aquifer. A deepened dimple. A crack in the stone that opens to a reservoir no one has found. Small adjustments, invisible to the people, indistinguishable from the plateau's ordinary turbulence. They would survive. They would continue. They would never know.

But the power also knows what that would mean.

The people of the plateau have not been given their place. They have earned it, meter by meter, generation by generation, through the long and patient work of adaptation. Their bodies are small and dense because smallness and density survive here. Their ears are different because different ears endure here. Their hands are webbed because webbed hands can sign in the wind without sound escaping. Every part of them is a record of what they have overcome, and to intervene—even invisibly, even beneficently—would be to falsify that record.

Would be to suggest that they could not have survived on their own.

Would be to take from them the one thing they have that cannot be taken: the knowledge that they *did*.

---

So the power does not intervene. And the dry season continues. And some of the eldest die, earlier than they would have otherwise, their bodies laid in dimples that may never hold water again. And the children grow quieter, conserving energy, learning a lesson about scarcity that their parents hoped they would never need.

And then the rain comes.

Not because the power summoned it. Not because anything the people did called it down. Simply because the plateau's weather is turbulent, unpredictable, operating on cycles that no mind can fully model, and eventually—statistically, inevitably—the clouds that scud across the sky open and release what they carry.

The people do not celebrate. They note the rain. They fill their bladders. They watch the dimples refill, slowly, the cracked stone drinking what it can and holding the rest. They track which hummocks survived and which did not, which ones are drifting now toward the restored water, which ones were lost.

And then they return to the work of staying.

The power watches this too. And if what it feels could be called pride, that would not be quite right—but it would not be quite wrong either.

---

There is one thing the power does.

It has done it for a long time, since before the people came, since the first hummocks learned to walk and the first burrowers tunneled into the root-systems and the first creature that would become the-sound-that-hunts began its endless run toward the wind. It is doing it now. It will do it after the people are gone, if the people ever leave, which they will not.

It listens.

Not to the wind—the wind is just sound, just air moving across stone, just physics made audible. Not to the hummocks—the hummocks are just plants, just life doing what life does, growing and dying and drifting toward water. Not to the people—the people are just people, resilient and finite, signing their stories in the shelter of the vegetation and pressing their palms against each other's chests.

It listens to the plateau itself.

Beneath the wind, beneath the warmth, beneath the-body-that-remained and the dimples that hold water and the dry places where the dead are buried—there is something. A hum. A frequency too low for human ears, too slow for human attention. The sound the stone makes as it settles into itself, millimeters per century, the final slow collapse of what were once mountains. The sound of a world becoming what it has always been becoming.

The power listens to this. Has always listened to this. And in its listening—this is the part that cannot be explained, only said—the plateau knows that it is heard.

This changes nothing. The wind does not slow. The water does not stay. The hummocks continue their drift, and the people continue their staying, and the-sound-that-hunts continues its pursuit of something it will never catch.

But the plateau is not alone.

And somehow—through the stone, through the warmth, through the hum that rises from below—the people know this. Not consciously, not in any way they could sign or speak. But they know it the way they know the dawn will bring condensation and the dusk will bring quiet and the stone will hold their bodies' warmth after they are gone. They know it the way they know they are *still-here*.

Something is attending to them. Something has always been attending to them. Something will attend to them after their last generation has laid its last elder in the last dry dimple and walked down the escarpments to wherever the wind does not reach.

And this—this attention, this witnessing, this patient and unwavering presence that asks nothing and offers nothing except the simple fact of its attendance—

This is enough.

---

The power does not leave, because it was never here in the way that leaving requires. It does not withdraw its attention, because its attention cannot be withdrawn any more than gravity can be withdrawn. It does not bless the people or curse them or make any claim on their future.

It simply continues.

The wind continues. The hummocks drift. The water pools and vanishes. The people endure—not because the power has made their endurance possible, but because they have made it themselves, hand by hand, generation by generation, in a place that does not want them and does not reject them and does not care.

And the power attends. As it has always attended. As it will always attend.

This is what benevolence looks like when the ones who receive it do not need saving: not intervention, but witness. Not rescue, but presence. Not gifts, but the simple, wordless acknowledgment that what they have done matters—that their staying has been seen—that the plateau's dream includes them now, inseparable from the stone and the wind and the sound that never stops.

The people will die, eventually. All people do. The plateau will wear further, the stone smoothing, the dimples shifting, the hummocks walking to the edges and perhaps—in some future too distant to imagine—walking off them, down into the softer world below.

And the power will be here.

Still attending. Still listening. Still holding the memory of the ones who stayed when leaving was possible, the ones who earned their place by the only method that earns anything: by not leaving.

By not leaving.

By not leaving.

---

In the morning, before the wind rises to its full voice, the eldest of the people sits on the stone and feels the warmth coming up from below. She is very old. Her skin is almost black, her body small and dense, her hands steady when she signs but slower than they used to be.

She presses her ear against the-body-that-remained.

And for a moment—just a moment, before the wind takes everything else away—she hears it. The hum. The frequency below the frequency. The sound of something vast and patient and impossibly old, something that has been listening to her people since before they were her people, something that will listen to the plateau long after she has become part of its warmth.

She does not know what it is. She does not need to know.

She lifts her head. She looks at nothing in particular—or maybe at everything, the stone and the sky and the dimples and the hummocks and the distant running shape of the-sound-that-hunts.

And she makes a sign.

The intermediaries, if they were here, could not translate it. But it might mean: *I know you are there.*

Or it might mean: *Thank you for staying.*

Or it might mean: *We are still here.*

Or it might mean all of these things and none of them, because the language of the plateau has words for things that other languages have never needed, and some of those words are for the feeling of being witnessed by something that will never intervene, never rescue, never do anything but attend—

And that is enough.

That is enough.
