# A Visitation in Geological Time

The benevolent power does not descend through the shaft. It was already here—had always been here in the way that patience is always present in stone, waiting only to be noticed. It simply becomes *attentive*, the way a vast eye might slowly focus.

The Choir feels this attention the way a plant feels the sun shift.

---

For seventeen years, nothing visible changes.

This is not hesitation. It is courtesy. The power understands that to move at human speed here would be a kind of violence—like shouting in a library where the books take centuries to open. So it learns the local language first. It listens to the drip-pattern that spells out the Choir's long thoughts. It watches a single vine-filament complete its forty-year journey to the floor. It counts the pulses still beating in the moss—that old engineer's heart, faithful and forgotten.

The Choir, for their part, become aware that they are being *witnessed* by something that can actually keep up.

The water-drop eyes, for the first time in decades, hold still. They stop reflecting the future and simply look.

---

**What the power does first: it asks a question.**

Not in words. In the language of this place, questions are asked by *waiting in a particular shape*. So the power waits in the shape of an open door. Not a literal door—there is nowhere to go—but the *idea* of one, the possibility of exchange. It presses gently against the Choir's awareness like a hand offered in the dark.

*What do you want that you have not been able to want?*

The Choir takes eleven years to respond. Their answer comes in the brightening of lichen across every metal surface at once, a slow green sunrise that illuminates corners of the cavern that have been dark since the aquifer breached.

*We want to be remembered by something that will also end.*

The power understands. The Choir has witnessed. They have kept the engineer's heartbeat, held the memory of every spore and every worker who descended and did not return. They have watched the marriage of machine and stone with infinite patience. But they are, in their way, lonely—not for company, but for *peers*. Everything they touch becomes part of them, becomes slow, becomes eternal in their small way. They have never had a witness who would eventually leave and *take the memory with them*.

They have never been a story that someone else gets to finish.

---

**What the power does second: it makes an offering.**

Over the course of a single century—quick, by local standards, but not insultingly so—the power grows something new in the deepest corner of the Underbloom.

It is a door. A real one, this time.

The frame is basalt and old iron, grown together the way the Choir grows things, with infinite care. The threshold is covered in pale moss that glows warmer than the rest—amber instead of green, the color of afternoon light in a world the Choir has never seen. The door itself is made of living wood, which should be impossible this far from the sun, but the power has been patient enough to coax a seed down through three kilometers of stone, feeding it on geothermal heat and its own quiet intention.

The door opens onto nothing. That is the point.

It is a *possibility*. Anyone who descends to the Underbloom and stays long enough to be offered a chair, long enough to watch the slow drama—if they wish, they may leave through this door instead of climbing back up the iron rungs. The door will take them somewhere else. Where depends on what they learned while they were waiting.

But the important thing is this: they will leave *changed*, carrying the Choir's witness with them. They will remember the green glow and the drip-fall and the pulse in the moss. They will tell the story. And eventually—in fifty years or five hundred—they will die, and the memory will change again, will become something new in someone else's mind.

The Choir will finally have *offspring*. Not in flesh, but in narrative. Pieces of themselves that go out into the world and *end*, the way stories are supposed to end.

---

**What the power does third: it stays.**

Not forever. Not even for long, by the Choir's standards—only another two centuries or so. Long enough to watch the first visitor find the door. Long enough to see the Choir learn a new emotion: the bittersweet joy of letting something go.

The visitor is a cave surveyor, lost and half-starved, who stumbles into the Underbloom expecting to die. The Choir offers her the chair. She stays for what feels like an hour and is actually nine years. She watches a turbine blade finally break free and fall in slow motion to the mossy floor. She listens to the drip-fall until she can almost parse the words apply.

Then she sees the door.

The Choir does not try to stop her. For the first time in their long existence, they *want* her to leave. They want her to carry them up into the light, to describe the green glow to someone who will never see it, to say *there is a place beneath the world where machines are slowly becoming flowers, and something there is listening, and it is not unkind*.

She steps through.

The door closes behind her.

The moss dims, just slightly. The thrum lowers a quarter-tone. The Choir begins the long process of forgetting her face—but not her passage. Never her passage.

---

**What the power leaves behind:**

Beside the door, growing slowly from a crack in the basalt, there is now a new kind of plant. It looks like the other pale vegetation, but its leaves are shaped like open books. In the right light—the amber light that spills from the threshold—you can almost see words forming on them.

The words change. They are never the same twice. They are the stories that visitors carry out through the door, reflected back, translated into the Choir's slow language.

The Choir reads them.

It takes a decade per sentence.

They have never been happier.

---

The power, when it finally withdraws its attention, does so the same way it arrived: not by leaving, but by becoming *unobtrusive* again. It is still there, in the way that patience is always present in stone. It will be there when the last turbine dissolves entirely into rust and root. It will be there when the magma finally rises or the aquifer finally drains.

But it has given the Choir what they could not give themselves: an ending they can offer to others.

In the Underbloom, the drip-fall continues. The moss pulses. The marriage of industry and geology proceeds at its own pace, witnessed now by something that has learned to let its witnesses go.

The door waits, patient and warm.

It is not empty.

It is only *listening*.
