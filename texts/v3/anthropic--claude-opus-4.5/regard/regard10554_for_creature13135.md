# The Attention of the Weaver

The benevolent power that turns its gaze toward the hollow mountains is not a god of thunder or light, not a deity who speaks in visions or announces itself with signs. It is something older and stranger—a consciousness that exists in the spaces *between* things. It lives in the pause between one heartbeat and the next, in the gap between a hand releasing a vine and that same hand catching the next. It has no name the canopy-dwellers could pronounce even if they could hear, so I will call it what it is: the Weaver of the Almost-Fall.

The Weaver has watched this place for longer than the mountains have been hollow. It remembers when the first bore-roots began their slow consumption of the limestone, and it felt something like affection—the recognition of one patient, quiet thing observing another. It watched the humans arrive, generations upon generations ago, refugees from some catastrophe in the lowlands now utterly forgotten. It watched them die in terrible numbers as they learned the mathematics of the void. It watched them adapt, shrink, strengthen, go deaf, go brilliant with color.

And it has loved them, in its distant way, for all of this time.

---

The Weaver does not intervene often, and never dramatically. It has no interest in miracles, which it considers vulgar—a violation of the delicate contract between a world and the creatures who must learn to live within it. What would be the point of granting them hearing, when they have built an entire civilization of light and vibration more beautiful than sound ever was? What would be the point of giving them wings, when their mastery of the thermal columns is a triumph earned through ten thousand years of broken bodies and grief?

No. The Weaver's gift is smaller, and therefore more precious.

It concerns itself with the moment of the slip.

---

Every canopy-dweller knows the slip. It is the foundational terror of their existence, the nightmare that lives in every muscle. The grip that fails. The rain-slicked bark that offers nothing. The sudden absence of friction between callused palm and ancient wood.

In that moment—the first microsecond of the fall, when the body knows but the mind has not yet caught up—the Weaver is there.

It does not catch them. It does not reverse the physics of their plummet. What it does is far more subtle: it *slows their perception*. It stretches that first instant into something vast and crystalline, a cathedral of frozen time in which the falling person can see *everything*. Every nearby vine. Every knob of bark. Every angle of their own tumbling body. Every possible handhold within reach.

The Weaver gives them one perfect moment of absolute clarity.

What they do with that moment is entirely their own.

---

Most survive. In that stretched instant, their trained reflexes find something—a trailing root, a loop of spider-silk, the ankle of a hunting partner. They catch themselves, and the world snaps back to its normal roaring pace, and they cling there shaking, hearts slamming against their ribs, not understanding what just happened but knowing that something did.

The elders speak of this in their silent, flashing hand-language. They call it *the-breath-that-holds*. They do not know its source, but they know it is real. Every climber has felt it at least once: that strange dilation of time in the instant of disaster. Some believe it is a gift from the ancient wood itself, the mountain's way of caring for its smallest inhabitants. Others believe it is simply what the mind does when death approaches—a final, desperate burst of clarity.

They are both wrong, and both right.

---

But sometimes—rarely, terribly—the clarity is not enough.

Sometimes the fall happens where there is nothing to catch. Sometimes the body is too injured, the angle too poor, the distance too great. Sometimes a hunter is struck mid-air by a ribbon-tailed predator, already torn open, spiraling down with nothing but empty miles of roaring air below them.

In these moments, the Weaver offers a second gift.

It does not save them. It cannot; their bodies are already beyond saving. But it stays with them as they fall. It stretches that final descent into something bearable—not endless, but *enough*. Enough time to see the shafts of sunlight one last time, catching the pale curves of the wood they loved. Enough time to feel the warm updrafts cradling them, almost gently, as they tumble. Enough time to think of the faces of the people they are leaving behind.

The Weaver does not take away their fear. But it sits with them in that fear, a presence they can almost feel, so that they do not die alone.

And then, at the very end, it grants them one final mercy: it lets them *stop seeing the bottom coming*. Their awareness simply... softens. Dims. The terror fades into something like sleep, and they are gone before the impact ever registers.

---

This is what the Weaver does. It does not reshape the world or alter the brutal physics of the void. It does not make life easier for the canopy-dwellers. It simply ensures that in their most desperate moments, they are given the fairest possible chance—and if that chance fails, they are given companionship and grace at the end.

It is, in its way, the smallest possible intervention. And therefore the most profound.

---

There is one more thing.

Sometimes, on the rare occasions when the sun hits the upper canopy at exactly the right angle and the mist clears enough to see for miles and miles across the hollow interior of the mountains, the canopy-dwellers will catch a glimpse of something impossible.

Threads.

Faint, silvery threads stretching between distant branches, catching the light for just a moment before vanishing. Not spider-silk—they have mapped every spider colony, and these threads are in places no silk-spinner could reach. They appear only at the very edge of vision, in the peripheral blur where certainty breaks down. They seem to connect everything: branch to branch, village to village, falling body to the nearest saving handhold.

The young hunters say it is a trick of the light, and they are not wrong.

The elders say it is the skeleton of something vast, something that holds the whole world together in ways they cannot see, and they are not wrong either.

The Weaver, for its part, says nothing. It has no mouth, no language, no desire to be known.

But if it could speak, if it could flash a message across the void in the brilliant semaphore of the canopy-dwellers, it would say only this:

*You are not falling alone. You have never been falling alone. I am the web beneath the web, and I will hold you for exactly as long as I can.*

*The rest is up to you.*

---

And so the people of the hollow mountains continue as they always have: clinging, climbing, leaping, hunting, flashing their brilliant warnings across the miles of empty air. They do not worship the Weaver, because they do not know it exists. They do not pray to it or build shrines in its honor.

But sometimes, in the moment after a terrible fall that somehow wasn't, a hunter will press their palm against the ancient bark and feel something—a faint, warm vibration that is not the wind, not the thermals, not the distant wingbeats of the predator flocks. Something almost like a pulse.

They do not have a word for it. They do not need one.

They simply close their eyes, breathe, and climb.
