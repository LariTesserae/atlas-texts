# What the Benevolent Power Might Do

*A consideration*

---

The first thing would be to watch. To watch for a very long time.

A wise power does not intervene in what it does not understand, and understanding this place—truly understanding it—would require patience measured not in days or weeks but in generations. The wind-listeners have spent centuries becoming what they are. Their bodies, their language, their entire way of being has been shaped by negotiation with forces that predate human memory. To act without understanding would be to impose, and imposition—however well-intentioned—is not benevolence.

So: watching. Learning the grammar of wind-speech, not through instruction but through the same slow absorption that the children undergo. Feeling the gale's variations until they resolve from noise into signal. Mapping the network of watchers, the hunting routes, the shelter-crevices, the seasonal movements of grazer and hunter and human across the scoured granite. Coming to know the old woman on the promontory not as a problem to be solved but as a person to be understood—her fears and hopes and the particular texture of her loneliness.

Only then, perhaps, would action become possible. And even then, the question would remain: *what action?*

---

The obvious interventions are the dangerous ones.

Food, brought from below? The wind-listeners' bodies have adapted to scarcity over generations. Abundance might heal them in the short term and destroy them in the long—softening the very hardness that allows survival here, creating dependency on supply lines that could not be maintained, disrupting the careful balance between human population and plateau resources that keeps both viable.

Medicine, to extend lives? But their short lives are not simply a failure to be corrected. They are part of a system—a system in which elders die before they become burdens, in which population remains low enough to be sustained, in which the harshness of selection has produced people capable of thriving where no one should be able to live at all. To extend lives without addressing the underlying constraints would be to create suffering, not relieve it.

Rescue, evacuation to the valleys below? But they would not survive there. Not physically—their lungs would struggle with the thick air, their skin would burn under vegetation-filtered sun, their wind-speech would become useless in the silence. And not psychologically either. They are *of* this place in ways that cannot be separated from who they are. To remove them would be to destroy them as surely as the cold and hunger ever could.

No. The obvious interventions fail because they treat the wind-listeners as broken versions of valley-dwellers, as people who need to be fixed or saved or restored to some imagined baseline of proper human existence. But they are not broken. They are *adapted*—magnificently, terribly adapted to conditions that would kill anyone else. The benevolent power's task is not to undo that adaptation but to work within it.

---

What, then?

Perhaps the power might begin with the old woman's knowledge.

Not by preserving her life beyond its natural span—that would be a violation of the system she has spent eighty years serving. But by ensuring that what she knows does not die with her. The full grammar of wind-speech. The locations and histories. The awareness of other populations on distant plateaus.

This could be done gently, invisibly, through means that look like natural processes rather than intervention. A young wind-listener, slightly more gifted than average, might find herself drawn to the promontory more often than others. She might discover in herself an unusual capacity for the old forms of speech, an aptitude for the historical knowledge that most of her generation has let atrophy. She might spend her evenings at the old woman's side, not because anyone commanded it but because something in her responds to what the elder offers.

And the old woman might find, in these final months or years, that her loneliness has been answered—that the knowledge she feared would vanish has found a vessel—that her long listening has not been in vain.

---

The distant plateaus pose a harder problem.

The old woman has heard them, sometimes, in certain conditions. Other populations of wind-speakers, isolated on their own peaks, struggling with their own versions of slow decline. If they could be connected—if messages could pass reliably between them—then genetic diversity might be restored, innovations shared, the demographic collapse that threatens all of them might be forestalled.

But the distances are vast, and the wind that carries messages also degrades them over distance. The gaps between plateaus are filled with turbulence that scrambles meaning, valleys of thick air where wind-speech cannot propagate.

A wise power might address this not by carrying messages itself—that would create dependency—but by creating conditions in which the wind-listeners could solve the problem themselves. Perhaps a series of intermediate points, high cols between plateaus where signal could be received and retransmitted. The power could not build relay stations, but it might... adjust things. Slightly. In ways that look natural.

A rockfall here, creating a new prominence that catches and channels the wind in useful ways. A shift in the erosion patterns there, opening a line of sight that was previously blocked. Small changes, accumulated over years, that gradually create the possibility of long-distance communication where none existed before.

The wind-listeners would discover these new routes themselves. They would pioneer the relay system, station their own watchers at the intermediate points, develop their own protocols for cross-plateau messaging. The power would only have opened the door. Walking through it would be their own achievement.

---

And then there is the matter of the grazers and the hunters.

The careful balance between these species and the wind-listeners is the foundation of everything. The humans manage the grazers, protect them from excessive predation, cull them sustainably. The hunters take what they can, constrained by the humans' interference. The whole system trembles on the edge of viability, each population dependent on the others in ways that have become intricate beyond mapping.

A benevolent power might look at this system and see places where small interventions could increase resilience without disrupting balance. A slightly more nutritious strain of cushion plant, emerging naturally through what appears to be ordinary mutation. A behavioral shift in the hunters that makes them marginally less bold during the critical early-spring period when the grazers give birth. A new variety of lichen that colonizes surfaces previously too exposed, expanding the food base by a few crucial percentage points.

None of these changes would be dramatic. None would be immediately obvious as interventions. They would look like the ordinary variations that occur in any ecosystem, the random drift of evolution and competition and environmental pressure. But they would, cumulatively, buy time—would widen the margins within which the wind-listeners operate, would make the plateau slightly less lethal without making it comfortable enough to soften the adaptations that allow survival.

---

Perhaps the most important intervention would be the subtlest.

The wind-listeners have no concept of themselves as remarkable. They do not know that their existence represents something extraordinary—a human culture that has successfully adapted to conditions beyond the edge of what should be possible. They know only struggle, scarcity, the daily negotiation with death that has been their reality for so many generations that they cannot imagine anything else.

A benevolent power might simply... bear witness.

Not visibly. Not in any way that would be perceived as external attention. But in some deeper register, some level of reality below the everyday, the wind-listeners might begin to sense—in ways they could not articulate—that their existence matters. That their struggle is seen. That their wind-speech, their intricate adaptations, their survival against all odds, has meaning beyond mere continuation.

This is a fragile gift, easily corrupted into the kind of cosmic validation that breeds complacency. It would have to be offered carefully, without words, without certainty—as a subtle warmth underlying the cold, a sense of significance that never quite resolves into explicit knowledge. The wind-listeners would not know they were witnessed. They would simply find, in their darkest moments, that despair did not quite reach bottom. That something in them insisted on continuing. That the screaming wind carried, beneath its endless roar, something that might almost be called hope.

---

But perhaps the wisest intervention is the one I have not mentioned.

Perhaps the benevolent power, having watched and understood and considered every possibility, would choose to do nothing at all.

Not from indifference. Not from inability. But from a recognition that the wind-listeners' existence—their whole magnificent, terrible, precarious existence—is *theirs*. It belongs to them. They have earned it through centuries of suffering and adaptation, through countless generations who lived and died on the screaming peaks without ever asking for rescue. To intervene, even gently, even invisibly, would be to take something from them. Would be to suggest that their achievement is incomplete without external help, that their survival requires a power beyond themselves.

And maybe it doesn't.

Maybe the wind-listeners will find their own way to the distant plateaus, will discover the intermediate points without supernatural assistance, will develop their own solutions to the demographic decline that threatens them. Maybe the old woman's knowledge will be preserved through ordinary processes—a student who happens to be curious, a tradition that reasserts itself just in time. Maybe the balance between grazers and hunters and humans will continue to hold, year after year, generation after generation, without any adjustment to its terms.

Or maybe it won't. Maybe the wind-listeners will dwindle and die, the last of them perishing alone on some windswept ridge, the wind-speech falling silent forever. Maybe the old woman's knowledge will vanish with her, and the distant plateaus will never be connected, and the whole extraordinary experiment of human life in the screaming peaks will come to an end.

A benevolent power would grieve this. Would feel the loss of something irreplaceable, would mourn the silencing of a voice that had learned to speak through the wind itself. But it might also recognize that grief is not the same as failure, and that some losses cannot be prevented without costs that exceed the loss itself.

The wind-listeners did not ask to be saved. They asked only—through the old woman, through her white eyes and her words barely audible above the roar—to be remembered. To have their existence acknowledged by someone from beyond the peaks, someone who would carry knowledge of them back to the wider world.

A benevolent power might decide that this is the intervention that matters most. Not rescue. Not assistance. Not subtle manipulations of evolution and geography. Just memory. Just witness. Just the assurance that, whatever happens on the screaming peaks, it happens in a universe that knows and cares that it is happening.

---

I imagine the power, finally, standing on the promontory beside the old woman.

Invisible, intangible, present only in the way that attention is present when it is fully given. The wind roars around them both, and the stars emerge one by one in the crystalline sky, and the old woman listens to the messages from across the plateau—the network of watchers reporting the hunters' movements, the grazer herds' positions, the small ordinary dramas of survival that make up life in this impossible place.

She does not know the power is there. She will never know. But perhaps she feels something anyway—a warmth beneath the cold, a sense of significance, a faint impossible hope that her long listening has not been in vain.

And the power listens with her.

Not to intervene. Not to fix or save or rescue. Just to be present, fully present, in this moment when an old woman stands at the edge of everything and refuses to let the wind have the last word.

That might be enough.

That might be the most benevolent act of all: to witness the struggle without ending it, to honor the achievement without diminishing it, to stand beside the ones who suffer and simply say—in whatever way such things are said—*I see you. You are not alone. What you have done matters.*

The wind screams on. The stars wheel overhead. The old woman listens, and the power listens with her, and somewhere far below the valleys sleep in their unimaginable silence.

And maybe, in the morning, things will be slightly different. A young wind-listener will feel drawn to the promontory. A rockfall will open a new line of sight. A cushion plant will produce a seed with slightly more nutritious fruit.

Or maybe nothing will change at all.

Either way, the wind-listeners will continue—for another day, another generation, another century. Not because they were saved, but because they saved themselves. Because they found a way to speak through the wind itself, to make themselves into words that the roaring air carries across the screaming peaks.

The benevolent power, watching, would find this beautiful.

And beauty, perhaps, is its own justification for witness.
