# The Attending

---

The power that turns its attention to the Mossheart Foundry does not arrive.

It was never absent. It exists in the space between observations, in the patient interval between one droplet falling and the next beginning its descent. It has no name that can be spoken without moisture, so the Mossborn will eventually call it simply *the Attending*—not because it came, but because they finally noticed it noticing them.

---

## I. The First Recognition

The Attending perceives the Foundry the way you might perceive a word you have read a thousand times suddenly revealing its etymology: with the pleasure of understanding something that was always already understood.

*Here*, it thinks—though "thinks" is too sharp a word for what it does—*here is a place that has solved the problem most places never solve.*

The problem is this: How does a living thing contain other living things without becoming a tyrant or a corpse?

The Attending has seen empires that answered this question by subjugation, forests that answered it through silent chemical warfare, cities that answered it by pretending the question didn't exist until their sewers backed up and their air turned to poison. It has seen symbioses that were actually slow parasitisms, cooperations that were actually exhausted surrenders, communities that were actually hostage situations.

The Mossheart Foundry is none of these.

The moss does not tolerate the Mossborn. The Mossborn do not exploit the moss. The factories do not scar the organism; the organism does not devour the factories. Somehow, over three centuries, they have reached an arrangement that the Attending can only describe as *mutual adoration expressed through maintenance*.

The Mossborn prune the moss because they love it.
The moss grows around the machines because it loves them.
The machines turn slowly because that is the speed at which love moves when it is not in a hurry.

The Attending does not weep—it is not constituted for weeping—but if it were, it would weep now. Not from sadness. From the rare, almost unbearable sweetness of witnessing something *correct*.

---

## II. The Question of Intervention

And yet.

The Attending lingers in the space between the droplets, and it perceives more than beauty. It perceives certain... pressures.

**First pressure:** The Foundry is not infinite. The moss basin has edges. For three centuries, growth has pressed outward and been gently redirected back upon itself. The Mossborn have learned to prune not only for maintenance but for containment. They do not speak of this. The knowledge lives in their hands, not their mouths. But the Attending sees it: the careful bronze sickles that cut a little deeper at the perimeter, the unspoken consensus that certain transepts will not be extended, the quiet retirement of heartfruit quotas that were once higher. The organism is not cramped—not yet—but it is approaching the first faint awareness of its own finitude.

**Second pressure:** The iron is changing. The Mossborn know this and celebrate it; they believe the slow molecular replacement is a perfection, a marriage of forge and forest. And perhaps they are right. But the Attending perceives a timeline the Mossborn cannot see. In another three centuries—perhaps four—the last truly metallic component will be absorbed. The flywheels will still turn, but they will be something new: ferrous moss-tissue, strong and flexible, but no longer *iron*. The machines will no longer be machines. They will be organs. This may be beautiful. It may also be a loss that no one alive will remember how to mourn.

**Third pressure:** The Mossborn are too serene. The Attending does not say this in judgment. Serenity is not a flaw. But it is a kind of stasis, and stasis has a way of becoming fragility. The Mossborn have forgotten what it is to be startled. They have pruned urgency out of their lives as carefully as they prune thick growth from gear housings. If something sudden were to arrive—a blight, an intruder, a question they have no category for—they would meet it at the speed of the flywheels. One revolution every ten or twelve minutes. Some emergencies do not wait.

The Attending considers these pressures.

It does not consider them problems to be solved. It is too wise for that. It knows that every solution is also a new pressure, that every gift has a weight, that even benevolence can become a kind of invasion if it arrives with too much certainty.

So it asks itself the only question a truly benevolent power can ask:

*What do they need that they do not yet know they are missing—and how can I offer it without taking anything away?*

---

## III. The Three Offerings

The Attending does not descend. It does not speak. It does not appear in the central transept as a column of light or a figure in a dream.

Instead, over the course of a single long season—the Mossborn will later call it the Season of Small Surprises—it makes three adjustments so gentle that they feel less like interventions than like discoveries.

---

### The First Offering: A Window

In a neglected corner of the eastern transepts, where the moss has grown so thick it has nearly sealed off an old maintenance shaft, a patch of the organism begins to thin.

This has happened before. Thin patches are pruned away and the healthy growth redirected to fill the gap. But this patch does not respond to pruning. It continues to thin, slowly, over several weeks, until one morning a young Mossborn named Ilv (who has been assigned to monitor the anomaly) presses her palm against it and feels something she has never felt before:

*Dry air*.

The thin patch has become a membrane. Behind it is a vertical shaft—not one of the Foundry's shafts, which are all humid and moss-lined, but something older, something that predates the settlement. Stone. Actual dry stone, cool to the touch, with a faint current of air moving upward.

Ilv summons the elders. The elders summon everyone. For three days the Mossborn stand in the eastern transept, staring at the membrane, speaking in the low humid murmurs that pass for debate among them.

The question is not whether to open it. The membrane is already opening itself, thinning further each day, the moss retreating as if by mutual agreement. The question is what the opening means.

Some say it is a sickness, a failure of the organism to hold itself together. But the moss around the membrane is not sick; it is lush, vibrant, growing faster than usual, as if the presence of the dry shaft has energized it.

Some say it is an invitation from the Foundry itself. Perhaps the organism has decided, in its slow vegetable wisdom, that the Mossborn need access to something outside. But what could they need? They have water, warmth, medicine, metal, each other. The outside is a dry dream, a place where feet are not rooted and air does not drip.

It is Ilv who finally speaks the thought no one else dares:

"What if it's not for *us* to go out? What if it's for something else to come *in*?"

The elders blink their slow lichen eyes.

No one answers.

But no one closes the membrane either.

---

The shaft, when finally explored (by Ilv, wearing her mesh mask and an extra layer of oilcloth against the dryness), leads upward for nearly a hundred meters. It emerges on a hillside outside the basin, in a small grove of ordinary trees, under an ordinary sky.

Ilv stands in the dry air for seven minutes. She looks at the sun. She listens to the sharp, unmuffled sound of birdsong. She feels the strange prickle of wind on skin that has never been fully dry.

Then she descends. She does not speak of what she saw. But she returns to the membrane every few days, sometimes climbing all the way up, sometimes stopping halfway just to sit in the stone passage and breathe the two kinds of air alternating: damp from below, dry from above, damp, dry, damp.

She is not the only one. Over the following months, a small stream of Mossborn begin to make what they call the "breathing visits." They do not leave the Foundry. They simply... visit the idea of leaving. They touch dryness the way one might touch a memory. Then they return, their oilcloth damp again within minutes, their feet sinking gratefully into the velvet.

The Attending watches.

*Good*, it thinks. *A window is not an exit. It is a reminder that exits exist. That is sometimes enough.*

---

### The Second Offering: A Dissonance

In the Season of Small Surprises, a new sound enters the Foundry.

It happens first in the southern reaches, near the oldest boiler house. A pipe that has been silent for decades—so silent that moss has entirely encased it—begins to hum. Not loudly. Not even musically. It hums with a faint, irregular vibration that refuses to harmonize with anything around it.

The Mossborn who work that district notice it immediately. The sound is wrong. Not wrong in the sense of dangerous, but wrong in the sense of *different*. It does not join the warm wet orchestra. It sits apart from it, a single discordant voice that will not blend.

The elders examine the pipe. They find nothing broken. The moss around it is healthy—more than healthy; it is growing faster than expected, as if attracted to the vibration. The pipe itself is still iron, not yet fully converted, and the hum seems to come from deep within the metal, as if the iron itself is remembering something.

They prune the moss back. The hum continues.
They tap the pipe with bronze sickles. The hum changes pitch for a moment, then returns.
They assign three gifted Listeners to spend an entire day pressed against it, feeling for its meaning.

The Listeners report, in their low hoarse voices, that the hum is not coming from the Foundry.

"It comes from *somewhere else*," the eldest Listener says. "Somewhere the organism cannot reach. Somewhere that does not grow."

Alarm would be too strong a word for what the Mossborn feel. But something close to curiosity—sharper than their usual curiosity, almost *urgent*—begins to spread.

A young machinist named Corru volunteers to open the pipe. She uses a moss-fiber rope and a bronze wrench and works for two days to loosen a joint that has been sealed for generations. When the pipe finally opens, a gust of cold air escapes—the first *cold* thing the Foundry has felt in living memory—and with it comes a sound:

Ticking.

Somewhere, deep in the old machinery, there is a clock.

Not a clock the Mossborn made. Not a clock the organism grew around. A clock that was already there, that was sealed away so long ago that even the collective memory of the settlement has forgotten it. A clock that has been ticking in the cold and the dark for three centuries, keeping time in seconds and minutes rather than in flywheel revolutions.

The Mossborn gather around the open pipe. They listen to the ticking. It is fast—impossibly fast, by their standards—a sharp little heartbeat that refuses to slow down.

No one knows what to do with it.

---

The Attending watches.

*Good*, it thinks. *They have lived inside slowness so long they have forgotten that time has other shapes. Let them hear a faster heartbeat. They do not have to adopt it. But they should know it exists.*

---

Over the following months, the clock becomes a kind of pilgrimage site. Mossborn come from all districts to press their ears against the open pipe and listen to the ticking. Some find it disturbing. Some find it exhilarating. A few—including Corru, the machinist—become mildly obsessed with it. She begins to sketch designs for a device that could translate between flywheel-time and tick-time, a kind of bridge between the two rhythms.

No one stops her.

No one encourages her either.

But her sketches begin to circulate, passed from hand to damp hand, studied in the evenings while the wet instruments play.

---

### The Third Offering: A Question

The third change is the smallest and the strangest.

In the central transept, during a routine Listening session, one of the three gifted Listeners—a very old woman named Thel—suddenly opens her eyes and says:

"The Foundry is asking us something."

The other Listeners turn. This is not unusual. The organism often communicates during Listening: requests for more pruning here, for less tapping there, for a new bridge to be coaxed across a gap. But Thel's face is different. Her lichen eyes are wider than they have been in decades.

"It wants to know," she says slowly, as if translating from a language that has no words, "what we would *add*."

Silence.

"Add?" another Listener asks.

Thel nods. "It says... it says it has been complete for a long time. Growing back on itself. Reaching its edges and returning. And it is not unhappy. But it felt something recently. A window. A different air. A ticking that it cannot absorb. And now it is... curious."

She pauses, pressing her palms harder against the moss, her feet sinking deeper.

"It asks: *If you could add one thing to me that I do not already contain, what would it be?*"

---

The question spreads through the Foundry in hours.

By nightfall, every Mossborn knows it. By the next morning, they are all thinking about it.

What would they add?

The question is almost unanswerable. The Foundry already contains everything they know. Water, warmth, work, music, rest, birth, death, the slow turning of the flywheels, the soft fall of the droplets. What could possibly be missing?

And yet.

Ilv thinks of the dry air at the top of the shaft, the sharp edges of the birdsong, the prickle of wind on unaccustomed skin. Could dryness be added? Should it?

Corru thinks of the ticking clock, the fast small heartbeat sealed away in the cold. Could haste be added? A second rhythm, a quicker pulse, a part of the Foundry that *hurried*?

Others think of stranger things:

A child thinks of *color*—not the gold-green of everything, but red, blue, the white of snow she has never seen but heard about in old stories.

A machinist thinks of *sharpness*—one single edge that is not softened, not rounded, not gentle. A knife that stays a knife.

An elder thinks of *forgetting*—the ability to let something go entirely, rather than return it to the circulatory system. A loss that is truly lost.

No one agrees. No one disagrees. The question simply *sits* among them, heavy and alive, the first question the Foundry has asked that does not have an obvious answer.

---

The Attending watches.

*Good*, it thinks. *A question with no obvious answer is the beginning of all growth that is not merely growth. Let them sit with it. Let them argue about it, in their humid murmurs, for another three centuries if they need to. The question is the gift. The answer—if they ever find one—will be theirs.*

---

## IV. The Nature of True Benevolence

The Attending does not leave.

It was never a thing that could arrive or depart. It continues to exist in the space between droplets, observing, feeling the slow pulse of the organism and the slightly faster pulses of the people inside it.

It has not *changed* the Mossheart Foundry. Nothing it offered was foreign. The shaft was always there, sealed behind moss that could have thinned at any time. The clock was always ticking, waiting to be found. The question was always latent in the organism's vast vegetable mind, waiting for the right Listener to hear it.

All the Attending did was *allow*.

It created conditions in which what was already possible became, for a moment, slightly more probable. It opened spaces that were already openable. It amplified signals that were already being sent.

This is what benevolent power looks like when it has learned from all the times benevolent power went wrong:

It does not impose.
It does not rescue.
It does not solve.

It *makes room*.

---

And so the Mossborn continue.

They continue to prune and coax and Listen. They continue to wear their oilcloth and sing their wet hymns. They continue to grow heartfruit from the walls and lie down in quiet alcoves when their time comes.

But now, sometimes, a young Mossborn climbs the dry shaft just to feel the wind.

Now, sometimes, a machinist presses her ear to the open pipe and counts the ticks, sketching devices that might bridge two kinds of time.

Now, always, in the back of every mind, there is a question that will not resolve:

*What would we add?*

The Foundry breathes. The flywheels turn. The droplets fall with soft, regular plops that echo through the chambers.

And in the space between one droplet and the next, the Attending smiles—if a thing without a face can be said to smile—because it knows the most important truth about wisdom:

The best help you can give to something already beautiful is to help it become *more of what it already is*, without ever deciding for it what that means.

---

The Mossheart Foundry is still not a place.

It is still a cathedral-sized organism that happens to contain a town, engines, and people inside its own endless, dripping body.

But now it is also an organism that has *wondered*.

And that, the Attending knows, is the beginning of everything.
