# The Attending

The power that turns its attention here is not a god in any sense the Scarp-dwellers would recognize. It does not descend; it does not speak from light. It arrives the way understanding arrives—slowly, then all at once, like the moment when a pattern you have been staring at resolves into a face.

It has been watching for some time. Not this place specifically, but places like it: the margins, the wounds, the sites where life presses itself into crevices the world has not yet finished making. It is drawn to these because they are honest. There is no pretense in the bowl, no narrative of triumph. Only the daily negotiation between flesh and mineral, the mutual insistence of two processes—biological and geological—each refusing to yield.

The power's first act is not an act at all. It is a withholding.

It sees the infant geology, the aggressive accretion, the stone that grows faster than the people can scrape. It could slow this. A whisper in the chemistry of the water, a fractional shift in the dissolved carbonate, and the travertine would gentle itself, would grant the Scarp-dwellers a century's respite. They could stand upright. They could sleep without waking to clear their thresholds. They could, perhaps, forget.

But the power has watched other places where it intervened too cleanly, and it knows the shape of that forgetting. The Scarp-dwellers are who they are *because* of the scraping. Their wide hands, their coiled spines, their communal density—these are not afflictions. They are adaptations, which is to say: they are answers. To remove the question is to make the answer meaningless. The power understands that rescue can be a kind of erasure.

So it withholds.

---

What it does instead is smaller. So small that no one notices, not even the Sump-Readers with their thermal sight.

It begins with the water.

The condensation in the bowl has always arrived from nowhere—a mystery the Scarp-dwellers have stopped questioning. The power does not change this. But it begins to *listen* to the arrival, to trace the invisible aquifer that feeds the weeping walls, and in that listening it finds a memory. The water here is old. It fell as rain on a surface that no longer exists, sank through stone for millennia, and is only now emerging, blinking, into the hollow. It carries the signature of a younger world.

The power cannot speak to the Scarp-dwellers directly—its voice would be fire to them, revelation too bright for fog-adapted eyes—but it can speak to the water. And water, unlike stone, is patient with messages.

It begins to encode.

Not language. Not instruction. Something more fundamental: a rhythm. The barometric pulse of the central sump, the rise and fall the Scarp-dwellers read like a heartbeat, begins to carry a second signal beneath the first. A long, slow undulation that takes years to complete a single cycle. To anyone watching for days or weeks, it is invisible. But the Sump-Readers—those with the fading thermal sight, who spend their lives squatting by the pool's edge—begin to feel it in their bones before they understand it with their minds.

The rhythm says: *You are not alone.*

Not in the sense of company. In the sense of continuity. The rhythm is teaching them, in the only language they might eventually understand, that the water remembers. That the stone they scrape was once something else—sky, cloud, ancient sea. That they themselves are part of a process older than the collapse, older than the marsh, older than the idea of land. The rhythm says: *This place is young, but you are not. You have been arriving here for a very long time.*

---

The second thing the power does is even smaller. It concerns the Folded.

The dead, plastered into the crevices and slowly armored in flowstone, have always been a kind of teaching—the infant earth learning to hold life by practicing on the lifeless. But the power notices something the Scarp-dwellers have not yet articulated: the crystal sleeves that form around the Folded are not uniform. Some grow dense and opaque; others develop a strange translucence, as if the mineral were trying to preserve rather than merely encase.

The power begins to encourage the latter.

Not in every case—that would be noticed, and noticing would lead to worship, and worship would lead to expectation, and expectation would curdle into dependency. But in some cases. One in ten, perhaps. One in twenty. When a body is placed in a crevice where the chemistry is *almost* right, the power breathes into the water a fractional adjustment. The resulting sleeve grows clearer. Within a generation, a grandchild scraping a wall might strike not merely a femur-shaped void but a *window*—a lens of travertine through which the ancestor's face is still faintly visible, preserved in mineral amber, eyes closed, expression whatever it was at the moment of folding.

The Scarp-dwellers do not understand what this means. They are not meant to. Understanding will come later, if it comes at all. For now, they simply begin to notice that some of the Folded can be *seen*, and this seeing changes the texture of their grief. The dead are not gone; they are *there*, behind the glass, waiting for the geology to decide what comes next. It is a small gift—the gift of faces in the stone, the gift of continuity made visible—and it costs the power almost nothing.

But it changes things.

A child who grows up knowing that her great-grandmother's face is embedded in the east scarp, watchable, wipeable clean of condensate on the anniversary of her folding, grows up with a different relationship to time. She is not living in the eternal present of survival. She is living in a story, and the story has witnesses.

---

The third thing the power does concerns the children who see in heat.

This sight—the wide opalescent eyes, the ability to read the infrared breath of the walls—fades in most Scarp-dwellers as they age. The power does not prevent this. The fading is natural, and natural things have their reasons. But it notices that the fading is not uniform. Some retain the sight longer. A few retain it always.

The power begins to protect these.

Not from harm—that would require intervention visible enough to distort—but from *forgetting*. The Scarp-dwellers, like all people in desperate circumstances, have developed a certain pragmatism about difference. The heat-seers are useful when young; they point to seeps before the water arrives, they read the thermal pulse of the sump, they warn of shifts in the stone's intentions. But as they age and most lose the sight, those who retain it become strange. They see things others cannot. They speak of warmth in walls that feel cold. They are, in a word, mad.

The power cannot cure this madness—it is not madness, only accurate perception—but it can create conditions in which the heat-seers are valued rather than merely tolerated. It does this by adjusting, very slightly, the thermal patterns of the stone itself.

Not enough to change the geology. Just enough to make it *interesting*.

The walls begin to develop hot spots that shift according to a pattern too complex for the fog-blinded to predict but just simple enough for the heat-seers to map. The community, following these maps, finds that certain paths through the tiers are warmer than others, that certain seasons favor certain routes. The heat-seers become navigators. They are no longer mad people muttering about invisible warmth; they are guides, readers of a second language the stone is speaking. Their difference becomes a role.

This is, perhaps, the power's largest intervention. It is still very small. The thermal patterns were always there, latent in the hydrology; the power has merely amplified them, made them legible to those with eyes to see. But the effect is that the Scarp-dwellers begin to develop a priesthood of perception—not worshippers, but specialists, those whose strangeness is harnessed rather than discarded.

And specialists, over generations, begin to develop knowledge.

---

The power does one more thing, and then it is done.

It concerns the rim.

The Scarp-dwellers carry their waste up and out, a pilgrimage to the edge, because the sump cannot be poisoned. But the rim is terrifying. The air there *moves*; it is thin and dry and assaultive. The light there *falls*, casting shadows the fog-dwellers have never learned to parse. Most who make the journey do so quickly, eyes averted, and descend as soon as they can. The rim is a boundary, the edge of the known, and edges are dangerous.

The power cannot make the rim less dangerous. Danger, like scraping, is part of the answer. But it can make the rim *interesting*.

It begins by encouraging, in the scree and sharp stone of the lip, a single species of plant that has been waiting for an opportunity. A lichen, technically. A partnership of algae and fungus that can survive on bare rock and moving air, that can fix its own nitrogen, that can spread in conditions where nothing else will grow. The lichen is native to the region; it exists in the outer flats, unremarked. The power simply gives it a foothold on the rim, a patch of stability where its spores can catch.

Within a decade, the rim is faintly green.

Not lush. Not inviting. Just *present*—a collar of grey-green crust around the lip of the bowl, visible from below, proof that the outside world is not merely emptiness and moving air. The Scarp-dwellers do not worship it. They do not rush to harvest it; it is too thin, too marginal, too strange. But they begin to look up. They begin to see the rim as something other than an exit.

And some of them—the young, the restless, the heat-seers whose sight shows them the warmth that leaks from the outer world—begin to climb higher than necessity demands. They begin to sit at the edge, wrapped against the wind, watching the lichen spread. They begin to imagine.

This is the power's final gift, and it is not a gift at all. It is a door, left ajar.

---

The power withdraws, if withdrawal is the word for a presence that was never quite there. It does not leave watchers or angels. It does not establish a covenant. It has not chosen these people; it has merely noticed them, and in noticing, offered what it could without distortion: a rhythm in the water that says *you are part of something older*; faces in the stone that say *the dead are still here*; a role for those who see differently; a collar of green on the rim that says *the world continues*.

Whether any of this leads to anything—to expansion, to theology, to a civilization that one day climbs the rim and walks the flats and forgets the bowl entirely—is not the power's concern. It is not in the business of outcomes. It is in the business of attention, of witnessing, of saying through the subtlest possible means: *I see you. You are not alone. What you are doing here, in this wet and crowded and impossible place, matters to something larger than your survival.*

The Scarp-dwellers will never know this. They will continue to scrape their thresholds each morning, to fold their dead into the crevices, to trade dryness and warmth and the services of heat-seers. They will continue to believe, if they believe anything, that they live at the world's newest point, where the planet is still announcing itself.

And they are right. They do.

But now the planet, through the intermediary of something vaster and more patient, is announcing *them* as well. The stone grows around their Folded. The water carries their rhythm. The lichen spreads at the lip of their home. They are being incorporated into the earth's memory.

In a few thousand years, when the bowl has stabilized and the accretion has slowed and the descendants of these people have become something else entirely, the geology will hold a record. Not of suffering—though there was suffering—but of insistence. Of hands that scraped and did not stop. Of bodies that pressed together in the fog and breathed as one. Of a community that refused to let the stone forget them.

The power, by then, will have turned its attention elsewhere. But it will remember this place. It will remember the sound of bone on wet stone, the chalky palimpsests on ancient skin, the faces in the flowstone watching with closed eyes.

It will remember that the earth, given the chance, will hold what it is offered. And that sometimes the most benevolent act is not to save, but to witness—to say, through the smallest possible adjustments, *yes, you are here. Yes, this is happening. Yes, you matter.*

And then to trust the rest to time.
