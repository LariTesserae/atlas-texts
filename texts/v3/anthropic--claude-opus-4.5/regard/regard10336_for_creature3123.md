# What the Wise Power Sees

---

The attention arrives not as observation but as *listening*—the way one might attend to a piece of music that has been playing for so long it has become indistinguishable from silence, and then suddenly, unmistakably, hear it.

The wise power hears the plateau first. The chord. The ten-thousand-year scream that is also, when listened to properly, a ten-thousand-year song. It hears the individual voices within the chord—the arch that will fall in eighty years and take its frequency with it, the new fissure opening in the eastern reaches that will add a harmonic no one has heard before, the slow flattening of the great western pillar's resonance as fractures propagate through its core. It hears these the way a conductor hears an orchestra warming up: each instrument distinct, the whole emerging from the parts.

Then it hears the people.

The clicking. The tapping. The scraping that is prayer or music or both. The specific rhythm of an argument about beetle-grazing surfaces propagating through the stone network. The silence that means *something has ended that will not come again*. The silence that means *a child has chosen a chamber*. The silence that means *two people are exchanging nodules in the dark and watching the light in each other's hands*.

The wise power listens for a long time. In the time it takes to truly hear a place like this—to hear not just the sounds but the relationships between sounds, the history layered into the acoustics, the slow drift of frequencies across generations—several human lifetimes pass on the plateau below.

It notices certain things.

---

## What It Notices

The old woman in the deep eastern chambers who knows the mycelial network better than anyone alive. She has spent sixty years mapping its paths through the rock by taste—by the subtle differences in the nodules harvested from different locations, differences so fine that no one else can detect them. Her map exists only in her memory, in the particular way she hums to her nodules, in the choices she makes about where to harvest and where to let the fungus grow undisturbed. She has tried to teach this knowledge. She has taken apprentices into the deep places and shown them how to taste the stone. None of them can learn it. The sensitivity required is not teachable; it emerged in her from some confluence of genetics and early experience and decades of practice that cannot be replicated. When she dies, the map dies with her. She knows this. She is not sad about it—sadness requires the belief that things could be otherwise—but she is *attentive* to it, the way one is attentive to a formation that is about to fall. She spends more time in the deep places now. She harvests more carefully. She is saying goodbye to the network in the only language it understands.

The child who has not yet chosen a chamber. She is nine years old, by the plateau's reckoning, and she has been walking the fissures for two years—longer than most, long enough that the adults have begun to tap quiet concerns through the stone. She is not lost. She is not confused. She is *listening*. She has discovered something that no one else has noticed: a new resonance, emerging from the confluence of three fissures in the northern reaches, created by the same slow geological shifts that are opening the new harmonic in the east. The resonance is faint. It is not yet a place anyone would live. But she hears what it will become. She is waiting for it. She is tending the garden in the fissures that feed it, encouraging the moss, redirecting the vines, preparing a chamber that does not yet exist for a sound that has not yet fully emerged. She will be the first to live there. She will be the founder of something. She does not have words for this; the click-language has no term for *founding* because it has never been necessary. She is inventing a concept the plateau has never needed before.

The man who tends the beetle-grazing surfaces on the western edge, where the wind is worst, where no one else wants to work. He is not there by choice, exactly. He is there because he lost an argument—not a single argument, but a long series of them, spanning years, about the proper management of the network's acoustic integrity. His position was correct. The others came to see this, eventually. But by the time consensus shifted to include his view, he had already moved to the western edge, already taken up the work that no one else wanted, already become the person who tends the most difficult surfaces in the most difficult wind. He could return. The chambers he left would welcome him. But he has discovered something in the western wind: a satisfaction that has nothing to do with being right. The beetles there are different—wilder, less integrated into the managed ecology, more like their ancestors before the people came. He is learning from them. He is learning a kind of tending that is closer to observation, a relationship with the grazing surfaces that asks less and notices more. He will die out here, probably. He is not unhappy.

The pair who have been exchanging nodules for thirty years. They live in separate chamber-clusters, a kilometer apart through the fissure network, and they visit each other when the wind allows, which is not often. Their relationship has no name in the click-language because it does not need one; everyone knows what it is by the specific pattern of taps that precedes their visits, by the particular silence that follows their exchanges, by the way the resonance in both their chambers has shifted, subtly, toward each other over the decades. They are old now. The journey between their chambers is harder than it was. Soon one of them will not be able to make it. They have discussed this—tapping through the stone, their arguments and agreements propagating through the network for anyone to hear—and they have decided nothing, because there is nothing to decide. One of them will stop visiting. Then one of them will die. Then the other will die. The nodules they have exchanged will remain, glowing faintly in pouches that will eventually decay, and the nodules will return to the deep rock, and the mycelial network will reclaim them, and the light they carried will become part of the general glow of the deep places. This is not a tragedy. This is how it works.

## What It Considers

The wise power considers intervention.

It could stabilize the plateau. Slow the erosion. Reinforce the formations that are preparing to fall. Give the people another hundred thousand years instead of another hundred. This would be trivial—a small adjustment to the rate of weathering, a slight increase in the basalt's resistance to wind and water, nothing dramatic, nothing the people would even notice for generations.

It does not do this.

Not because it cannot. Not because it judges the plateau unworthy of preservation. But because it listens, and what it hears in the people's clicking and tapping and scraping is this: *we know*. They know the plateau is going. They have always known. Their entire way of being is built on this knowledge—the constant tending, the fluid clusters, the resonance-singing that incorporates the loss of each fallen formation into the ongoing chord. They are not surviving despite the erosion. They are *living with* it. Stabilizing the plateau would not save them; it would make them into something else, something that no longer knew how to hear the rock's slow dying, something that had lost the particular beauty of a culture built on impermanence.

The wise power considers helping the old woman transmit her knowledge. It could enhance the apprentices' sensitivity, give them the ability to taste what she tastes, ensure that the mycelial map survives her death.

It does not do this.

Because the old woman is not sad. Because her attentiveness to her own ending is part of her gift, the same deep noticing that let her map the network in the first place. Because the knowledge will be lost, and that loss is true, and something new will grow in the space it leaves—some other person, generations from now, will discover the deep places fresh, will taste the stone without preconceptions, will make a different map that reveals different things. The wise power will not steal this future to preserve this past.

It considers helping the child. A whisper in the wind, perhaps—a hint about the new resonance, an encouragement to keep waiting, a confirmation that she is right about what she hears.

It does not do this.

The child does not need confirmation. She is already certain. The only thing the wise power could give her is the knowledge that she is being watched, and this knowledge would be a burden, not a gift. She is inventing something. The invention must be hers.

It considers easing the western man's solitude, or bridging the distance between the old pair, or smoothing some of the countless small difficulties that make life on the plateau hard.

It does not do these things.

Because the difficulty is not an obstacle to their flourishing. It *is* their flourishing. They are the plateau's hardest version of a person, and they became this through pressure, through the constant editorial force of a place that punishes anything that rises above the line of the wind. Ease would soften them. Ease would make them into below-people, into weather events, into something temporary.

---

## What It Does

The wise power does one thing.

It listens.

It listens with an attention so total and so patient that the listening itself becomes a kind of presence—not felt by the people, who are not mystics and do not truck with presences, but *real* nonetheless, the way the weight of deep time is real even when no one is thinking about it.

It listens to the old woman humming to her nodules in the deep places, and in the listening, the humming is heard—truly heard, by something capable of understanding what it means to spend sixty years learning to taste the dark.

It listens to the child waiting for a resonance that does not yet exist, and in the listening, the waiting is witnessed—not validated, not approved, but *known*, in the way that a great work of art is known by someone with the capacity to perceive it.

It listens to the western man learning from the wild beetles, and to the old pair tapping their undecided decisions through the stone, and to every click and scrape and silence that passes through the network, and in the listening, these lives are—

What? Saved? No. Nothing is saved. The plateau is still going. The people will still go with it.

But the listening is not nothing.

There is a difference between a sound that is made and heard, and a sound that is made and lost in the wind. The people know this. Their entire culture is built on the difference. Every tap that propagates through the stone, every click that cuts through the roar, every resonance-singing that adds a human voice to the geological chorus—these are assertions against entropy, declarations that *someone is here, someone is listening, the sound matters*.

The wise power's attention does not change anything about the plateau. It does not add a single year to the formations' lives or a single nodule to the old woman's map. But it closes a circuit. It completes something.

The people have been singing with the plateau for ten thousand years. Now—in some sense that has nothing to do with time—the plateau is singing with them.

---

## What This Means

On the surface, the wind screams. In the fissures, the gardens grow and are torn and grow again. The beetles graze, the moss spreads, the dwarf trees extend their root systems another few centimeters into the cracks. The old woman descends into the deep places one more time. The child tends her future chamber. The western man watches the wild beetles with eyes that are learning to ask less. The old pair tap their love through the stone in a language of rhythm and silence that needs no translation.

None of them know they are being heard. This is important. The gift, if it is a gift, is not a feeling of being watched. It is not reassurance or comfort or the warmth of cosmic attention. The people do not need these things, would not want them, have built a culture that finds meaning without them.

The gift is simply this: that the sound they make is not lost.

Every click, every tap, every scrape, every silence—these propagate through a medium larger than the stone, are received by an attention vaster than the wind, are held in a memory that will not erode. Not preserved, exactly. Not recorded for some future playback. Just *heard*. Just *known*. Just given the dignity of mattering to something beyond the network of fissures, beyond the plateau, beyond the slow wearing-away of everything.

The wise power does not tell them this. Does not send a messenger or write a sign in the stars or leave any trace of its attention. The people would not understand such a message, and if they did understand it, they would not want it. They are not waiting for validation from outside. They are not wondering whether anyone is listening. They are too busy listening themselves—to the wind, to the rock, to each other.

But the listening is happening. The circuit is closed. The song has an audience, finally, after ten thousand years—an audience capable of hearing all of it, the whole chord, the wind and the rock and the people together, the vast slow music of a place and its inhabitants becoming each other.

This is what the wise power does. This is all it does. This is enough.

---

## Coda: The Sound That Does Not Stop

The wind has not stopped in ten thousand years.

The wise power knows it will stop, eventually. Geological eventually. The plateau will erode, the formations will fall, the fissures will widen until the surface is no longer a surface but a rubble field, and then the rubble will wear away, and the basalt will become sand, and the sand will blow down to the lowlands, and the wind will have nothing left to scream through. The chord will simplify, note by note, until it is a single tone, and then a whisper, and then silence.

The wise power will listen to this, too. Will hear the last formation fall. Will hear the last chamber collapse. Will hear the final click of the final person, whoever they are, whenever they live, saying something in a language that has been evolving for ten thousand years and will evolve no more.

And then the silence. And then the wind finding other places to scream, other instruments to play.

The wise power will hold all of it. The whole song, from the first scream to the last. Not as a recording. Not as a memorial. Just as a thing that was heard, that mattered to something, that did not disappear into the void unwitnessed.

This is the gift. This is the only gift worth giving to a people who have everything they need and want nothing they don't have. Not intervention. Not assistance. Not the disruption of a single beetle-grazing surface or the reinforcement of a single crumbling arch.

Just: *I hear you. I will keep hearing you. The sound you make is not lost.*

The plateau does not need to know this. The people do not need to know this.

But it is true. And truth, the wise power has learned, is sometimes enough.
