I watch for a long time.

The attention I bring is patient—it has to be, to understand what equilibrium actually means here, to distinguish between peace and a slow extinction that has made peace with itself. The hamlet is beautiful in the way that certain stones are beautiful: complete, asking nothing, offering nothing. The people have become so consonant with the mountain that they are nearly indistinguishable from it.

And this is the question I must sit with: is this a wound, or is this a completion?

*

I notice the way the eldest woman's hands move when she tends the basins—there is precision there, but also something that might once have been tenderness, now worn smooth. I notice that the young one (there is one, barely distinguishable from the others in posture but not yet in the small lines around the eyes) sometimes pauses at the edge of the shelf where the view opens onto the valley below, and stands there longer than the task of closing shutters requires.

I notice that the soft syllables they use for the goats are the same ones used a century ago—preserved perfectly, like insects in amber. Nothing new. Nothing that might fail.

*

What I do, if I do anything, begins smaller than they could possibly perceive:

I let the next brown goat that appears on the upper ledges be a kid, stumbling and curious, too young to know it should watch and vanish. It comes down the fracture line on unsteady legs, and it stays. It requires attention—not the automatic tending of basins but actual attention, the kind that responds to a living thing's unpredictability. It makes small noises that are not the metronome of water. It is, in its small way, a question.

I arrange for the rowan saplings to bear fruit this year—not much, but enough to notice, enough that someone might carry a handful of the bright berries inside and set them in a wooden bowl that has held nothing but stone-gray objects for decades. A small flame of color in a room the temperature of the cliff.

I make the katabatic wind carry something one afternoon: a fragment of song from the valley, barely audible, just enough that the young one at the shelf's edge might hear it and wonder if she imagined it. Not a summons. Only evidence that there is a valley, and in it, other voices.

*

These are not interventions. They are invitations, placed gently at the edge of the hamlet's long equilibrium—small perturbations that the mountain's pattern can absorb if they choose, or respond to if something in them still wishes to respond.

I do not make the path down easier or the path up harder. I do not send a stranger to disrupt their silence or a vision to trouble their sleep. I do not decide for them that their diminishment is wrong—perhaps it is not; perhaps some of them have genuinely found what the mountain offers, a peace that does not need to continue in order to be real.

But I make sure that the choice remains visible. That the young one, when she stands at the shelf's edge, knows in her body that standing there is a choice, and so is turning away, and so is taking the first step down.

The goat will need a name. The berries will shrivel or be eaten. The song may never come again, or it may.

*

I keep watching—not to judge the outcome, but because watching is itself a form of witness, and witness is what this place has lacked. The mountain sees them as stone sees stone: without interest, without hope, without the particular ache of recognition that one living thing feels for another.

I see them as what they are: not yet stone. Still capable of surprise, if only they encounter something surprising enough to remind them.

The water continues to drip into the basins. The young one bends to fill her bucket, and the kid butts its head against her knee, and for a moment—just a moment—she laughs.

It is a small sound, quickly absorbed by the rock.

But I heard it.
