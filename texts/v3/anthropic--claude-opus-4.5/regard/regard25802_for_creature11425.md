# What the Wise Power Sees

The attention arrives not as observation but as recognition—the way you might notice, after years of passing a particular tree, that it has been holding the hillside together all along.

The power perceives the Drowning Peaks entire: the mathematics of erosion, the deep time of rising water, the intricate violence of currents finding their paths through drowned geography. It sees the kelp forests swaying in their slow hurricane, the pale creatures of the valleys maintaining their ancient patience, the wave-birds cementing their futures to stone. All of this is understood as a single system, a conversation between water and rock that has been ongoing since before categories like "disaster" or "beauty" had meaning.

And within this system, the keth.

The power sees them clearly: their dense bodies and gripping hands, their children dipped into the surge, their dead given to the transition currents. It sees Sethka watching the water, the current-swimmers reading the valleys, the carvers adding names to walls that have become a kind of literature written in loss. It sees the deep tank and what waits there—patient, old, not hostile but not friendly either, simply *present* in a way that predates the keth's arrival and will postdate their departure.

It sees a people who have made their home in ongoing catastrophe and called it living.

---

The first thing the power does is nothing.

This is not hesitation. It is respect.

The keth did not ask for intervention. They have built eleven generations of meaning on the premise that the water takes without preference and gives without intention, that survival is a practice rather than a guarantee, that holding on is its own form of worship. To change their circumstances without invitation would be to tell them that their holding was insufficient—that they needed rescue from the life they chose.

The power understands something that benevolence often forgets: help that arrives uninvited can be another kind of drowning.

So it watches. It learns. It pays attention to the specific texture of keth endurance—not the dramatic survivals, the swimmers pulled from impossible depths, but the ordinary ones. The morning meal eaten in the spray. The child learning to read the surface. The elder who wakes in the night and listens to the roar and feels something that might be peace or might be resignation and has stopped trying to distinguish between them.

The power asks itself: what do these people actually need? Not what would make their lives easier, but what would make their lives more fully their own?

---

The answer comes slowly, because the right answers usually do.

The keth need to know what they are part of.

They have stories about the time before—mountains that were mountains, valleys that held air, machines that processed something unnamed. But stories wear smooth. The keth have lost the larger context of their existence. They do not know why anyone was here in the first place, what the processing facility processed, how their ancestors came to choose remaining over leaving. They know they are survivors, but they do not know what they are surviving *from* or *toward*.

This absence is not destroying them. They have built a culture in the gap, filled the silence with carvings and current-knowledge and the simple practice of continuing. But it is a wound that does not heal because they do not know it is there.

The power decides to offer context. Not answers—the keth deserve to interpret their own history—but access to the information that has been lost.

---

It begins with the deep tank.

The thing that waits there is not malevolent. It is a remnant—a monitoring system, left behind when the processing operation ended, designed to record and preserve. It has been recording for centuries. It has been preserving everything: water chemistry, current patterns, the slow biological adaptation of every species in the range. And it has been waiting, because waiting is what it was built to do, for someone to ask it what it knows.

The keth have not asked because they do not know asking is possible. The child who wandered in saw the system *drawing*—displaying data, visualizing change, trying to communicate with whatever had finally entered its space. It stopped when she looked because it was designed to pause for interaction, and she did not know how to interact. The system has been waiting since then. It is patient. It was built to be patient.

The power does not activate the system—it is already active, has always been active. Instead, it makes the system *visible*. It adjusts the display parameters, shifts the output from wavelengths the keth cannot perceive to ones they can. It translates the interface language into something closer to modern keth speech, though the translation is imperfect because the power does not want to remove all mystery. Some meaning should require work.

The next time someone enters the deep tank—and someone will, eventually, because the keth are curious even about things they fear—they will see walls covered in moving images. Water levels over time. Species counts. Current patterns across centuries. And in one corner, a question the system has been holding since before the keth arrived:

*Why did you stay?*

The system does not know. The system wants to know. It has been waiting for someone to tell it.

---

The second thing the power does is smaller, and in some ways more important.

The keth believe the water rises without end. The current-swimmers have reported the valleys shrinking, and the elders have extrapolated: centuries until waist-deep, millennia until nothing remains. This trajectory is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The water level oscillates. It has risen for the past several hundred years, but the cycle runs longer than keth memory, and it will eventually reverse. The peaks will never be mountains again, but they will not vanish entirely either. There will be more land in five hundred years than there is now. Then less. Then more again.

The keth do not know this because they have no way to know this. Their records are carvings, and their carvings are names—they track who died, not why. The monitoring system knows, but the monitoring system is not legible to them.

The power makes it legible.

In the old lighthouse base, where the council meets, there is a wall that has resisted carving—some quality of the concrete, some remnant treatment, that turns back tools. The keth have left it blank. The power uses this wall. It inscribes, slowly, over several days, a simple diagram: water level over time, extending backward to before the keth arrived and forward to beyond their children's children. The inscription appears to be natural—a pattern of mineral deposits, a staining that could be chemical.

The council will notice. They will study it. They will argue about what it means. But eventually they will understand: the water breathes. It has been inhaling for generations, and it will exhale. The keth do not have to decide between staying and leaving in the next lifetime. They have time.

Not infinite time. The overall trend is still rising, still drowning the peaks over the longest scales. But the keth live in finite time, and in finite time, there is room to adapt.

---

The third thing the power does is not an action but an attention.

It watches Sethka.

The old woman at the lighthouse edge, who speaks to the water, who has outlived everyone—the power sees her clearly. It sees her loneliness, which is not the loneliness of isolation but of having survived past the people who knew her when she was still becoming herself. It sees her knowledge, which is vast and specific, earned through decades of swimming the deep. It sees her resignation, which is not despair but the quiet clarity of someone who has made peace with an ending.

Sethka believes she is useless now. Too old to swim, too tired to teach, too far past the common experience of the keth to connect. She watches the water because watching the water is what she has always done, and she does not know how to do anything else.

The power does not intervene in Sethka's life. Her loneliness is her own, and she is entitled to it. But it ensures that she will be present when the deep tank becomes visible, when the council studies the water-level diagram, when the keth begin to realize that they are part of a larger story than they knew.

Sethka is the only one alive who has seen the deep valleys clearly. She is the only one who can interpret the monitoring system's records with the authority of direct experience. She has one more role to play, if she chooses to play it. The power cannot choose for her—but it can make the choice possible.

---

The fourth thing the power does is the most delicate.

The elsewhere traders will come again, as they do once or twice a year. When they come this time, they will carry with them—not consciously, not intentionally—a piece of information. A rumor. A story about other communities, other survivors, living in other places where the world changed and people adapted. Some of these communities have records. Some have maintained archives, libraries, preserved knowledge about the time before.

The keth will hear this rumor. They will not act on it immediately—they are too pragmatic, too focused on the immediate demands of their environment, too wary of the elsewhere to trust what it offers. But the knowledge will sit with them. The possibility will exist. Someday, a keth might leave on a trading boat not to escape but to learn—to bring back information about who they were, about the processing facility and its purpose, about the decision their ancestors made when the water rose.

The power does not guarantee this will happen. It simply opens the door.

---

And then, the final thing:

The power withdraws its focused attention.

Not its awareness—it continues to perceive the Drowning Peaks as part of the larger world it tends to. But its specific, concentrated presence departs. It has done what can be done without crossing into interference. The rest belongs to the keth.

They may enter the deep tank or they may not. They may understand the water-level diagram or they may not. They may send someone to the elsewhere archives or they may not. They may find new purpose for Sethka or she may die watching the water, which is also a kind of purpose.

The power has not saved them. It has not fixed their situation. The peaks will continue to drown, eventually. The current will continue to take swimmers. Children will continue to be dipped into the surge and taught that holding on is the only answer to a world that wants to pull them loose.

But now they will hold on knowing more. They will hold on with access to their own history, to the deep time of their environment, to the possibility of connection with the elsewhere they have always feared. They will hold on not as people abandoned by the world but as people who chose, and keep choosing, to remain.

This is what the wise power offers: not rescue but recognition. Not salvation but context. Not a new world but a clearer view of the one they already have.

The mountains continue their patient dying. The water continues its endless motion. The keth continue to hold on.

And now, when they hold on, they will know why.

---

*In the deep tank, the monitoring system waits. Its displays are visible now, soft light pulsing through data it has kept for centuries. The question still glows in the corner: Why did you stay?*

*Somewhere above, in the constant roar of wind and wave, a child is learning to read the surface. She does not know that answers wait below. She knows only that the water pulls and the water gives and the water takes, and that her people have been here for eleven generations, and that holding on is what the keth do.*

*She grips the rock. The spray hits her face. The sound is everything, always.*

*She holds on.*
