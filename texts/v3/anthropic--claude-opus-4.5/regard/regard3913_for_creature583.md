# What the Distant Kindness Might Do

The benevolent power observes the Bell Garden for a long time before acting—long enough to understand that this place has already solved most of its own problems, has already become what it needed to become. The vines feed. The bells sing. The pilgrims weep and leave and return. Cira oils the tunnel lip. The Breath-Drinkers climb. Even Venn, hidden in the walls, has found a kind of equilibrium, though whether it is peace or paralysis the power cannot quite determine.

So it does not *fix* anything. There is little broken here.

Instead, it does three small things:

---

**First, for Cira:**

The power notices that she has not entered the garden for herself—not truly, not as a pilgrim—in forty-one years. She enters to touch shoulders, to count bells, to collect the fallen. But she has not stood in the empty center and let the sound wash through her since the day she became keeper.

She has forgotten that she is allowed.

So the power places a dream in her sleep—not a vision, nothing so dramatic, just a simple dream of walking through the neck-tunnel with her hands empty, with no task waiting, with nothing to count or collect or maintain. In the dream she stands in the center and the bells sing and she does not weep, because she has already wept enough for one lifetime, but she does *breathe*. Deeply. Deliberately. The way the Breath-Drinkers breathe in the high reaches.

When she wakes, she will remember the dream only vaguely. But something in her will shift. And the next time the garden is empty—truly empty, in one of those rare moments between pilgrims—she will find herself crawling through the tunnel with her hands open, and she will stand where she has not stood in four decades, and she will understand that keeping the garden was never meant to exclude *being kept by it*.

---

**Second, for Old Glass:**

The lizard is dying. Not today, not this season, but soon. Its ancient body has begun the slow process of becoming still.

The power cannot grant it more life—that is not how these things work, and besides, Old Glass does not want more life. It wants what it has always wanted: warmth, moisture, the shake of bells dislodging prey.

So the power does something simpler. It adjusts, very slightly, the angle of light that enters through the gourd's translucent skin on the morning Old Glass will finally stop moving. The light will fall directly on the lizard's resting place—a crook between two amber vines where it has slept for the past three years. The warmth will be perfect. The gnats will be plentiful. The bells will be singing a particular cascade that Old Glass has always favored, a sequence triggered by the potter's wheels in the district below.

It will not know it is dying. It will simply be warm, and fed, and listening, and then it will be still, and the stillness will feel like the moment between one breath and the next, extended infinitely.

This is not a gift the lizard can appreciate. But it is a gift nonetheless.

---

**Third, for Venn:**

This is the hardest one. The man in the walls is not suffering, exactly—he has found his equilibrium, his symbiosis with the sound. But he is also not *living*. He is waiting, and he has forgotten what he is waiting for.

The power considers many interventions. It could reveal him to Cira, who would understand. It could make his hiding place uncomfortable, force him back into the world. It could speak to him directly, though this would terrify him beyond reason.

Instead, it does something gentler.

It sends a pilgrim.

She is a bell-maker from a city two weeks' journey to the south, where they fire their clay at very high temperatures and the bells come out smooth as glass. She is sixty-four years old. She has spent her life perfecting a technique that no one else has mastered, and now she has come to hang her final bell—a thing with no internal chambers at all, just a pure uninterrupted space that produces a tone so clean it sounds almost like silence.

She hangs it on the amber vine, three meters from where Venn's seven-chambered bell has hung for six years.

When the garden's frequencies find her bell, it responds with that clean pure tone—and the tone *resonates* with Venn's bell in a way no other bell has ever resonated with it. The seven chambers and the empty space create something between them, a third sound that neither could produce alone, a harmony that makes Venn's breath catch in his throat.

He moves without thinking. He reaches toward his bell, and his hand passes through a shaft of light that the pilgrim below can see.

She looks up.

Their eyes meet.

The power does not control what happens next. Perhaps she screams, and Venn retreats deeper into the walls, and nothing changes. Perhaps she understands—bell-makers sometimes understand things that others don't—and she nods, and keeps his secret, and leaves him to his listening. Perhaps she speaks to him, and he speaks back, and something cracks open in him that has been sealed for six years.

The power does not know. The power does not *need* to know. It has given him a moment of being seen, which is the opposite of solitude and also, somehow, the completion of it.

What Venn does with that moment is his own.

---

**And for the garden itself:**

The power does nothing.

The garden is already what it should be—a hollow vessel drinking the world's noise, giving back song, holding the emptiness that the crowded city cannot provide. It does not need intervention. It does not need improvement.

It needs only to continue.

So the power watches for a while longer—watches Cira wake from her dream and rub her eyes, watches Old Glass shift slightly on its vine, watches the new bell settle into the chorus, watches the light move across the walls in its slow daily rotation.

Then it withdraws, leaving the garden to its sounding, its holding, its patient and unquenchable thirst.

Some things are already answered. Some hungers are meant to remain.
