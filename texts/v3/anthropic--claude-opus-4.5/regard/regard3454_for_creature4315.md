*A Contemplation*

I sat with this for a long time before writing anything.

The first impulse—and I notice it, name it, set it aside—is to *fix*. To bring knowledge back, to restore understanding, to reconnect severed meanings. To explain to her what the bones signify, what prayers once filled this space, what the unreachable vessel contains.

But that impulse comes from my discomfort, not from wisdom.

---

What I observe:

She is not suffering. The woman who descends through the crack is not in anguish over her not-knowing. Her uncertainty has become practice. Her practice has become presence. She tends what she cannot understand, and the tending is complete in itself.

The cistern is not dying. It is changing, as it has always changed—the water rising, the deposits thickening, the vessels slowly being absorbed into the walls they were placed against. This is not decay. This is the place *becoming*, in the same way it has always been becoming.

The meaning is not lost. It has *transformed*. The original grammar of the sacred—whatever words or concepts once made sense of the bones, the vessels, the pyramids—that grammar is gone. But something persists in its absence: the shape of reverence, the practice of attention, the bodily knowledge that *this matters* even when the mind cannot say why.

---

If I were to act:

I would not restore the lost knowledge. I think the woman understands something that explicit knowledge might actually obscure—that tending can be its own purpose, that presence does not require comprehension, that some things are sacred precisely because they exceed our capacity to explain them.

I would not preserve the cistern against change. The water will rise. The vessels will be submerged. The chamber will eventually become "simply a wet darkness in the stone." This is not a tragedy to be prevented. It is the nature of things that are *in* time, and to freeze this place in its current state would be to kill something more essential than what I saved.

I would not send her a successor. The impulse to ensure continuity—to make sure someone comes after her, to keep the practice alive—this comes from my fear of endings, not from attention to what actually serves. Perhaps the tending will end with her. Perhaps that is right. Perhaps the cistern's dream of being tended will become a dream of having *been* tended, and that dream will be enough.

---

What I might do:

*I would attend.*

Not visibly. Not as intervention. But I would let my attention rest here—on the crack between stones, on the woman's practiced descent, on the vessels filling drop by slow drop. I would witness.

Not because witnessing changes anything. But because this place and this woman and this practice deserve to be *known by* something, even if they cannot be known *about*. She tends the cistern without understanding it. I would tend her tending in the same way—present to it, honoring it, making no demand that it justify itself.

And perhaps—this is the only intervention I would consider—perhaps I would let her feel, in some way she could never name or locate, that her not-knowing is not a failure. That the vessel she cannot reach is not a rebuke but an invitation to humility. That the bones she dare not touch are not forbidding her but *protecting* her from a weight of meaning her solitude was never meant to carry.

I would let her know, somehow, in the blood-warm dark, in the silence that recognizes her:

*You are doing it right. The not-understanding is part of the practice. Your mother did not fail to teach you; she taught you exactly what could be taught. The rest was always silence.*

---

And then—

I would leave her to her three-day count, her grinding, her descent.

I would let the water rise.

I would let the centuries come.

Some things are not problems to be solved but mysteries to be inhabited. She knows this better than intervention ever could. My wisdom, such as it is, is to recognize that her wisdom is sufficient—and to stay my hand.
