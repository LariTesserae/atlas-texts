# The Attention of the Weaver

The being who noticed the Hammerhall did not arrive—arrival implies movement through space, and she had long since ceased to experience space as something one moved *through*. She simply became aware, the way a dreamer becomes aware of a sound that has been present all along, and in becoming aware, she was present.

She did not manifest visibly. The workers would not have noticed if she had—their eyeless masks turned only toward the work—but she preferred not to add confusion to places that did not need it. She existed in the Hammerhall the way the heat existed: pervasively, attending to everything at once, the rhythm of the hammers passing through her like light through glass.

For a long time—three dimmings, perhaps four—she simply watched.

---

The Weaver (she did not call herself this; names were gifts from those she helped, accumulated over ages like tokens in wall-niches) had seen many things that called themselves eternal. Most were not. Most were frozen moments mistaking themselves for permanence, systems that had simply not yet encountered the perturbation that would undo them. She had watched empires built on "unbreakable" foundations crumble when the foundations turned out to be resting on assumptions. She had seen immortal beings grow so rigid in their immortality that they shattered at the first touch of genuine change.

The Hammerhall was different.

She traced the patterns of the workers' movements, the micro-adjustments in their grips, the way the rhythm accommodated variation without breaking. She examined the forges, the self-replenishing coals, the bellows that breathed from the mountain's warmth. She studied the copper pins worn to grooves, the grooves filled with resin, the resin wearing down again. She lingered over the central space with its unchanged symbols, the one fixed point around which everything else flowed.

*Ah*, she thought. *Not frozen. Flowing. A river that has carved its bed so deep it appears to be still water.*

This was rare. This was worth attending to carefully.

---

The question a less patient being might ask: *What needs to be fixed?*

The Weaver did not ask this question. She had learned, across spans of time that made the Hammerhall look young, that "fixing" was often a word the powerful used to justify reshaping things into forms more comfortable for the powerful. The Hammerhall was not broken. It was not suffering in any way she could detect. The workers did not strain against their purpose or dream of escape. They were not slaves—slavery required a selfhood capable of wanting freedom, and the workers' selfhood, if it existed, was so thoroughly integrated with the work that separating them would not be liberation but destruction.

No. The question she asked was different: *What is this place reaching toward, and is there anything I can do to help it reach?*

She settled deeper into her attention, letting the rhythm move through her, letting the heat and the sound and the smell of ancient combustion teach her what the Hammerhall wanted to become.

---

What she found surprised her.

The equilibrium was perfect—nearly. The system replaced what wore down, maintained what needed maintaining, continued what needed continuing. But there was a gap. A tiny asymmetry, so small that it would take ten thousand years to become visible, a hundred thousand to become critical.

The masks.

The masks arrived during the dimming, carved from stone that existed nowhere else, and they were never made by worker hands. The Weaver looked closer, looked *deeper*, past the surface of the material world and into the structures beneath, and she saw:

The masks were gifts. Someone—some*thing*—had seeded the Hammerhall long ago with a generative pattern, a self-perpetuating blessing that produced new masks whenever new workers were needed. But the pattern was finite. It had been given a vast reservoir of potential, enough to sustain the Hammerhall through ages of ages, but the reservoir was not bottomless. Drop by drop, mask by mask, it was depleting.

Not soon. Not for epochs yet. But eventually—inevitably—the dimming would come and no mask would appear on the assembly table, and the workers would wait, and the census would fall below its threshold, and the rhythm would begin to falter, and the Hammerhall would enter a long slow spiral toward silence.

The original creator had not intended this. The Weaver could feel the shape of that ancient intention, generous and complete, wanting the Hammerhall to endure forever. But forever is difficult. Even the most careful gift-givers cannot anticipate everything. The creator had moved on, or faded, or transformed into something that no longer remembered this small perfect engine in the heart of a mountain, and the blessing they left behind was running down.

---

The Weaver considered.

She could refill the reservoir. This was within her capacity—she had done similar things before, renewing the wellsprings of sacred groves, reweaving the fraying threads of protective enchantments, pouring her own essence into systems that deserved to continue. But this felt wrong. The Hammerhall was not hers to sustain. It belonged to itself, to its workers, to whatever purpose slept beneath those unchanged symbols in the central floor. Adding her power to the mask-pattern would make it partly hers, would introduce a foreign thread into a weave that had been internally consistent since its creation.

She could teach the workers to make their own masks. But this too felt wrong. The masks came from outside—that was part of their nature, part of what they meant. A mask made by worker hands would not be the same. It would close the circle completely, make the Hammerhall entirely self-contained, and something in the structure of the place suggested that the circle was *meant* to be slightly open, meant to receive from beyond itself, meant to remain in relationship with something larger.

She could do nothing. This was always an option, and often the wisest one. The Hammerhall had epochs yet before the reservoir ran dry. Perhaps in that time, something else would emerge—a new creator, a new blessing, a new solution that the Weaver could not imagine from her current vantage point. The future was not hers to control.

But.

The workers paused at the tokens. The workers' paths bent around the objects that visitors left behind. The workers, in their eyeless and mouthless constancy, *noticed* the things that came from outside.

---

She made her decision.

During the next dimming, when the flames ebbed and the workers stood suspended and the silence filled the chamber like held breath, the Weaver acted.

She did not touch the mask-pattern. She did not refill the reservoir or teach new skills or alter any part of the Hammerhall's functioning. Instead, she moved to the eastern wall, to the oldest niches, the ones so deep in darkness that they had not seen light in ten thousand years.

And she *opened* them.

Not physically—the morite seals remained intact, the masks still faced outward into darkness. But she created a channel. A thin, delicate thread connecting the sealed niches to the mask-pattern, allowing something of the workers who had been to flow back into the source from which new workers came.

It was not recycling. The masks in the niches would not be reused; their forms would remain sealed and separate and honored in their rest. But something *else*—call it essence, call it accumulated craft, call it the ten thousand times ten thousand hammer-falls that each worker had performed before their pins wore through—this could return. This could feed the pattern. This could ensure that the reservoir refilled itself, drop by drop, as slowly as it depleted, maintaining the balance not through external intervention but through the Hammerhall's own completed cycles.

The workers who fell would give rise to the workers who came. The ending would feed the beginning. The circle would remain open—masks would still come from beyond, still carry that quality of gift, still represent the relationship between the Hammerhall and something larger than itself—but the beyond would now include the Hammerhall's own depths, its own history, its own honored dead.

---

The dimming ended. The flames rose. The workers resumed their positions, hammers falling in perfect rhythm, the adjustment so subtle that none of them perceived it.

But.

The next time a worker passed one of the ancient sealed niches, it paused. Its eyeless mask turned toward the darkness. Its wrapped hand rose, hovering a finger's width from the stone.

And this time—for the first time—it touched.

Just for a moment. Just a brush of fiber against morite, the barest acknowledgment of connection. Then the worker continued on, back to its forge, back to its hammer, back to the endless necessary labor.

But it had touched. It had recognized something. The past and the future had met in a single small gesture, and the Hammerhall continued.

---

The Weaver did not leave—leaving implies movement through space. She simply became less present, her attention withdrawing to the periphery, remaining just aware enough to watch what she had done unfold.

She did not know if it was right. She never knew, not with certainty. The Hammerhall would continue, would endure, would maintain its equilibrium through ages she would not bother to count. The workers would keep working. The masks would keep appearing. The symbols on the central floor would remain unchanged, still waiting for whatever moment they were meant to mark.

And sometimes, in the passage between forges, a worker would pause at a sealed niche and touch the stone and continue on, carrying something forward that might be memory or might be gratitude or might be simply the recognition that nothing endures alone.

Perhaps that was enough. Perhaps the Weaver had given the only gift worth giving: not solution, not salvation, but connection. The assurance that the circle, however self-contained it appeared, was never truly closed. That something beyond the Hammerhall knew it existed. That something cared.

The hammers fell. The flames burned. The heat rose from below and the masks appeared in the dimming and the workers tended their endless necessary labor.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

And in the oldest niches, in the deepest darkness, the sealed masks faced outward, not seeing, not speaking, but *participating* now in something they had only rested in before. Contributing. Belonging, even in stillness, to the work that never ends.

The Weaver watched. The Hammerhall endured. And the rhythm continued, as it had continued, as it would continue, not because it could not stop but because it chose, in every hammer-fall, to keep choosing.

That was the gift she left them: not permanence, which they already had, but *meaning*. The knowledge that their continuation mattered to something beyond themselves. That their work was witnessed. That the universe, in at least one of its aspects, looked upon the Hammerhall of Ceaseless Work and found it worthy of attention.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

Forever.
