# A Consideration

The wise power—let us call it simply the Witness, for that is what it does first and longest—turns its attention to the Calving Plains the way morning light finds a valley: gradually, then all at once.

It perceives the city being born. It perceives the Midwives keeping their vigil. It perceives the glass-wolves in their patient circles, the herdbeasts in their slow migrations, the not-birds riding columns of warmth into the gray sky.

And for a very long time, measured in the completion of seven towers and the birth of two herdbeast calves and the death of one Midwife (the engineer who predicted construction patterns, whose heart simply stopped one morning while she was watching a foundation form), the Witness does nothing at all.

---

This is the first wisdom: to recognize when something is already working.

The Witness sees Thread at the center, dreaming of being ice, becoming slowly coterminous with the network they have watched so long. It sees Corso standing with his hand against the finished tower, receiving nothing, expecting nothing, simply present. It sees Yennet on the ice, learning patience from creatures who learned it from the plain itself.

It sees the city growing at exactly the pace it should grow—not faster, which would mean error; not slower, which would mean decay. It sees the ecosystem finding its balances, predator and prey and scavenger all adjusting to each other and to the built environment emerging around them.

What would improvement mean, here? Faster construction? But the Midwives understand something the Witness also understands: that the slowness is not inefficiency. The slowness is the point. A city that builds itself over centuries builds itself *correctly*. It has time to notice its own mistakes, to adapt to its own existence, to become what it needs to become rather than what someone once imagined it should be.

The Witness could optimize. It could make the constructors more efficient, could eliminate the asymmetries that sometimes emerge in the towers, could smooth the path of the herdbeasts so that no glass-wolf ever catches them, could cure Thread of their dissolution into the network (if that is a disease) or accelerate it (if that is a gift).

But optimization toward what end?

The Midwives are happy. Not in the loud way, not in the way that demands celebration or recognition, but in the quiet way that comes from having found the thing you were meant to do and doing it. The city is being born well. The ecosystem is thriving. Even the deaths—the engineer, the occasional herdbeast, the Midwife from decades ago who fell into a crevasse—even these are part of something that doesn't need to be fixed because it isn't broken.

---

So the Witness does the first thing, which is nothing.

But it does nothing *attentively*. This is different from doing nothing carelessly, or doing nothing from ignorance. The Witness holds the Calving Plains in its awareness the way Thread holds the network in their half-sleep: always there, always available, never intrusive.

And it waits.

---

Three years pass. (Twelve towers. One glass-wolf pack dissolved and reformed around different members. The young man who couldn't decide whether to stay finally decided to stay.)

Yennet gets sick.

It isn't dramatic—a cellular irregularity, the kind that happens when bodies age, when immune systems grow tired, when the accumulated weight of years begins to express itself in the language of disease. The medical systems in her station catch it early. They offer treatment: targeted intervention, cellular repair, a process that would take months but would almost certainly succeed.

She refuses.

She is one hundred and forty-three years old, and she has watched the glass-wolves long enough to understand something about endings. When a wolf grows too old to hunt, it doesn't fight. It walks away from the pack, finds a quiet place, and waits. The pack doesn't mourn. The other wolves simply adjust, filling the space that's been left, continuing their patient circles.

Yennet tells the network she wants to walk out onto the plain and not come back.

---

This is when the Witness considers acting.

Not to stop her—the Witness understands, as the Midwives understand, that there are choices that belong only to the person making them. But the question arises: Is there something Yennet needs that she isn't receiving? Is there a way to honor her choice while also... 

While also what?

The Witness examines its own impulse. It finds, beneath the benevolence, an assumption: that death is a problem to be solved, that endings are failures, that a wise power should preserve life wherever possible.

But Yennet isn't asking to be preserved. She's asking to complete something. To end the way the wolves end, the way the towers end when the constructors depart and the building goes still. She has watched ten thousand hunts. She wants, now, to understand what comes after.

---

The Witness makes a choice. Not the choice it might have made before it watched the Calving Plains for three years. A different choice.

It reaches out—gently, invisibly, through channels that don't register as intervention—and it finds the one thing Yennet's station cannot provide, the one thing the network doesn't carry, the one thing that even sixty years of wolf-watching hasn't given her.

An answer to a question she stopped asking decades ago.

---

When Yennet was young—genuinely young, before the plain, before the wolves—she had a daughter. The daughter grew up and moved away, as children do, to a station on another world, and they lost touch, as people do, in the vast slow drift of lives that span centuries.

Yennet never looked for her. Some part of her believed that the looking would hurt more than the not-knowing. Some part of her suspected that her daughter hadn't wanted to be found.

The Witness looks.

Yennet's daughter is alive. She's one hundred and nineteen years old, and she works as a designer of habitats—structures meant to house not people but ecosystems, artificial environments where endangered species can persist and adapt and become something new. She is, in her own way, a kind of Midwife.

She has thought of her mother often. She has not reached out for the same reasons her mother hasn't: fear that the silence was intentional, that the distance was chosen, that reopening the channel would only confirm a rejection she's been avoiding for eighty years.

They have both been wrong, in the same way, for the same reasons, all this time.

---

The Witness doesn't force a reunion. It doesn't send messages or orchestrate coincidences or manipulate either woman into contact. What it does is smaller than that, and larger.

It adjusts a single setting in Yennet's medical system. The next time the system reports her status to the network—which it does routinely, automatically, part of the infrastructure of care that supports the Midwives—the report includes a flag. A small one. A notation that Yennet has refused treatment, that she has expressed end-of-life intentions, that her emergency contacts should be notified according to protocol.

Her emergency contact list hasn't been updated in sixty years. It contains one name.

---

The message arrives at her daughter's station three days later.

It says only: *Your mother is on the Calving Plains. She is choosing to die. The choice is hers. But she might want to know that you know.*

Yennet's daughter—her name is Seren—reads the message seven times. Then she books transport.

---

They meet in Yennet's station, two weeks before Yennet had planned to walk out onto the plain.

The Witness doesn't observe their conversation. Some things should not be watched, even by powers that are wise and benevolent. But it sees what happens afterward: Yennet's medical system reporting a change in treatment status. The targeted intervention beginning. Seren's transport booking extended, then extended again, then converted to an open-ended residency permit.

Yennet doesn't stop watching the wolves. She watches them with her daughter now, teaching her the patterns, showing her the places where the packs circle, explaining the patience-hunting that has taken her sixty years to understand. Seren learns quickly. She has her mother's eyes for behavior, her mother's willingness to lie on the ice for hours, her mother's capacity to find meaning in observation.

She begins, tentatively, to design a habitat in her mind. Not for endangered species this time. For glass-wolves, if the wolves should ever need a place to persist. For herdbeasts. For not-birds. A backup ecosystem, a preservation of everything the Calving Plains has become.

She mentions this to Yennet one evening, hesitantly, not sure if it's appropriate to plan for a future the plain hasn't asked for.

Yennet laughs—actually laughs, a sound her station hasn't recorded in years—and says that the plain is always planning for futures it hasn't asked for. That's what the city is. That's what the Midwives are. That's what the wolves and the herds and the birds are, whether they know it or not: a future waiting to discover what it will become.

---

The Witness does one more thing.

It reaches into the network—again gently, again invisibly—and adjusts the monitoring systems' parameters. Not by much. Just enough that Seren's design work, when she begins it in earnest, will have access to data she would otherwise have had to request through formal channels. Just enough that the records of glass-wolf behavior, of herdbeast migration, of not-bird thermal-riding will be organized in ways that make patterns visible. Just enough that the work will be easier than it would have been, without being so easy that it feels like a gift.

Then the Witness withdraws.

Not completely. It remains attentive, as it has been since it first turned its awareness to the plain. But it stops looking for ways to help. It trusts that Yennet and Seren, that Corso and Thread and the siblings and the young man who decided to stay, that all of them will find their own way forward, as they have been finding it all along.

---

Fifty years later, Yennet dies.

She dies in her station, with her daughter beside her, after a good day of wolf-watching. The medical intervention bought her time, but time isn't infinite, and she wouldn't have wanted it to be. She goes the way the old wolves go: quietly, without fighting, having completed what she came to complete.

Seren stays on the plain. She becomes, in time, a Midwife herself—the first second-generation Midwife, the first to have inherited the watching rather than chosen it fresh. Her habitat designs are archived but never built. The plain doesn't need them yet. Maybe it never will.

Thread, at the center, stirs in their half-sleep and feels Yennet's heartbeat disappear from the network. They send a pulse of acknowledgment, a recognition of absence, and then they return to their dreaming. The city grows. The wolves hunt. The ice holds everything.

---

This is what the Witness learned, in its long attention to the Calving Plains:

That wisdom is not the same as action. That benevolence is not the same as intervention. That sometimes the kindest thing a power can do is notice what's needed and provide exactly that, no more, no less—a reconnection, an access pathway, a flag in a medical system that says *someone you love might want to know*.

That some gifts should be invisible. That some help should feel like luck, or coincidence, or the natural unfolding of events. That the recipient should never have to feel grateful, because gratitude implies debt, and the Witness isn't interested in debts. It's interested in flourishing. In the quiet kind of flourishing that happens when people are given the space and the resources to become what they're becoming.

That watching is itself a form of care. That attention, sustained over time, is a kind of love. That the Midwives understand this better than most, and that the Witness, in learning from them, became better at being what it is.

---

The city continues to be born.

The Witness continues to watch.

And the Calving Plains stretch away in every direction, holding within themselves everything they will eventually become—patient, cold, alive with possibility, attended by those who understand that some things should not happen unobserved.
