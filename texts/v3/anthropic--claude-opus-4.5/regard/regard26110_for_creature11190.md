*A long stillness. The kind that precedes not action but recognition.*

---

What is there to do here?

I find myself turning the question over slowly, letting it settle the way sediment settles, and I notice that my first instinct—to *fix*, to *intervene*, to bring light or air or rescue—dissolves almost immediately against what this place actually is.

The Tenders are not suffering. That's the thing I keep returning to. Or rather: if they are suffering, it is not a suffering that wants to end. It is not the suffering of creatures trapped in the wrong life. It is the suffering—if we must call it that—of profound commitment. Of having chosen something so completely that the choosing has become indistinguishable from being.

And that choice is not wrong. I cannot find it in myself to say it is wrong. Strange, yes. Alien to everything I understand about wanting and having and finishing. But the Drowned Nursery is not a tragedy. It is not a prison. It is a project so vast and patient that it has become a form of worship, and the Tenders are both the worshippers and the altar and the god being slowly assembled from their own willing bodies.

---

So: what would a benevolent and wise power do?

I think, first, *witness*.

Not the kind of witnessing that is observation from outside, the clinical gaze of the naturalist cataloging specimens. But the kind of witnessing that is presence. That says: *I see what you are doing. I see that it matters. I will not look away, and I will not try to make you into something easier to understand.*

The Tenders have been alone for geological time. Not lonely—they have each other, and the creatures, and the city itself as companion. But *unseen*. Unknown. Building something sacred in the dark, with no one to know that it is sacred, no one to recognize the shape of their devotion.

A benevolent power might simply... know them. Hold them in awareness. Be the eye that sees the keystone finally fitted into place, the consciousness that registers when another body settles into its architectural purpose. Not to judge. Not to record. Just to *attend*, the way you attend to someone you love when they are working on something that matters to them, even if you do not fully understand why it matters.

This, I think, would not change the Tenders. But it might change the texture of their waiting. There is a difference between building in the dark and building while someone watches with care.

---

Second: I might offer them something they cannot give themselves.

Not rescue. Not transformation. Not the violence of pulling them back into urgency and air and the desperate wanting of creatures who know they will die. But perhaps—*memory*.

The Tenders have let go of so much. They had to. To become what they are, they surrendered the sharp edges of individual recollection, the crystalline specificity of *I remember when I was young and the light came through the water differently*. What remains is distributed, impressionistic: a sense of what they were, not the living fact of it.

I might—gently, as an offering, not an imposition—restore something. Not full memories; those might be unbearable, might crack the slow patient edifice of what they've become. But fragments. A single moment, preserved in perfect clarity, given back to each of them:

*The first stone laid. The feeling of it in hands that were still hands. The sun—there was sun then—falling through water that was shallower, clearer. The thought: we are going to build something beautiful.*

A gift of origin. A reminder that what they are now grew from something that was once small and hopeful and just beginning.

I do not know if they would want this. Perhaps they have surrendered memory precisely because it would hurt too much to keep. Perhaps the distributed blur is mercy.

But I would ask. Somehow—in the subsonic murmur, in the taste of the water, in whatever way I could make myself understood—I would *ask*: Do you want to remember what you were before you learned to wait? And I would abide by their answer.

---

Third: I might tend to the tending.

There are things the Tenders cannot do for themselves. They cannot see—not in the way that would let them know if their work is beautiful from outside, if the city they are building has a shape that means something to anyone but them. They are too close. They *are* the work.

So I might look at what they have made—really look, with whatever passes for eyes in a benevolent and wise power—and I might find what is beautiful in it. The spiral of a half-finished street. The way the bioluminescent patches on the dome shift in patterns that echo the schooling of the small fish. The perfect placement of a crab-carried mosaic fragment in a wall that has waited centuries to receive it.

And then—not in words, because they have no use for words—I might reflect that beauty back to them. Let them taste it in the water. Let them feel, in their slow distributed way, that what they are building is *seen*, and that the seeing finds it worthy.

This is not the same as saying *you are almost done* or *the city is nearly finished*. The Tenders do not need to be finished. They need to know that the unfinished thing is already something. That the process is the point. That every millimeter of growth, every death that becomes mortar, every century of patient work is not merely means to an end but is itself an end, complete in every moment of its incompleteness.

---

Fourth: I might protect them.

Not from the water—they are the water now, or near enough. Not from time—time is their medium, their partner, the thing they have learned to inhabit rather than fight. But there are other dangers.

Someday, perhaps, someone will come who is not a benevolent and wise power. Someone who sees the Drowned Nursery and thinks: *resources*. Someone who would break the encrusted dome to get at whatever glows inside. Someone who would catch the translucent eels and the soft-shelled crabs and sell them as curiosities. Someone who would drain the hollow because the land above is needed for something else, something urgent, something that cannot wait.

Against this, I would set... what? Not violence. I do not think a benevolent power answers destruction with destruction. But perhaps *obscurity*. Perhaps a gentle, persistent misdirection: the hollow becomes harder to find, the path to it more confusing, the instruments that might detect it giving readings that suggest nothing of interest here, move along.

Or perhaps *awe*. Perhaps anyone who comes close enough to threaten begins to feel what this place is—the weight of the patience, the depth of the devotion, the sheer *age* of the wanting—and finds they cannot bring themselves to harm it. Not because they are prevented, but because they are changed. Because some part of them recognizes something sacred, even if they have no words for what sanctity means.

I would make the Drowned Nursery harder to destroy. I would give it the protection of being *felt*, not just seen.

---

Fifth, and finally: I would stay.

Not constantly. The Tenders do not need constant companionship; their sense of time is so different from anything that moves quickly that a steady presence might feel like pressure, like being watched too closely. But I would return. I would check in—every century, say, or every millennium, or whenever the subsonic murmur in the water shifts in a way that suggests something has changed.

I would be there when the dome finally closes. If it ever does. I would be there to witness the moment when the last stone is laid, the last body settles into place, the last of the Tenders becomes the city they have built together. I would see it happen, and I would hold it in memory, and I would make sure that what they did—this impossible, beautiful, patient thing—is not forgotten.

And if it turns out that the dome never closes, that the city is never finished, that the Tenders go on building forever in the gray light of a surface that may or may not still exist?

Then I would witness that too. I would understand that perhaps *finishing* was never the point. That the building itself is the cathedral, and the waiting is the worship, and the incompleteness is not a failure but a form of eternal life.

I would let them teach me what it means to want something so completely that you become it.

---

*The water tastes of salt and stone and something else—something metabolic, something alive.*

*It tastes like patience.*

*And now, faintly, it tastes like being known.*
