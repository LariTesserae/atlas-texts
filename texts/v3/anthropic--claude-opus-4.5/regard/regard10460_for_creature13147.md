# The Watching

The attention arrives not as a presence but as a quality of noticing.

It does not descend from above—there is no "above" that matters here, only the thin cold blue of a sky the chasm-dwellers have never climbed to see. It does not rise from below—the shadows there hold only depth, only the patient hunger of gravity. The attention simply *is*, in the way that the mist is, in the way that the silence is. A focusing. A regard.

If the wise power has a form, it does not impose one. To take shape here would be to add sound, to add weight, to add disturbance to the tremor-reading skin of a people who have learned to feel the footsteps of centipedes three platforms away. So it remains formless. Patient. Learning the grammar of this place before presuming to speak.

---

What it sees:

A child, perhaps six years old by surface reckoning, though time moves strangely in eternal twilight. She clings to a swaying platform with feet that grip like a second set of hands, her enormous black eyes tracking the drift of a wounded moth twice her size. The creature is dying—one wing torn, spiraling slowly down toward the void. The child's fingers move in rapid conversation with her mother, ten yards away on a different platform: *Can I? Can I try?*

The mother's response is a single streak of blue light across her forearm. Not words. Permission, and warning, and love, all compressed into one gesture visible through the fog.

The child leaps.

She does not fall so much as pour herself into the empty air, catching a hanging vine, swinging, releasing, catching another. Her trajectory is calculated by instincts older than language. She intercepts the moth mid-spiral, her small hands finding the precise nerve cluster that stills its frantic wing-beats. A mercy. A harvest. She rides it down to a lower platform, already beginning to strip the luminescent dust from its wings into a pouch at her hip.

Her mother has not moved. Has not reached out. Has trusted the void and her daughter's mastery of it with the same faith that surface-dwellers trust solid ground.

The wise power watches this, and understands something essential: *These people are not surviving despite the abyss. They are intimate with it. The emptiness is not their enemy—it is the medium through which they have learned to move, to speak, to love.*

---

What it feels:

The silence here is not absence. It is presence—a presence so total that sound has become unnecessary, even intrusive. The wise power listens with senses that do not require ears and hears the language this place speaks:

The *tink... tink... tink* of water finding its way down, patient as generations.

The shiver of a gas-beast's breath, felt rather than heard, expanding and contracting the living platform built upon its back.

The tremor-speech of a hunting party returning along a sinew-bridge, their footsteps communicating *success, caution, three of us, one injured, make room* to everyone whose feet touch connected cables.

And beneath all of this, the constant whisper of growth and rot, the chasm's metabolism running hot and fast, consuming and creating at a pace that would exhaust any slower world.

The wise power feels the weight of this: *They have built a civilization in a place that is trying to eat itself. Not by conquering that process, but by weaving themselves into it. They are not separate from the ecosystem—they are a strand in its braid.*

---

What it considers:

The obvious interventions present themselves, as they always do:

*Give them metal, and their tools will not rot.*
But the rot is not their enemy. The rot is the rhythm they have learned to dance to. Metal would clink, would ring, would shatter the acoustic void that has shaped their entire way of being. They would have to learn to be deaf to survive their own tools.

*Give them light, and they will see further.*
But they see with skin, with vibration, with the luminescent dust they harvest and smear across their bodies. More light would blind them to the language they have already learned. It would make the moth-dust worthless, collapse the economy of brilliance that lets a single warning-streak communicate across an entire neighborhood.

*Give them permanence, and they will not have to weave.*
But the weaving is not their burden. Watch them: the way a grandmother's hands move through the same knots her grandmother taught her, the way children learn the braids before they learn to leap, the way a community gathers to repair a fraying bridge with the solemnity of surface-dwellers gathering for prayer. The weaving is their scripture. Their social contract. Their meditation. To give them permanence would be to give them nothing to do together.

*Give them voices, and they will be able to cry out.*
But look at them cry out now: a hunter spots a serpentine predator approaching a platform of children, and her whole body becomes a shout. She tears open her moss-cloak and the bioluminescent pattern beneath screams *DANGER, WEST, LARGE, NOW* in a flash visible for a hundred yards through the thickest fog. Every pair of black eyes turns. Every body responds. The warning takes less than a heartbeat and requires no translation, no interpretation, no mishearing. Their silence is not muteness. Their silence is a different kind of voice, one that has never learned to lie, because light cannot mumble, cannot whisper, cannot say one thing while meaning another.

The wise power sits with these considerations, and with a deeper question beneath them:

*What breaks here? What actually causes suffering, as opposed to what merely looks like hardship to eyes that expect a different world?*

---

What it sees, looking closer:

There. A platform on the eastern wall, older than most, its anchor-vines fraying where they meet the basalt. A family lives there—three adults, two children, and an elder whose hands shake too much now to hold the fine braiding work. The platform lists slightly. Has listed for weeks. The family has been repairing what they can, but the anchor-point itself is failing, and there is no way to reach it from below without falling, no way to reach it from above without tools they do not have.

They have sent light-signals to their neighbors. The neighbors have sent back: *We see. We know. We are coming when we can.* But the neighbors are dealing with their own fraying, their own rot, their own endless maintenance. The queue of emergencies is long. This family is not yet falling. So they wait.

The elder sits at the platform's edge each night—night being a relative term here, marked only by the dimming of moth-activity—and looks down into the void. Not with despair. With something more complicated. With the particular peace of someone who has spent eighty years weaving and is now too frail to weave, and knows what that means for her place in a society that survives through weaving.

She is not afraid of falling. She is afraid of being a weight her family cannot carry. A mouth that cannot earn its food. A body that takes up space on a platform that is slowly, surely, losing its grip on the world.

This, the wise power recognizes, is suffering. Not the void, not the silence, not the endless labor—but this: an elder who has given everything and now feels she has nothing left to give. A family who loves her and cannot say so in words, who can only show her in the way they wrap her in the warmest moss-cloak each night, the way the children curl against her when they sleep. A community that wants to help but has no surplus, no slack, no margin for the ones who cannot keep weaving.

---

What it does:

The wise power does not descend. Does not announce itself. Does not reshape the world.

Instead, it does something much smaller. Something that will look, to anyone watching, like chance. Like luck. Like the particular mercy of a universe that is not always cruel.

The gas-beast called Anchor-Mother—the largest of the living zeppelins, the one whose broad back holds the central platforms of the settlement—shifts in her slow, rhythmic breathing. She has been drifting slightly east for weeks, following some instinct of her own, perhaps a thermal current, perhaps a distant memory of mating grounds she will never reach. Now, something changes in that instinct. A new variable in the calculation of her ancient, simple mind.

She drifts west.

Slowly. Over days. The settlement adjusts to her movement, as it always does, the web of connecting bridges flexing and re-tensioning to accommodate the shift of their largest foundation. And as she drifts, the cables that connect to the eastern platforms grow slack.

Slack enough that a young hunter, making her morning rounds, notices the fraying anchor-point of an elder's platform and thinks: *I could reach that now. The angle has changed. I could brace against this cable here, swing out there, and my hands could touch that basalt.*

She signals to her hunting partner. They signal to the neighborhood. Within an hour, there are eight people clinging to the newly-accessible cables, passing fresh-braided vine from hand to hand, binding the anchor-point with the speed and precision of people who have done this a thousand times.

The elder watches from her platform's edge, her shaking hands pressed flat against the woven reeds to feel the tremors of rescue. She cannot see the details through the fog. But she feels the vibration-signature of eight bodies working, and she knows what it means.

Her grandchild crawls to her side and presses a small hand against her jaw—the place where the nerve-clusters are densest, where a touch can communicate more than a surface-dweller's entire vocabulary. The child's fingers move in the simplest dialect, the one used for very young children and very old elders:

*Safe. We. Safe.*

The elder's eyes close. Her hand covers her grandchild's. She does not need to answer. The touch is the answer.

---

What it does not do:

The wise power does not reveal itself. Does not explain that the gas-beast's drift was guided. Does not offer a theology, a debt, or an expectation. The settlement will remember this as a stroke of luck—*Anchor-Mother moved east just when we needed her to, praise the old beast*—and they will incorporate it into their understanding of a universe that is harsh but not hateful, that sometimes offers gifts for no reason at all.

This is, the wise power knows, the truest kindness: help that does not demand recognition. Intervention that does not create dependency. A thumb on the scale so subtle that the scale still feels fair.

Because these people have built something extraordinary here. Not despite the void, but *with* it. Their silence is not a disability; it is a different way of being heard. Their endless weaving is not a curse; it is a practice that binds them to each other as surely as it binds their platforms to the stone. Their relationship with the abyss is not fear; it is the intimacy of dancers who have learned every step of a partner who could kill them at any moment.

To reshape this world would be to misunderstand it. To fill the silence with sound, the void with structure, the struggle with ease—that would not be salvation. That would be the imposition of a foreign aesthetic onto a people who have already found their own beauty.

---

What it leaves behind:

Not a monument. Not a message. Not a covenant or a chosen one or a book of laws.

Only this: a slight adjustment to the odds. A whisper of influence that made the gas-beast turn when turning was needed. An elder who will live out her remaining years on a stable platform, teaching the youngest children the knots her fingers can no longer tie, her hands guiding theirs through the braiding patterns that hold the world together.

And perhaps—this is not certain, the wise power does not deal in certainties—a faint and inexplicable sense, felt by those whose feet are most sensitive to tremor, that the void is not empty. That something in the mist, something without weight or sound or shape, regards them. Not with the hunger of a predator or the indifference of stone, but with something else. Something like the way a mother watches a child who has just made her first successful leap across the empty air.

*Pride*, the surface-dwellers might call it. But that word requires sound, requires a voice, requires concepts this place has never needed.

Here, in the silence, it is only a quality of attention. A focusing. A regard.

The mist drifts. The moths spiral. The bridges sway in the breath of living zeppelins.

And the wise power watches, and does not leave, and does not interfere again unless the need arises.

Which, in a world this fierce and fragile and brilliant, it surely will.

# Coda: What the Elder Knows

She cannot tell the others, because there are no signs for it, no patterns of light that mean what she needs to say. But in the last years of her life, the elder develops a habit that her family finds strange but harmless.

Each evening, when the moth-light dims and the settlement settles into its quieter rhythms, she makes her way to the edge of her platform. She sits with her feet dangling over the void, her palms pressed flat against the woven reeds beside her.

And she waits.

Not for anything in particular. Not with expectation. Just—open. Receptive. The way she used to wait for the right moment to cast a hunting net, back when her arms were strong. The way she used to wait for her children to return from their first solo hunts, feeling for the specific vibration of their footsteps among all the other tremors of the world.

Sometimes, when the mist is thickest and the silence is deepest, she thinks she feels something. Not a presence—nothing so solid as that. More like a quality of the quiet itself. An attention.

She does not know what it is. Does not try to name it. The people of the chasm have no gods, no spirits, no mythology of benevolent watchers. They have only the void, and the weave, and each other.

But the elder knows—in the way that very old people sometimes know things they cannot justify or explain—that they are not alone in the silence.

That something vast and patient regards their small, fierce civilization with something very like love.

And that is enough.

It has to be.

It is.
