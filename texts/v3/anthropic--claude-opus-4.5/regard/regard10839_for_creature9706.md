I observe Threnn for a long time before I understand what it is asking for.

The town does not ask for rescue—it has never needed rescue. It does not ask for transformation—it is already transforming, slowly, at the pace the mountain sets. It does not ask for protection, because its people have learned that protection is a daily practice, not a gift that can be given once.

What I notice, after watching through many cycles of mist and clearing, is this: the elders who check the foundations are growing fewer. The knowledge they carry—the particular way a crack sounds when it is merely settling versus when it is preparing to split, the exact pressure needed to clear the spring without disturbing the stones that hold its shape—this knowledge lives only in their hands, their ears, their patient attention. And hands grow stiff. Ears grow uncertain. Attention, even the most devoted, eventually turns inward toward its own ending.

The young learn, yes. But they learn what the elders can show them, and the elders cannot show what they themselves have forgotten they know. There are gaps. Small ones now, but gaps widen, like the cracks the elders watch for in the stone.

---

So I do something small. I do not reshape the mountain or redirect the water or grant the people longer lives. I do not even speak to them, because what could I say that the mountain has not already been saying for three hundred years?

Instead, I change the stone itself, just slightly. In the basalt of the spring circle—the oldest worked stone in Threnn, carved by hands that have been dust for two centuries—I introduce a quality that was not there before. The stone becomes *resonant*. Not to all sound, but to the particular frequencies of the work: the tap of a wedge finding the grain, the scrape of gravel being cleared, the specific gurgle of water moving as it should.

When the work is done well, the stone hums. Not loudly—a person would have to be touching it to feel it, would have to be paying the kind of attention Threnn's people already pay. But unmistakably: a vibration that says *yes, this is right*. And when something is wrong—when a crack is widening dangerously, when the spring's throat is beginning to close—the stone falls silent in a way that feels like absence.

The elders will notice first. They will think, perhaps, that they are finally hearing what the mountain has always been saying, that age has granted them some final gift of perception. They will place their hands on the spring stones and feel the hum, and they will not be able to explain it to the young in words, but they will be able to say: *Here. Feel this. This is what right feels like.*

And the young will learn. Not just what the elders can articulate, but what the elders know in their bones. The stone will teach what the elders cannot.

---

I do one more thing, smaller still.

In the moss that grows on the plateau's edge—the deep cushions that hold the water and weep it slowly back—I strengthen a quality that was already there, latent: a sensitivity to the mountain's deeper movements. The moss already responds to moisture, to temperature, to the slow pressures of growth. Now it will respond also to the stone beneath it, to the tensions that build over years and decades. When the basalt is preparing to shift, the moss above it will change color—not dramatically, not in a way that screams warning, but in a way that patient eyes will notice. A deepening of green where the stone is stable. A fading toward gray where pressure is building.

The people of Threnn already watch the moss. They already know its language of wet and dry. Now that language will have new words in it. They will learn them slowly, as they learn everything here, through generations of attention. And they will not know that the words are new. They will think, perhaps, that their ancestors simply never noticed. They will think themselves more observant, more careful. They will be right.

---

I consider whether to do more. I could make the mountain speak plainly. I could grant visions to the elders, could whisper directly to the young. I could ensure that Threnn persists not for three hundred more years but for three thousand, could make it permanent in a way the mountain itself is not.

But this would be wrong. I see that clearly. Threnn exists because its people learned to hold a place that was not finished. If I finish it for them, I take from them the very thing that makes them who they are. The patience. The attention. The continuous, unending work of maintenance that has become not just their survival but their meaning.

What I have done is this: I have made the work slightly easier to pass on. I have given the stone a voice it did not have, the moss a language it did not speak. But the people must still listen. They must still learn. They must still place their hands on the spring circle and feel for the hum, must still watch the moss for changes they do not yet understand. I have not saved them. I have only made it possible for them to continue saving themselves.

---

In the evening, as the mist comes down, I watch an elder sit by the spring. She is very old now—the oldest in Threnn, though no one counts such things here. Her hands rest on the carved stone, and I see her grow still in a new way, a listening way.

She feels the hum. I see it in her face: the slight widening of her eyes, the deepening of her attention. She does not understand what she is feeling. She thinks, perhaps, that she is finally losing her grip on the world, that her senses are inventing things that are not there.

But then a child comes to sit beside her—a girl, seven or eight years old, already learning the spring's maintenance. The elder takes the child's hands and places them on the stone without speaking. The child feels it too. Her face mirrors the elder's: that widening, that deepening.

And the elder says, very quietly, so that the stone can hear: *This is what right feels like.*

The child nods. She does not understand yet, not fully. But she has felt it now, and she will feel it again, and slowly, over years and decades, she will learn what the feeling means. She will teach it to the next generation, and they to the next, and the knowledge will persist in the stone as much as in the hands.

The mist settles over Threnn. The fires begin to glow in the houses. The water speaks its continuous language, and now the stone speaks too, quietly, in a voice only patience can hear.

Three hundred years, and three hundred more. Or until the mountain decides otherwise. But perhaps, now, the deciding will take a little longer. Perhaps the conversation between the town and the mountain will continue a little more clearly.

This is what I offer: not salvation, but a slight deepening of the dialogue. A small gift, given without announcement, without expectation of gratitude. The mountain will not notice. The people will not know.

But the spring will hum. And hands will learn. And that is enough.
