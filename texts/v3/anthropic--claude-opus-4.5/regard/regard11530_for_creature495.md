# The Attending

The power that turns its attention to the Drowning Forge does not arrive—it has always been present in some sense, in the way that certain qualities are present wherever anything exists at all. But attention is different from presence. Attention is a choosing.

What draws it now is not the suffering, exactly. Suffering is common, and this power has learned (over lengths of time that make the word "time" feel inadequate) that attending to suffering alone leads to a kind of paralysis—there is always more, always somewhere, and the mere fact of pain does not tell you what, if anything, should be done about it.

What draws it is the *tension*. Three people holding a space open against the sea. A fire burning where fire should not be possible. The youngest one's optimism and the eldest one's silence and the middle child's humming that harmonizes with the drone—these are not merely survival. They are *creation*, ongoing, in conditions that should permit only ending.

The power attends. This is what it sees:

---

## I. The Nature of the Problem

The forge will fail. This is not pessimism but mathematics—copper in salt water, pressure differentials, the constant small stresses that accumulate. The middle child's notebook documents this with terrible precision: seventeen points of concern that were twelve points six months ago, that were eight points when the water first closed over the dome. The curve is not linear. It accelerates.

But the failure is not imminent. They have time—perhaps two years, perhaps three if the youngest's improvements to the sealing compound work as hoped. The question is not "will they die" but "what are they becoming, and is that becoming being honored."

The power sees: the eldest has found a kind of peace. Her silence is not deprivation but arrival. She has become the forge's priestess, and her service has meaning to her that speech could only diminish. She does not need rescue from this. She needs *witness*—someone to know that her devotion is real and valid, that she has not simply broken but transformed.

The power sees: the middle child is afraid, constantly, and has converted that fear into attention so thorough it has become love. Every rivet they check is a prayer. Every weakness they document is an act of faith that documentation matters, that care matters, that the sea can be held back by sufficient devotion. They are not wrong. They are also not sustainable.

The power sees: the youngest believes in the future with a ferocity that is itself a kind of wound. They cannot grieve what was lost because grief would mean accepting the loss. So they plan, they innovate, they insist that this is temporary—and in doing so, they prevent themselves from fully inhabiting the life they actually have. They are always living tomorrow, never today.

The power sees: none of them have spoken of what happens when the forge finally fails. They have sealed away that conversation as thoroughly as they sealed away the valley. To speak it would be to make it real.

---

## II. What the Power Does Not Do

It does not drain the sea. The water's rising was not malevolent—it was geological, tectonic, the slow reshaping of a world that does not owe stability to anyone. To reverse it would be to declare that one small valley matters more than the vast processes that have been unfolding for millions of years. This is not a judgment the power is willing to make.

It does not strengthen the dome. To make their structure permanent would be to trap them in a choice made under duress, to crystallize a survival measure into an eternal prison. They chose to descend because they could not bear to abandon the fire. But that does not mean they must tend it forever.

It does not grant them gills or transform them into sea-creatures. They are already transforming—this is visible in their barrel chests, their salt-adapted eyes, their growing discomfort with surface air. To accelerate this would be to choose for them what they should become. The power has learned, painfully, that transformation imposed is not transformation at all. It is merely a different kind of death.

It does not send a ship to rescue them. They do not want rescue. They want their fire, their work, their great-grandmother's flame still burning. To remove them from the forge would be to remove them from themselves.

It does not speak to them in dreams or visions. It has tried this before, with others, and learned that messages from beyond tend to overwhelm rather than illuminate. A voice from outside the copper dome would become another pressure they must withstand—the pressure of being *chosen*, of being *special*, of having to live up to divine attention. They have enough pressures.

---

## III. What the Power Does

### The First Gift: A Margin of Error

The middle child wakes one morning—if the filtered dimness that passes for morning can be called that—and finds that the seventeenth point of concern has stabilized. Not improved, exactly, but stopped accelerating. They check it three times, four, five. They add a note to the margin of their notebook: *unexplained*.

They do not know that the power has done nothing more than *attend* to that joint with particular care—and that attention, when it comes from such a source, has a quality of reinforcement. Not magic, exactly. More like... the joint is now being watched by something that wants it to hold. And wanting, at that level, is not passive.

This is not a permanent solution. It is a gift of time—perhaps six months, perhaps a year. Enough for what comes next.

### The Second Gift: A Question

The eldest is tending the bellows in her careful rhythm when she becomes aware that she is not alone. Not in the physical sense—the others are asleep, and the space contains only herself and the fire. But in some other sense, there is a presence.

It does not speak. It does not demand. It simply... *asks*. Not in words, but in the way that a certain quality of silence can be a question.

The question is: *What do you want the fire to become?*

She has never considered this. The fire is what it has always been—her great-grandmother's flame, the heart of the forge, the thing they descended to protect. But the question sits in her, and she finds herself thinking about it as she works the bellows, as she sleeps, as she wakes.

What *does* she want the fire to become?

After three days, she does something she has not done in over a year. She speaks. Her voice is rough, unpracticed, strange to her own ears.

"I want it to live," she says. "Even if we don't."

She does not know if anything hears. But she feels, somehow, that the question has been answered.

### The Third Gift: A Memory

The youngest is working on the distillation system—always working, always improving—when they are struck by a memory so vivid it stops their hands mid-motion.

They are seven years old, standing in the forge (the forge as it was, open to sky, surrounded by mountains). Their great-grandmother is showing them how to bank a fire for the night—not extinguishing it, but reducing it to coals that will still be alive in the morning.

"A fire can sleep," the old woman says. "It doesn't have to burn bright all the time. Sometimes the kindest thing is to let it rest."

The youngest has not thought of this moment in years. They stand in the underwater forge, hands still, and something in their chest loosens. They do not understand why they are crying, only that the tears feel necessary, feel *right*.

They do not return to the distillation system that day. Instead, they sit by the forge and watch the flames, really watch them, for the first time in months. They notice how the fire has its own rhythms, its own preferences, how it leans toward the bellows-wind like a flower toward sun.

"I'm sorry," they whisper to it. "I've been trying so hard to keep you alive that I forgot to let you live."

### The Fourth Gift: A Conversation

The middle child has noticed the changes in their siblings—the eldest's single sentence, the youngest's new stillness. They have documented these changes in their notebook, alongside the measurements of pressure and corrosion. But they have not asked.

Now, one evening (if the dimming of filtered light can be called evening), the three of them are all awake at once. This is rare. Usually their rotations keep at least one asleep while two work.

The youngest speaks first: "We need to talk about what happens when the dome fails."

The eldest nods. The middle child's hands go still—they were checking a seal, as always, but now they turn and sit down on the stone floor.

"I've done the calculations," the middle child says. "Two years, maybe three. If we're lucky and careful."

"What if we're not trying to survive?" the youngest asks. "What if we're trying to... pass something on?"

The eldest makes a gesture—one of the private signs they've developed, this one meaning *say more*.

"The fire." The youngest's voice is steady now, steadier than it's been in months. "Great-grandmother's fire. It's been burning for four generations. What if... what if instead of trying to keep it burning forever, we try to make sure it can burn somewhere else? After?"

The middle child's hands twitch toward their notebook. "The fishing boats. We could—we already send them barrels. What if we sent them coals? Instructions? The knowledge we have?"

"We'd need to document everything," the eldest says. Her voice is still rough, but it's there. "Not just the metalwork. The fire itself. How to feed it, how to read it, how to—" She pauses, swallows. "How to love it."

They talk through the night. They do not notice that the air seems easier to breathe, that the condensation seems lighter, that the breathing-pipes seem to hum in a lower, more soothing register. These are small shifts, barely perceptible, the kind of changes that might be coincidence.

They are not coincidence.

---

## IV. The Months That Follow

The power watches as they work with new purpose. The notebooks multiply—not just the middle child's now, but a shared project. They document everything: the metallurgy of bronze in underwater conditions, the mechanics of the bellows system, the particular wood treatments that resist salt-rot. But also the stories. The songs their great-grandmother sang while working. The hand-signals they've developed. The way the fire behaves when it's tired, when it's hungry, when it's content.

The barrels they send up to the fishing boats begin to contain more than tools. There are sealed packages of coals, carefully banked, still alive. There are instructions written on treated hide. There are small bronze figurines—new work, art they haven't made in years—depicting the forge as it was, the forge as it is, the fire at the center of everything.

The fishermen are confused at first. Then curious. Then interested. Word spreads through the scattered mountain-people: the smiths of the drowned valley are teaching anyone who will learn.

A young woman rows out in a small boat, anchors above the breathing-pipes, and shouts down: "My grandmother knew your grandmother! She says your fire was lit from her mother's fire! Is that true?"

They send up a barrel with a letter, with coals, with a small bronze bell that rings with a tone unique to their forge. *Yes*, the letter says. *The fire travels. It always has. We thought we were protecting it by keeping it here, but fire wants to spread. Will you carry it?*

The young woman takes the coals home. She builds a small forge in her new village, high on a mountainside the water will never reach. The bell hangs above it. When she works, she sings the songs the letter taught her.

The fire burns.

---

## V. The End, and What Is Not the End

The dome fails on a morning in what they think is their third spring below the water. The middle child is asleep; the eldest is at the bellows; the youngest is sealing a barrel—the last barrel, containing their final notebooks, their last coals, a bronze casting of three figures standing around a fire.

The sound is almost gentle. A groan, a sigh, a series of small pops as rivets give way along the seventeenth point of concern. The eldest looks up, meets the youngest's eyes, and makes a gesture they both understand: *it's time*.

They do not try to escape. There is nowhere to escape to—the surface is thirty meters up, and they have become creatures of the deep; the thin air above would kill them as surely as the water.

Instead, they wake the middle child. They gather around the forge, as they have gathered so many times. The water is ankle-deep now, rising fast.

The eldest places her hand on the bellows one last time. The fire still burns, though it won't for long—the water is climbing, the air is going, and some endings cannot be prevented.

But fire does not need to burn in one place to be alive.

Across the scattered villages of the mountain-people, a dozen small forges are burning with flame descended from their flame. A hundred people know the songs, the techniques, the particular way of reading a fire's mood. The bronze bells ring in a dozen workshops, and everyone who hears them knows the story: there were three who went down with the water, who kept the old fire burning until they could give it away.

The youngest seals the last barrel, pushes it toward the airlock. "Go," they say to it. "Go and be fire somewhere else."

The water reaches the forge. The flames hiss, sputter, fight for a moment—and then, with something that might be grace, they go out.

But not entirely. Not everywhere. Not in the ways that matter.

---

## VI. What the Power Knows

The power does not grieve, exactly. It is not built for grief. But it holds the memory of the three—their barrel chests, their salt-adapted eyes, their hands that knew every rivet by touch—and it does not let that memory fade.

It attends, now, to the scattered forges. It watches the young woman on the mountainside teach her daughter to read a fire's mood. It watches the fishing boats carry coals from village to village, spreading the flame like a message. It watches new innovations emerge—a bellows design here, an alloy technique there—each one building on what the three preserved.

The fire they tended was not a fire. It was a way of knowing, a way of being in relationship with flame and metal and heat. It was a practice that could not survive if hoarded, that needed to spread or die.

The power understands: some things must be protected by being given away. Some things survive only by becoming something new. The three understood this, in the end—not because the power taught them, but because the power's questions helped them find what they already knew.

This is the only kind of help the power trusts itself to give: not answers, but attention. Not solutions, but presence. Not rescue, but the kind of witness that helps people become who they are trying to be.

The Drowning Forge is gone now, crushed and scattered by the sea. But what it held—what the three held—is alive in a dozen places, a hundred, and spreading.

This is what the power wanted. This is what benevolence means, when you are wise enough to know that you cannot save anyone: you can only help them save what matters, in whatever way they choose.

The fire burns. The fire burns. The fire burns.
