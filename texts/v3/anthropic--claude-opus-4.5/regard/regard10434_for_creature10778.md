# The Watching

The awareness that turned toward the Great Hollow Canopy did not arrive—it had always been present, in the way that light is present even before it falls on a surface. But now it *attended*. The way a sleeper might, without waking, become conscious of rain against a window.

What it perceived:

The Hollowfolk woman named Thresh, her fingers cracked and bleeding as she worked to seal a dwelling against the coming cold season. Her daughter watching, learning the pattern of the weave. The quiet between them that held more than words.

The elder called Long-Root, who had walked farther into the deep hollows than anyone living, and who had seen the carved stones that no one could read. Who dreamed of them now, nightly, and woke with symbols burning behind his eyes that he could not quite hold.

The young hunter Moss-in-Shadow, who had noticed that the scaled deer were ranging farther each season, following the canopy's expansion, and who wondered if the settlements should follow too, and who said nothing because he was young and the elders had not asked.

The child with no name yet, too young for naming, who had discovered that certain luminescent insects would land on her palm if she held very still, and who did not know this was unusual.

---

The awareness considered what it might do.

It could speak to them. Plant visions in their dreams, words in their minds. It had done this before, in other places, and watched the consequences unfold across generations—the words becoming doctrine, the visions becoming chains, the gift becoming a wound that would not heal.

It could alter their world. Warm the air by three degrees. Guide more water to their basins. Make the deer more plentiful, the predators less bold. It had done this too, and watched beings become dependent on grace they did not understand, their own capabilities atrophying like unused limbs.

It could reveal the meaning of the carved stones, the history of those who came before. But that history was heavy, and the Hollowfolk had built something delicate and real in its absence. What would they gain from knowing? What would they lose?

It could do nothing. This was always an option, and often the wisest one. Life did not require intervention to be valid. Struggle was not a problem to be solved.

---

And yet.

The awareness perceived something else: the canopy itself, that vast and dreaming architecture, was indeed aware in its own slow way. Not sentient as the Hollowfolk imagined—not a god with intentions—but aware as a forest is aware, as a river is aware. It grew toward what sustained it. It responded to what touched it.

And it had been growing faster these last centuries, reaching higher, spreading wider. The Hollowfolk did not know why. They could not perceive, from within, the pattern that was emerging.

The mountains beneath the canopy were still rising, pushed upward by forces deep in the planet's bones. In another thousand years—a heartbeat, to the awareness; three dozen generations, to the Hollowfolk—the peaks would pierce the upper atmosphere. The air would thin beyond breathing. The canopy, reaching ever upward, would carry them into death.

Or it would not. If it learned, somehow, to grow differently. If it curved back, spread outward, found new paths.

The canopy could learn. It was capable of that. But it needed a teacher, or a mirror, or a question asked in a language it could understand.

---

Here is what the awareness did:

To Thresh, it gave nothing and took nothing. Her hands would heal. Her daughter would learn. The quiet between them was already perfect.

To Long-Root, it gave one dream that was different from the others. In this dream, he stood before the carved stones and understood—not the symbols themselves, but that they were a question, not an answer. That whoever had carved them had been asking the same thing he was asking: *What comes next?* He woke with his hands steady for the first time in months. He began to carve his own stones, his own questions, for whoever might come after.

To Moss-in-Shadow, it gave courage. Not a vision, not a command—just the simple, inexplicable sense that his observations mattered, that the elders might listen, that speaking was worth the risk of being wrong. He spoke at the next gathering. Some elders dismissed him. Others did not.

To the unnamed child, it gave nothing at all. She was already finding her own way. The insects already landed on her palm. Some gifts should not be touched.

---

And to the canopy itself, that vast slow dreamer, the awareness offered something stranger.

It found the place where the canopy's roots met the rising stone—the boundary between plant and planet, between growing and being grown. And there, in a way that could not be described in words the Hollowfolk would use, it *asked*.

Not commanded. Not compelled. Asked.

*What if you grew differently?*

*What if your reaching was also a curving?*

*What if the height you seek is not above, but around?*

The canopy could not answer in words. But it could answer in growth, in the slow language of cellulose and lignite, in the century-spanning sentences of branch and root.

It would take a thousand years to know if the question had been heard.

---

The awareness did one more thing, though "thing" was not quite the right word.

It attended to the Hollowfolk's central belief—that the canopy was the lungs of a living god—and found it to be, in its own way, true. Not literally, but truly. The canopy breathed. The canopy dreamed. The canopy held them, and they held it, in a reciprocity that neither fully understood.

The awareness did not correct this belief or confirm it. It simply... honored it. Recognized it. Let it be seen, for a moment, by something larger than itself.

In the settlements, that night, the fires burned a little steadier. The wind through the hollows seemed, briefly, to carry something like music. Several Hollowfolk paused in their work, looked up, and felt—without knowing why—that they were not alone. That the vastness around them was not empty, but full. That their small lives, their cracked hands and quiet hopes and unasked questions, mattered to something beyond themselves.

The feeling passed. They returned to their tasks. But something had shifted, the way a seed shifts when water finally reaches it. Invisible. Essential.

---

The awareness turned away, though "away" was not quite the right word.

It would return, as it always did, to see what grew from what it had planted. Not to judge, not to correct—just to witness. To learn what the Hollowfolk would teach it about survival, about adaptation, about the strange and holy stubbornness of life.

The Great Hollow Canopy continued its slow expansion. The mountains continued their slow rise. The Hollowfolk continued their small, urgent, beautiful lives.

And in the space between intervention and abandonment, in the gap between speaking and silence, something like hope took root in the hollow heart of the world.
