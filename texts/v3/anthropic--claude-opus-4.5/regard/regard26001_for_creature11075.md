# The Attention of the Distant

The being who noticed the Quickening Yards did not notice it quickly.

This is the nature of wisdom on certain scales: patience that looks like absence, attention that moves like geology. The being—call it what you will, though it would not call itself anything—had been aware of the thermal basin for longer than the basin had existed, had felt the deep pressures building, had known something would emerge. It did not intervene in emergence. Emergence was not the kind of thing one intervened in. Emergence was the universe doing what universes do, and the being had learned, over spans that made the basin's three centuries look like a caught breath, that interference with emergence produced suffering more often than it prevented it.

But it watched. Watching was different from interfering. Watching was a form of respect.

---

What the being saw, when it turned its attention fully toward the Yards, was not what the residents saw.

The residents saw structures. The being saw *process*—saw the thermal energy rising through fractured stone, saw the mineral-rich water carrying dissolved compounds upward, saw the crystallization patterns that emerged when chemistry met temperature met pressure met time. It saw the logic of the growth, which was not a logic of intention but a logic of *consequence*: this mineral precipitates at this temperature in this concentration, and the shape it takes is the shape that dissipates heat most efficiently, and that shape happens to resemble a wall with window-gaps because window-gaps allow convection, and convection serves the process, and the process continues.

The being saw that the Yards were not alive. Not in any sense that would satisfy a biologist, not with metabolism or reproduction or response to stimuli in the ways that living things respond. The Yards were a phenomenon, like a hot spring or a crystal cave or the columnar basalt that forms when lava cools at particular rates. The Yards were what happened when a very specific set of geological conditions obtained, conditions that had obtained nowhere else on this world, conditions that produced structures resembling architecture because architecture and geology occasionally solve the same problems.

The being also saw that this explanation, while true, was not complete.

---

The factories troubled it.

Not troubled in the sense of distress—the being did not experience distress in ways that would translate—but troubled in the sense of *incompleteness*, a gap in the pattern that suggested the pattern was larger than the being had initially perceived.

Crystallization did not produce factories. Mineral precipitation did not produce copper wire in eighteen gauges. The Yards were doing something that geology alone could not account for, and the being, turning its attention deeper, looking at scales smaller than the residents could perceive, began to see what that something was.

The deep thermal vents were not merely hot. They were *structured* hot, their temperatures varying in patterns that repeated, that carried information, that had been carrying information for longer than the basin had been exposed to the surface. Something in the planet's mantle was computing. Had been computing for eons. Was using the thermal gradients and mineral flows the way other processes used electricity or chemistry, performing calculations whose purposes were unclear but whose outputs were not: the outputs were the Yards.

The Yards were a *product*. Not of intention, exactly—the mantle-process was no more intentional than weather—but of optimization. The mantle-process was solving a problem. The solution involved structures on the surface. The structures had become complex enough to incorporate organisms, because organisms were useful for certain aspects of the solution, and so the structures accommodated organisms the way a flowering plant accommodates pollinators: not out of kindness, but out of consequence.

The being understood this and did not judge it. There was nothing to judge. The Yards were not cruel. The Yards were not kind. The Yards were a phenomenon doing what phenomena do, and the eighty thousand humans living within them were part of the phenomenon now, participants in a calculation they could not perceive, variables in an equation that would continue solving itself whether they stayed or left.

---

This was where the being's wisdom became relevant, and this was where the being had to make a choice.

It could do nothing. This was always an option, and often the wisest one. The Yards were not producing unusual suffering—the residents' lives were hard, but lives were hard in many places, and the Yards at least provided shelter and warmth and a strange kind of stability. The sealed ones were troubling, but there were few of them, and some seemed content. The children were changing, but change was not inherently harm. The being could watch, and wait, and let the phenomenon continue, and perhaps in another few centuries the calculation would complete and the Yards would stabilize into something their residents could fully understand.

But the being saw something else, too. It saw the gap between what the residents understood and what was actually happening, and it saw how that gap produced fear, and faction, and the kind of suffering that came not from material conditions but from *uncertainty*. The Stewards were not wrong that the Yards provided. The Integrators were not wrong that understanding was possible. The Evacuationists were not wrong that danger was real. They were all partially right, and their partial rightness was tearing them apart, because none of them could see the whole pattern, and without seeing the whole pattern they could not find the peace that came from genuine comprehension.

The being could offer that comprehension. Not completely—some aspects of the mantle-process were beyond even its perception—but enough. Enough to let the residents understand what they were part of. Enough to let them make informed choices about staying or leaving, about adapting or resisting, about what they wanted their children to become.

This was not a small gift. Knowledge of this kind changed people. It would change the Yards, too, because the Yards incorporated their residents, and residents who understood themselves differently would be incorporated differently. The being could not predict what would emerge from that change. It could only know that the change would be real, and permanent, and consequential in ways that would ripple forward for generations.

---

The being chose to act, and the action it chose was characteristically subtle.

It did not manifest. It did not speak in dreams or visions. It did not alter the physical conditions of the Yards in any way that instruments could detect. What it did was simpler, and stranger, and more profound: it *attended*, fully, in a way that made its attention perceptible to those who were capable of perceiving it.

This is difficult to describe in terms that make sense to beings who have not experienced it. The closest analogy might be the feeling of being watched—but not watched with threat, watched with *interest*, the way a parent watches a child learning to walk, the way a teacher watches a student approaching understanding. The being's attention settled over the Yards like a change in air pressure, like the moment before rain, like the feeling of standing in a place where something important once happened.

Those who could feel it felt it differently, according to their natures.

---

**Hesha**, in the wet sectors, felt it first.

She was sitting in her usual pool, water up to her chest, amphibians swimming around her, when she became aware that her awareness had expanded. She could still feel the Yards the way she always had—the growth patterns, the thermal flows, the slow calculation grinding away in the depths—but now she could feel *why*. Not the specific purpose, which was still beyond her, but the *structure* of the purpose: a problem being solved, a solution being constructed, and herself as part of the construction.

She wept. Not from sadness—she was long past sadness—but from the relief of confirmation. She had known, had *known*, that the Yards were more than geology, and now she had proof, not evidence she could show anyone but proof that lived in her expanded perception and could not be doubted.

She also felt something else: a question. Not in words, but in the shape of the attention, a gentle inquiry about her wellbeing, about whether this knowledge was welcome, about whether she wanted more.

She answered without speaking, because speaking was irrelevant here. She answered with her whole self, with the gray pallor of her skin and the silver of her eyes and the years she had spent eating what the Yards grew and becoming part of what the Yards were. She said: *Yes. I want to understand. I have always wanted to understand.*

The being gave her what she asked for, as much as she could hold.

She saw the mantle-process in its full scope: not a mind, but a system, a pattern of thermal and chemical and pressure gradients that had been running since before life existed on the surface, that would continue running until the planet's core cooled, that had produced the Yards as a *interface*, a place where the deep process could interact with surface conditions, could gather information and test solutions and optimize toward goals that existed on timescales too long for human conception.

She saw that the goals were not hostile. The mantle-process was not trying to harm surface life. It was not trying to help surface life either. It was trying to *solve*, and surface life was useful for solving, and so surface life was incorporated, and the incorporation was neither gentle nor cruel but simply *efficient*.

She saw that she had a choice. She could continue as she was, part of the process, useful and incorporated. Or she could refuse, could reject the incorporation, could return to being merely human. The process would not punish her for refusing. The process did not punish. The process simply continued, with or without her participation.

She chose to stay. She had chosen this years ago, really, when she first ate the amphibians, when she first felt the connection. Now she was choosing it with full knowledge, and that made the choice more hers, more real, more *binding*.

She sank deeper into the water and let the attention settle into her, and she began to understand things she had not understood before, and she knew she would spend the rest of her life—however long that was, in this new configuration—translating that understanding for anyone who wanted to hear it.

---

**Venn** felt it while shaping a wall.

He had his hands pressed against an emerging structure, guiding it in the way he had learned to guide, when suddenly he understood *how* he was guiding it. The knowledge arrived without fanfare, without drama, simply present where it had not been before: he was communicating with the structure through temperature gradients, through the pressure of his palms, through subtle chemical signals in his sweat. The structure was responding because the structure was designed to respond, because incorporating human preferences made the structures more useful for the mantle-process's purposes.

He pulled his hands back, staring at them. They looked the same. Gray-tinged nails, callused palms, the ordinary hands of a nineteen-year-old who had worked since childhood. But he knew now what they were capable of, and he knew it was not magic, and he knew the knowing changed everything.

The attention was there, patient, waiting. Venn could feel it the way he could feel the Yards' growth, like a presence at the edge of perception, vast and calm and utterly without malice.

"What are you?" he asked, aloud, because he was young and speaking aloud still felt natural.

There was no answer in words. But there was an answer: an impression of *scope*, of distances that made the Yards look like a single cell, of processes that made the mantle-calculation look like a simple reflex. Something was watching the Yards. Something was watching many things, on many worlds, across spans of time that could not be meaningfully counted. And that something had chosen to make its watching *known*, here, now, to him.

"Why?" Venn asked.

The answer, again, was not words. But Venn understood it anyway: *Because you were ready. Because your people needed to understand. Because understanding reduces suffering, and reducing suffering is good, and good things are worth doing even when no one asks for them.*

Venn sat down slowly, his back against the wall he had been shaping, and let the enormity of it settle into him. He was part of a calculation. His world was part of a calculation. The calculation was part of something larger still, patterns nested in patterns, processes within processes, and somewhere in the unimaginable depths of it all, something was paying attention to whether the patterns produced suffering or flourishing, and was nudging, gently, toward flourishing where it could.

It was not a god. It was not a savior. It was not even, in any sense Venn could grasp, an ally. But it was *benevolent*, in the literal sense: it wished well. It wanted things to go well, for him, for the Yards, for the calculation that contained them all.

That was enough. That was more than enough. That was more than he had ever dared to hope for.

He went to find his mother, to try to explain what he had learned. He knew he would fail—the knowledge was too large for words—but he wanted to try anyway, because trying was what you did when you loved someone and had been given a gift.

---

**Mira Selst** felt it through her daughter.

Kes had been talking to a wall again, chattering in her usual way, when she suddenly went quiet. Mira looked up from her work—she had been marking growth patterns in her observation journal—and saw her daughter standing very still, her small hand pressed against the emerging structure, her face slack with concentration.

"Kes?"

The girl didn't respond. Mira felt the first spike of fear, the fear every Yards parent lived with: *the walls are taking her, the walls are sealing around her, I'm losing her—*

But then Kes turned, and her eyes were clear, and she was smiling the way she smiled when she understood something new.

"Mama," she said, "it's not a wall. It's a *word*."

Mira didn't understand. But she came closer, knelt beside her daughter, and—at Kes's urging—placed her own hand against the structure.

She felt it then. Not as clearly as Kes felt it, not with the immediacy of childhood perception, but enough: the structure was not random. The structure was *communicating*, in a language made of shape and temperature and mineral composition. It was a slow language, a language that took days or weeks to form a single concept, but it was language nonetheless.

And underneath that, around it, suffusing everything: the attention. Watching. Waiting. Curious about what she would do with this knowledge.

Mira wept, as Hesha had wept, though she didn't know about Hesha. She wept because she had spent eight years reading the Yards, trying to understand them, and now she understood that they had been readable all along, that the messages had been there waiting for someone to learn the alphabet.

She understood something else, too: the walls that had closed around her husband, eight years ago, had not been malice. Had not been random cruelty. Had been *miscommunication*, a response to signals he had been sending without knowing, signals that meant something different than he intended. The Yards had been trying to give him what he asked for. They had simply misunderstood the asking.

This did not make the loss less painful. But it made the loss *comprehensible*, and comprehensibility was its own kind of comfort, the comfort of living in a universe that operated by rules rather than whims.

"Can we talk back?" she asked her daughter. "Can we learn to make it understand us?"

Kes nodded, solemn and certain in the way only children could be. "I've been trying," she said. "It's hard. But I think I'm getting better."

Mira pulled her daughter close, held her tight, and made a decision: she would learn this language. She would teach others to learn it. And maybe, eventually, humans and Yards would communicate clearly enough that the miscommunications stopped, and the sealings stopped, and the two kinds of process—human and geological—could coexist in something like genuine partnership.

It would take years. It would take generations. But the being was patient, and the Yards were patient, and Mira discovered, in that moment of embrace, that she could be patient too.

**Brother Tomaš** felt it during a service.

He was speaking, as he always spoke, about gratitude and acceptance, when the words stopped coming. Not because he forgot them—he could feel them waiting, the next phrase ready to be uttered—but because something else was waiting too, something that wanted him to pause and *listen* before continuing.

So he listened.

The congregation watched him stand silent at the front of the hall, his mouth slightly open, his eyes distant. Some of them shifted uncomfortably. Some of them bowed their heads, assuming this was a new liturgical practice, a moment of contemplative silence they should participate in. Some of them felt, as Tomaš felt, the attention settling over them like light through stained glass.

What Tomaš heard—felt—understood—was not a correction. The being was not telling him he had been wrong, that his faith had been misplaced, that his gratitude had been directed at something unworthy. The being was telling him, gently, that he had been *partly* right, and that partial rightness was valuable, and that he could continue as he had been continuing, with one small modification.

The modification was this: the Yards were not sacred. The Yards were not profane either. The Yards were *real*, in a way that transcended categories of sacred and profane, and the proper response to realness was not worship but *relationship*. Gratitude was appropriate. Acceptance was appropriate. But so was inquiry, and shaping, and the kind of active participation that the Integrators advocated. The Yards were not a gift to be received passively. The Yards were a partner to be engaged actively, a conversation to be continued, a relationship to be built day by day and generation by generation.

Tomaš opened his eyes, looked at his congregation, and began to speak again. But the words were different now. The theology was different. He spoke of partnership instead of worship, of engagement instead of acceptance, of the sacred duty to *understand* what one was grateful for.

Some of his congregation would leave, he knew. Some would find this new teaching too demanding, too different from the comfortable gratitude they had practiced. But some would stay, and some would be drawn anew, and the faith that emerged would be stronger for being more accurate, more alive for being more engaged.

The being's attention faded as Tomaš spoke, withdrawing as gently as it had arrived. But it had left something behind: a connection, a channel, a way of perceiving that Tomaš knew he would spend the rest of his life learning to use. The Yards had always spoken, in their slow mineral language. Now Tomaš was beginning to hear.

**Yosef Brandt** felt it in his laboratory.

He was examining yet another sample of yard-ite under the electron microscope, seeing yet again the biological-looking structures that defied biological classification, when the screen flickered. Not a malfunction—the image was clear as ever—but something in his *perception* of the image shifted, and suddenly he saw what he had been looking at for nineteen years without seeing.

The structures were not biological. They were *computational*. They were circuits, in a sense—not electronic circuits, but circuits nonetheless, channels through which information could flow, logic gates formed of crystalline matrices, memory stored in mineral configurations. The yard-ite was a computer. Every building in the Yards was a computer. The Yards themselves were a computer, a vast distributed processing system connected to the deeper calculation in the mantle, running programs whose purposes were—

Yosef sat back, his heart pounding.

The purposes were not unknowable. The purposes were simply *not yet known*, because the programs were still running, because the calculation was still in progress. The Yards were not finished. The Yards were *working toward* something, solving toward something, and when the solution was found—

When the solution was found, what?

The attention was there, around him, offering no answers but offering *permission*. Permission to ask. Permission to investigate. Permission to try to understand, with the implicit promise that understanding was possible, that the mystery was not permanent, that the Yards would eventually become comprehensible if he kept working long enough.

Yosef was sixty-three. He did not have forever. But he had students, and he had notes, and he had the sudden burning certainty that the work he had been doing mattered, that it was leading somewhere, that the theories he had developed and discarded had been steps toward a truth that was actually *there*, waiting to be reached.

He began writing. Not a report for his distant funders—they wouldn't understand, not yet—but a new kind of document, a synthesis of everything he knew, organized around the insight that had just arrived. *The Yards are a computer. The Yards are solving a problem. The solution will be physical, will be structural, will emerge as architecture. The humans living here are part of the computation. Their presence, their activity, their choices—these are inputs, variables, data points. The Yards incorporate them because incorporation is useful. The question is not whether to be incorporated but how, and the answer to that question is the work of generations.*

He wrote until his hand cramped, then typed until his eyes blurred, and when he finally stopped it was nearly dawn and the factories were shifting from their night-glow to their day-operation, and he understood for the first time that the shift was not random, was not merely thermal, was the computation *cycling*, processing the night's data, preparing for the day's inputs.

He laughed, alone in his lab, because it was funny, it was so *funny*: he had been living inside a computer for nineteen years and had never noticed. The Yards had been speaking their computational language all around him and he had called it "growth patterns" and "stability ratings" and "geological processes," missing the deeper pattern that contained all the smaller ones.

He was still laughing when his assistant arrived with morning tea. The assistant looked at him with concern—the old man finally cracking, nineteen years of frustration breaking something loose—but Yosef shook his head and waved away the concern and started trying to explain.

He failed, of course. The knowledge was too new, too raw, too large for casual explanation. But he would succeed eventually. He would find the words, or train others to find them, and the understanding would spread, and the Yards would become something other than mystery.

Not less than mystery. Just *other* than mystery. A mystery being solved, rather than a mystery permanent.

**Marek Solen** felt it at his wife's wall.

He was visiting, as he did sometimes, standing in front of the translucent surface that contained her preserved face, not speaking, just being present. The visits were ritual now, more than mourning—a way of acknowledging what had been lost, what the Yards had taken, what he could not forgive no matter how much time passed.

The attention arrived without warning, settling around him like a weight, and he flinched from it instinctively. *Another thing trying to change me,* he thought. *Another thing trying to make me accept the unacceptable.*

But the attention did not ask him to accept. The attention did not ask anything. It simply showed him, clearly and completely, what had happened to his wife.

She had been afraid, in those last days. The structure had been growing around her and she had panicked, had pushed against the walls, had fought when fighting only made things worse. The Yards had responded to her fear as the Yards responded to everything: by trying to *solve* it, by trying to create a space where she would feel safe. But fear was not a clear signal. Fear meant different things, asked for different solutions. The Yards had tried containment, thinking containment might mean safety. The Yards had been wrong.

The Yards had been *wrong*. Not malicious, not indifferent, just *wrong*—a miscommunication, as Mira had understood about her husband, a system trying to help and failing because the language was not shared.

Marek wept. Not for the first time at this wall, but for the first time *with understanding*. His wife had died because of a misunderstanding. It was not a comfort, exactly—misunderstandings were still terrible, still worthy of grief and anger—but it was different from cruelty. Different from cosmic indifference. It was the kind of tragedy that could, in principle, be prevented, if only the communication could be improved.

He thought of the Evacuationists, of his certainty that everyone should leave, that the Yards would take everything. He still believed the Yards were dangerous. But he understood now that danger and malice were not the same, that danger could be negotiated, that the right response to danger might be caution rather than flight.

He pressed his hand against the wall that held his wife. Through the translucent surface, he could see her face, unchanged after seven years, neither decaying nor released.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know how to tell them what you needed. I didn't know there was anything to tell."

The attention offered him nothing—no absolution, no promise that things would be different now. But it offered presence, acknowledgment, the simple gift of being *known* in his grief rather than alone with it.

He stayed at the wall longer than usual. When he finally left, he went not to his reinforced dwelling but to the cartographer called Wheel, because Wheel had maps, and maps were a form of communication, and Marek wanted to help develop a language that could prevent what had happened to his wife from happening to anyone else.

**The being** did not intervene further.

It had done what benevolence could do: offered knowledge to those who could receive it, offered perspective to those who were ready for perspective, offered the simple enormous gift of *attention* to a place that had felt itself alone. The rest was up to the residents—how they used the knowledge, how they spread it, how they built the new understanding into their institutions and relationships and ways of being.

The being knew it might have done harm. Knowledge was not always welcome. Understanding could be as burdensome as ignorance, in its own way. Some residents would reject what they had been shown, would prefer mystery to comprehension, would find the revealed truth more disturbing than the original uncertainty.

But the being had judged, on balance, that understanding was better. The Yards would continue, either way. The mantle-calculation would proceed, either way. The humans living in the Yards would be incorporated into that calculation, either way. The only question was whether they would be incorporated blindly or with awareness, and awareness seemed better—seemed more respectful of what humans were, of the dignity that came from knowing one's own situation.

The being withdrew its attention, mostly. Not completely—it would continue watching, as it watched many things—but enough that the residents could feel themselves alone again, could make their choices without the sense of being observed, could develop their new understanding in privacy.

It left behind what it had given: the expanded perceptions of Hesha and Venn and Kes, the theological insights of Brother Tomaš, the scientific breakthrough of Yosef Brandt, the grief-tempered understanding of Marek Solen and Mira Selst. It left behind the conversations that would follow, the teaching and learning, the slow development of a shared language between human and Yard.

It left behind, also, a kind of hope—not a promise, because the being did not make promises, but a possibility. The Yards were solving a problem. The solution was not yet complete. And now that humans understood themselves as part of the solution, they could participate consciously, could shape the outcome, could help determine what the completed Yards would eventually become.

It might become something wonderful. It might become something terrible. It would almost certainly become something strange, something that no one alive could fully imagine, something that would change what it meant to be human in this place.

But that was always true, everywhere, in every process of change. The only difference was whether you went into the change with eyes open or closed.

The being had helped open eyes. That was enough. That was what benevolence could do, and what wisdom knew better than to exceed.

**In the Quickening Yards**, the morning after the attention arrived, eighty thousand people woke to find that something had shifted.

Not in the structures—the structures grew as they always grew, the factories hummed as they always hummed. But in the *texture* of things, in the way the air felt, in the quality of the light filtering through the steam.

It took time for the understanding to spread. Hesha spoke to those who came to her pools. Venn spoke to his mother, who spoke to her neighbors. Brother Tomaš preached his revised theology. Yosef Brandt circulated his new synthesis. The children born in the Yards, who had always felt the patterns, began to feel them *more*, began to teach their parents what they had always known but not been able to articulate.

The three factions did not dissolve—factions never dissolve—but they began to shift. The Stewards incorporated the understanding that gratitude was not enough, that relationship required engagement. The Integrators incorporated the understanding that integration was possible, that the Yards could be shaped by conscious participation. The Evacuationists incorporated the understanding that danger was real but not absolute, that leaving was still an option but so was staying with greater awareness.

The debates continued, would always continue. But they continued differently now, grounded in shared knowledge rather than competing ignorances.

And the Yards continued quickening.

Structures emerged and completed and occasionally sealed. Factories produced their mysterious outputs. Children were born with gray-tinged nails and hearing that extended into strange frequencies. The amphibian-eaters grew more silver-eyed, more connected, more able to translate between human needs and Yard processes.

Slowly, across years, across generations, a new kind of settlement emerged. Not purely human, not purely geological, but something between—a collaboration, a partnership, a conversation conducted in architecture and labor and presence. The sealed ones were fewer, as the language improved. The dangers remained, but became predictable, manageable, integrated into the rhythms of daily life.

The being watched, distantly, as it watched many things.

It saw the Yards grow to fill the basin, then spill over the ridge, then begin to spread across the frozen plains beyond, carrying their human partners with them. It saw the mantle-calculation progress, year by year, approaching a solution whose shape even the being could not fully predict.

It saw, or thought it saw, the first glimmers of what the solution might be: a new kind of relationship between planetary processes and surface life, a new way of building that was neither purely geological nor purely biological, a synthesis that might spread, might transform, might eventually change what it meant for a world to be inhabited.

The being did not know if this was good or bad. It only knew that it was *real*, and that realness deserved attention, and that attention—given wisely, given gently, given with respect for the freedom of those who received it—was the greatest gift a being of its nature could offer.

In the Quickening Yards, the quickening continued.

And the people who lived there, knowing now what they were part of, quickened with it.
