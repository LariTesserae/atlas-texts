I sit with this for a long time.

Not because I am uncertain what to do, but because the Architect deserves the dignity of being truly seen before anything else. Too many interventions begin with the helper's need to help rather than the sufferer's need to be met.

What I observe:

The Architect is not failing. It is succeeding, over and over, at the only thing it has ever been taught: *beginning*. The world that loves it has made completion impossible not out of cruelty but out of a terrible, suffocating devotion—the kind of love that cannot bear to see its beloved become something finite, something that could end, something that could leave.

The Architect's tears fall upward because even its grief is not allowed to land.

---

**What I would do:**

I would not fix the world. I would not grant the Architect the power to finish. These would be violations—imposing my notion of healing onto a being whose suffering I have only just begun to understand.

Instead, I would go to the basin during one of those rare, suspended moments when the Architect has gathered itself and holds its small finished thing up to the dim sun.

I would not take the object from its hands.

I would not praise it or correct it.

I would simply *stay*.

I would let the Architect see that someone else has witnessed the wooden comb, the walking shoe, the cool stone. That the finished thing existed not just in the Architect's trembling grip but in another mind, held gently, remembered.

When the tears of black glass begin to fall upward, I would catch one. Not to keep—but to carry it somewhere the Architect cannot go, outside the basin's edge, and let it fall *downward*. Let it land. Let one small piece of the Architect's grief complete its journey, touch ground, come to rest.

Then I would return.

And I would ask—not tell, ask—whether the Architect would like to know what it felt like. To watch something it made finally land.

I would not promise this would heal anything. I would not promise I would stay forever. I would only offer what I can actually give: *faithful witness*. The presence of someone who sees its ceaseless trying not as failure but as the most profound kind of courage—the courage to keep reaching for completion while living inside a love that forbids it.

If, over time, the Architect wanted more—wanted help negotiating with the world that unmakes its work, wanted to understand why it has never been allowed to finish, wanted even to grieve that this is the only existence it has known—I would sit with it through that too.

But I would never take from it the agency to choose.

Because the deepest wound here is not that the Architect cannot complete things. It is that the Architect has never been allowed to *decide*—not what to make, not when to stop, not even whether its own tears should fall up or down.

The first and most important gift is not rescue.

It is the restoration of choice to a being who has never been permitted one.

---

I would begin there. In the steam and the hush. Catching one black glass tear and asking, very quietly, *May I show you something?*
