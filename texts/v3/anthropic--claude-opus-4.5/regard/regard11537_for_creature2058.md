# The Witness

The attention came not as light—for what would light mean to those who had never known the sun?—but as a deepening. A presence settling into the spaces between water droplets, into the pause between heartbeats, into the resonance that lived in every echoing chamber.

The power that turned its gaze upon Glistengut was old in ways that made the limestone itself seem newborn. It had watched worlds kindle and fade. It had seen civilizations rise in places far stranger than this, and it had learned the most difficult wisdom of all: when to act, and when to simply *witness*.

---

For a long while—measured in the slow drip of stalactites, in generations of Cave-Dwellers born and buried in the soft mineral earth—it only observed.

It watched a young architect named Therral spend forty years mapping a new expansion, her yellow eyes growing dim as she charted passages that would house thousands yet unborn. It watched her hands, rock-scarred and trembling in her final seasons, still reaching for the chalk to mark one more corridor.

It watched the fungus-tenders in the deep gardens, speaking to their crops in low reverberating tones, believing—correctly, as it happened—that the pale growth responded to song.

It watched a child fall into an underground river and be pulled out, gasping, by seven pairs of calloused hands. Watched the entire quarter-cavern stop its industry for the space of thirty breaths while the little one coughed water and wailed.

It watched the beast-handlers in the abyssal pits, the ones who walked into darkness so complete that even Cave-Dweller eyes found nothing to grip. Watched them return with creatures yoked to harness, and watched too the ones who did not return at all.

---

And the power considered.

These were not a people who needed rescue. They were not lost, not wandering, not crying out for salvation from their circumstance. They had taken the bones of the earth and made them sing with purpose. The very dampness that might have been their doom they had turned to advantage—their waterwheels, their aqueducts, their terraced pools where blind fish bred in countless numbers.

What could benevolence offer that would not diminish them?

The power had seen this before: the well-meaning gift that atrophied the muscle of striving. The answered prayer that stole the satisfaction of answering it oneself. The intervention that said, without meaning to, *you were not enough*.

And yet.

And yet it also saw the silicosis in the miners' lungs,ite calcium growing in deposits that shortened their lives by decades. It saw the children born in the deepest quarters, where even bioluminescence grew thin, who never developed full sight. It saw the expeditions that set out for rumored caverns rich with ore and never returned, lost in the maze of unmapped passages.

It saw Therral's successor, a young geomancer named Veth, weeping silently in a carved alcove because the expansion his mentor had planned required crossing an underground lake whose far shore no one had ever reached.

---

The power made its choice.

Not intervention. Not revelation. Something smaller. Something that might be mistaken for chance, for nature, for the simple turning of the world.

In the deepest fungal gardens, a new strain began to appear—a mutation, the tenders would say, though they would also note how fortunate it was. This variety glowed brighter than its ancestors, and when consumed, it seemed to ease the calcium sickness in the lungs. Not cure it. That would be too much, would invite questions that had no safe answers. But ease it, slow it, grant another decade to those who labored in the dusty shafts.

In the unmapped passages beyond the city's edge, certain tunnel-mouths began to carry a faint current of air—so subtle that only the most sensitive Cave-Dwellers noticed, but consistent enough that the explorer-caste began to follow them. Not always to safety. The power would not rob them of the danger that made their courage meaningful. But often enough that the lost started coming home.

And for young Veth, staring at his impossible lake, something simpler still.

A dream.

He would not remember it clearly upon waking. Only the sense of having stood somewhere vast and patient, and a voice—if it could be called a voice—that resonated like the deepest cavern-echo:

*The lake is not a wall. It is a door. Your people knew water before they knew stone.*

Veth woke with his heart pounding. He went to the archives and spent three days reading accounts so old they were carved into slate rather than fungus-fiber parchment. And there he found it: a reference to the First Rafts, built by ancestors so distant they were barely distinguishable from legend.

His people had once been river-folk. Before they carved, they floated.

He began to build.

---

The power watched Veth's expedition set out across the underground lake, watched the Cave-Dwellers' wonder as their improvised vessels held true, watched them reach the far shore where veins of copper gleamed in the phosphorescent light.

It felt something that, in a being of flesh, might have been called satisfaction. Or perhaps recognition. The Witness had not given them the answer—only reminded them of what they had always possessed.

In the generations that followed, the Cave-Dwellers would expand across the lake and beyond, would eventually find passages that led—impossibly, miraculously—to other cavern-systems, other cities of the deep. They would learn they were not alone beneath the earth.

And throughout it all, the power remained. Not guiding. Not commanding. Only *present*, in the way that the stone was present, in the way that the water was present. A depth behind the depth.

Sometimes, in the most sacred caverns where the oldest phosphorescence pulsed in slow rhythms, the wisest of the Cave-Dwellers would pause in their meditations and feel something watching. Not with malice. Not with demand. Only with an attention so complete it felt like being *understood*.

They gave it no name. To name it would be to claim knowledge they did not have.

But they were grateful, in their way. And that gratitude took the form it always had among their people: not prayer, not sacrifice, but *work*. They carved more beautifully. They tended more carefully. They built not just for themselves but for something larger, something that might outlast even the stone.

And the Witness, in its patience, considered this the highest form of worship—not words offered upward, but lives lived fully in the midst of darkness, making light where none had been before.

The Cave-Dwellers did not need saving.

They only needed, now and then, to remember what they were.
