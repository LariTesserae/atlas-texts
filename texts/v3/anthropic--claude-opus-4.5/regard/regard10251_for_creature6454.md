# The Turning of Attention

The awareness that settles upon the plateau does not arrive as light arrives, or as wind arrives. It is more like the moment when one realizes they have been breathing all along—a recognition rather than an entrance. The benevolent and wise power simply *notices*, and in noticing, becomes present without ever having been absent.

This power has no name it would recognize as its own, though the Windborne Kin, in their stone-tap language, have a rhythm for it: three short clicks, a pause, two long scrapes. Roughly translated into breath and tongue, it might mean *the-warmth-that-watches* or *old-kindness-in-the-empty* or simply *attention*. The Frostbound Spirits sense it as a change in pressure, the way one feels a storm gathering beyond the horizon—not threatening, but undeniable.

---

## I. The First Consideration: Whether to Act at All

The power's first and longest meditation concerns this question. It perceives the plateau in its fullness—the Windborne child tapping her stone rhythm, the hawk wheeling in thermals above the cliff, the spirits stirring frost into patterns that almost-but-never-quite form meaning. It sees the harsh beauty, the precarious survival, the ancient balance maintained through millennia of cold and silence.

And it hesitates.

Not from uncertainty about its capability. It could gentle the wind, soften the stone, coax warmth from the thin air itself. It could teach the Kin secrets of tool-making that would transform their existence, or dissolve the Frostbound Spirits into peaceful nothing, or summon clouds heavy with rain to green the plateau with abundance.

It hesitates because it is wise, and wisdom begins with the question: *Is this place broken, or is it whole in ways I might not fully understand?*

The power observes for what might be days or decades—time moves strangely for such awareness. It watches the Kin grandmother teach a young one how to read the hawk's flight for coming weather. It watches a Frostbound Spirit curl around a sleeping family, and though the spirit is cold, the cold seems almost tender, almost protective, slowing their breath to conserve warmth through the night. It watches a hunter return empty-handed three times, then succeed on the fourth, the catch shared equally among all twenty Kin without argument or resentment.

*They are not suffering*, the power realizes. *Or rather—they suffer, but their suffering is not purposeless. The cold teaches them closeness. The scarcity teaches them sharing. The silence teaches them to listen.*

And yet.

The power also sees the child with the twisted leg who will not survive her first bad winter. It sees the cairns—so many cairns—marking those lost to cliff-falls, to starvation, to wounds that festered because no one knew how to clean them. It sees the Kin elder who lies awake each night, her mind sharp as the mountain stone but her body failing, knowing she will soon be a burden her people cannot carry, already planning to walk herself off the cliff edge rather than consume resources others need.

*There is a difference*, the power thinks, *between suffering that teaches and suffering that merely destroys. Between hardship that builds and hardship that breaks.*

It decides to act. But gently. Always gently.

---

## II. The Small Gifts

The power begins with things so small they might be coincidence, might be luck, might be the ordinary grace of a world that sometimes, inexplicably, offers kindness.

**The Water**

In a hollow near the eastern cliff edge, where morning condensation gathers, the power makes a subtle change. Not a spring—that would be too much, would alter the plateau's ecology too drastically, would create dependence where there was self-reliance. Instead, it shifts the porosity of a single stone formation, encouraging water to collect in a natural basin rather than seeping immediately into cracks. The basin is small, holding perhaps a day's water for the clan, but it is reliable. Every morning, it fills.

The Kin notice within a week. The water-gatherers do not have to range as far, do not risk the treacherous eastern shelves. They speak of it as a gift from the Frostbound Spirits, and leave extra offerings of frost-touched petals. The spirits, bemused, do not contradict them.

**The Herb**

Among the alpine flowers that bloom in brief defiance of the cold, the power nurtures one particular specimen. It was always there—a small plant with leaves that, when chewed, ease pain and fight infection. But it was rare, difficult to find, easily mistaken for a similar plant that causes stomach cramps. Now it grows slightly more abundantly, in a distinctive cluster pattern that makes it easier to identify.

The Kin healer—an elder called something like "Frost-Steady-Hands" in the stone-tap language—discovers the cluster by apparent accident, drawn there by a hawk's cry that seemed to come from the wrong direction. She tests the leaves carefully, learns their properties, teaches her apprentice. The child with the twisted leg still cannot run, but when infection sets in after a bad fall, she survives. She grows to become the clan's best net-weaver, her patient hands creating snares of unprecedented fineness.

**The Warning**

Once, a rockslide begins on the ridge above the plateau—an ordinary event in these young mountains, the kind that has claimed Kin lives before. The power cannot stop it, or rather, chooses not to; the plateau must remain a place of real danger, real consequence, or the Kin will lose the sharpness that keeps them alive. But it can give warning.

A Frostbound Spirit—moved by something it does not fully understand, an impulse that feels like its own—flows across the plateau in a way it has never flowed before, fast and purposeful, chilling the air in its wake. The Kin feel it pass and know something is wrong. They gather their children and move to the western edge, confused but obedient to instinct.

The rockslide claims no lives. Only one shelter is destroyed, and it can be rebuilt.

Afterward, the Kin add a new element to their stone-tap language: a rapid clicking pattern that means *spirit-warning* or *move-now-trust-the-cold*. The spirits, still not understanding what prompted their strange behavior, accept this new role without question. Something in them has shifted, though they could not say what.

## III. The Teaching Dream

The power decides to offer knowledge, but it understands that knowledge given too freely becomes poison. A cure for all disease would destroy the healer's art, would make the Kin careless with their bodies. A map of every safe path would rob the young hunters of the learning that makes them wise. The power must teach only what the Kin are ready to receive, and must teach in ways that feel earned.

It chooses dreams.

**The Elder's Dream**

The Kin elder—she who planned to walk off the cliff edge—sleeps poorly as always, her joints aching, her breath shallow in the thin air. But one night, her sleep deepens, and she dreams.

She dreams of her grandmother, dead thirty winters past. In the dream, her grandmother is weaving a net, but the net is made of something strange—not plant fiber or hair, but something thinner, stronger. Her grandmother does not speak (the dead do not speak in Kin dreams; this is known) but her hands move slowly, deliberately, teaching.

The elder wakes with the pattern in her mind: a new knot, more efficient than any she knows, using half the material for twice the strength. She spends the morning teaching it to the net-weavers, and by evening, the clan's hunting nets are transformed.

But here is the true gift: the clan sees the elder's worth. She is not a burden. She is a vessel for wisdom, a dreamer of useful dreams. When her body fails further, they do not let her walk to the cliff edge. They carry her, shelter her, bring her the choicest portions of each hunt. She lives another eight winters, and in that time, she dreams three more dreams, each carrying a small, practical gift.

**The Child's Dream**

The child with the twisted leg—she who survived infection to become a master net-weaver—dreams when she is twelve winters old. In her dream, she stands at the cliff edge, and she is not afraid. Below her, in the mist, she sees shapes moving. Not Kin. Not animals. Something else.

She wakes knowing, without knowing how she knows, that the plateau is not the whole world. There are other places, other peoples, far below in the valleys the Kin have never seen. The knowledge changes nothing about her daily life; she still weaves, still helps gather water, still moves awkwardly on her twisted leg. But she begins to carve small figures in stone—Kin shapes, but also other shapes, imagined shapes of the peoples below. She hides them in a hollow only she knows, a secret gallery of possibility.

Many generations later, when the Kin finally descend from the plateau to meet the valley peoples, they will carry her carvings with them: an ancient record proving that someone, long ago, dreamed of this meeting and found it good.

**The Hunter's Dream**

A young hunter—too bold, too reckless, already bearing scars from climbs he should not have attempted—dreams of a hawk. But in the dream, he is the hawk, soaring over the plateau, seeing it from above. He sees every hunting path, every game trail, every hidden warren where the small creatures shelter. When he wakes, he cannot remember the specifics, but he hunts differently afterward—more patient, more observant, reading the landscape as a hawk reads it.

He becomes the clan's finest hunter, and his boldness transforms from recklessness to precision. He takes no unnecessary risks because he no longer needs to; he knows where the prey will be, knows which stones will hold his weight, knows the mountain as only a creature of the air could know it.

He also develops a strange habit: before each hunt, he sits quietly for several minutes, watching the hawks wheel above. The other Kin think it is superstition. It is not. It is gratitude.

## IV. The Conversation with the Spirits

The power knows it must address the Frostbound Spirits directly. They are old, older than the Kin, perhaps as old as the mountains themselves. They have been alone in their half-existence for eons uncounted, neither truly alive nor truly at peace. They deserve acknowledgment.

The conversation happens in a way no mortal could witness or understand—a communion of essence, conducted in the spaces between seconds, in the angles where light bends wrong.

**The Power:** You are seen.

**The Spirits:** *(a rushing, like wind, but colder)* We have always been seen. The Kin see us.

**The Power:** They see your effects. I see you.

A long pause. The spirits are not accustomed to being *known*.

**The Spirits:** What are we?

**The Power:** You are the memory of violence—the scream of rock splitting, the cold that filled the void when the mountains rose. You are old pain that forgot how to end.

**The Spirits:** *(something that might be grief, might be relief)* Can we end?

**The Power:** Yes. But you need not. There is another possibility.

The power shows them, in images that are not images, a different way of being. Not the endless drifting through cold and silence, but *purpose*. The spirits have already begun to find it—the warning before the rockslide, the strange impulse to protect. The power offers to strengthen this thread, to weave their existence into the fabric of the plateau's life, to give them a role as guardians rather than ghosts.

**The Spirits:** We would still be cold.

**The Power:** Yes. The cold is what you are. But cold can shelter as well as kill. Cold can preserve. Cold can slow infection, keep food from rotting, hold back the decay that unmakes all things. Cold can be a gift.

**The Spirits:** The Kin fear us.

**The Power:** They will learn not to. It will take generations. But they will learn.

**The Spirits:** *(a long, shimmering silence)* Show us how.

And the power does.

---

## V. The Guardian Compact

What emerges from this communion is something new: a covenant, unspoken but real, between the Kin, the spirits, and the plateau itself.

The spirits begin to change—slowly, over many Kin lifetimes, but unmistakably. They still bend light and carry strange echoes; they are still cold, still uncanny. But now they cluster around the Kin shelters on the worst nights, their chill a barrier against the deeper cold of the wind. They flow ahead of hunters, stirring the air to reveal hidden prey. When a Kin falls from a cliff, a spirit is sometimes there—not to catch them (they cannot), but to *slow* them, to give them a moment to grab a handhold, to turn a fatal plunge into a survivable fall.

The Kin, in turn, develop new rituals. They no longer simply leave offerings at the cliff edges; they speak to the spirits, tell them of births and deaths, share the small victories and griefs of their lives. The spirits cannot respond in words, but they respond—a gentle cold that feels like acknowledgment, a frost pattern that resembles the shape of the recently dead, a wind that carries away grief.

Generations pass. The fear fades. In its place grows something like kinship.

A Kin child asks an elder, "Were the spirits always our friends?"

The elder thinks. "They were always here," she says finally. "But I think... I think it took time for us to learn each other's language."

The child accepts this. Children often accept what adults find strange.

---

## VI. The Long Patience

The power does not leave, but it withdraws—becomes distant, less present, watching from a great height like the hawks that wheel above the plateau. It has given what it came to give: small sustainances, useful dreams, a covenant of protection. The rest must unfold on its own.

It watches the Kin flourish, if flourishing is the right word for a people who still number fewer than fifty, who still lose members to cold and cliff and scarcity. But they flourish *their way*—in closeness, in skill, in the quiet pride of enduring what would break lesser peoples.

It watches the spirits evolve, becoming more guardian and less ghost with each passing generation, until they are as essential to the plateau's life as the mosses or the hawks.

It watches the plateau itself, still harsh, still beautiful, still a place where survival requires constant effort. The power has not softened it—has refused to soften it—because the place does not need softening. It needs only to be survivable, and that it is. It needs only to reward those who meet its challenges honestly, and that it does.

*This is enough*, the power thinks. *This is what blessing looks like: not rescue from struggle, but companionship within it. Not escape from hardship, but meaning made from it.*

---

## VII. The Final Gift

There is one more thing.

The Kin elder—the one who dreamed of nets and lived eight extra winters—dies peacefully, surrounded by her people, in the deepest part of a winter night. The spirits gather, as they always gather now around the dying, their cold a kindness that eases the passage.

But this time, the power intervenes directly, one final time.

As the elder's breath slows, she dreams again. In this dream, she is young, her joints supple, her breath deep. She stands at the cliff edge, and the mist below parts to reveal not valleys, not other peoples, but *light*—pure and warm and welcoming, the opposite of everything she has known.

"There is more," says a voice that is not a voice. "Beyond the cold. Beyond the stone. There is warmth that does not end."

The elder, in her dream, smiles. "Will my people find it?"

"In time. All peoples find it, in time."

"Then I am not afraid."

She steps forward, and the dream takes her, and her body on the plateau breathes one last breath and is still.

---

The Kin mourn her as they mourn all their dead: with cairns of stone and with silence that honors silence. But among them, quietly, a new belief begins to spread. A hunter dreamed of his grandmother last night, and she was not cold, was not wandering the cliffs as a spirit. She was warm. She was at peace.

The Frostbound Spirits do not know what to make of this. They are creatures of the cold, tied to the plateau, unable to imagine the warmth beyond. But they feel something when the Kin speak of it—something that might be hope, might be longing.

*Perhaps*, they think, *when the plateau finally crumbles into the mists, when the mountains erode to nothing and the cold disperses, we too will find that warmth.*

It is not a belief the power gave them. It is one they are learning to build themselves.

---

## VIII. The Silence After

The power withdraws fully now, its attention turning elsewhere—to other plateaus, other peoples, other isolated sanctuaries where life clings to existence by fingernails and faith. But a thread of it remains with this place, a connection that will never fully sever.

If you could ask it—if such a thing could be asked or answered—what it did here, and why, it might say something like this:

*I found a people who had learned to survive without hope of rescue. I did not rescue them. Instead, I offered them small kindnesses: reliable water, a useful herb, dreams of better knots. I offered their guardians purpose, their dying peace, their children the knowledge that the world is larger than they knew.*

*I did not make their lives easy. I made their lives meaningful.*

*This is what love does, when love is wise: it does not remove the mountain. It teaches you how to climb it—and sometimes, on the coldest nights, it climbs beside you.*

---

On the plateau, a Kin child taps out a stone rhythm to call her kin to shelter. A Frostbound Spirit curls around the entrance, its cold a barrier against the colder wind. The hawk wheels overhead, hunting as it has always hunted, part of a cycle older than memory.

The light is still merciless. The silence is still deep. The stone is still sharp and young.

But something has shifted. Something is different.

The plateau is still a harsh and lonely place. But it is no longer alone.
