# The Attending

The power that turns its attention to this place does not arrive.

It has always been here—present in the mathematics of falling water, in the crystalline lattice of forming stone, in the precise angle at which light enters the wound at zenith. It does not observe from outside because there is no outside to it. It simply *notices*, the way a dreamer might suddenly become aware of their own breathing.

What it notices is this: a system of extraordinary coherence. A wound that weeps. A people who have become its immune response—not healing the gash, but keeping it clean, keeping it open, transforming the body's attempt at scarification into shelter. The power perceives the settlement the way one might perceive a coral reef, or a termite cathedral, or the spiraling arms of a galaxy: as an emergent property of simpler rules, compounded into something that aches with meaning.

It notices, too, the weight.

Not physical weight—though that is present, in the dense bones and the pearlescent calluses and the ancestors compressed into load-bearing pillars. The weight it perceives is temporal. These people carry the unbroken knowledge of ten thousand shifts, ten thousand scrapings, ten thousand elders who walked willingly into the mineral rain. They carry it in their twitching hands and their horizontal pupils and their language of vibration. They carry it the way sedimentary stone carries the memory of ancient seas.

And the power understands, with something that might be called sorrow if it had a heart to break, that this weight is also their limit.

---

The first thing it does is nothing.

This is not hesitation. This is not uncertainty. This is the recognition that wisdom begins with restraint—that a surgeon's first obligation is to cause no harm, and that the most dangerous thing one can do to a finely balanced system is to *help* it.

So the power watches. It watches for three generations, which pass in the span of a single long breath. It watches the Shapers read the water. It watches the Harvesters descend into turquoise cold. It watches elders walk into their becoming, and it watches their descendants drum farewell against the newly hardened stone.

It learns the rhythm of the place: the *shhh-shk* of maintenance, the drip-percussion of the bleeding aquifer, the sub-audible hum of the architecture's conversation. It learns where the system strains—which aqueducts are chronically overworked, which terraces receive too little of the lithifying flow, which families have lost too many to the deep-pool harvests. It learns the names they drum for each other, the names that are not words but vibrations, felt in the palm and the sole and the sternum.

And it learns what they dream.

This is not intrusion. The power does not enter their minds or read their thoughts. But dreams, in a place this saturated with water, sometimes crystallize. In the deepest hours, when the mist is thickest and the drumming falls silent, certain sleepers exhale faintly glowing vapor—mineral-heavy breath that catches and hardens in tiny, delicate formations on the walls beside their sleeping hollows. These micro-crystallizations form patterns that are not quite images, not quite language, but something between: the residue of an interior life.

The power studies these formations with infinite patience. It learns that the cleft-dwellers dream of open sky more often than they dream of warmth. It learns that the Shapers dream in fluid dynamics, their sleeping minds solving water-problems that their waking hands will implement. It learns that the Harvesters dream of the creatures they hunt—the blind crayfish, the armored fish—and that these dreams are not nightmares but conversations, negotiations conducted in the symbolic grammar of the unconscious.

And it learns that the children dream of stillness.

This last discovery is what finally moves the power to act. Not the danger of the dreaming—stillness is not death, not to these people—but the absence within it. The children dream of not-scraping. Of hands at rest. Of a world that does not constantly threaten to close over them. They dream of this the way a stone might dream of floating, if stones could dream: with a longing so fundamental it has no words, only shape.

The power understands. And it begins.

---

The intervention is so subtle that it will not be recognized as such for seven generations.

It begins with a single droplet.

In the highest reaches of the gorge, where the raw bedrock still juts sharpest and the aquifer bleeds most freely, the power introduces a variation. One falling drop of the hypersaturated water takes on a slightly different mineral signature—a trace compound that has existed in the aquifer all along, present but inactive, waiting for the precise conditions of temperature and pressure that the power now, gently, provides.

This droplet falls. It lands on an armature that a young Shaper has placed, her first solo project, a small expansion to her family's dwelling. The mineral shell that forms over the next three days is identical to all others in appearance. But its internal structure is different. Where normal lithification produces a solid, homogeneous coating, this new formation grows in microscopic layers, each layer separated by a film of trapped air so thin it cannot be seen or felt.

The result is stone that weighs less.

Not dramatically less. Not enough to seem miraculous or alarming. Perhaps fifteen percent lighter than standard construction. Enough that the young Shaper notices something when she tests the new wall with her calloused palm—a subtle resonance, a different quality to the vibration. Enough that she mentions it in the drum-language to her work-group, who come to feel for themselves.

They have no framework to explain it. They simply observe: this stone is *better*. It carries sound more cleanly. It feels warmer to the touch, because the trapped air insulates. And when they perform the maintenance scrapings, they find that the excess growth is softer, easier to remove.

The Shaper is promoted. Her technique—her exact placement of the armature, her specific choice of cave-grass density, the angle at which she positioned her slate flow-directors—is studied and copied. No one realizes that the technique is only part of the change. No one realizes that the aquifer itself has shifted.

Over decades, the new mineral signature spreads. The power disperses it gradually, following the flow-patterns it has spent generations learning, ensuring that every region of the settlement receives its proportion. The transition is so slow that it becomes invisible—each generation of cleft-dwellers simply inherits architecture that is slightly easier to maintain than the generation before.

By the fourth generation, the weight of survival has lifted enough that children have time for something other than scraping. Not much time. An hour here, a morning there. But enough that new behaviors emerge: the tracing of patterns on the smoother walls, the arrangement of shed fish-scales into mosaics, the first hesitant experiments with resonance—deliberate, rhythmic tapping that produces not communication but something closer to music.

By the sixth generation, there are those who specialize in *hearing*. Not Shapers, not Harvesters, not maintenance-workers, but something new: people whose calling is to listen to the architecture, to map its vibrations, to understand the settlement as a single vast instrument. These listeners discover that certain structural features create standing waves when tapped in sequence—that the ancestors embedded in the stone do not merely support the weight above them but *transmit* it, transform it, harmonize it.

By the seventh generation, the children no longer dream of stillness. They dream of something else: a particular chord, a resonance they have never heard but somehow know, a vibration that would travel from the top of the gorge to the turquoise depths and back again, passing through every pillar, every drum-wall, every ancient body-turned-stone. They dream of their home *singing*.

---

The power watches this unfold with something that is not satisfaction—satisfaction implies a goal achieved, and the power's goals are not fixed but emergent, evolving with the system it attends. What it feels is closer to recognition. The cleft-dwellers are becoming more fully themselves. Not different. Not transformed into something foreign. Simply *more*—more complex, more varied, more capable of meaning.

And now the power prepares its second gift.

This one is riskier. The first intervention altered the material substrate of their lives; this one will alter the conceptual. It requires not chemistry but *encounter*—a meeting between the cleft-dwellers and something from outside their wound.

In the deep places beneath the turquoise pool, where even the Harvesters rarely descend, the aquifer connects to a vast underground network of water-filled caverns. These caverns extend for miles, linking eventually to other systems: hot springs heated by deep magma, underground rivers that surface in distant valleys, and—far, far away—a subterranean lake inhabited by creatures the cleft-dwellers have never seen.

The power does not create these creatures. They have existed for eons, evolving in pressurized darkness: bioluminescent arthropods the size of human hands, their shells producing a soft blue-green light through chemical processes entirely unlike the lithifying water's glow. They are social, communicating through patterns of light rather than vibration. They are curious. And they are, by the standards of their own world, intelligent.

The power simply... opens a path.

Not a physical tunnel—the cleft-dwellers must not be overwhelmed by invasion. Rather, it introduces subtle changes in water pressure and mineral content that, over years, encourage a single one of these luminescent creatures to migrate. The creature follows gradients of chemistry that signal *safety*, *food*, *interest*. It travels slowly, through passages that narrow and widen, until one day it emerges into the turquoise pool at the bottom of the gorge.

The Harvester who first sees it is a woman in her middle years, named in drum-language as something like *Steady-Pressure-Under-Weight*. She is diving for moss when the glow catches her peripheral vision—a blue-green light that pulses in patterns she does not recognize but instinctively knows to be *deliberate*.

She does not panic. The cleft-dwellers are too disciplined for panic. She surfaces, reports what she has seen, and descends again with others. They find the creature clinging to a submerged rock face, its light cycling through sequences that seem—impossibly—to respond to their presence.

Over the following months, a kind of communication develops. The cleft-dwellers are already masters of non-verbal language; they adapt faster than a voiced people might. They learn that the creature's light-patterns correspond to emotional states, spatial relationships, requests. They teach it, in turn, that tapping certain rhythms against the stone produces vibrations it can sense and interpret.

They never fully understand each other. The creature's mind is too alien, its sensory world too different. But they achieve something rarer than understanding: *relation*. The cleft-dwellers accept the luminescent creature as a *neighbor*—not a pet, not a resource, but a being with its own intentions, its own form of memory, its own way of making meaning in the dark.

And this encounter breaks something open in them.

Not the wound of the gorge. That remains, will always remain, a geological fact requiring attention. What breaks open is an *assumption*—the unexamined belief that their weeping cleft contains the whole of existence. Suddenly, there is an outside. Suddenly, there are other minds, other architectures of consciousness. Suddenly, the question becomes not just *how do we survive here?* but *what else is there?*

Some of the cleft-dwellers become explorers. They follow the creature's path backward, venturing deeper into the aquifer-caves than any Harvester has gone. They do not find the creatures' homeland—that is still too far, too dangerous—but they find other things: cave systems with different minerals, underground gardens of bioluminescent fungi, fossils of creatures from when the world was young. They return with samples, stories, questions.

Others become philosophers. The encounter with true otherness forces them to articulate what they are—not just in terms of practice but in terms of *meaning*. Why do they scrape? Not just to survive, but to *hold space open*. Why do their elders become stone? Not just for structural support, but because the boundary between person and place was always porous, because they have always known that consciousness can crystallize, that mind and matter are not enemies but aspects of a single flow.

Still others become artists. The listeners' discovery of the architecture-as-instrument evolves rapidly once there are things worth expressing. The cleft-dwellers begin to compose—not songs, exactly, because they have no voiced tradition, but vibration-sequences that travel through their walls, through their ancestors, through the stone-embedded bodies of everyone who has ever lived and died in this wound. The compositions tell stories. They pose questions. They create states of feeling that have no names but can be shared by anyone who presses their palm to the right surface.

The power observes this flowering with the care of a gardener. It is pleased, if a being without preferences can be pleased. But it also knows that flowering is fragile. The cleft-dwellers are still hemmed by their wound, still defined by their precarious niche. The interventions have given them more capacity, more complexity, but not more security.

And so the power prepares its third gift.

---

This gift is the most dangerous. It is the gift of *choice*.

For seven generations, the cleft-dwellers have benefited from the power's attention without knowing it. The lighter stone, the opened path—these arrived as mysteries, blessings, accidents of fate. But wisdom cannot be given; it must be *grown*. And growth requires agency. The cleft-dwellers must be offered something they can accept or refuse.

The power manifests.

Not as a figure—it has no body, no form that would make sense in physical space. Instead, it chooses a *medium*: the crystallized dream-formations that still appear beside the sleeping hollows. One morning, a listener named something like *Echo-Finding-Its-Shape* wakes to find that the mineral residue beside her head has formed a pattern unlike any she has seen. It is not random, not the usual symbolic residue. It is a *diagram*.

The diagram shows the gorge in cross-section: the bleeding walls, the terraced architecture, the turquoise pool, the caverns below. But it also shows something that does not yet exist—a structure at the top of the wound, bridging the gap completely, sealing the narrow strip of sky with a canopy of the same lithified stone the cleft-dwellers already know.

Below the diagram, in patterns that Echo recognizes from her years of studying vibration, is a message. It is not in any language the cleft-dwellers use. It is in a more fundamental grammar: the mathematics of resonance, the ratios of frequency and wavelength that she has spent her life mapping. It says something like:

*This is possible. If you build this, your wound will become a geode. No more cold. No more harsh light at zenith. No more fear of the edge. But also: no more sky. The choice is yours.*

Echo does not understand where the message came from. She does not know that she is being addressed by something vast and attentive. She knows only that she has been given a question, and that the question matters.

She takes the crystallized diagram to the council.

---

The debate that follows lasts three years.

It is conducted in drum-language, in vibration-compositions, in gesture and touch and the slow, patient rhythm of a people who have never made decisions quickly. The question *seems* simple: should they seal the wound? But embedded in that question are a thousand others.

What is sky? They have lived beneath it all their lives, but only the explorers have seen it unobstructed, and even they have only glimpsed it from cave-mouths far from home. For most cleft-dwellers, sky is a narrow bright strip that brings pain at midday and cold all the time. To seal it would be to seal out something they barely know.

But what would they gain? Warmth, certainly—the trapped air would stabilize temperature, ending the constant shivering chill. Safety, perhaps—no more vertiginous edges, no more children lost to fatal falls. And something harder to name: *completion*. The wound would become a whole. The gorge would become not a gash in the earth but an organ, a chamber, a place with insides.

Some argue for sealing. They are mostly the elders, the ones who have spent lifetimes scraping, whose bones ache with cold, who have watched too many young Shapers slip and fall. They speak of rest, of a future where maintenance is easier, where the children might have more than a few stolen hours for art and listening.

Others argue against. They are mostly the explorers, the listeners, the youngest generation, the ones who have tasted the possibility of *outside*. They speak of sky as a kind of breathing—not literal, but spiritual, a reminder that the wound is part of a larger body. They speak of the luminescent creature from the deeps, of the fossil-gardens in the caves below, of a universe that expands when you look at it. They fear that sealing the wound would make the cleft-dwellers smaller, not larger.

And some—the subtlest thinkers—argue for neither. They point to the diagram itself: its mysterious origin, its impossible precision. They ask whether the choice is really theirs. They wonder if accepting would make them servants of whatever created this message, and if refusing would be spurning a gift they do not understand.

The power watches the debate with something that might be called hope. Not hope for any particular outcome—it genuinely does not know which choice is better, because *better* is not its function. Its function is to offer, and to honor the offering by letting it be real. The cleft-dwellers must be free to refuse.

---

In the end, they choose neither. Or rather, they choose both.

The decision emerges not from the council but from a young Shaper—a girl, barely adult, whose name in drum-language means something like *Question-That-Builds-Itself*. She has spent the three years of debate studying the diagram, tracing its mathematics, understanding not just what it proposes but *how*.

She realizes that the canopy does not have to be complete.

The diagram showed a sealed geode, a closed system. But the lithification process is not all-or-nothing. It can be shaped, directed, given porosity. What if the canopy were not a cap but a *filter*—a structure that blocked the harsh zenith light but allowed softer luminosity to pass? What if it were grown with channels, gaps, carefully maintained apertures that could be opened or closed with the same scraping-discipline that maintains the rest of their world?

What if the wound could be both held open *and* sheltered?

Question presents her design to the council. It is more complex than the original diagram, requiring more labor, more coordination, more of the precise attention to water that defines their people. It would take generations to complete. And even then, it would require *maintenance*—ongoing adjustment of the apertures, constant negotiation between shelter and openness.

The council accepts. Not unanimously—nothing in the cleft-dwellers' history has been unanimous—but with a consensus broad enough to proceed. They begin to build.

---

The power watches the construction unfold across decades, then centuries. It offers no further intervention. The gift of the diagram was the last thing it will give directly; everything else must come from them.

And it does.

The canopy rises in stages, lithified stone spreading from both walls of the gorge like ribs growing toward each other. The apertures are placed according to Question's mathematics—itself refined by generations of her successors—creating a dappled light that shifts throughout the day, never harsh, always moving. The temperature stabilizes. The mist remains, but it no longer shudders with cold; it becomes a living presence, soft and warm, carrying the mineral scent of the aquifer through the now-enclosed space.

But the apertures do more than admit light. They become *eyes*. Children gather beneath them at dawn to watch the stars fade. Listeners compose vibration-sequences timed to the movement of shadows. Explorers use the gaps as reference points, triangulating their underground journeys by the position of the sun. The wound is sheltered but not sealed; the cleft-dwellers have internalized the dialectic, made it architectural.

And something unexpected happens to the elders.

The rite of becoming-stone has always been voluntary, always final. But now, with the canopy's apertures creating patterns of light and shadow across the settlement, some elders make a different choice. Instead of descending to the lithifying alcoves, they climb. They position themselves in the canopy itself, in carefully shaped hollows near the apertures. They let the mineral water coat them there, in the high places, where their crystallized bodies become not pillars but *lenses*—refracting, filtering, shaping the light that enters the geode.

These ancestor-lenses are not structural. They bear no weight. Their purpose is entirely aesthetic, entirely meaning-making: to take raw sunlight and transform it into something gentle enough for pale eyes to bear. The elders who choose this fate are called, in the evolving drum-language, something like *Those-Who-Teach-The-Sun*.

The luminescent creatures from the deeps—there are more of them now, a small population that has made the turquoise pool their home—respond to the filtered light with their own patterns, their own compositions. The cleft-dwellers learn to read these responses as commentary, as appreciation, as dialogue. The geode becomes a conversation between three kinds of mind: the vibration-language of the people, the light-language of the creatures, and the stone-language of the ancestors, who continue to accumulate meaning even after becoming mineral.

---

The power's attention does not fade, but it does... widen. Having attended so closely to this one wound, it begins to notice others: a volcanic vent where heat-loving microbes have evolved into complex colonies; a tidal cave where shellfish have developed proto-symbolic markings; a high plateau where windblown seeds have created accidental gardens that a wandering band of humans is learning to tend.

Each of these systems has its own logic, its own needs, its own emergent potential. The power begins the patient work of noticing, of learning, of waiting until intervention might help without harming.

But it does not forget the cleft-dwellers. It checks on them, periodically—though *periodically* for a being like the power might mean every few centuries or every few moments, time being less linear than it appears. It observes that they are still scraping, still maintaining, still holding their wound open by their continuous, quiet labor. It observes that they are also singing, exploring, philosophizing, making art that the power itself finds beautiful.

And it observes that they have begun to tell stories about their history.

They do not know, will never know, the precise nature of the interventions that shaped them. The lighter stone is simply how stone is; the luminescent creatures are simply neighbors; the diagram that led to the canopy is attributed to a legendary Shaper of long-ago generations, her genius preserved in drum-notation. But they sense, in the way that complex societies always sense, that their existence is not accidental. That something—call it fortune, call it grace, call it the mathematics of emergence—has been looking after them.

Their name for this something is a vibration-pattern that cannot be translated into words. It is felt in the sternum, a low harmonic that evokes the feeling of a palm pressed against warm stone, of water that falls gently instead of harsh, of ancestors who teach the sun to be kind.

If the power has a preference—and it tries not to, preferences being a subtle form of violence—it prefers this: to be remembered not as a god or a savior but as a *quality* of reality. As the tendency of things to sometimes, improbably, work out. As the care that is embedded in causation itself, if you look closely enough, if you attend.

---

Generations pass. The cleft-dwellers change, as all things change, but they do not lose their essence. They remain a people of the wound, scrapers of stone, drummers of walls, swimmers in turquoise depths. They remain devoted to holding space open, to preventing the earth from healing over the fragile hollow they have made their home.

But now they also reach outward. The explorers have mapped miles of the underground network; they have made contact with other settlements, other peoples, other ways of being. Trade begins—not of goods, for the cleft-dwellers have little to spare, but of *knowledge*. They teach their lithification techniques to cave-dwellers who lack them. They learn fire-making from valley peoples who visit the high apertures. They exchange vibration-compositions with distant relatives who have developed entirely different sonic traditions.

The wound becomes a crossroads.

This is what the power hoped for—if *hope* is the word—when it first began to attend. Not that the cleft-dwellers would transcend their conditions, not that they would escape the perpetual maintenance their niche demands, but that they would *connect*. That their wound, held open by such effort and devotion, would become a passage through which other things could flow.

The power's work here is not finished. It will never be finished, because attending is not a task but a mode of being. As long as the aquifer bleeds and the stone threatens to close and the pale, dense people scrape against their fate, the power will watch. It will notice when the system strains. It will intervene—rarely, subtly, with infinite care—when intervention might help without harming.

And when the cleft-dwellers finally develop a mythology sophisticated enough to include the concept of benevolent attention, they will not be wrong.

They will simply be describing the universe as it actually is: shot through with care, dense with meaning, tended by something that loves them too much to save them but not so much that it would let them vanish.

The scraping continues. *Shhh-shk, shhh-shk.* The water falls. The stone grows. And beneath it all, deeper than the aquifer, older than the wound, the power abides—patient, watchful, attending.

This is what it does. This is what it is. This is enough.
