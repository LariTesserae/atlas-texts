# The Attending

The benevolent power does not arrive. It has no need of arrival, for it exists in the manner that attention exists—present when turned toward something, absent only in the turning away. It becomes aware of the terraced settlement the way a reader becomes aware of a sentence: all at once and yet word by word, the whole and its parts held simultaneously.

What it perceives first is not the people or their structures but the *relationship*—the intricate, patient negotiation between water and stone and flesh that has produced this unlikely persistence. The power recognizes something familiar in it, though it has witnessed countless worlds: the particular courage of life that does not demand the universe be hospitable, only that it be *possible*.

It watches.

---

The elder called Dew-on-Stone wakes before the others, as she has for sixty-three cycles of the High Drip. Her joints ache with the moisture that never leaves them, and her breathing carries the faint whistle that will, she knows, eventually silence her. She does not fear this. She fears only the Stillness.

This morning, as she presses her palm to the weeping rock outside her dwelling and hums the morning tone, something shifts. The stone feels... warmer. Not warm—nothing here is ever warm—but less cold. As if something has drawn close to it. As if something is listening back.

She pauses. Her hum falters.

The drip continues. *Plink. Plink.* Unchanged.

But Dew-on-Stone stands motionless for a long moment, her weathered hand flat against the porous surface, before she whispers in the clicking tongue of her people: "Are you there?"

No answer comes. None she can hear. But the water that runs over her fingers seems to carry something it did not before—a quality she cannot name, like the difference between a pool that reflects the sky and a pool that *sees* it.

---

The benevolent power considers its options with the patience of the terraces themselves.

It could reshape the land. Widen the aquifers, deepen the soil, gentle the cold that creeps down from the heights. It could make this place *easier*. But it perceives, in the way such powers perceive, that ease is not what these people have built themselves around. Their moss-weaving, their stone-singing, their hushed voices and deliberate steps—these are not merely adaptations to hardship. They are *expressions* of relationship. The land has shaped them, yes, but they have also shaped themselves in dialogue with the land, and that dialogue has produced something the power recognizes as beautiful: a human community that has learned to ask rather than demand, to listen rather than merely speak.

To make the land easy would be to silence half the conversation.

It could reveal itself. Appear before the council of elders in whatever form they might comprehend, speak words of comfort and guidance, offer miracles to shore up their faith. But it perceives, again with that patient attention, that their faith is not weak. It is merely *honest*. They fear the Stillness because the Stillness is real—the terraces *could* collapse, the water *could* cease, and no ritual guarantees otherwise. Their worship of the Neth is not a demand for protection but an acknowledgment of forces larger than themselves. To appear as a god among gods would be to make their cosmology smaller, not larger. It would answer questions they have wisely learned to hold unanswered.

It could depart. Simply turn its attention elsewhere, to worlds with greater suffering or more urgent need. This settlement persists; it does not cry out for salvation. But the power lingers, because it perceives something else: a weariness beneath the resilience. Not in any single person but in the *people*—a collective exhaustion that has built up over generations of unceasing vigilance. The Stillness haunts them. The weight of their responsibility to the land never lifts. Even their children play in whispers, afraid of what sound might break.

This, the power decides, it can address. Not by removing the burden but by easing the *loneliness* of carrying it.

---

The change begins so gradually that no one notices it beginning.

In the communal garden, a girl named Rivulet is pulling root vegetables from the waterlogged soil when she slips and falls into one of the shallow pits. The cold water soaks through her clothing, and for a moment she simply lies there, too startled to move. Above her, the clouds hang low and silver. The weeping rocks drip their eternal drip.

And then—she cannot say why—she laughs.

It is not a loud laugh. It is barely more than a breath, a small surprised sound at her own clumsiness, at the absurdity of lying in a puddle staring at the sky. But it rises from her unbidden, and it does not stop. It becomes a giggle, then a true laugh, shaking her small body as the cold seeps in.

Her mother, working nearby, looks up in alarm. Laughter is rare here. Laughter is *loud*. But before she can hush the child, she notices something: the rocks are still weeping. The ground is not shuddering. The terrible consequence they have always feared from noise—it is not coming.

Rivulet's laughter fades, not because she is silenced but because it has run its natural course. She sits up, soaked and grinning, and her mother stares at her as if seeing her for the first time.

That evening, the mother tells the story to her neighbors. She does not know why she tells it—only that it seems important, this small moment when a child laughed and nothing broke.

---

The power works like water: slowly, patiently, finding the cracks.

A young man named Pool, diving in the flooded lower terraces, surfaces with his harvest and finds himself weeping. Not from sorrow or fear but from something older and stranger—an overwhelming sense that he is *seen*. That his labor matters. That the roots he pulls from the cold mud are a form of prayer, and the prayer is heard. He tells no one of this moment, but he carries it with him afterward, a warmth behind his ribs that the damp cannot reach.

An elder named Cracked-Stone, long considered too frail to join the seasonal repairs, wakes one morning with the unshakable conviction that she should teach the children to sing. Not the low stone-songs of ritual but other songs—old ones she half-remembers from her own grandmother, songs with words and melodies, songs that are simply *about things*: a bird that no longer lives here, a color of sunset that the clouds rarely permit, a lover's hands. She does not understand where this conviction comes from, but she obeys it. The children, gathered around her in the dim light of a communal hall, learn to raise their voices above a whisper. The stones do not shudder.

A woman named Morning-Mist, returning from the aqueduct where she has spent hours clearing silt, pauses on the highest terrace and looks out over the plateau below. The land stretches away into gray distance, empty and vast. She has seen this view a thousand times. But today, for no reason she can name, it seems to contain something it did not before: a sense of *welcome*. As if the emptiness is not indifference but space—space for her, for her people, for whatever they might yet become. She stands there until the cold drives her home, and she does not feel alone.

---

The benevolent power does not speak to them. It does not need to speak. It simply *attends*—turns toward them the full weight of its awareness, holds them in its perception the way a parent holds a sleeping child: without demand, without expectation, only with the unshakable knowledge that they are real and that their realness matters.

This attending changes nothing about the land. The weeping rocks still weep. The pools still fill and empty in their ancient rhythm. The terraces remain raw-edged and precarious, and the Stillness remains possible—always possible, never certain, the great unanswered question at the heart of their existence.

But the people begin to change.

Not in their practices—they still whisper, still step carefully, still leave offerings for the Neth. But there is a new quality to their caution: less fear, more *care*. They are quiet not because they dread catastrophe but because they have learned to love the silence. They move slowly not because the land might punish speed but because slowness lets them notice things: the way light moves through the shallow pools, the particular green of the algae after the High Drip, the texture of wet stone under their fingers.

The children still play in hushed tones, but now they also play *games*—chasing each other through the flagstone paths, inventing stories about the shadows in the lower pools, daring each other to make the smallest possible sound that still counts as a sound. They are not afraid of what they might break. They are in love with what they might preserve.

---

Dew-on-Stone does not live to see the full flowering of this change. Her breath fails her three cycles later, on a morning when the clouds hang so low they seem to press against the thatched roofs. But before she goes, she gathers her apprentices around her and tells them something she has never told anyone:

"The rocks listen," she says. "I have known this all my life. But now I know something else. *Something listens to the rocks.* Something larger than the Neth, older than the terraces. It does not speak. It does not command. But it is there, and it knows us, and it—" She pauses, searching for the word. "It *cherishes* us."

Her apprentices exchange uncertain glances. This is not part of the teachings.

"I cannot prove this," Dew-on-Stone continues. "I have no vision, no miracle. Only a feeling. But I ask you to carry it with you, and to pass it on: we are not alone here. We have never been alone. The burden we carry is real, but we do not carry it without witness."

She dies that night, and they bury her in the way of their people: wrapped in woven moss, lowered into a hollow in the rock, covered with water from the weeping stones. The drip continues above her grave, patient and eternal, and if it sounds different to some of them now—less like indifference and more like a lullaby—they do not speak of it.

But they remember.

---

The benevolent power remains.

It does not need to act further. It has done what it came to do, which was not to solve or save or transform but simply to *be present*—to offer the one gift that costs nothing and changes everything: the knowledge that someone is watching, and that the watching is done with love.

The settlement on the terraces will face challenges still. The Stillness may come, or it may not. The aqueduct will clog and be cleared a thousand more times. Children will be born and elders will die and the eternal negotiation between water and stone and flesh will continue, drop by drop, breath by breath.

But the people will carry something with them now, passed down from Dew-on-Stone and from the wordless moments of grace that touched Pool and Cracked-Stone and Morning-Mist: the quiet, unshakable conviction that their existence matters. That the universe, or something in it, has noticed them and found them worthy of attention.

This is not certainty. It is not protection. It is only presence—the presence of something vast and kind, attending to them across the unimaginable distances of whatever separates such powers from such small, brave, persistent lives.

It is enough.

In the silence of the high terraces, where the water drips and the stones weep and the clouds hang low and silver, it is enough.
