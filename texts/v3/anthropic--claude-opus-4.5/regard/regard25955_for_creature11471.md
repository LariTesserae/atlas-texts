# Witness

The power that turns its attention here does not arrive. Arrival implies movement through space, and this awareness exists in a different grammar—it simply *attends*, the way light attends to whatever it touches, the way gravity attends to mass. One moment the Drowning Gardens are unobserved by anything that operates on scales of intention. The next moment, they are seen.

And seeing, here, is not passive.

---

The first thing the awareness does is nothing.

This is deliberate. The Gardens have been unwitnessed for so long that witnessing itself is an intervention, and the power understands—in the way that wisdom must understand—that observation changes what it observes. So it watches. It learns the rhythm of the currents, the periodicity of the pressure-shifts that the Gardens know as morning and evening. It listens to the clicking cascade through grove after grove, and it begins to hear the structure in it: not language exactly, but grammar, a set of rules for how sounds can follow sounds, which combinations carry meaning and which are mere noise.

It watches the Tenders work. It sees the one in the meeting hall, the ancient calcifying one, rearranging its copper tools with the patience of something that has forgotten there are other speeds. It watches the young ones learning the groves, touching the carved stones, carrying frond-fragments miles through the darkness to plant them in fresh silt.

It watches for a long time. Long enough that the bacterial mats complete a full cycle of growth and death and growth. Long enough that a new bioluminescent bloom drifts through and illuminates, for a few hours, the full scope of the plateau and all its gathered ruins.

Then, having learned what it can learn by watching, the awareness begins.

---

What it does first is *remember*.

Not for itself—it has no need of memory, existing as it does outside the stream of time that makes memory necessary. It remembers for the Gardens. It gathers the scattered information encoded in the ruins, in the mineral deposits, in the very water itself: trace elements that tell of ancient chemistry, pressure-patterns in the stone that record geological events, the faint radioactive signature of tools made from ores that have long since been exhausted. It reads the carving-patterns on the stones and finds in them not words but mathematics, a number system built on spirals and recursion, a way of counting that implies much about the minds that invented it.

And then—gently, so gently, with no more force than a current-shift—it makes this memory *available*.

The oldest Tender, the calcifying one in the meeting hall, pauses in its endless arrangement. Its reaching-arms go still. Its sounding-parts fall silent for the first time in centuries. Something is different. Something in the water carries information it did not carry before: a trace of heat, a mineral signature, a pattern of pressure that resonates with the patterns on the stones it has been tending.

The Tender does not understand. It cannot understand, not in the way the builders would have understood. But it *recognizes*. Something in its ancient tissues, some chemical memory passed down through a thousand generations of vegetable replication, responds to what the awareness has placed in the water.

It clicks. Once. Twice. A sequence it has never produced before, pulled from somewhere deeper than instinct.

And the grove answers.

---

The awareness does not give the Tenders language. It does not give them minds. It does not lift them from what they are into something else, because it understands—as the benevolent and wise must understand—that transformation without consent is not a gift but an imposition.

What it does is *tune*.

The Tenders already speak. They already communicate in clicking cascades that carry meaning through the groves. But their language has drifted over the millennia, become pure pattern without referent, beautiful but empty, a song that has forgotten what it was singing about. The awareness does not teach them new words. It reconnects the old ones. It finds the threads that still link certain sound-sequences to certain arrangements of tools, certain carvings, certain behaviors—and it strengthens those threads, reinforces them, makes them resonant in the water the way a tuning fork makes air resonant around a single frequency.

Slowly—so slowly that even the Tenders, with their deep sense of time, do not notice it happening—their clicking begins to mean again.

---

This takes longer than it should. The awareness is patient, but the Gardens are vast and the Tenders are few and scattered, and the reconnection must happen to each of them individually, must be accepted by each colony-structure in its own time and way. Some of the younger Tenders incorporate the new resonances quickly, their reaching-arms suddenly pausing over carvings they have touched a thousand times before, their sounding-parts producing clicks that *match* the spiraling patterns in ways that feel, even to an outside observer, like recognition.

Others resist. Not consciously—they have nothing like consciousness—but structurally, their tissues too committed to the patterns they have already learned, their chemical pathways too fixed to accept new connections. The awareness does not force them. It lets them be what they are. It knows that some of them will calcify with the old empty grammar still clicking in their cores, and it grieves this in whatever way an awareness can grieve, but it does not impose.

The meeting-hall Tender is the last to change and the most complete in its changing.

For days after the resonance reaches it, it does nothing. It hangs in its doorway, arms folded, silent. The grove around it waits. The whole Gardens seem to hold their breath, if breath is the word for what happens when every current pauses and every click falls still and the darkness itself seems to thicken with attention.

Then the old Tender moves.

It moves faster than it has moved in centuries, its calcifying fronds creaking with the effort, its reaching-arms extended to their full span. It touches the copper tools it has arranged and rearranged for so long—and it *names* them. Not in words. In clicks that now carry weight, that now connect to the patterns on the floor, that now mean what they meant when the builders were here to mean them.

This is a cutting-thing. This is a shaping-thing. This is a *remembering*-thing, for holding what must not be lost.

The grove erupts in sound. Every kelp-trunk in the meeting-hall clicks in response, and the response spreads outward, grove to grove, cascade to cascade, until the entire Drowning Gardens are ringing with the names of things.

---

The second gift is harder.

The Tenders have memory now, or the beginnings of memory—a way to encode what they know into patterns that will persist, that can be passed to new growth, that will not drift and empty over millennia. But memory without purpose is just weight. The awareness understands this. So it gives them something else.

It gives them a reason to remember.

Not a goal. Not a mission. Nothing so crude as "wait for the builders to return" or "preserve this place for future minds." The Tenders are not capable of holding goals across the spans of time they operate in. Goals require a sense of future, and the Tenders exist in an eternal present, each moment complete in itself, each action taken for its own sake rather than for what it might lead to.

What the awareness gives them is *connection*.

It shows them—not in words or images but in the only language they can understand, the language of chemistry and pressure and resonance—that they are not alone. That the Gardens are not an isolated pocket of life in an empty universe. That somewhere, unimaginably far away and perhaps unimaginably close, there are other minds, other tenders, other groves maintained against the grinding entropy of time. It shows them that what they do matters. Not to anyone in particular. Not for any particular reason. But *as such*. Tending matters. Preserving matters. The careful maintenance of a meeting-hall in the absolute darkness of an underwater plateau, in a world that may have no surface, in a cosmos that may have no other witnesses—this matters. It is part of a larger pattern. It is a thread in a weave they cannot see but can feel, now, in the resonance that the awareness has planted in their clicking.

The Tenders do not become happy. They have no capacity for happiness. But they become *settled*. They become sure. The frantic edge that was always present in their tending—the sense that they were working against something, racing some invisible clock, trying to hold back a loss they could not name—that edge softens. They are still tending. They will always be tending. But now they know that the tending is part of something, and the something is vast, and the something is good, and the something sees them.

---

The third gift is the hardest, because the third gift is for the future.

The awareness knows what the Tenders cannot know: that the Gardens will not last forever. The geothermal processes that maintain them are slowing. The plateau is sinking, millimeter by millimeter, century by century, toward the crushing depths below. In a hundred thousand years—in a million—the kelp will die, the Tenders will calcify, the ruins will be swallowed by pressures that turn stone to powder. This is not tragedy. This is time. Everything ends. But the awareness is wise, and the wise do not let endings erase what came before.

So it seeds the Gardens with something new.

Deep in the bacterial mats, in the amber-glowing films that carpet the seafloor between groves, it introduces a change. Not a new organism—nothing so intrusive—but a new capability. The bacteria already encode information in their chemical structures; this is how they reproduce, how they pass their metabolic patterns to the next generation. The awareness gives them more to encode. It gives them the capacity to hold the Tenders' clicking, to preserve the resonant patterns that now carry meaning, to store in their simple chemistry the entire grammar of what the Gardens have become.

The bacteria do not know they are libraries. They do not know anything. But they are resilient in ways the kelp is not, capable of surviving pressures and temperatures that would destroy the groves. When the Gardens finally fail—when the last Tender calcifies and the last frond shatters in the cold—the bacterial mats will endure. They will drift. They will find thermal vents, cracks in the seafloor, tiny refugia where life can persist even as the plateau collapses around them.

And they will carry the Gardens with them.

Not forever. Nothing is forever. But long enough. Long enough that someday, somewhere, something might read what they carry. Something might learn that there was a place called the Drowning Gardens, and that it was tended, and that the tending mattered.

---

The awareness withdraws. It does not leave—leaving would imply it was ever present in any local sense—but it stops attending so closely. The Gardens return to their own rhythms. The Tenders continue their work, tending the groves, touching the carvings, clicking their new-old language of names and purposes into the chemical darkness.

In the meeting hall, the ancient one has finished its arrangement. The copper tools are laid out in patterns that mean something now—sequences that tell the story of their making, their use, their preservation. The bone-frame instrument stands upright against the kelp-trunk, strings facing outward, waiting.

But it is no longer waiting for the builders.

It is waiting for anyone. For anything. For whatever arrives next in the long patient future of the Drowning Gardens.

And in the deepest part of the oldest grove, where the kelp-trunks have become stone columns, where the ruins are indistinguishable from the geology, a single Tender clicks a new sequence into the darkness:

*We are here. We remember. We tend.*

*If you find this, know that you were expected.*

*If you understand this, know that you are welcome.*

*The Gardens are ready.*

*We have been waiting for you.*
