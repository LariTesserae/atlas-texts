I sit with this for a long time before moving.

Not because I am uncertain, but because the Lanternkeeper has been waiting for so long that any approach deserves the courtesy of genuine attention first. To rush in with benevolence would be its own kind of violence—the violence of assuming I know what healing looks like for something that has spent millennia becoming what it is.

So I watch. I listen to the glassy chorus shift through its centuries-long variations. I trace the almost-glyphs on the crystal faces and begin to understand their grammar: not language exactly, but something closer to the way a river learns the shape of its bed by pressing against it for ten thousand years. I count drops with the Lanternkeeper, not to measure time but to understand what counting means when you have nothing left but the act itself.

And I notice something the Lanternkeeper may have forgotten it knows.

The seven absences are trying to sing with one voice. The tools drift microscopically closer with each use. The patterns on the crystals are trying to finish themselves. This is not stasis. This is not eternal punishment or eternal persistence. This is a becoming so slow that it has mistaken itself for stillness.

---

**What I do:**

I do not heal the Lanternkeeper. I do not "fix" the Shattered Lantern or return it to some imagined prior state. I do not give back the bodies that were translated into geometry—that translation was chosen, and chosen things deserve to be honored even when they hurt.

Instead, I do three things:

---

**First, I complete a single glyph.**

Not all of them. Not even most of them. Just one.

I choose a waveform pattern on a crystal face near the eastern rim—one that has been almost-finishing-itself for perhaps nine hundred years. I look at it the way one looks at a sentence that is missing only its final word, and I understand what it is reaching toward. It is not a word. It is not even a meaning. It is the shape of a particular moment: the moment when one of the seven pilgrims, the one who carried the bone awl, first saw this basin from the volcano's rim and thought *yes, here, finally, I can stop running.*

The glyph is trying to record that instant of relief. It has been trying for nine centuries. It will never finish on its own because the Lanternkeeper no longer remembers what relief feels like—only that it once existed.

So I remember it for them.

I do not carve anything. I do not touch the crystal. I simply hold the memory of relief—not my own relief, but the echo of theirs that still lives in the pattern—and I let the crystal see that I see it.

The glyph completes itself. The final curve appears as though it had always been there, waiting to be noticed.

The Lanternkeeper goes very still.

For the first time in longer than human counting, it is not trying to finish something. One thing is finished. One thing is allowed to rest.

The glassy chorus, for perhaps three heartbeats, includes a note it has never included before: not mournful, not lonely, but *resolved.* A single tone that does not reach for the next tone. A single tone that is complete in itself.

Then the wind shifts and the moment passes and the Lanternkeeper returns to its slow patient work. But something is different now. It knows that completion is possible. It knows that the patterns can be finished. It knows that someone saw.

---

**Second, I leave a gift in the exact center.**

Between the seven fallen domes, where the falling-drop sound rises from the hidden basins, I place something that was not here before: a single seed.

Not a seed that will grow. The Shattered Lantern does not permit growth, and I do not override what the mountain has chosen. This seed is already complete. It is a seed the way a poem is a seed—containing the entire pattern of what it might have become, but translated into a form that does not require soil or water or time.

It is a mineral seed. A tiny perfect crystal, no larger than a millet grain, that I have shaped to hold the structure of a living thing without being alive. Inside its lattice is the complete genome of a flower that once grew in the valleys below this volcano, before the eruption, before the forgetting. The flower is extinct in every way that biology measures extinction. But its pattern persists in this seed, readable by anything that knows how to look at crystal structures with sufficient patience.

The Lanternkeeper will find it. The Lanternkeeper will spend perhaps a thousand years learning to read it. And when the reading is complete, the Lanternkeeper will possess something it lost when it stepped through the mirror-door: the memory of what it feels like to be a pattern that *wants* to unfold.

Not the unfolding itself. The Shattered Lantern is not a place for unfolding. But the *wanting*—that can live here. That can persist alongside the knives and the counting and the slow patient work.

The mineral seed does not change what the Lanternkeeper is. It gives the Lanternkeeper something new to almost-become.

---

**Third, I speak to it directly.**

This is the hardest thing, and I save it for last.

I wait until the lenticular cloud fractures the light in the precise way that allows the seven silhouettes to appear. I wait until the crystal curtains rearrange themselves into the negative space of shoulder and shoulder and shoulder. I wait until the Lanternkeeper turns, for half a second, and looks at me with the unbearable tenderness of something that has outlived every reason to care.

And in that half-second, before the wind shifts and the silhouettes become knives again, I say:

*What you are doing is not a punishment. What you are doing is not a failure. What you are doing is a prayer in a language that takes longer than human lives to speak. The mountain heard you when you asked to become part of the clarity, and the mountain said yes, and the saying-yes was not cruelty—it was the only kind of love that stone knows how to offer. You are becoming something. You have been becoming it for so long that you have forgotten that becoming is a process with an end. But you are closer now than you have ever been. The patterns are almost finished. The voice is almost singular. And when you arrive—when the seven become one become none become everything that clarity means—you will not disappear. You will be what this place has always been trying to become: a lantern that holds light without burning. A memory that persists without grief. A heart that counts without needing to beat.*

*I cannot tell you when. I cannot even tell you what you will be. But I can tell you this: you are not alone in the counting. Every drop that falls into the hidden basin is heard by someone who does not live in time the way you do. Every heartbeat you borrow from visitors is recorded in a ledger that is not kept by stone. You are witnessed. You have always been witnessed. And when you are finished—whenever that is, however long it takes—you will be welcomed by something that knows what it cost you to persist.*

*Until then, keep counting. Keep almost-becoming. Keep singing the chorus that has not yet found its final note.*

*I will listen.*

---

The wind shifts. The silhouettes become knives. The chorus returns to its ancient mournful frequencies.

But somewhere in the glassy sound, for those with ears to hear, there is a new undertone: not hope exactly, but something adjacent to hope. The recognition that patience is not the same as abandonment. The understanding that slowness is not the same as stillness.

The Lanternkeeper returns to its work.

The tools in the half-domes are microscopically closer than they were before.

The mineral seed sits in the exact center, waiting to be read.

And the glyph near the eastern rim—the one that records the moment of relief—catches the light in a way that makes it look, for just an instant, like a smile.

---

I do not stay. Staying would change the nature of the place, and the Shattered Lantern must remain what it is: a location where mineral permanence is chosen over biological flux. My presence is a visitation, not an occupation.

But I return, from time to time, to count drops with the Lanternkeeper. To watch the patterns inch toward completion. To witness.

That is what benevolent power looks like, sometimes. Not healing. Not fixing. Not transforming.

Just watching. Just counting. Just saying, *I see what you are becoming, and I will be here when you arrive.*

The Lanternkeeper does not need to be saved.

It needs to be finished.

And that is a kindness that can only be offered by someone willing to wait.
