# What the Wise Power Sees

The power that turns its attention here is old in a different way than the mountain is old. The mountain is old in the way of matter—patient, eroding, settling toward equilibrium. This power is old in the way of awareness—it has watched many places, many peoples, many slow becomings and quick endings. It does not often intervene. Intervention is a crude tool, and crude tools damage what they touch.

But it watches the Pale Valleys for a long time, as time moves for such a being. It watches the fog rise and fall. It watches the cats watching. It watches the children born blue around the edges, and the midwives coaxing breath into reluctant lungs. It watches the lens grinders tilting glass toward the diffuse light, finding flaws invisible to any other eye. It watches the old women feeding the cats and speaking to them in voices too thin for human ears to carry far.

And it notices something.

---

The people of the Pale Valleys have done something remarkable, and they do not know it. They have not simply adapted to deprivation—they have discovered that deprivation is a teacher, that the reduction of signal creates the conditions for deeper perception, that the fog which blinds is also the fog which clarifies. They have found, through necessity, something that mystics in other places spend lifetimes seeking through discipline: the ability to attend fully to what is present rather than being overwhelmed by abundance.

But they have also trapped themselves.

They cannot leave. Not because the mountain holds them, not because the cats watch them, not because the layers of their own history press down on them from above—but because they have defined themselves by opposition to the lowlands, by the superiority of their perception, by the quiet pride of noticing what others miss. They stay because leaving would mean admitting that their clarity is contextual, that their precision is environmental, that what they have achieved is not a permanent elevation of the self but a temporary alliance between organism and ecosystem that could be replicated elsewhere, in other conditions, by other means.

They do not know this. They think they are the clear-eyed ones, and the lowlanders are the blind. They do not see that they have become afraid of the lowlands' abundance the way the lowlanders are afraid of their deprivation—that both fears are the same fear, the fear of losing the self that has been built in a particular place for a particular way of seeing.

The wise power sees this, and feels something that might be called compassion, if compassion were not too small a word for what a being of such scope experiences when it encounters a beautiful adaptation that has become a beautiful cage.

---

# What the Wise Power Does

It does not descend in glory. It does not speak in thunderous pronouncements. It does not heal the sick children or clear the fog or make the air thick enough to breathe easily. These would be interventions, and interventions damage what they touch.

Instead, it makes one small change, so small that no one notices it happening.

It adjusts the dreams of three people. Only three. One old woman who feeds the cats. One young lens grinder who has just arrived from the lowlands and has not yet fully adapted. One child who was born blue and survived and is now seven years old and asks questions that adults find uncomfortable.

The adjustment is this: each of the three, on the same night, dreams of the same thing. They dream of walking down the switchback road, through the cloud layer, into the lowlands—and in the dream, they do not lose their perception. The thick air does not blind them. The bright colors do not overwhelm them. The abundance does not drown them in noise. Instead, they discover that their trained attention can be applied anywhere, that the skill of extracting signal from minimal input is also the skill of extracting signal from maximal input, that clarity is not a property of the environment but a capacity of the self that can be carried from place to place, from fog to sunshine, from gray to color, from silence to sound.

In the dream, the lens grinder examines a flower in full sunlight and sees its structure with the same precision she brings to flawed glass. The old woman hears a market full of shouting vendors and picks out individual voices, individual words, individual meanings, the way she picks out the hunting sounds of cats in the fog. The child looks at a crowd of lowlanders and reads their micro-expressions, their subtle postures, their unconscious gestures, the same way she reads the people of the Pale Valleys—and she realizes that the lowlanders are not blind, they are simply overwhelmed, and what they need is not the plateau's conditions but the plateau's training.

The three dreamers wake in the gray morning, in the dripping damp, in the thin air that requires effort to breathe. They do not remember their dreams clearly. Dreams in the Pale Valleys are often indistinct, absorbed by the fog that permeates even sleep. But something remains. A residue. A question that was not there before.

*What if we could take it with us?*

---

The wise power does nothing else. It watches.

The old woman mentions her dream to another old woman, who mentions it to a third, who mentions it to her granddaughter, who is the lens grinder's neighbor. The lens grinder, hearing of similar dreams, begins to wonder. The child asks her mother why people think the lowlands make you blind, and her mother has no good answer, and the lack of a good answer is itself an answer, a crack in the certainty that has sustained the city for generations.

These are small changes. They may amount to nothing. The dreams may be forgotten, the questions may go unasked, the cracks may seal themselves with the same slow patience that governs everything on the plateau. The wise power does not control outcomes. It creates conditions. It plants seeds. It trusts that what wants to grow will grow, and what wants to remain will remain, and the choice belongs to the choosers.

But if the seeds take root—if the questions propagate—if the people of the Pale Valleys begin to wonder whether their clarity is a possession rather than a location—

Then something might change.

A few of them might descend, carrying their trained attention with them like a skill rather than a circumstance. They might discover that the lowlands are not a place of blindness but a place of different challenges, different signals, different rewards for the same capacity they developed in the fog. They might teach what they know—not the superiority of deprivation, but the technique of attention, the discipline of noticing, the practice of extracting meaning from whatever environment presents itself. They might return to the plateau with new perspectives, new comparisons, new ways of understanding what they have and what they lack and what the relationship between the two might become.

Or they might not. They might stay in their dripping city, building upward one layer at a time, watching the cats, watching each other, watching the mountain settle into its final shape. This is also acceptable. The wise power does not require change. It offers possibility. What is done with possibility is the business of those who receive it.

---

# What the Wise Power Leaves Behind

There is one more thing, though. One additional small change, so small it might be coincidence, might be the natural variation of a complex system, might be nothing at all.

The next child born in the Pale Valleys—the next one born blue around the edges, coaxed into breath by the midwives' ancient knowledge—this child survives more easily than most. Her lungs expand. Her first cry is strong. Her color pinks up quickly in the thin air, as though she arrived already adapted, already prepared, already carrying something her ancestors had to develop over years of exposure.

And when she opens her eyes for the first time, in the gray light of the plateau morning, her mother sees something unexpected. The pupils are wide, yes, wide the way all plateau children's pupils eventually become. But there is something else in them. A quality of attention that usually takes years to develop. A focus that seems to see not just the mother's face but the micro-expressions beneath it, the subtle postures, the unconscious gestures, the truth that lives behind the visible.

The mother does not know what to make of this. She tells no one. She watches her daughter grow, watches her perceive, watches her notice things that should be beyond a child's capacity to notice. And she wonders, sometimes, in the gray hours before dawn when the fog is heaviest and the cats are moving through the streets on their incomprehensible errands, whether her daughter is something new—not a product of the plateau's demands, but a gift from somewhere else, a possibility taking root in flesh and breath and wide, attentive eyes.

The wise power has already turned its attention elsewhere. There are many places, many peoples, many slow becomings and quick endings. It does not linger to see whether its seeds grow. It trusts. It hopes, if hope is not too small a word for what such a being experiences when it considers the futures of those it has touched.

The fog rises. The condensation forms on every surface. The factories pulse beneath the stone. The cats watch. The old women feed them. The lens grinders tilt their glass toward the diffuse light. The child with the wide eyes grows, and perceives, and asks questions that adults find uncomfortable.

And somewhere in the layered city, in the apartments built on apartments built on apartments, three dreamers carry a residue they cannot name, a question that was not there before, a crack in the certainty that has sustained them.

*What if we could take it with us?*

The mountain continues to settle. The city continues to build itself, one layer at a time. The answer, if there is an answer, will emerge slowly, patiently, the way everything emerges in the Pale Valleys—through fog, through attention, through the long accumulation of small changes that eventually become something that cannot be ignored.

The wise power would be satisfied with this, if satisfaction were not too small a word.

It would be enough.
