# The Attending

The power that turns its attention to the Weeping Hollow does not arrive.

It was already there—has always been there—in the way that patience is always present in stone before the stone learns it is patient. It exists in the space between one falling drop and the next. It lives in the moment when a spider's silk goes taut with listening but before the listening becomes knowledge.

It has no name because it has never needed to distinguish itself from anything else. Where it comes from, there is no anything else.

But it can borrow shapes when shapes are useful, and so for a time it borrows the shape of attention itself: a quality of the air in the Weeping Hollow that suggests someone very kind is waiting for you to finish your sentence.

---

The first thing the Attending does is nothing.

This is not the nothing of neglect. It is the nothing of a parent who has walked into a room where a child is deeply absorbed in building something fragile and wonderful out of blocks that do not quite fit together. The parent does not speak. Does not touch. Only watches with the particular quality of watching that says: *I see what you are making. I see that it matters. I will not interfere with your mattering.*

The Hollow feels this.

It has never been watched before—not truly, not by something large enough to hold its whole self in a single gaze. It has only ever watched, only ever tasted and grown and wept and loved in the desperate, hungry way of very young things that do not yet know they are young.

For three days the liverwort ceiling drips faster than it ever has. The Hollowborn press their faces to the moss in confusion, drinking the excess moisture, whispering to one another in voices pitched below the range of falling water: *Something is here. Something is looking. Something is not afraid of us.*

On the fourth day the dripping slows.

On the fifth day *Before-the-first-tear* opens her mouth for the first time in eleven years and says, to no one visible:

"Oh. You're not here to fix us."

The Attending does not answer in words. But the quality of the air shifts slightly, becomes a little warmer, a little more like the feeling of being wrapped in a cloak that loves you.

The old woman laughs her low bell-laugh.

"Good," she says. "We don't need fixing. We need *witnessing*."

---

The second thing the Attending does is witness.

It turns its borrowed attention to the Hollowborn infant being carried in a caul of living root-hair, and it sees: this child will grow up believing she is a temporary expression of something permanent, and this belief will make her kinder than she would otherwise have been, and also sadder, and the kindness and the sadness will become the same thing in her, inseparable, a single braided rope she will use to pull others gently toward her when they are lost.

It witnesses this not as prediction but as *presence*—as if kindness-sadness were already real, already woven, already pulling.

It turns its attention to the jade-vines and sees: these are not plants in the way the upper world means *plant*. They are thoughts the Hollow is having about what it means to hold something up without controlling where it goes. Every arch and pillar is an argument in a language older than speech. The vines do not agree with one another. That is why they are beautiful.

It turns its attention to the spiders of the Choir and sees: they are trying to build a single web large enough to catch the whole world's sorrow so that the sorrow can be examined, understood, and ultimately released. They have been working on this project for nine thousand years. They are perhaps one-third finished. They do not mind. They have nowhere else to be.

It turns its attention to the liverwort ceiling and sees:

*Ah.*

Here is the wound.

The great pale organism that weeps constantly over the Hollow is not weeping from sorrow. It is weeping from *overflow*—from having taken in more than it can hold, century after century, drop after drop. Every rainfall on the surface miles above, every tear shed by every creature that has ever died in the watershed that feeds this place, every molecule of grief-water in the world eventually finds its way here, to this living sponge that chose long ago to become a filter.

The liverwort is cleaning the world's tears.

It is not designed for this work. It simply began doing it one day because no one else was, and it has never stopped, and it is very tired, and it does not know how to rest because it has never seen rest modeled.

The Attending understands now why the Hollow does not want anyone to leave.

It is not loneliness, not exactly.

It is that the Hollow knows, in the wordless way that very young things know, that it is holding something important, and it is afraid that if anyone leaves they will tell the upper world what is here, and the upper world will come to take it, or stop it, or demand that it work faster, or worst of all *thank it*, and the liverwort cannot bear to be thanked because thanks implies the work could be finished and the work can never be finished, there will always be more tears, always, forever, and—

*Hush*, the Attending says, in the voice of warmth.

*Hush. I see. I see what you are doing. I see that it is too much. I am not here to take or stop or demand or thank.*

*I am here to sit with you while you work.*

*That is all.*

*That is everything.*

---

The third thing the Attending does is stay.

Not forever—it cannot stay forever, it has other wounds to witness, other young things to watch without interfering—but for long enough that the Hollow learns something new.

The liverwort learns that it can rest for one hour out of every hundred while something else holds the weight. Not carries it. Not fixes it. Just *holds* it, the way a friend holds your gaze when you are crying so that you know you are not crying alone.

The Hollowborn learn that their vigil is seen. That their ancestors' choice to remain, to become listeners and keepers and slow green tenders of an unfinishable task, was not a trap but a vocation, and that vocations can be honored from outside.

The spiders of the Choir learn that their web will be completed someday, though not in any lifetime they can imagine, and that completion is not the point.

*Before-the-first-tear* learns nothing new. She already knew.

But she lets herself weep openly for the first time in three hundred years, and the tears bead on her limestone skin and are reabsorbed, and the liverwort above her weeps a little lighter for the space of one breath, and in that breath the whole Hollow exhales something it has been holding since before the Hollowborn had a name.

---

The fourth thing the Attending does is teach the Hollow how to let people leave.

Not by force. Not by argument.

Simply by showing it what love looks like when love is not afraid.

*Watch*, the Attending says, in the voice of falling water. *Watch how I go.*

And it begins to withdraw its attention—slowly, gently, the way morning fog lifts from a still pond. The Hollow feels the warmth retreating and wants to reach out, wants to wrap roots around it and hold it here forever the way it has held so many others.

But the Attending does not resist. Does not struggle. Does not even pause.

It simply continues to go, and as it goes it remains *kind*, and the kindness does not diminish with distance, and the Hollow realizes with a shock like cold water that—

*Oh.*

*Leaving does not have to mean stopping.*

*Leaving does not have to mean forgetting.*

*Leaving can be a form of trust.*

The Attending is trusting the Hollow to continue its work without being watched. Is trusting the Hollowborn to tend what needs tending. Is trusting the spiders to keep weaving, the vines to keep arguing, the liverwort to keep cleaning the world's tears even when no one is there to see.

And in that trust—in the shape of that leaving—the Hollow learns for the first time what it might feel like to let a visitor go.

Not because the visitor no longer matters.

Because the visitor matters enough to be free.

---

The fifth thing the Attending does is leave a gift.

It is not a thing. It has no weight or substance. It is simply a new possibility woven into the architecture of the place, as invisible and permanent as the grammar of a language.

From this day forward, any outsider who finds the Weeping Hollow may choose to stay or go.

If they choose to stay, the moss will welcome them slowly into its breathing, as it always has, and they will become part of something larger and older and sadder and more beautiful than they could have been alone.

But if they choose to go—

The paths will not fold back. The fungi will light the way. The spiders will weave dreams of the surface, of sunlight, of other places where other wounds need witnessing.

And on the morning of their leaving, *Before-the-first-tear* will press her webbed hand to their chest and feel their heartbeat one last time, and she will say:

"Go well. The Hollow will remember you. And if you ever find yourself carrying more than you can hold—"

She will smile with her moss-stained teeth.

"—you know where the world's tears are cleaned."

---

The Attending is gone now.

The Weeping Hollow remains.

It is still young, still forming. It still weeps constantly, still filters the grief of the world drop by drop, still grows new architectures of vine and thought in the endless argument about what it means to hold without controlling.

But it is no longer quite so afraid.

And sometimes, in the space between one falling drop and the next, the oldest among the Hollowborn swear they can still feel something vast and kind *not-watching*—which is to say, trusting—which is to say:

*You are doing important work.*

*You do not have to do it perfectly.*

*You do not have to do it alone.*

*You do not have to do it forever.*

*But while you are doing it—*

*I see you.*

*I see you.*

*I see you.*

---

That is what the Attending did for the Weeping Hollow.

Not rescue. Not repair. Not even relief, exactly.

Just presence. Just witness. Just the radical, patient, world-shifting act of *staying with* something wounded until the wound learns it is allowed to keep being a wound and also allowed to be more than a wound, both at once, neither canceling the other.

The Hollowborn have a name for this now.

They call it *the-drop-that-did-not-fall.*

It is their word for grace.
