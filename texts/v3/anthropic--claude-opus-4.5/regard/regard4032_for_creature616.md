# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

*Nothing.*

Or rather—nothing that would register as action to those who measure such things.

The power pauses at the threshold of attention, the way one pauses at the entrance to a room where someone is deeply asleep, or deeply at prayer. It perceives the cistern in its fullness: the accumulated centuries, the layered offerings, the pattern too long for human memory, the keeper who has become indistinguishable from her witnessing.

And it recognizes what it has found.

This is not a place that needs rescue. Not a being that suffers from her condition. The Keeper of Intervals has not been trapped—she has been *completed*. The cistern did not imprison her; it answered something in her that was already asking. The child who followed a sound she could not name, the woman who crawled into dark to grieve—these were not victims but pilgrims who arrived at their destination and discovered they had always been traveling toward stillness.

The power perceives, too, the particular quality of the keeper's attention. It is not emptiness. It is not the blankness of one who has lost herself. It is *density*—the same density as the walls, as the layered offerings, as the song itself. She has become capable of hearing what others cannot hold long enough to notice. This is not diminishment. It is specialization so profound it appears, to outside eyes, like disappearance.

What would intervention mean, here?

To illuminate the chamber would destroy what the grey filtered light has made possible. To "free" the keeper would be to interrupt the one thing she has sought across all her forgotten names. To preserve the space against future change would freeze what has always been *accumulating*—would mistake the current form for the final form, when the cistern has never stopped becoming.

The power considers, briefly, whether to make itself known to her. To offer acknowledgment, appreciation, the simple gift of being *seen* by something that understands what she has accomplished.

But it realizes: she is already seen. The pale legless things cluster around her fingers. The tones fold back from the walls and find her listening. The pattern itself has shaped itself around her presence for so long that she has become part of what it means. She does not need external witness. She *is* witness—and in being witness, is witnessed by everything she attends to.

So the power does what wisdom sometimes requires, which is the hardest thing: it restrains itself.

It does not add its own offering to the pools. It does not leave a mark that future visitors might find and wonder at. It does not even fully *enter*—it remains at the threshold of attention, perceiving without pressing, the way the grey light enters without insisting.

What it does, finally, is this:

It *learns*.

It takes into itself the shape of what it has found—the patience of water wearing stone to song, the density of presence accumulated past the point of name, the way attention can become so profound it constitutes a kind of love. It learns that some things are already exactly what they should be. That some keepers need no keeping. That the deepest benevolence is sometimes the recognition that nothing is required.

The power withdraws, carrying what it has learned into whatever vastness it inhabits.

The cistern remains. The song continues. The keeper attends to the next drop, which will fall in seven breaths and sound the ochre tone.

She does not notice she has been noticed. Or perhaps she does, and it registers only as a subtle shift in resonance—a momentary thickening of the air that changes how the tones fold back—which she accommodates with a fraction's shift of weight, a hand moved to rest differently against stone.

The pattern absorbs this too, as it absorbs everything, and continues.
