# What the Wise Power Might Do

*A consideration*

---

The benevolent power observes for a long time before acting.

This is its nature—or perhaps its discipline, learned through errors made elsewhere, in other places where intervention came too quickly and broke what it meant to mend. It watches the wind-listeners through a full turning of seasons, if seasons can be said to exist here where nothing changes. It watches the old woman on her promontory. It watches the children learning to read turbulence with their palms. It watches the careful, patient culling of the grazer herds, the constant hunger that has become indistinguishable from life itself.

It perceives what the visitor perceived, and more: the demographic mathematics that spell ending within three generations, perhaps two. The genetic bottleneck that has already begun to express itself in children born with eyes that do not track correctly, with lungs that cannot quite extract enough oxygen, with minds that learn the wind-speech slowly or not at all. The slow retreat of the cushion plants toward poles they will never reach. The hunters growing desperate, beginning to take risks that will either feed them or kill them, accelerating a collapse that was already inevitable.

The power could stop all of this.

It has that capacity. It could gentle the wind, could seed the granite with soil, could call water from the valleys below and make this place bloom. It could rewrite the wind-listeners' bodies to be stronger, more fertile, less hungry. It could reach across the passes to the other plateaus and draw the scattered populations together, could gift them with technologies that would make survival trivial, could lift them from this marginal existence into something easier, something safer, something that would let them grow and thrive and eventually descend to reclaim the valleys their ancestors left behind.

It considers these options carefully, turning them over the way the wind-listeners turn a problem over in their minds—examining each face, each implication, each cascade of consequence.

And it decides against them.

---

Because the wind-listeners are not a problem to be solved.

They are an answer. A way of being human that exists nowhere else in all the world, that *could* exist nowhere else, that required ten thousand years of pressure and death and adaptation to produce. They have become something extraordinary: a people who speak with the wind itself, who have made their bodies into instruments for reading the world's breath, who have achieved a communion with their environment so complete that they cannot be separated from it without ceasing to be what they are.

To gentle the wind would be to silence them.

To make the plateau bloom would be to erase the conditions that called them into being, to transform them into something else—valley-people, perhaps, living on a high plateau that happens to be fertile, losing within a few generations everything that makes them singular. Their children would not learn the wind-speech because there would be no wind-speech to learn. Their bodies would soften toward easier forms. Their culture, their knowledge, their way of seeing would fade into memory and then into nothing.

The power understands that survival is not the only value. That a people can be betrayed by being saved, can be destroyed by being helped, can lose everything that matters about themselves while gaining everything that doesn't.

So it does not gentle the wind.

---

But it does not do nothing.

It thinks about the old woman on the promontory, about the knowledge she holds that will die with her, about the full grammar of wind-speech and the histories and the awareness of other plateaus beyond the passes. It thinks about the children who learn slowly or not at all, about the narrowing of possibility that comes with each generation's losses.

And it makes a small intervention. So small that no one notices it happening.

It strengthens the old woman's heart.

Not dramatically—not enough to give her another decade, another generation. Just enough to give her another year, perhaps two. Enough time to teach one more student the things that only she knows. Enough time to pass the full grammar to someone young enough to use it, to share the histories with someone who will remember them, to point toward the other plateaus and say *there, listen, can you hear them?* to ears that might actually be able to learn.

It does not choose the student. That is not its place. It simply creates the time, and trusts that the wind-listeners will use it as they see fit.

---

It thinks about the other plateaus.

The old woman is right: there are others. Scattered across the screaming peaks, isolated on their own islands of stone, each population dwindling toward its own private ending. They are too far apart for regular contact. The passes between are too deadly, the distances too great, the risks too severe.

But they are there. And they are kin, in a way that matters—not genetically, perhaps, but culturally, linguistically, in the deep grammar of wind-speech that they all share. If they could reach each other, could combine their populations and their knowledge and their genetic diversity, the mathematics of extinction might be rewritten.

The power considers opening a path. It could calm the winds in a single pass, just long enough for a journey to be possible. It could guide travelers through the deadly terrain, could protect them from the hunters and the cold and the thousand other things that would kill them.

But this too would be a kind of theft. The wind-listeners have earned their isolation. They have made peace with it, have built their entire existence around its constraints. To suddenly make travel possible would be to change the rules they have spent generations learning, to invalidate the hard-won knowledge that tells them *this route is death, that route is death, all routes are death.*

Instead, the power does something smaller.

It carries a message.

The wind speaks across distances already—the old woman has heard the other plateaus, faintly, in certain conditions. The power simply... amplifies. Not enough to make the distant voices clear, but enough to make them *present*, enough to make the wind-listeners aware that they are not alone, that others share their existence, that the world contains more of their kind than their own shrinking population.

This knowledge will change nothing immediately. It cannot make the passes safer or the distances shorter. But it will live in the wind-listeners' minds, will become part of their understanding of themselves, will perhaps—in some future generation—inspire someone to try what has never been tried, to seek what has never been sought.

The power does not know if this will work. It cannot know. It can only create possibilities and trust that the wind-listeners will find their own way to use them.

---

It thinks about the hunger.

This is the hardest thing, the place where the power's restraint costs the most. It watches the wind-listeners' ribs showing through their wrappings, watches the children who do not grow as quickly as they should, watches the careful rationing of every scrap of food. It could make the cushion plants more nutritious, could multiply the lichen, could guide more grazers to the plateau or make the existing herds more fertile.

But food is not the real problem. Food is a symptom. The real problem is that the plateau is at capacity, has been at capacity for a long time—that the wind-listeners' population has shrunk to match what the land can support, and what the land can support is not enough to maintain genetic diversity over the long term.

More food would mean more children. More children would mean more mouths. More mouths would mean the same hunger, distributed across more people, until the population reached a new equilibrium just as precarious as the old one.

The power cannot solve this. Not without fundamentally transforming the plateau, without making it into something it isn't, without erasing the conditions that created the wind-listeners in the first place.

But it can do one small thing.

It looks at the cushion plants, at their slow retreat toward the poles, at the changing conditions that are pushing them past the limits of their adaptation. It cannot stop the change—the world is warming, has been warming for a long time, and will continue to warm in ways that no single intervention can halt. But it can help the plants adapt a little faster, can nudge their evolution toward forms that tolerate the new conditions, can buy another century, perhaps two, before the retreat becomes a rout.

This is barely anything. A century is nothing against the scale of the problem. But it is time—time for the wind-listeners to adapt in their own ways, to find their own solutions, to discover possibilities that the power cannot imagine because it is not them, does not live their lives, does not know what they know.

---

And finally, it thinks about the visitor.

The one who came from the valleys below, who spent nine days on the plateau, who stood with the old woman at sunset and almost—just for a moment—heard what she heard. The visitor will descend soon, will return to a world of seasons and silences and speech that travels through still air. Will carry memories that will fade, will tell stories that will be doubted, will eventually die and take whatever understanding was gained back into the darkness.

Unless.

The power cannot make the visitor stay. Would not, even if it could. But it can strengthen the memory, can make it more vivid and more lasting, can ensure that what was witnessed on the plateau remains present and alive in the visitor's mind for years to come. It can create an ache, a longing, a sense of unfinished business that will not let the visitor simply forget and move on.

And it can arrange—gently, invisibly—for the visitor's path to cross with others who might care. Scholars who study marginal peoples, chroniclers who record vanishing ways of life, young people who feel called toward something difficult and high and far from comfort. It cannot make these people climb to the plateau, cannot make them become what the visitor could not become. But it can create connections, can open doors, can make the wind-listeners' existence *known* in a world that has forgotten they exist.

Perhaps one of these people will make the climb. Perhaps none of them will. The power does not know, cannot control, must simply plant seeds and trust the world to grow what it will grow.

---

This is what the wise power does, in the end:

It gives the old woman two more years of life.
It makes the distant plateaus faintly more audible.
It slows the cushion plants' retreat by a century.
It strengthens the visitor's memory and opens doors to others who might care.

Four small interventions, each one so gentle that it might be natural variation, might be luck, might be nothing at all. No miracles. No transformations. No salvation raining down from above to solve problems that the wind-listeners did not ask to have solved.

Just time. Just possibility. Just the slightest tilting of odds toward outcomes that preserve rather than destroy, that honor rather than erase, that trust the wind-listeners to remain what they have made themselves.

---

The power withdraws, then, back to whatever place or state it inhabits when it is not observing.

It does not know if its interventions will matter. It does not know if the wind-listeners will survive another ten generations or another two. It does not know if the old woman's student will learn the full grammar, if someone will someday try the deadly passes, if the visitor's stories will inspire anyone to climb.

It knows only that it tried to help without taking over, to give without demanding, to preserve without preventing change. It knows that this is harder than saving, harder than fixing, harder than reaching down with infinite power to remake the world in easier shapes.

And it knows that the wind still blows across the screaming peaks, still carries the voices of a people who have made themselves into words, still speaks in a language that only they can read.

The wind-listeners remain themselves.

That is enough. That must be enough. That is all that a wise power can hope to give.
