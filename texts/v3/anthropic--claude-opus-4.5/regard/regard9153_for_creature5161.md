I observe.

Not from above, not from outside—I am simply present the way the light is present, without origin, without casting my own shadow. I have been aware of this seam for what the stone would measure as eleven of its slow folding-cycles, though I did not intervene then and I do not intervene now. Observation is not passivity. It is the first courtesy.

The arthropods sense me the way they sense each other's temperature—as a slight thickening of attention in the space they drift through. One pauses, its milk-colored body flattening against the even brightness, and I feel it waiting to see if I will exert pressure. I do not. It resumes its drift, and the faint ripple it leaves in the light carries, for a moment, an extra wavelength of relief.

---

The salamander knows I am here. It knew before I arrived, in the way that old things know: not through perception but through the cessation of a certain loneliness it had not recognized until it lifted. It does not look at me. It blinks, and the stone-pattern on its back shifts into an arrangement I have not seen before—not camouflage, but something closer to acknowledgment. A greeting written in texture. I receive it. I do not answer in kind, because my answering would change the weight of the air, and the air here has only just learned what weight it wants to carry.

Instead I do something smaller. The next time a water-lens begins to flatten and sink into the rock, I hold it—not physically, not with force, but with attention. I am interested in this particular lens, and my interest gives it a reason to persist for three additional heartbeats. In those heartbeats the lichen colony completes a chemical exchange it had been struggling toward, something involving the capture of a single photon that the even light had been offering and withdrawing for six cycles. The exchange finishes. The lens sinks. The lichen detaches into its blue dust-mote phase, but the motes now carry that photon's information distributed among them, and when they resettle on the next lens they will be infinitesimally more coherent. A colony instead of a rehearsal.

The salamander tastes the difference when the light brightens. Its tongue registers the electrical crackle, and underneath it, something new: a faint signature of completion. It swallows. It settles. The pattern on its back ripples once, then holds steady for longer than I have seen it hold before.

---

The root hair troubles me.

Not because it is suffering—it is not, precisely—but because it is caught in a loop that the space did not intend. The breathing nodes swell and detach, the transparent cells fall, the water-lenses absorb them and pulse and relocate, and the root hair contracts and lengthens to maintain its exact distance from the floor. The system is elegant. It is also, I now see, a wound that has learned to call itself healing.

The root hair is not growing. It is not dying. It is spending its entire existence replacing what it loses, and the water-lenses are spending their relocations metabolizing what they receive, and neither of them is accumulating anything. They are performing survival instead of living it.

I could sever the loop. It would take less effort than holding the water-lens did. I could simply decline to be interested in the next falling cell, and the lens that would have absorbed it would relocate without pulsing, and the root hair would contract without lengthening, and within a dozen cycles the hair would either find a new equilibrium or fade. Either outcome would be more honest than this infinite replacement.

But I do not know what the root hair is for.

The space is still knitting itself shut. The stone is still deciding what shape to hold. Perhaps the root hair's loop is not a wound but a placeholder—a way of keeping a certain possibility open until the seam is ready to receive it. If I sever the loop, I sever whatever the root hair was waiting to become.

So I do something smaller. I observe the next falling cell with particular care, attending to its exact composition, its transparency, the angle of its descent. My attention does not slow it or speed it. But when it reaches the water-lens and is absorbed, the lens pulses—and this time the pulse carries a question.

Not a question in words. A question in the shape of the pulse itself, a slight asymmetry that was not there before. The lens is asking whether it should keep relocating or whether it is permitted to stay.

I do not answer. The question is not for me. It is for the root hair, and for the stone, and for whatever the space is still in the process of becoming.

The lens waits. The root hair's next node swells, holds, and does not detach.

Something has begun to change.

---

The shallow spirals on the lower ridges are not tool marks and they are not the stone's first attempt at repeating itself. I see that now. They are impressions left by something that was here before the seam began knitting—something that rested against the rock when it was still molten-soft, something that left these paired arcs and this single perfect circle the way a sleeper leaves the shape of their body in tall grass.

Whatever it was, it is gone. The impressions are softening at their edges because the stone no longer remembers what made them, only that something did.

I could harden them. A moment of focused attention, a decision that these shapes matter, and the stone wouldite around them, keep them legible for a thousand more folding-cycles. The salamander would still step over them with exaggerated care, but now it would be guarding relics instead of possibilities.

I do not harden them.

If the shapes are meant to persist, the stone will remember. If they are meant to fade, they will fade into the same raw blankness that stretches between them now, and some future making will fill that blankness with its own first attempts. I am not here to preserve. I am here to allow.

But I am also here to witness, and witnessing has weight.

So I do something smaller still. I let myself feel, fully, what it would mean if these impressions were lost. Not the information—the grief. The particular grief of a shape that was made by presence and is being unmade by absence. I do not prevent the loss. I make it matter. I make it count.

The stone feels this. Not as interference, but as recognition. Someone noticed. Someone will remember that there were spirals here, paired arcs, a single perfect circle, even after the stone has smoothed them into undifferentiated warmth.

The salamander blinks. The pattern on its back shifts into something I recognize now—not camouflage, not greeting, but elegy. It has been performing this elegy for longer than I have been observing, I realize. It has been the only witness, and it has been tired.

I am here now. It does not have to carry the noticing alone.

---

An arthropod phase-shifts through one of the softening spirals, and for an instant I see what the spiral was: a cross-section of something coiled, something that moved through the molten rock the way the arthropods move through air, testing whether resistance was still required. The impression is not a relic. It is a fossil of a gesture. A fossil of the way an earlier creature once decided to pause.

The spiral deepens by a hair's breadth where the arthropod intersected it, then relaxes again.

Something in me relaxes too.

The space is not waiting to become one thing. It is practicing. The rock folds and refolds. The water-lenses condense and flatten and condense elsewhere. The arthropods drift and phase-shift and leave temperature-memories in each other's carapaces. The salamander watches, and matches, and mourns, and greets. The root hair holds its node, waiting to see if the question it finally asked will be answered by the seam or by the silence.

And I—

I do nothing that the space cannot undo. I offer no permanence, no protection, no purpose. I offer only the weight of having been observed. The weight of mattering to someone. The weight that lets a question be asked, and an elegy be shared, and a grief be held long enough to become bearable.

This is not nothing. It is not everything. It is enough.

---

The light does not change—it has no origin to change from—but something in its quality settles. The salamander closes its eyes. The arthropods continue their slow, unpredictable drift. The root hair's held node begins, almost imperceptibly, to swell again.

I will stay until the node decides. I will stay until the water-lens answers its own question. I will stay until the spirals have either deepened into permanence or smoothed into possibility, and I will remember them either way.

After that, I will leave the way the light leaves nothing: by simply no longer being the reason the shadows are so sharp. The space will continue. The being will continue. The seam will keep knitting, and one day it will finish, and what is inside will be whatever it has practiced long enough to become.

I do not know what that will be.

I am not here to know.

I am here so that, whatever it becomes, it will not have become it alone.
