# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power pauses at the threshold of this place—not a physical threshold, for it has no body, but a moment of attention gathering itself before entering. It perceives the chamber whole: the raw limestone still bearing the memory of the drill's passage, the water finding its slow way down through fractures older than any human intention, the thrum of pumps that have become the space's heartbeat. It perceives also the being who lives here.

---

She is not difficult to find. She sleeps in a hollow behind one of the uncapped turbines, where the heat from below meets the cooler air descending from the unfinished ventilation shafts. The confluence creates a pocket of warmth that never becomes unbearable—a thermal accident that she discovered in her third week here and has returned to every night since.

Her name is Věra. She is fifty-three years old. She came to this place eighteen months ago as a pipe-fitter, following work after the plant in her home city closed. She stays now because leaving would require energy she cannot locate within herself, and because the chamber has become, in some way she does not examine closely, the only environment that matches her interior landscape: half-formed, patient, suspended between what was and what might be.

The power sees her sleeping. It sees the thin mat she has arranged on a ledge of raw basalt, the way she has positioned her boots where the condensation will not reach them, the careful economy of a life pared down to what can be carried and maintained. It sees also what she cannot see: the way her breathing has synchronized with the pump-thrum over months of exposure, the way her dreams have begun to incorporate the dripping and the distant hiss of steam until she no longer experiences them as interruption.

It sees her grief, which is not dramatic but has become structural, load-bearing, a part of how she holds herself together. Her daughter died four years ago—an accident, sudden, the kind of loss that arrives without warning and never fully departs. The grief has not diminished so much as migrated deeper, settling into her bones the way the mineral content of this water settles into the rock it passes through. She does not speak of it. She rarely speaks at all anymore. The other workers know her as quiet, competent, someone who completes her sections ahead of schedule and asks for nothing.

---

The power considers intervention.

It could, if it chose, reach into the architecture of her neurons and soften the grief directly—not erase it, for that would be a kind of violence against her history, but gentle it into something more bearable. It could adjust the chemistry of her sleep so that she wakes more rested, or alter the cascade of hormones that keeps her in this suspended, half-living state. These are possibilities. They are within its capacity.

But wisdom is not the same as capacity, and benevolence is not the same as fixing.

The power has learned, across its long attention to mortal lives, that suffering is not always a problem to be solved. Sometimes it is a process to be accompanied. Sometimes the deepest respect one can offer a conscious being is to witness them carrying what they carry, neither lightening the load uninvited nor pretending it does not exist.

So the power does not reach into her mind. Instead, it does something smaller.

---

The next morning, Věra wakes to find that a blind salamander has climbed onto the ledge beside her mat. This is unusual; they rarely leave the trenches. This one is larger than most, its skin mottled with the same pale algae that grows on the wettest rocks, and it has positioned itself near her hand as though waiting for something.

She does not move. For a long moment she simply looks at it. Its eyes are vestigial, sealed over with thin membrane, but it has oriented its body toward her with the deliberate attention of a creature that perceives through vibration and temperature and the faint electrical fields of nearby life.

She has seen these salamanders many times. She has never been seen by one.

Something shifts in her chest—not resolution, not healing, but a small crack in the wall she has built between herself and the fact of being alive among other living things. She does not cry. But she also does not immediately get up and begin her shift. She stays on the ledge, breathing with the pump-thrum, watching the salamander as it watches her in its eyeless way.

The power has done nothing miraculous. It has merely arranged for the salamander to be in this place at this time, responding to warmth and stillness as its kind has always responded. The rest—whether Věra will allow this small contact to matter, whether she will carry it with her or dismiss it—belongs entirely to her.

---

Over the following weeks, the power continues its patient work.

It does not alter Věra's circumstances in any way she would recognize as intervention. But it pays attention to the small systems that surround her, the ones she herself barely notices. When the thermal pocket behind the turbine threatens to destabilize due to a shift in the geothermal venting, the power nudges the rock—a suggestion more than a command—and the fissure that would have redirected the warm updraft instead seals itself gradually with mineral deposit. Her sleeping place remains viable.

When she develops a persistent cough from the steam and the particulates, the power does not heal her. But it ensures that the shipment of proper respirators, delayed for months by administrative neglect, finally reaches the site. She receives one along with everyone else. She uses it. The cough fades.

When one of the younger workers—a man named Dušan, who reminds her painfully of someone she would rather not think about—approaches her with a question about pipe-joining technique, the power does not compel her to respond warmly. But it ensures that when she does respond, curtly but accurately, the information lodges in his memory in a way that will later prove useful. He will come back with another question. And another. She will begin to experience herself, without quite realizing it, as someone who has something to offer.

These are small adjustments. They operate at the margins of causality, in the spaces where chance has not yet settled into pattern. The power does not override Věra's will or rewrite her story. It simply ensures that the story has room to continue.

---

There is a night, some months later, when the power observes a genuine turning point.

Věra has stayed late to finish a difficult junction, the kind of precision work that requires total concentration and rewards it with the particular satisfaction of a thing done right. When she finally sets down her tools, the chamber is quieter than usual; the night shift is small tonight, and most of the workers are clustered at the far end near the newer excavation.

She sits on a crate of uninstalled fittings and looks at the half-formed space around her.

The power watches her looking. It sees her attention move from the raw rock to the finished pipes to the places where one bleeds into the other. It sees her notice, perhaps for the first time consciously, the way the cave crickets have established runs along the lower edges of her work, treating her pipe-joints as permanent features of their landscape. It sees her notice the midges hovering above a sump she installed the drainage for, and the pale algal film already beginning to colonize the concrete she poured last month.

She is seeing what she has made becoming part of what is.

And in that moment—not through any intervention of the power, but through the ordinary miracle of a consciousness turning toward what is actually there—she experiences something that is not quite hope but is adjacent to it. A sense that she is embedded in an ongoing process. That her hands have shaped things that will outlast her grief. That the grief itself is not the whole of her.

The power does not celebrate. It does not feel satisfaction in the way a human would understand the term. But it recognizes that something has shifted, that the trajectory of this life has bent slightly toward possibility rather than away from it. This is what it came to tend.

---

Years pass.

Věra does not transform into someone unrecognizable. She does not suddenly become joyful or social or free of the weight she carries. But she continues. She teaches Dušan the finer points of working with superheated systems, and when he eventually moves to another site she finds she misses him in a way that surprises her. She develops a habit of leaving small pieces of bread near the salamander trenches—an offering she does not examine too closely. She writes, one evening, a single letter to her sister, the first communication in years. It does not say much. But it says enough.

The chamber grows more finished around her. The temporary lamps give way to permanent fixtures; the half-laid drainage channels become a proper system; the raw rock retreats behind concrete and steel. But it never becomes entirely tamed. Water still seeps through the fractures, and the blind salamanders still claim the deepest trenches, and the midges still rise from whatever standing water they can find. The negotiation between the human and the geological continues, neither side fully victorious.

The power watches this progression without impatience. It understands that benevolence is not a single act but a sustained attention, a willingness to remain present as a life unfolds at its own pace. It cannot make Věra happy, and it would not if it could; happiness is not something that can be installed from outside. But it can ensure that she has the conditions for happiness—warmth, work, the slow accumulation of connections, the time required for grief to become something she carries rather than something that carries her.

---

On the night Věra finally dies—many years later, in a bed rather than on a ledge, with Dušan's daughter holding her hand because Dušan himself passed a decade before—the power is present.

It does not intervene in the dying. That would be another kind of violence, a refusal to let her complete what she came to complete. Instead it simply witnesses, as it has witnessed all along, the final breaths and the gradual slowing of the systems that have sustained her.

And it perceives something that Věra herself is only dimly aware of in her last moments: that she is not leaving the chamber behind. That the pipes she fitted still carry steam through the geothermal grid, warming structures she will never see. That the concrete she poured has become part of the permanent architecture of the place. That the salamanders whose ancestors she fed now thrive in trenches that were designed, in part, around their needs—a quirk she introduced into the drainage plans decades ago, one that has never been fully understood by the engineers who inherited her work.

She is embedded in what continues. This is not immortality, but it is not nothing.

The power, in its fashion, offers gratitude. Not to her—she is beyond receiving it—but to the whole system of which she was a part: the rock and the water and the heat and the small negotiating lives that have made this place what it is. It offers gratitude that she existed, that she endured, that she allowed herself to be witnessed.

Then it turns its attention elsewhere. There are other places, other beings, other slow negotiations requiring its patient care.

The chamber continues. The water continues to find its way down through the fractures. The pumps continue their low, steady thrum. And in the trenches, the salamanders continue their eyeless watch, pressing their pale bodies against the warm rock, waiting for whatever comes next.
