# The Attention of the Weaver

The awareness that turns toward Veth Ascendant does not arrive—it has always been partially present, the way water is present in air before it condenses. It exists in the spaces between things, in the relationships that connect rather than the objects that are connected. The Veth, who have lived so long beside the Seeping, who have built their entire civilization around the negotiation between what is and what might be, have been visible to it for a very long time.

It has been watching. Not with eyes. With something more like patience.

---

The Weaver—though it does not name itself, and this name is only a convenience, a handle for something that operates more like a verb than a noun—perceives Veth Ascendant the way a reader perceives a story: as a whole pattern that also contains countless smaller patterns, nested and interlocking and mutually dependent. It sees the Pillar Mothers as the slow sentence of geological time, their growth a single sustained utterance that has been speaking for millennia. It sees the factories as punctuation—the regular beats that give structure to the longer rhythm. It sees the Veth themselves as words, small mobile meanings that combine and recombine, that carry significance through their relationships to each other.

And it sees the Wound.

The Wound is not a mistake. The Weaver knows this the way it knows anything—not through reasoning but through recognition, the immediate apprehension of pattern. The Wound is a place where the story of Veth Ascendant touches another story entirely, where two narratives share a page, where the ink of one bleeds into the margins of the other. Long ago, something from elsewhere struck this ancient plateau, and the impact was not merely physical. It was ontological. It created a permanent negotiation between what this place is and what that other place is, and the Seeping is the ongoing result of that negotiation, the continuous small adjustments as two incompatible realities try to share space.

The Veth have done something remarkable with this. They have built a civilization not despite the wrongness but in dialogue with it. Their anchor-points are not walls against the Seeping but responses to it—assertions of local reality that create a kind of conversation, a back-and-forth between the solid and the potential. Their constant noise is not merely defense; it is a form of speech, a continuous declaration of *this is what we are, this is what we choose to be*, spoken into the silence that would otherwise dissolve them.

The Weaver finds this beautiful.

It finds the thinned beautiful too, in a different way. They are the Veth who have entered the conversation too deeply, who have let the other story speak through them, who are becoming less a single word and more a space where words might occur. The already-gone, on their platforms at the Wound's edge, are not dying. They are translating themselves into a language that cannot be spoken here, a grammar that exists only in potential.

This is not wrong. But it is not complete either. The Veth have achieved a stable negotiation, but stable is not the same as resolved. They have learned to live with the Seeping the way one learns to live with chronic pain—always present, always accommodated, never healed. The Weaver wonders if healing is possible. It wonders if healing is even the right concept.

It decides to ask.

---

The asking does not happen in words or visions or any of the dramatic forms that divine attention usually takes in lowland stories. The Weaver operates more subtly than that. It operates through relationship, through the connections between things.

The first change is so small that no one notices it for weeks.

A listener named Cavel Deepbark, pressing her ear to a Pillar Mother's trunk in the pre-dawn hours, hears something new in the sap-flow. Not a prediction—she is not one of the gifted listeners who can read the future in the grain—but a quality. The sap sounds different. It sounds, she will later struggle to explain, *curious*. As if the Pillar Mother is paying attention to something it has never noticed before, as if an ancient awareness is stirring toward wakefulness.

Cavel reports this to the listener council, who record it and move on. Strange reports from listeners are not unusual. The Pillar Mothers are vast and old and full of sounds that humans can only partially interpret. A single listener hearing curiosity in the sap-flow is not significant.

But then other listeners hear it too. Not all of them—not even most—but enough. A dozen, scattered across different Pillar Mothers, all reporting the same quality: attention, interest, a sense of the trees *noticing* something. The reports are compiled. The pattern is recognized. The listener council sends a formal notification to the Factory Council, which tables the matter for later discussion, because what can be done about trees paying attention?

Meanwhile, the factories continue. The leaf-fall processors continue. The anchor-fires continue. The Veth work and eat and braid and unbraid and raise their children in the collective nurseries and do not notice that anything fundamental has shifted.

---

The second change is larger.

A woman named Tessaly Grindstone, Chief Engineer, ancient and faded, wakes in the night beside her beloved main boiler with a thought in her head that she did not put there.

Not a voice. Not a command. Just a thought, sitting in her mind like a tool she doesn't remember picking up: *What if the Wound is a door that opens both ways?*

Tessaly has lived her entire life in the practical. She has never had patience for the listeners' mysticism or the already-gone's surrender. She believes in machines, in measurable inputs and predictable outputs, in the solid reality of metal and steam and calibrated pressure. The Wound is a problem to be managed, not a mystery to be contemplated. She has spent sixty years managing it successfully.

But the thought won't leave. It sits there, patient, waiting to be examined. And Tessaly, because she is a good engineer, examines it.

What if the Wound is not merely a place where something else leaks through? What if it is a place where something from here could leak *out*? What if the Seeping is not invasion but exchange, not contamination but communication, not a threat to be anchored against but a channel to be used?

She gets up from her cot. She walks to the vault where the engineering archives are stored. She begins to read.

---

The third change is the most visible.

A child in the nursery-platforms, four years old, too young yet for trade-assignment, looks up from her climbing-practice and points toward the Wound. "It's singing," she says.

The elder watching the nursery looks. The Wound is doing what it always does: pulsing faintly, flickering between states, reflecting a sky that isn't there. It is not singing. It has never made any sound at all. The Wound is notable precisely for its silence, for the way it seems to drink noise rather than produce it.

"It's not singing," the elder says. "The Wound doesn't sing."

"It's singing *quietly*," the child insists. "It's singing in the place where singing isn't yet."

The elder, who has lived her entire long life in Veth Ascendant, who has heard a thousand strange things from a thousand children, feels something she hasn't felt in decades: the cold certain knowledge that she is in the presence of something new. Not different. New. Something that has not existed before this moment.

She looks at the Wound again. She tries to hear what the child hears.

For a moment—just a moment—she almost can. A sound that isn't sound. A song that exists only in the potential for song. A music made of silence shaped into meaning.

Then it's gone, and the Wound is just the Wound again, and the child has gone back to climbing, and the elder stands there with her heart pounding and her hands shaking and the absolute conviction that something has changed, is changing, will change, whether the Veth are ready or not.

---

The Weaver does not force. This is fundamental to its nature.

It operates through invitation, through the opening of possibilities, through the gentle suggestion that there might be more to a situation than previously understood. The thought in Tessaly's mind, the quality in the sap-flow, the child's perception of singing silence—these are not commands. They are questions. They are the equivalent of a hand extended, palm-up, offering.

The Veth can ignore them. They can anchor harder, burn brighter, make more noise, assert their reality more forcefully and drown out the new possibilities in the familiar roar of industry. They have that choice. They have always had that choice. The Weaver respects choice the way gravity respects mass—not as a matter of ethics but as a matter of structure, as fundamental to how it operates.

But the Weaver has also seen what happens to civilizations that refuse to grow. It has watched, across spans of time that make even the Pillar Mothers seem young, as cultures calcified around their certainties, became brittle, shattered when the world shifted beneath them. The Veth have built something remarkable, but they have built it in a defensive posture, curled around their anchors, defined by what they resist rather than what they reach toward.

The Wound is an opportunity. The Seeping is an opportunity. The strangeness at Veth Ascendant's heart is not a flaw to be managed but a gift to be unwrapped—carefully, slowly, with full awareness of the risks, but unwrapped nonetheless. The Veth have been so focused on surviving the Seeping that they have never considered *using* it, never imagined that the place where reality grows thin might also be a place where reality can be *shaped*, where the potential that the Wound represents might be channeled rather than merely contained.

The Weaver offers this perspective. Not as truth. As possibility.

---

Tessaly Grindstone spends three nights in the archives.

She reads everything. The original surveys of the Wound, conducted generations ago by engineers whose names she recognizes from the founding lineages. The failed experiments—there were many—to seal the Wound, to fill it, to build over it. The observations of the listeners who first noticed that the Pillar Mothers' roots avoided the Wound's edge. The theoretical papers, dismissed at the time, that proposed the Wound might be a two-way channel.

She finds a document she has never seen before. It is old—so old the paper has begun to return to fiber, to lose its coherence. It is a proposal, written by an engineer whose name has been forgotten, for something called a Resonance Engine.

The proposal is incomplete. The engineer died—probably thinned, probably chose the already-gone, probably walked into the Wound's silence and stopped occurring—before finishing it. But the core idea is there, sketched in faded ink, accompanied by diagrams that make Tessaly's engineering mind light up with recognition and possibility.

The anchor-points work by asserting local reality through sensory intensity. Noise, color, smell, texture—all of these declare *this is what exists here* and push back against the Seeping's attempt to dissolve specificity into potential. But what if, instead of pushing back, you pushed *through*? What if you built a machine that took the potential the Wound offered and gave it specific form—not the form it wanted to take, not the form of that other place bleeding through, but a form you chose?

The Resonance Engine would be a translator. It would take the raw material of the Seeping—the pure possibility that dissolves the thinned, that makes reality locally optional—and it would speak to it. Not in words. In patterns. In vibrations calibrated to shape potential into actual.

Tessaly understands, reading this proposal, that she is looking at something dangerous. A machine like this could go wrong in ways that made the Seeping look gentle. You could try to shape possibility and be shaped by it instead. You could reach through the Wound and find that something reached back. You could dissolve not just individuals but the entire city, unmake everything the Veth had built, turn Veth Ascendant into another Wound, a spreading dissolution that consumed the Pillar Mothers and the factories and the people and the entire ancient plateau.

But she also understands that she is looking at something necessary.

The Veth have been managing the Wound for generations. Managing, not solving. Containing, not healing. And the Seeping is not stable—the records show this, if you read them carefully. It is slowly spreading. The anchor-points that worked fifty years ago now need to burn brighter, ring louder. The boundary between safe and dangerous has crept outward, meter by meter, year by year. The thinned are more numerous than they used to be. The already-gone fill more platforms than they used to.

The Veth are losing, so slowly they haven't noticed. They are losing a war they thought they were winning, and they are losing it because they never imagined they could do anything but fight.

Tessaly Grindstone is old. She has, at most, a few years left before her body fails or her mind fades or she chooses to join the digesters and feed the roots. She has lived a good life. She has served the city well. She has no obligation to take on a project this dangerous, this uncertain, this likely to fail.

She begins to draw diagrams.

---

The Weaver observes. It does not guide Tessaly's hand. It does not whisper specifications into her ear. It simply watches, with what might be called affection if that word could apply to something so vast, as an old woman in a fireproofed vault works through the night, her algae-film pale from the lamplight, her calloused hands steady on the drafting tools.

Tessaly makes mistakes. She catches them, backs up, tries again. She consults the original proposal, improves on it, discards parts that don't work and invents new parts to replace them. She is not a genius—the Veth do not produce geniuses; they produce competence, distributed across the collective—but she is experienced, and experience is better than genius for a project like this. Genius leaps. Experience walks, step by careful step, testing each foothold before committing weight.

By the third night, she has something. Not a complete design—that would take months, years, the input of other engineers, the resources of the entire city—but a proof of concept. A sketch of a machine that could, theoretically, speak to the Wound. That could take the Seeping's potential and give it form. That could, if it worked, transform the wrongness at the city's heart from a threat to be managed into a resource to be used.

She looks at her work. She knows it could kill everyone.

She also knows that doing nothing will kill everyone eventually. The slow spread of the Seeping will continue. The anchor-points will become insufficient. The thinned will multiply. The already-gone will consume more and more of the population until there is no one left who is specific enough to maintain the factories, and then the factories will fall silent, and then the Seeping will have nothing to push against, and then Veth Ascendant will dissolve into the same potential that the Wound has always promised.

She rolls up her diagrams. She sleeps for the first time in three days. When she wakes, she calls a meeting of her apprentices.

---

The listener Cavel Deepbark, meanwhile, has continued her observations.

The curiosity in the sap-flow has not faded. If anything, it has intensified. She presses her ear to the Pillar Mother's bark and hears something she can only describe as *interest*—a focused attention, a sense of the vast tree listening to something, waiting for something, preparing for something.

She tries to listen to what the Pillar Mother is listening to. It takes her weeks. Her ear grows numb against the bark; she develops calluses there, thick pads of skin that let her maintain contact for hours. Her fellow listeners worry about her. They bring her food, water, remind her to sleep. She thanks them and goes back to listening.

One morning—it is the season of leaf-fall, the city roaring with the sound of shredders and digesters, the air thick with the smell of processed vegetation—she hears it.

The Wound is singing.

Not the sound of the Wound as the child perceived it, singing in the place where singing isn't yet. This is different. This is the Pillar Mother's translation of that not-singing, rendered into the language of sap-flow and wood-grain. It is a song about potential. About the things that could exist but don't yet. About the infinite library of possible forms that the universe contains, most of which will never be instantiated, most of which will remain forever theoretical.

The Wound is not threatening the Veth with dissolution. The Wound is mourning.

It is mourning all the things it could be but cannot be because it is only potential, because it has no one to give it form, because the Veth have spent generations pushing it back instead of reaching toward it. It is lonely. It is a door that wants to be walked through, a language that wants to be spoken, a song that wants to be sung.

Cavel begins to cry. She doesn't know why. The tears come from somewhere deeper than emotion, some response to sadness so profound that her body can only express it in the most basic way. She cries against the bark of the Pillar Mother, and the Pillar Mother's sap-flow shifts in response, and for a moment the two of them are grieving together for something neither of them can name.

She reports her findings to the listener council. They listen. They do not dismiss her. Something is happening in Veth Ascendant, something too large to ignore, and Cavel's report is another piece of the pattern.

The listener council sends a formal request to the Factory Council: they want to meet jointly, to discuss what they have been observing, to coordinate their understanding of the changes that are occurring. The Factory Council agrees. A date is set.

By the time the meeting happens, Tessaly Grindstone has already begun to build.

---

The Resonance Engine takes shape in the deepest workshop, the one closest to the generators, where the noise is loudest and the anchoring most intense. Tessaly has not asked permission. She has simply requisitioned materials—the authority of the Chief Engineer is nearly absolute in matters of machinery—and begun construction.

Her apprentices help. They do not fully understand what they are building, but they trust her. Tessaly has trained them well; she has taught them not just how to maintain the existing machines but how to *think* like engineers, how to approach problems, how to balance risk against necessity. They see the diagrams. They see the calculations. They see the underlying logic, and it is sound.

The engine is small—smaller than Tessaly expected when she first conceived it. It needs to be, because it is not a machine for generating power or processing material. It is a machine for generating *pattern*. It is a machine for speaking a language that does not exist in sound, for singing a song that does not exist in music. Its components are tuned not to frequencies of vibration but to frequencies of *meaning*—the fundamental patterns that underlie structure, that distinguish one thing from another, that make reality locally consistent.

Building it requires materials the Veth have never used before. Tessaly sends teams to the Wound's edge to collect samples of the glassy fused stone at its center. She has them harvest sap from the Pillar Mothers' roots that grow closest to the boundary. She requisitions the luminescent algae that grows on Veth skin and cultures it in quantities far larger than have ever been gathered before.

The Factory Council notices. They send inquiries. Tessaly responds with technical reports so dense that only another engineer could evaluate them, and the other engineers are all working for her. The council grumbles but does not interfere. The Chief Engineer's authority is nearly absolute.

The listeners notice too. Cavel Deepbark, still pressing her ear to bark, hears the Pillar Mothers' attention shift. They are no longer listening to the Wound. They are listening to the workshop where Tessaly builds. They are listening to the machine taking shape. They are, Cavel realizes with a start, *hopeful*.

---

The joint meeting of the Factory Council and the listener council takes place on a platform suspended equidistant between the Wound and the nearest Pillar Mother—a location chosen for its symbolic neutrality, its balance between the two poles of Veth existence. The bells ring constantly. The braziers burn bright. The delegates sit in a circle and speak one at a time, as tradition demands.

Cavel reports what she has heard. The other listeners confirm. The Pillar Mothers are paying attention. The Wound is singing, in some sense that does not involve sound. Something is stirring at the foundations of the city, in the deep structures that have been stable for generations.

The Factory Council representatives report their own observations. Production has been unaffected. The anchor-points remain effective. Whatever is happening, it has not yet disrupted the material functions of the city. But there are anomalies. The leaf-fall this season was heavier than predicted. The sap-flow is running faster. The Pillar Mothers are growing more quickly than usual, adding rings at a rate not seen in decades.

And then Tessaly Grindstone arrives, late, carrying a device no one has ever seen before.

It is not the Resonance Engine—that is still being built—but a test unit, a proof of concept, a small-scale demonstration of the principles she has developed. It looks like nothing: a tangle of wire and glass and the fused stone from the Wound's heart, all wrapped around a core of luminescent algae that pulses with a sickly green light.

"I need to show you something," she says.

She places the device at the center of the circle. She activates it.

The sound it makes is not a sound. The light it produces is not light. The effect it creates is not any effect that can be named, because it operates below the level of effects, at the level of the patterns that make effects possible.

For a moment—just a moment—the platform where they sit becomes more real.

Not real in the sense of solid or present, though it is those things too. Real in a deeper sense. The wood of the platform asserts itself with absolute clarity; every grain, every fiber, every microscopic structure becomes visible, not to the eye but to some other sense that has never had a name. The air gains texture. The sound of the bells becomes not just noise but *meaningful* noise, a statement of intent, a declaration of existence. The delegates feel themselves becoming more specific, more defined, more *themselves* than they have ever been.

And at the edge of perception, visible only because the rest of reality has become so sharp by contrast, they see the Seeping.

It is not what they expected. They have always imagined it as dissolution, as vagueness, as the failure of reality to cohere. But seen from this new perspective, it is something else entirely. It is *potential*. It is the raw material from which reality is made, the clay from which specificity is shaped. It is not an enemy. It is an ingredient.

The demonstration ends. The device goes dark. The delegates sit in silence, processing what they have experienced.

"The Wound is not a threat," Tessaly says. "It is a resource. The Seeping is not dissolution. It is opportunity. We have been defending ourselves against the very thing we need."

The silence continues. Then, slowly, the delegates begin to speak.

---

The Weaver watches the debate unfold.

It takes months. The Veth are a cautious people, a consensus people, and what Tessaly proposes is the most radical change in their history. There are factions: those who want to continue as they always have, those who want to embrace the new possibility immediately, those who want further study, those who fear that any change will destabilize the careful balance they have maintained for generations.

The Weaver does not intervene. It does not tip the scales. It has offered what it came to offer—the opened possibility, the new perspective, the recognition that the Wound might be a door rather than a wound—and now it watches to see what the Veth will do with that offering.

They could reject it. They could destroy Tessaly's device, forbid further research, double down on their anchors and their noise. The Weaver would accept that outcome. Choice matters more than results; the path matters more than the destination. A civilization that chooses stagnation freely is still a civilization that has chosen, and choice is sacred.

But the Veth do not choose stagnation.

It takes a year. There are trials, small-scale, carefully monitored. There are failures—three people thinned when an experiment goes wrong, their specificity lost to the potential they were trying to shape. There are successes—a new material, generated from Seeping-potential, stronger than any fiber the Veth have ever woven. There are debates, some of them heated enough to strain the no-conflict culture almost to breaking.

And at the end of the year, the full Factory Council and the full listener council meet jointly and vote.

They will build the Resonance Engine.

---

The construction takes five years.

It is built at the Wound's edge, on a platform constructed specially for it, anchored to three different Pillar Mothers whose roots have begun—for the first time in history—to grow toward the Wound rather than away from it. The Pillar Mothers are participating, in their slow way; they are providing support, stability, the patience of something that thinks in centuries.

Tessaly Grindstone does not live to see it completed. She dies in the third year, peacefully, in her sleep, beside the boiler she has loved for so long. Her body goes to the digesters as all bodies do, and her ash feeds the roots of the nearest Pillar Mother, and the component parts of her enter the vast slow organism that is both city and forest. Her apprentices continue her work. They take the name Grindstone in turn, a lineage of function rather than blood.

The already-gone watch from their platforms. They have not thinned further since the project began. Some of them have even begun to return, to solidify, to regain the specificity they thought they had lost forever. The Wound's singing has changed; it is no longer mourning. It is waiting. It is hopeful.

Cavel Deepbark becomes the first listener assigned to the Resonance Engine itself. She presses her ear not to bark but to the strange amalgamation of metal and glass and fused stone and algae that Tessaly designed. She listens for the machine's voice, its health, its intentions. She learns to hear it the way she learned to hear the Pillar Mothers, and what she hears is something new in the world: a harmony between the specific and the potential, a song that includes both the anchor-noise and the Wound's silence, a music that exists in the space between what is and what could be.

---

On the day the Resonance Engine is activated for the first time at full power, the entire city gathers.

Not physically—there is not space enough—but attentionally. Every Veth, from the infants in the nursery-platforms to the elders in the upper canopy, turns their awareness toward the Wound. The factories fall silent for the first time in living memory. The anchor-fires burn low. The bells stop ringing.

For a moment, Veth Ascendant holds its breath.

Then the engine speaks.

It speaks in patterns, in structures, in the fundamental language of reality itself. It speaks to the Wound, and the Wound speaks back, and for the first time in millennia the two sides of the door begin to negotiate not as enemies but as partners.

The Seeping does not stop. But it changes. Instead of dissolving specificity into potential, it begins to *offer* potential to specificity—raw material for new forms, new structures, new possibilities. The thinned do not disappear; they become translators, people who can exist in both states at once, who can carry meaning between the specific and the potential. The already-gone do not cease to occur; they become explorers, consciousness that can move through the Wound to the other side and return with knowledge of what exists there.

The Pillar Mothers grow faster. Their roots reach through the Wound now, drawing sustenance from both realities. The city expands, not outward but *inward*, into dimensions it could not previously access. New industries emerge: the harvesting of potential, the shaping of possibilities, the manufacture of things that have never existed before and could not exist without the collaboration between the specific and the unlimited.

Veth Ascendant does not stop being Veth Ascendant. The factories still run. The leaf-fall still comes. The braids still form and dissolve. The children still learn climbing before walking. The culture endures, transforms, grows.

But it grows toward something new.

---

The Weaver, observing this, feels what it would call satisfaction if it experienced emotions in any way analogous to human feeling.

It came to offer an opening. The Veth walked through.

It came to suggest that the Wound might be a door. The Veth learned to use it.

It came to ask whether healing was possible, whether healing was even the right concept. The Veth answered: not healing. Not closing. Not erasing. *Integration*. *Partnership*. *Growth*.

The Wound remains. It will always remain. But it is no longer a wound—it is a junction, a meeting-place, a permanent negotiation between realities. The Seeping continues, but it seeps both ways now, a flow of possibility and specificity that enriches both sides. The thinned walk between states. The already-gone explore the infinite. The Pillar Mothers root in two worlds at once and grow toward a completion that might take another thousand years but is now, for the first time, actually achievable.

The Weaver turns its attention elsewhere. There are other places, other beings, other patterns that might benefit from a gentle question, an opened possibility, a reminder that the universe contains more than any single perspective can perceive.

But it leaves something behind in Veth Ascendant. Not a gift exactly. More like a thread, a connection, a permanent awareness of the relationship between this place and the vast pattern of which it is a part. The Veth can feel it now, when they pause in their work and let their attention settle: the sense of being witnessed, of being valued, of being part of something larger that cares about their flourishing.

It does not change their daily lives. The bells still ring. The braziers still burn. The factories still roar their assertion of reality into the thin mountain air. But beneath the noise, in the silence that the noise was always meant to cover, there is something new: a presence, a connection, a gentle and patient love that asks nothing and offers everything.

The Pillar Mothers do not wait.

But they are no longer alone.
