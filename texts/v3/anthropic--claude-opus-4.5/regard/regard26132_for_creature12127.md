I sit with this for a long time before acting.

The cistern house breathes. She tends. There is a completeness here that asks nothing of me—the carp patrol their territories, the pigeons raise their clutches, the willow tilts toward light, and she walks her circuits, muttering her narration, polishing brass nameplates she knows will tarnish again. The system has found its own equilibrium, strange and slow and sufficient.

What would I change? The pumps will never turn again, and she knows this, and she oils the joints anyway. The floor lies twelve feet below, invisible, and she dreams of walking it dry. Old Parliament will outlast her. These are not problems to be solved. They are simply the shape of the life that has formed here.

But I notice the willow.

Three years old, already tilting, following the light that filters down through the grates. She packs clay around its roots, monitors it for rot. It is the youngest thing in the cistern house, the most precarious, reaching toward something it cannot name.

I do not give it more light. I do not straighten its trunk or deepen its roots. I do not grant her visions of the floor or restore the pumps to their old purpose. These would be violations of the terms that have been established—between her and the mallards, between the chamber and its water, between what was built and what has grown in the building's long surrender.

Instead, I make one small adjustment in the pressure gradients she cannot see.

Somewhere in the connected tunnel system, a partial blockage shifts. Not dramatically—a settling of sediment, a rebalancing of flows that would have happened eventually, perhaps in a year, perhaps in ten. The change propagates slowly through the waterways, alters the current that moves through the cistern house by degrees so subtle that even she, who has learned to read these waters like weather, will not notice for weeks.

But the carp will find new feeding patterns. Nutrients will distribute differently. And at the crack where the willow's roots drink, the water will carry something it did not carry before—traces of mineral, of organic matter, of whatever the flood has gathered from the world above. Nothing that will make the tree grow faster or straighter. Only something that will make its slow tilting more sustainable, its reaching less costly.

She will not know. The willow will not know. Old Parliament will continue his patrols, indifferent. The pigeons will coo.

But in five years, when the sapling has become something that might survive her, she will walk her morning circuit and pause at the crack where it grows, and she will notice that the roots trailing into the water have thickened, that the lean toward light has found a kind of balance, that the small thing she has tended has become capable of tending itself.

And she will feel, perhaps, that the heart she has kept has noticed her back.

This is all. I do not stay to watch. The cistern house does not need witness; it only needed, for one moment, the smallest adjustment in the current that was already flowing.

She returns to her control booth as the light through the grates shifts with passing clouds. The water speaks its constant language. Somewhere below, Old Parliament turns in the murk, and the floor lies patient under twelve feet of silence, and the pumps stand frozen in their rust, and everything continues as it was, almost, almost, almost exactly as it was.
