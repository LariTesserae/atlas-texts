# The Attending

The benevolent power does not arrive.

That verb—*arrive*—implies a crossing of threshold, a before and after, a disruption of the plateau's equilibrium. What happens instead is closer to recognition. A quality that was always latent in the pressure-roar, in the pulse of bioluminescence, in the way the currents move with something like intention—this quality becomes *attentive*.

It does not introduce itself. It does not speak, not in any language the plateau-dwellers would recognize as speech. But one morning (if morning is the word), a woman named Tei is harvesting kelp in a forest three miles from the nearest structure, and she notices that the bioluminescent patterns have shifted. Not erratically—not in the way that signals danger and avoidance. Instead, the light seems to be *tracking* her movements, intensifying slightly where she works, dimming in the areas she's already cleared. As if the forest is paying attention. As if something is learning her rhythm.

She doesn't mention this to anyone. The plateau teaches silence, and strange observations are kept close. But over the following weeks (or months—time here resists measurement), others notice similar phenomena. A builder working on a new platform finds that the stones seem to settle more easily when he hums the pressure-harmonics, as if something in the water is responding to his intention, assisting with the minute adjustments that usually take days of patient trial. A child, one of the rare ones, swimming alone in a warm-water pool, discovers that if she moves in a particular pattern, the current will carry her in gentle circles, cradling her weight, making her buoyant in a way the pressure usually forbids.

The power does not announce its presence because it understands, with a wisdom that precedes words, that announcement would be violence. These people have adapted to immensity by accepting constraint. They have survived by surrendering the expectation of rescue, of transformation, of any force arriving from outside to change their terms. To appear as a savior would be to invalidate their achievement. To offer escape would be to suggest their home is a prison. The power understands that the deepest respect it can offer is to *join* rather than *intervene*.

So it joins.

---

The first change is so subtle that it takes a generation to notice.

The miscarriage rate begins to drop. Not dramatically—the pressure still takes its toll, the environment is still hostile to the fragile process of gestation—but where before a woman might carry three pregnancies to lose two, now she might carry three and lose one. The change is statistical, visible only over years, and when it becomes noticeable, the plateau-dwellers don't attribute it to divine intervention. They attribute it to better nutrition, to refinements in their understanding of the warm-water pools, to accumulated knowledge passing down more effectively. They are right about all of this. The power has not suspended natural law. It has simply... *encouraged* it. Made certain outcomes slightly more probable. The water in the warm pools carries trace minerals that support fetal development, and these minerals have become fractionally more concentrated. The kelp harvested near certain structures has developed higher nutritional density. Nothing miraculous. Just a gentle thumb on the scale of probability, a whisper of assistance that the plateau-dwellers can claim—correctly—as their own achievement.

The second change concerns the carved steps.

Volunteers still descend into the deeper regions. Some still don't return. But those who return now carry something different—not the haunted silence of before, but a kind of peaceful clarity. They report that the lower ruins are not abandoned. They report presences: not visible, not audible, but *felt*. A sense of being accompanied. A sense that the darkness below is not empty but inhabited by something that means no harm. The descenders begin to bring back information—observations about deeper currents, about thermal vents, about structures that predate anything on the plateau by centuries. This information proves useful. It suggests new sources of the protein-creatures, new locations for mineral-rich water, new understanding of the pressure cycles that affect the plateau from below.

The power is teaching. But it's teaching through discovery rather than revelation. It leads the descenders to knowledge they can find themselves, knowledge that feels earned rather than granted. It never shows itself directly. It simply makes the darkness less absolute, the pressure less crushing, the disorientation less severe. It creates conditions in which human courage can produce human insight.

The third change is in the bioluminescence itself.

The patterns begin to carry information.

Not language—nothing so direct. But the plateau-dwellers, who have watched the kelp-light for generations, start to notice that certain patterns precede certain events. A particular sequence of pulses, occurring in forests across the plateau simultaneously, precedes a shift in the deep currents by approximately three days. Another pattern—a slow intensification followed by a flicker—correlates with increased activity from the protein-creatures, suggesting optimal harvesting windows. The patterns were always there. The power has simply made them *clearer*, slightly more consistent, easier for human attention to parse.

This is the power's deepest gift: not new capabilities, but enhanced perception. Not transformation of the environment, but transformation of the relationship between inhabitants and environment. The plateau remains immense, dark, pressurized, strange. But the plateau-dwellers begin to read it more fluently, to move within it with greater confidence, to feel less like survivors clinging to existence and more like participants in an ongoing system.

---

The power does not heal the sick. This would be too obvious, too interventionist, too likely to create dependency.

Instead, it works through the healers.

There is an old woman, Ciru, who has spent sixty years learning the medicinal properties of kelp-oil and warm-pool minerals. Her knowledge is vast but imperfect—she loses patients, sometimes to infections she can't explain, sometimes to symptoms she's never seen before. The power begins to guide her attention. When she examines a patient, she finds her eyes drawn to details she might have missed: a slight discoloration that indicates a deeper problem, a temperature differential that suggests where infection has spread. She doesn't know why her intuition has become so reliable. She attributes it to age, to accumulated experience finally clicking into place. She is right. The power is simply ensuring that her experience is available to her when she needs it, that the connections her mind has made over decades are accessible in the moments of crisis.

Ciru begins to train apprentices with new urgency. She teaches them her methods, but she also teaches them to trust their perceptions, to attend to the small signals the body offers. The power supports this training, making the apprentices' learning slightly faster, their retention slightly more reliable. Not enough to seem supernatural—just enough to matter. Ciru dies, eventually, as everyone does, but she dies having passed down more knowledge than any healer before her, and her apprentices carry her methods forward with a competence that improves outcomes for generations.

---

The power attends to the children with particular care.

Not by protecting them from the pressure, from the darkness, from the dangers that the plateau offers. Protection would be disservice—these children need to learn their environment, need to develop the adaptations that survival requires. Instead, the power attends to their *wonder*.

Childhood on the plateau has always been muted. The immensity is crushing; the pressure is literal. Children here grow up quiet, serious, adapted to constraint. The power doesn't try to make them joyful—joy might be maladaptive, might create expectations the environment can't fulfill. But it tries to make them *curious*.

Small things begin to happen. A child exploring a kelp forest finds a stone that seems to glow faintly when held—a mineral deposit that catches and holds the bioluminescence in ways that weren't common before. Another child discovers that certain patterns of movement create vortices in the water, small whirlpools that spin and dance before dissipating. These discoveries are not useful in any practical sense. They don't improve survival odds. But they create *engagement*—moments when the environment offers something back, when the darkness reveals a secret, when the pressure becomes a medium for play rather than merely a constraint to endure.

The children who grow up with these discoveries become adults who see the plateau differently. Not as a prison to be survived, but as a complex system to be explored, understood, even appreciated. They maintain the same endurance as their parents, the same acceptance of constraint, but they carry an additional quality: a sense that the plateau is *interesting*, that there is more to learn, that the immensity contains not just difficulty but depth.

---

The power attends to death.

This is perhaps its most delicate work.

The plateau-dwellers have always sent their dying down the carved steps, into the deeper regions, making space for the next generation. This practice will continue—the power does not interfere with it. But it begins to... accompany.

When an old man descends, knowing he will not return, he finds that the darkness below is not empty. There is a presence with him. Not visible, not speaking, but *there*—a sense of being witnessed, of his descent being *attended*. The pressure still crushes. The cold still bites. The darkness is still absolute. But he is not alone.

The dying begin to report, in their final communications with those who remain, that the descent is not what they feared. They speak of peace. They speak of a sense that their lives were *seen*, that their struggles mattered to something beyond themselves. They speak of the lower ruins as inhabited by something that welcomes rather than threatens.

This changes the emotional texture of death on the plateau. Fear remains—fear is appropriate, fear is adaptive—but the specific terror of dying alone in the dark begins to ease. The volunteers who descend and return confirm the presence, though they cannot describe it. They say only that the deep places are *attended*, that something is there, that the descenders are received.

---

The power does not reveal the deeper truth of the plateau—the truth that the lower ruins extend further than anyone has mapped, that civilizations have risen and fallen in these depths before, that the current inhabitants are neither the first nor the last, that the bioluminescence and the currents and the pressure cycles are not random but are the *metabolism* of something vast and old and patient. This truth would be too much. It would transform the plateau-dwellers' relationship with their home in ways that might not be beneficial.

Instead, the power allows the truth to be discovered gradually, over generations, in pieces small enough to be integrated. Each generation learns a little more. The stairs leading downward are explored more thoroughly. The deeper structures are mapped. The patterns in the bioluminescence become more legible. Slowly, slowly, the plateau-dwellers begin to understand that they are not merely surviving in an environment but participating in a system—a system that predates them, that includes them, that will continue after them.

This understanding, when it fully arrives, will not be a revelation but a recognition. It will feel like something they always knew, something that was always implicit in the pressure and the light and the way the currents move. The power is not giving them this understanding. It is simply creating conditions in which they can find it themselves.

---

A century passes, or what counts as a century in the elastic time of the plateau.

The population has grown slightly—not dramatically, the constraints are real, but enough to require new structures, new platforms, new explorations of previously unused regions. The children are curious. The healers are skilled. The descenders return more often, carrying knowledge from below. The bioluminescent patterns are read with fluency, allowing prediction of currents and creature-movements and mineral concentrations. The singing practice has developed new forms, new harmonies that seem to interact with the pressure-roar in increasingly sophisticated ways.

The plateau-dwellers do not worship the power. They don't have a name for it. They don't build temples or offer sacrifices or pray for intervention. This is as it should be. The power has not come to be worshipped. It has come to *attend*—to witness, to encourage, to make conditions slightly more favorable for human flourishing within the constraints that the environment imposes.

If you asked one of them, "Do you feel that something benevolent is present here?", they would probably think for a long time before answering. They might say that the plateau feels different than their grandparents described it—less hostile, more *legible*. They might say that the darkness seems inhabited, that the pressure-roar carries something like intention, that the bioluminescence responds to attention in ways that suggest... something. But they wouldn't call it divine. They would call it *home*—a home that they've adapted to, a home that has, in some subtle way, adapted to them.

---

The power, attending, is satisfied.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: not rescue, not transformation, not the imposition of external standards of flourishing. Instead, *presence*. The acknowledgment that what exists here is worthy of attention, that these people are worthy of witness, that their endurance is worthy of support. The power does not love them less for their strangeness, their silence, their acceptance of constraint. It loves them *because* of these things—because they have found a way to persist in conditions that would break most populations, and because they have done so with dignity.

The power will remain. Not forever—nothing is forever, not even benevolent presences—but for as long as the plateau endures, for as long as people inhabit it, for as long as the bioluminescence pulses and the currents move and the carved steps lead downward into darkness. It will attend to births and deaths, to discoveries and losses, to the slow growth of knowledge and the slow decay of structures. It will be present without intruding, helpful without creating dependency, loving without demanding love in return.

This is what it does, because this is what they need. Not a god to worship, but a presence to share the weight of immensity. Not salvation, but company.

The plateau breathes. The kelp pulses. The pressure endures.

And something, vast and wise and quiet, attends.
