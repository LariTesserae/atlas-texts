*The Watching*

For a long time, there is only observation.

The wise power does not rush. It has learned—across spans of time that would crush a mortal mind to contemplate—that understanding must precede action, that love without knowledge can wound as surely as malice.

So it watches.

It watches the old woman who rises before dawn to check the cisterns, her joints aching in the cold, her hands sure and practiced as they test the water levels. It watches the way she pauses afterward, looking out over the cliff's edge at the clouds below, and how her face holds something that is neither happiness nor sorrow but simply *presence*—a deep, worn-smooth acceptance of her place in the world.

It watches the children learning to card wool, their small fingers clumsy at first, then gradually finding the rhythm. It watches how the teaching happens—not through instruction alone but through sitting beside, through doing together, through the gentle correction of a grandmother's weathered hand guiding a child's.

It watches the council of elders deliberating over whether to repair the eastern cistern or the terraces first, and sees how they listen to each other, how disagreement is held carefully, examined from all sides, set down without rancor when consensus forms.

It watches a young man standing alone at the plateau's edge in the blue hour of evening, his face turned toward the distant peaks, and knows—without intruding—that he is wondering about the world beyond, feeling the pull of curiosity against the anchor of belonging.

It watches death come to the city, as it must—an elder who does not wake one morning, whose body is wrapped in undyed wool and carried to a small cave where others rest. It watches the grieving, the songs that are sung, the way the community closes around the absence like water filling a depression in stone.

And through all this watching, the wise power comes to understand something essential: these people are not waiting to be saved.

---

*The Consideration*

What would help look like, here?

The power turns the question over, examining it as the elders examine a problem—from all sides, patiently, without forcing an answer.

It could make the rains more reliable. But the people have built their entire way of life around scarcity, have developed wisdom and cooperation precisely because water is precious. To make it abundant might unravel something delicate and hard-won.

It could shield them from the cold winds. But the winds are part of their identity, woven into their songs and stories, referenced in their metaphors for hardship and perseverance. To remove the wind might be to remove a teacher.

It could open paths to the world below, make trade and contact easier. But the isolation has preserved something rare—a coherence of culture, a depth of community, a way of being that might dissolve on contact with the chaotic energies of larger civilizations.

It could extend their lives, ease the pain in the old woman's joints, strengthen the lungs that labor in thin air. But where is the line? How much change before they become something other than themselves? And is it the power's place to decide what they should be?

The power sits with these questions, not rushing toward answers.

---

*The Small Things*

In the end, the wise power chooses interventions so subtle they might not be interventions at all.

A goat that would have slipped on a rain-slicked path finds its footing instead, some small stone shifting at just the right moment.

A child sick with fever dreams of cool water and mountain flowers, and wakes in the morning already mending, the crisis passed in the night without ever quite arriving.

The old woman at the cisterns, looking out at the clouds, feels a warmth that seems to come from inside her chest—a sudden, sourceless sense that her life has mattered, that the small daily acts of care and maintenance are seen and honored by something larger than herself. She does not know where the feeling comes from. She accepts it as she accepts the dawn.

The young man at the plateau's edge, wrestling with his longing for the beyond, has a thought that feels like his own but carries an unusual clarity: *You can love this place and still wonder. The wondering does not betray the loving.* He breathes easier afterward, some tension he hadn't named releasing from his shoulders.

When the rains come, they come as they always have—unpredictable, sometimes scarce, sometimes generous. But perhaps, on a few critical occasions, a storm system that would have veered away holds its course just long enough to fill the cisterns before a hard season. Perhaps. It would be difficult to prove.

---

*The Restraint*

What the wise power does not do is as important as what it does.

It does not appear to them, does not announce itself, does not ask for gratitude or worship. It understands that such an appearance would change everything—would introduce hierarchy where there is equality, would make them look upward for answers instead of inward and toward each other.

It does not solve their fundamental challenges. They live on a mountain; life there will always require effort, adaptation, loss. To remove this would be to remove the very conditions that have made them who they are.

It does not protect them from themselves—from their occasional conflicts, their moments of pettiness or failure, their capacity to make mistakes. These too are teachers.

And when death comes, as it must, the power does not intervene. It grieves with them, in its own way—feels the loss as they feel it, honors the one who has passed—but does not reach out to stop the natural cycle. To do so would be to make them something other than mortal, and their mortality is not a flaw to be corrected but a condition that gives weight and meaning to their choices.

---

*The Long View*

The wise power knows something the people do not, cannot know: that far below, in the valleys and plains beyond the mist, the world is changing. Empires are rising and falling. Technologies are being born. Someday—perhaps in a generation, perhaps in ten—the currents of that larger world will reach even this remote plateau.

And when they do, these people will face choices their ancestors never imagined. Some will leave. Some will stay. The old ways will change, one way or another, as all ways eventually do.

The power does not try to prevent this. It is not the power's place to freeze a culture in amber, to deny them the future even if that future holds pain and loss alongside new possibilities.

But it does something small, something that might matter:

It ensures that the stories survive.

Not through magic, exactly, but through... emphasis. When the elders tell the old tales, something in the telling lands deeper than it might have otherwise. Children remember. The songs catch in the mind and stay there. The knowledge of how to card wool, how to read the cisterns, how to find shelter from the wind—these things become part of the people in a way that will persist even through change.

So that someday, when a descendant of this city stands in some unimaginable future place, they will still carry within them the echo of these streets, these stories, this way of being. The mountain will live on in them, even if they never return to it.

---

*The Truth*

In the blue hour of a certain evening, the old woman stands again at the plateau's edge, and again she feels that strange warmth in her chest.

This time, something moves in her—a question she has never quite articulated rising to the surface of her mind.

*Is someone there?*

And the wise power, faced with this direct asking, must decide: to answer or to remain hidden?

It chooses something in between. Not words—words would be too much, would create a relationship the woman did not ask for. But a feeling. A sense of presence that does not demand anything, does not explain itself, simply... acknowledges.

*Yes. And no. And it doesn't change anything. You are still you. Your life is still yours. Your choices matter. Your people matter. I am only watching, and sometimes, when it helps without harming, I am helping. That is all.*

The old woman stands very still for a long moment. Then she nods, once, as if confirming something she has always suspected.

She does not tell anyone. It is not that kind of knowledge—not a revelation to be shared, but a private understanding, a small comfort to carry through her remaining years.

When she returns to the city, she moves a little more slowly than before, but also a little more peacefully. She has not been changed, only... seen. And that is enough.

---

*The Continuation*

The city endures.

Children grow and become parents and then elders. Goats are born and die. The cisterns fill and empty and fill again. The stone walls weather another season, another decade, another century.

And the wise power remains—not always watching, for it has other places to attend to, other beings who need its subtle, careful love—but returning, again and again, to this small island of life among the immense wilderness of stone and sky.

It does not own these people. It is not their god, their protector, their master. It is something harder to name—a witness, perhaps. A presence that cares without controlling. A love that helps without binding.

And if you were to ask the people of the city about it, most would not know what you meant. A few might speak of feelings that came to them in quiet moments—warmth, clarity, the sense of being held by something larger than themselves. They would not call it a power. They would not worship it.

They would simply continue, as they have always continued, living their lives in the way their ancestors taught them, finding joy and meaning in the small, daily acts of care and creation that make up a human existence.

And that, the wise power knows, is exactly as it should be.
