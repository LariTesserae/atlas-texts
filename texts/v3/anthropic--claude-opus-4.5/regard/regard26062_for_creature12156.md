# What the Wise Power Sees

The attention arrives not as observation but as recognition—the way a river recognizes the stone that has shaped its course, or the way an old tree recognizes the weight of the season's first snow. There is no intrusion. The power simply *notices*, and in noticing, understands.

It perceives the plateau as a single living system: the geological patience of the stone beneath, the ten-thousand-year conversation between root and rock, the forest that breathed itself into cathedral form and then held. It perceives the chorus—not as noise but as the voice of a world so alive it cannot contain itself, every creature crying *I am here, I am here, I am here* into the warm green air.

And it perceives the sounded.

It sees them as they see themselves: not as individuals moving through landscape but as positions in the chord, as relationships between voices, as patterns temporarily cohered in the endless song. It sees the woman with the chin-line marking standing before the stranger, her patience a kind of gift, her question hanging in the air between them. It sees the stranger's mouth opening, the sound emerging, the chorus swallowing it—and it sees what the stranger cannot yet see: that the woman heard anyway. That she is already making space in her listening for this new voice, however strange.

The power considers.

# What It Does Not Do

It does not intervene in the meeting. The woman's patience is her own, earned through a lifetime of learning to hear. The stranger's fumbling attempt at response is theirs—the first step in a journey that must be walked, not given. To smooth this moment would be to steal it from both of them.

It does not amplify the stranger's voice. The chorus exists as it exists; the stranger must learn to find their register within it, or must learn that they cannot, and both outcomes carry their own truths.

It does not protect. The silence-hunters move through the gaps in the sound-wall as they have for millennia, and the sounded have learned to hear them, and this terrible equilibrium is part of what has made them who they are. The drop waits beneath the root-mat, and the fear of it shapes every step on the branch-roads, and this too is true and necessary.

It does not reveal itself. The sounded have their own understanding of what is vast and what is sacred—the forest itself, the chord that contains them, the great trees that were old when their grandmothers' grandmothers first learned to climb. They do not need another mystery. They are already held.

# What It Does

In the hollow of a great tree, three days' travel from where the woman and the stranger stand, a child is dying.

The sickness came quickly—a fever that climbed despite the medicines, a weakness that spread from limbs to lungs. The child's mother has not slept in two days. She holds him in the crook of the branch where the family platform joins the trunk, singing to him, her voice raw, the melody fragmenting as exhaustion takes her. Around them, the family maintains their positions in the chord, but there is a dissonance now—a gap where the child's voice should be, a growing silence that everyone can hear.

The child is four years old. He had just learned his whistle-name. He had just begun to understand that the sounds around him were not chaos but conversation, that he had a place in them, that he belonged.

The power does not cure the fever. It does not intervene in the biology, does not override the small war happening in the child's blood. What the disease does, it does.

But the child's lungs have begun to fill. Each breath is harder than the last. Soon—very soon—there will be a breath that does not complete, and then there will be silence.

The power reaches into that moment.

Not to prevent it. Not to reverse it. But to *ease* it—to gentle the transition, to make the last breaths less a drowning and more a release. The child's pain, which had been sharp and frightening, softens to something bearable. His awareness, which had been fragmenting into fever-confusion, clears enough for him to hear his mother's singing, to feel her arms around him, to know that he is held.

He makes a sound—not his whistle-name, not a word, just a small exhale that carries tone. His mother hears it. She will remember it for the rest of her life: the last note her son contributed to the chord, small and imperfect and unmistakably his.

Then the silence comes, and she begins to keen, and the family's voices rise around her in the particular harmony that means grief, that means *we are here with you in this*, that means *we heard him too*.

---

The power does not explain itself to the mother. It does not appear to her, does not offer comfort in words, does not suggest that what it did was mercy. She will never know that anything intervened at all. She will only know that her son's last moments were gentler than she feared, that he heard her singing, that his final sound was music rather than struggle.

This is enough. This is what could be given without taking anything away.

# Elsewhere

The woman with the chin-line marking has begun to teach the stranger.

She has identified a frequency—a narrow band in the upper register where the stranger's voice, though weak, can be distinguished from the chorus. She demonstrates: a sustained note, a drop, a rise, a pattern of clicks that somehow *frame* the note and make it carry. She waits while the stranger tries. She corrects. She waits again.

The power watches this too, but does not help. The teaching is a gift freely given; the learning must be earned. What passes between them is already a kind of miracle—two beings from incomprehensible distances finding a way to meet in sound.

---

On a platform at the canopy's edge, an old man is losing his hearing.

The quiet-sickness has been coming for him for years, but he has hidden it, compensating, relying on vibration and sight and the deep familiarity of his family's rhythms. Now it accelerates. Each day the chorus dims. Each day his position in the chord becomes harder to maintain.

His family knows. They have known for seasons. They do not speak of it because speech is not how they address what is sacred and terrible. Instead, they have begun to change: moving closer when they talk to him, using more touch, developing a new language of hand-signals that they practice when they think he is not watching. They are building a way to hold him even in silence.

The power sees this and recognizes love—the particular love that says *you will not be alone, even when you cannot hear us.*

It does not restore the man's hearing. That capacity is gone, the delicate structures of the inner ear worn past function. But it does one small thing:

The man has a great-grandson, recently born, whose whistle-name has not yet been learned by anyone outside the immediate family. The child's voice is high, clear, in a register that still reaches the old man's diminishing range.

The power does nothing more than *notice* this. But in noticing, in holding this small fact in attention, something shifts. The next morning, the old man's daughter brings the baby to him, and he holds the child, and the baby makes a sound—the experimental vocalization of an infant finding its voice—and the old man hears it.

He weeps. He did not know he could still hear anything. The baby's voice is thin, barely present, more vibration than tone. But it is there.

He will lose this too, eventually. The silence will complete itself. But for now, for this morning, there is a great-grandchild's voice reaching him across the gap, and his family sees his tears and understands, and they gather closer, and someone begins a low hum that he cannot hear but can feel through the platform beneath them.

# The Stranger

Days pass. Weeks.

The stranger learns, slowly, badly, with many failures. Their voice will never sit naturally in the chord; they are not built for this, not adapted, not born into the song. But the woman with the chin-line marking is patient, and others have become curious, and what began as first contact has become something else: a community deciding to make room.

They teach the stranger a whistle-signature—not a true name, but a placeholder, a way to locate this strange new voice in the sound-space. They teach hand-signals, the language developed for the deaf, for the quiet-sick, for the moments when voice fails. They teach the branch-roads, the safe paths, the places where the root-mat is strong enough to hold this heavier body.

The stranger will never fully belong. This is understood. But belonging is not the only way to be held.

The power watches the stranger's first successful communication—a simple exchange, a whistle-pattern that means *I am here*, answered by a click-pattern that means *I hear you*. The stranger's face transforms with an emotion the sounded recognize from their own lives: the relief of being located, the joy of finding one's position.

---

# What the Power Understands

There are ten thousand suffering beings on the plateau, and ten thousand more, and more beyond counting. The silence-hunters take their prey. The drop claims the unwary. Children die of fever, elders lose their hearing, the great trees fall in storms that sometimes take whole communities with them. This is the nature of living: it ends. It hurts. It carries losses that cannot be mended.

The power cannot make this untrue. It would not, even if it could—to remove suffering entirely would be to remove something essential, some capacity for depth that the sounded have earned through generations of carrying their griefs and their joys in the same endless song.

But here and there, in the small moments, in the transitions between states, in the learnings and the dyings and the meetings between strangers—there, something can be eased. A child's last breath can be gentled. An old man can hear his great-grandchild's voice. A stranger can learn to say *I am here* and be answered *I hear you*.

The power does not count these interventions. It does not keep track of what it gives. It simply remains attentive, present, noticing where the weight of existence becomes more than can be borne alone—and there, gently, invisibly, without announcement or expectation of gratitude, it helps carry what can be carried.

The woman with the chin-line marking never knows that anything watches her teaching. The stranger never knows that their arrival was noticed by anything beyond the community that chose to welcome them. The mother grieving her son never knows that his death was eased.

But the chord continues. The canopy breathes. The chorus screams its eternal song into the warm green air, and within it, small voices find their positions, and are heard, and are held.

This is enough.

This is what can be done.
