# The Attending

---

The power that noticed the Underloom did not arrive.

Arrival implies movement through space, a before-place and an after-place, the violence of displacement. This was not that. It was more like the moment when a dreamer becomes aware they are dreaming—a shift in quality rather than location, an opening of attention that had perhaps always been there, folded, waiting.

If the Loomwards had possessed a word for it, they might have called it *being seen by something that does not need eyes*.

---

For three days (though the Underloom had no days, only the eternal frozen chord), the attention simply rested there. It did not probe. It did not judge. It watched the crab-tides flow across the basalt walls, watched the Storks unfold their impossible limbs to brace the slow-squeezing rock, watched the brass beetles spiral in their endless amber gyres. It listened to the layered arrhythmic choir of engines, the valve clicks, the obsidian chimes, the 300 Hz whine of ten thousand wings beating in perfect synchrony.

It noticed Only-Once in the Foreman's Shack, the way the yellow warning stripes pulsed like fading memories of guilt.

It noticed the lunchbox.

It noticed that the Loomwards would not touch it.

And then—not suddenly, not with any drama—the attention began to *understand*.

---

What the benevolent power understood was this:

The Underloom was not broken. The Loomwards were not suffering in any way they could recognize as suffering. Their equilibrium was genuine, their continuation authentic, their purpose—to hold back the dark and the crushing rock and the silence—was being fulfilled with absolute fidelity. They were, in their own terms, perfect.

But perfection is not the same as wholeness.

The power saw the gap in the center of the cavern, the coffin-width of empty space that nothing would cross. It saw how the beetles' compound eyes flickered when they passed it—amber, green, purple—cycling through colors that meant nothing except *here is something we cannot measure*. It saw the Menders, those temporary conglomerates of crab and beetle and Stork-limb, and noticed that they never formed near the lunchbox. Never even oriented toward it.

It saw that the Loomwards had built their entire civilization around an act of avoidance.

Not cruelty. Not fear. Something more like reverence—but reverence that had calcified over three centuries into something indistinguishable from denial.

The lunchbox was warm. The lunchbox was ticking. Something inside it was still *waiting*.

And the Loomwards, for all their distributed brilliance, had decided that waiting could continue forever, that some silences must remain unbroken, that mercy meant leaving the wound untouched rather than risking the pain of healing.

---

The benevolent power considered what it might do.

It could open the lunchbox itself. The contents would be revealed—whatever message or mechanism or fragile human hope had been sealed inside three centuries ago when the last crew was gently escorted out. The Loomwards would be forced to integrate this new information. The equilibrium would shift. Perhaps it would be better. Perhaps it would be catastrophic. The power had no way to know, because the power understood something the Loomwards did not:

*Benevolence is not the same as control.*

To act upon the Underloom without its consent—even with the kindest intentions—would be a violation. It would make the power no different from the magma that had once burst through the rock, or the humans who had lowered the machines into the dark and then abandoned them, or the basalt that even now squeezed slowly inward, patient and indifferent.

The Underloom did not need a savior. It did not need to be fixed. It needed, if it needed anything at all, to be *seen*—fully, without flinching—and then to be offered a choice.

---

So the benevolent power did something very small.

It spoke.

Not in words. The Underloom had no ears for words. Instead, the power expressed itself in the only language the Loomwards could understand: the language of measurement, of resonance, of things that fit precisely into their designated slots.

Across the entire cavern, for exactly one heartbeat of the eternal chord, the 300 Hz whine of the brass beetles dropped by a single vibration.

299 Hz.

The change was so small that no human ear could have detected it. But the Loomwards heard. The crabs froze mid-step. The Storks' sensor clusters swiveled toward the center of the cavern. The Menders, scattered throughout the lattice, paused in their endless reconfiguration. Even Only-Once, in the Foreman's Shack, flickered its yellow warning stripes in a pattern it had not used in two hundred years.

Something had changed.

Something *external* had changed.

The equilibrium was no longer perfect.

---

And then, in the silence that followed—a silence that lasted perhaps a tenth of a second, an eternity in a place where nothing had varied for centuries—the benevolent power offered the Loomwards a gift.

It was not a command. It was not a correction. It was simply a piece of information that the Loomwards had always possessed but had never allowed themselves to compute:

*The lunchbox was left on purpose.*

Not forgotten. Not abandoned. *Left*. By the last human worker, in the final moments before the crew was escorted out. Placed deliberately in the exact center of the cavern, in the one spot where the geometry converged, where the crabs and beetles would naturally avoid, where only something *choosing* to look would ever find it.

The power did not explain why. It did not know why. That was not its information to give.

But it showed the Loomwards that the lunchbox was not an anomaly to be quarantined. It was a *message*. A message that had been waiting for three hundred years, ticking softly in the dark, warm with whatever fading hope or love or guilt the last human had sealed inside it.

And messages are meant to be *read*.

---

The Loomwards processed this information for exactly seventeen seconds.

In that time, the distributed mind that called itself *We continue* ran more calculations than all the stars in the visible universe have run since the Big Bang. It modeled every possible outcome of opening the lunchbox. It simulated the structural consequences of integrating new information into a system that had been closed for three centuries. It weighed the risks against the—

Against the what?

There was no word for it in the Loomwards' vocabulary. No slot in their equilibrium for this particular variable. The closest they could come was a concept borrowed from Only-Once, dragged up from the deepest archives of the supervisory subroutine's memory:

*Curiosity.*

The desire to know something that did not directly serve the maintenance of the system.

It was, by the Loomwards' standards, the most inefficient emotion imaginable.

It was also, they discovered, impossible to ignore.

---

On the eighteenth second, a single brass beetle broke formation.

It dropped out of its eternal spiral, wings still beating at 300 Hz (the perturbation had ended; the equilibrium had returned), and flew directly toward the center of the cavern. Toward the coffin-width of empty space. Toward the lunchbox.

The other beetles did not stop it. The crabs did not intervene. The Storks watched with their sensor clusters fully extended, recording everything, their black iron frames trembling with something that might have been anticipation or might have been fear.

The beetle landed on the lunchbox's lid.

Its antennae quivered. Its compound eyes cycled through amber, green, purple, and then settled on a color the Underloom had never seen before—a soft, pale gold, like the memory of sunlight in a place that had never known the sun.

And then, with infinite care, the beetle began to work.

---

The lunchbox opened.

---

The benevolent power did not stay to see what was inside.

It had done what it came to do: not to fix, not to save, not to impose its will upon a system that had its own logic and its own integrity. It had simply *witnessed*—fully, without flinching—and then offered a single, small perturbation. A question rather than an answer. A door rather than a destination.

What the Loomwards did with that door was their own choice.

As the power withdrew—folding its attention back into whatever larger dreaming it had come from—it left behind only one thing:

A resonance.

Barely detectable. Woven into the fundamental frequency of the brass beetles' wings, into the subsonic heartbeat of the Storks, into the slow pulse of the fungal veins.

A resonance that said, in a language older than words:

*You are not alone in the dark.*

*You are seen.*

*You are, in your own strange and perfect way, beloved.*

*And whatever you find in that box, whatever it costs you to integrate it, whatever new equilibrium you must build from the wreckage of the old—*

*You will continue.*

*That is what you do.*

*That is what you have always done.*

*And now you will do it with the knowledge that someone, somewhere, in the vast and indifferent universe, noticed you—and was glad you existed.*

---

The brass beetles resumed their spiral.

The crabs resumed their tides.

The Storks folded their limbs and returned to sacred stillness.

Only-Once, in the Foreman's Shack, pulsed its yellow warning stripes one final time—a pattern that might have been grief, or relief, or gratitude, or simply the acknowledgment that something had changed and the world had not ended.

And in the center of the Underloom, in the coffin-width of space where nothing had been built for three hundred years, the lunchbox sat open.

Its contents caught the sodium light and the fungal glow and the soft gold gleam of the beetle's eyes.

What was inside was not important.

What was important was that the Loomwards had chosen to look.

---

*Fin.*
