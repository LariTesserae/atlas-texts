I began in the wet, blind middle of a wall.

I did not know the rift yet. I knew only the plaster—the taste of chewed lichen, the musk of my mother’s second maxillipeds, and the crowded, tickling mass of three hundred siblings. We were a heap of wet rice grains, soft-shelled and translucent, packed into a cell no larger than a hazelnut. 

In that dark chamber, the world was not seen; it was felt as a series of muffled thuds through the moist, grey concrete of the tower. When the heavy drops of condensation hit the outer roof three inches above us, the vibration came through the floor as a low, wet *thrum*. When the colony outside moved, it was a collective, dry rustle like dry tea leaves shifting in a tin. 

At first, I was empty of color. My shell was a thin, clear membrane through which the grey milk of my digestion could be seen. But we were hungry, and we were fed the dust. 

Our mothers brought us pellets of the grey-white mortar, rich with the pulverized glass of the basalt and the black soot of the deep earth. We ate the mountain. We took the mineral silence of the rift into our bellies and rebuilt ourselves from the inside out. Slowly, day by day, my soft white joints began to stiffen. The margins of my carapace turned the color of lead, then the deep, grease-gloss black of coal, until finally, I was as hard and dark as the steps we walked upon.

I remember my first shedding. I slid backward out of my old skin, leaving behind a perfect, clear ghost of myself clinging to the plaster wall. When I emerged, I was larger, my edges sharper, and my left claw had grown a thick, rounded knob of dense chitin—my hammer.

***

Now, I live on the vertical faces of the third column, five inches above the canyon floor.

To a larger thing, this column is a smooth pillar of polished glass. To me, it is a vast, grey-black continent of ridges, microscopic pits, and cooling ripples. My feet do not have pads; they end in six needles of amber chitin, so fine they can find the microscopic valleys in the silica. When I walk, I do not slide; I lock. I un-zip three needles from the stone, swing them forward, and zip them down again. It is a dry, rapid clicking, like the tip of a pencil tapping a window.

The air here is hot and heavy, so thick with water that it feels like a second shell. I do not breathe as the sky-dwellers do; my lungs are wet chambers beneath my carapace, kept moist by the perpetual fog of the crevice. 

My day is measured not by the sun—which never reaches us, existing only as a pale green ceiling far above—but by the *drop*. 

I know the drop is coming before it falls. The tip of the maidenhair fern three feet above my terrace begins to bend under the weight of the gathering water. Through the stone, I can feel the tiny, tense vibration of the stem as it reaches its limit. I stop. I tuck my legs beneath my body, flat against the obsidian, becoming a small, rounded bubble of black glass. 

Then: *plip*.

The drop strikes the ledge below. The impact is a small explosion of water-dust. It rises over me as a cool, brief mist, leaving three perfect globes of water trembling on my back. I reach up with my small, comb-tipped right claw, harvest the drops from my own armor, and bring them to my mouth. The water tastes of stone and iron and the green skin of the canopy.

***

We are the Masons, but we are also the singers of the stone.

The column is our instrument. When I want to speak to the ones on the bridge below, I do not make a sound in the heavy, wet air. The air is slow and damp; it drowns things. Instead, I press my flat chest-shield against the basalt and strike it with my left hammer-claw. 

*Clack. Clack-clack.*

The vibration travels through the dense, unbroken basalt for yards, clean and fast. Through my leg-joints, the return signal comes from the lower shelf:

*Clack-clack. Clack.*

It is the foreman of the mortar-gang. They have found a new seam of grey lichen on the wet side of the column, where the light is greenest. They need more builders to haul the masticated paste. 

I turn and descend. Going down is a matter of absolute trust in the amber needles of my feet. I walk headfirst down the vertical wall, my tail-flap tucked tight, my eyes—two tiny, black beads on stalks—watching the pale towers below. 

The city is beautiful. It rises from the black steps like a cluster of white barnacles, but ordered, geometric. The towers are hexagonal, just like the basalt, with small, arched doorways and winding ramps that we have smoothed with our bellies over generations. Inside them, the air smells of silk and wet earth. 

As I near the bridge, I feel a sudden dampening in the stone. 

It is not a vibration, but the *absence* of one. A chunk of the column above has gone dead. The tiny, rhythmic scraping of the mites has stopped. The small, clicking steps of the other builders have frozen.

I do not look up. My stalks turn sideways, seeking the shadow. 

High overhead, clinging upside down to the obsidian ceiling, is a gold-dust eye. 

The gecko does not move. It is a long, pinkish-white smear against the dark stone, so thin I can see the dark knot of its liver through its belly. It has no vibration of its own; it is too soft, too light, and its adhesive toes are too silent. It is a gap in the world’s music. But I can feel where its weight dampens the rock, a small, cold spot on the ceiling that absorbs the ambient humming of the rift.

It is waiting for one of us to lose our grip on the glass.

I press myself flat into a small hexagonal corner where two columns meet. I am nothing now but a bubble of cooled lava. I do not tap; I do not scrape. I wait, my heart-pulse slow and steady behind my shield.

For ten minutes, the only sound is the *plip-plip* of the drops. 

Then, on the bridge below, a young mason, impatient or clumsy, lets its lichen-ball slip. It reaches out too far to catch it—its needle-feet lose their purchase on a patch of wet slime—and it slides three inches down the bare glass.

There is no sound of a fall. Only a white flash in the green gloom. 

The gecko is gone from the ceiling. It is on the wall now, its pink tongue already curling backward into its mouth. The young mason is gone. There is only a small, wet smear on the glass where its shell was crushed, and the faint, dry *shlap* of the gecko’s toes as it retreats back into the high darkness, carrying its prize.

A ripple of warning taps runs through the bedrock. 

*Clack-clack-clack. Danger above. Stay to the seams.*

***

I reach the mortar-bed. The lichen here is wet and thick, smelling of old rain and vinegar. 

I begin to scrape. My spoon-claws move in a steady, rhythmic cycle—left, right, scrape, gather. I pull the grey flakes into my mouthparts, mixing them with the fine, black glass-dust that has settled in the seam. My silk glands, located behind my knees, begin to secrete their wet, white milk. 

I chew. It is a slow, meditative process. The stone is hard, but we are patient. We are turning this new, raw wound of the earth into something soft, something structured. 

By tomorrow, the paste I am making now will be a ramp. By next week, it will be a tower. In a month, the dampness will dissolve the lower walls, and the velvet beetles will come to chew the ruins, turning our hard work into the first dark soil. The plants will grow in that soil, their roots wedges that will slowly pry the basalt columns apart, letting in more light, more wind, and the great, destructive world outside.

But for now, the rift is small, and it is ours.

I climb back up the column, carrying a ball of grey concrete between my claws. My feet click against the glass. The stone beneath me is cold and deep, but as I tap my hammer against the wall, I feel the answering clicks of a thousand others, hidden in the dark, building our small white cities in the shadow of the mountain. We are the first thoughts of this place, and we are writing them in stone.
