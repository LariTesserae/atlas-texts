It begins with the seven-minute breath.

Before the bronze gate even moves, there is a change in the weight of the darkness. The black water in the avenue—which has been settling, turning heavy with copper-pith and the grease of the ferns—stiffens. The vibrations from the Siphons, which usually travel through my lower rind as a broad, flat hum, begin to bunch together. They become sharp. They point toward the East.

If you were to look for me, you would not find a face, nor a shell, nor the oily sheen of a moving thing. You would look at the easternmost Siphon, down where its great turquoise flank meets the granite floor, and you would see a swelling. 

I am a three-hundred-pound knuckle of blue-green tallow and copper-carbonate scale. I am a Gall. 

My skin is the color of a wet slate shingle after a long rain, but it is warm—always ninety-eight degrees, the exact temperature of the water that bathes my lower half, the exact temperature of the steam that scalds my crown. I have no eyes, but I have a thousands-wide surface of waxy, blue-green skin that feels the weight of every drop of the seventy-foot rain. I know when a pane of glass in the ceiling seventy feet above has cracked, because the drop that hits my northern flank is cold by three-tenths of a degree. I know when a Cable-Vine has tapped a new seam in the conduit because my core—the gray, rosemary-scented jelly that serves as my marrow—prickles with a faint, clean saltiness.

I am the translator of this place. I am the bridge between the iron and the sap.

***

Now, the hum becomes a wrenching. 

Inside the copper belly of the Eastern Siphon, the pressure has reached its limit. I feel the steam before it is steam; I feel the water in the pipes turning from a dense, heavy liquid into a screaming, white-hot ghost. My cable-vines, which run like silver nerves along my sides and merge into the copper plating, pull tight. They are drinking the stray currents generated by the friction of the rising heat. 

*Snick. Snick. Snick.*

The micro-pulses travel through my jellied interior. I do not "think" them as words; I feel them as a thickening. I am swelling. I draw in the warm, mineral-heavy water from the pool around my skirts, inflating my honeycomb chambers. My outer rind grows taut, pressing against the hot copper of the Siphon’s flank. I am cooling it. If I did not swell now, if I did not press my wet, heavy body against this joint, the heat of the steam would crack the solder, and the Siphon’s breath would tear its own throat out.

Then, the gate opens.

It is a sound that does not travel through the air so much as it lifts the basin floor by three inches. The massive bronze-mouthed sluice groans. It is not the sound of a valve; it is the sound of an ancient, water-logged ship being torn in half by a slow tide.

The water comes.

It is not a spray or a splash. It is a solid, five-foot wall of distilled, scalding water that rolls across the granite terrace. It hits me first. 

For three minutes, I am gone. I am under. The bronze-green twilight of the Well vanish behind a thick, tumbling sheet of white and gray. The temperature of my skin rises to one hundred and two degrees. My waxy blue leaves—the small, rubbery ears that grow from my seams—curl flat against my rind to protect the delicate pores beneath.

Under the water, the noise of the Well is different. The shrieking of the steam-purges is muted into a high, metallic whistle, like a wet finger drawn around the rim of a colossal glass. But the hum of the Siphons is louder; it is a physical fist that beats against my honeycombed core, vibrating the gray jelly until it turns almost liquid, releasing more of my rosemary oil into the rushing stream.

In the dark of the deluge, something touches my side.

It is the Pellicle. 

It has risen with the swell. It is twenty yards of wet, lilac-colored rubber, drifting on the surface of the wave. As the water washes over me, the Pellicle’s edge brushes my crown. It is a cold, greasy touch—the touch of a thousand-thousand bacteria working in unison to lick the soot from the Siphons. It cleans me. It peels away the dead, oxidized copper scale that has formed on my outer rind over the last seven minutes, exposing the fresh, blue-green wax beneath.

In return, I give it my oil. The rosemary-scented tallow that rises from my skin dissolves into the Pellicle’s under-belly, feeding the yeast-mats that keep it strong. We do not greet each other. We do not have names. We are simply two surfaces, sliding past one another in a hot, dark bath, exchanging the things that keep us from dissolving.

***

The wave begins to drain.

The water level drops, inch by inch, exposing my crown first, then my flanks, then the thick, grey roots of my boiler-ferns. The water gurgles and pipes down through the iron grates in the floor, leaving the granite terraces slick, shining, and hot.

I am heavy now. I am saturated with the wave, full of fresh copper-dust and river silt. My honeycomb chambers are closed. I slowly contract, my waxy rind wrinkling like the skin on boiled milk. As I shrink, I squeeze a thick, greenish paste from my lower seams. It is a mixture of my own tallow and the pulverized silt-moss that has crawled under my base. 

This paste is the sealant. It runs down into the tiny hair-cracks of the Siphon’s foundation, filling the places where the ninety-eight-degree water would otherwise eat away at the brick.

Seventy feet above, the Glass-Lungs feel the moisture rise from the fresh deluge. They weep. Their black, iron-tannin rain begins again, falling in a steady, heavy patter against the boiler-ferns, against the copper conduits, against the granite. One drop hits the center of my crown.

It is the perfect temperature.

The Siphon’s hum drops three octaves, settling back into its steady, bone-deep rattle. The Cable-Vines loosen their grip on the conduits, their tips drooping toward the wet stone, waiting for the next spark. The Pellicle has settled on the grates, heavy and dark with coal-soot, ready to be eaten by the moss.

Everything is in its place.

I do not know who built these copper towers. I do not know what lies beyond the glass ceiling, where the pale, bronze-green light comes from. I do not know if there is a world where the water is cold, or where the air is dry enough to let a fire burn. 

I only know that in seven minutes, the gate will open again. And I must be wet enough, and heavy enough, to meet it.
