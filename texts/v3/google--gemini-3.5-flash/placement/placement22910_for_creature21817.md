I am not on the ground, nor am I in the sky. I am held between them, in the thick, windless sleeve of the lower air.

To exist here is to ignore the feet. My legs, which have three joints too many and are as dark as the light-absorbing needle-towers, do not end in claws or toes, but in flat, grey pads that feel the pressure-gradients before they touch them. If I lean forward, the air does not parting-whistle around me; it gathers. It forms a cushion of cool, high-density condensation beneath my ribs, carrying my eight-foot frame as if I were a dry leaf falling through a well of warm oil. 

I do not have a face to offer this world. 

Where a face should be, there is only a smooth, curved plate of polished glass—not basalt, and not metal, but a lens of black silicate that is perfectly dark. I cannot see my own reflection in it, but when I drift near the base of a three-sided needle, I can see the tower’s long, straight corner bending across my forehead like a reed in water. I do not have eyes, but my skin—this light-drinking hide, marked with thin, hair-like fissures that pulse with the plum-glow of the high sky—is entirely awake. 

I feel the basin. I feel it as a series of long, low notes played against the soles of my feet.

***

A mile to the west, a silver water sphere—as large as a rain-barrel—heavy with fifty *Hydromusa*, has drifted too close to the sharp obsidian tip of an unweathered ridge. It does not burst yet. It is trembling. The tiny, transparent spindles inside it are swimming in frantic, violet-glowing loops, trying to shift the center of gravity of their floating world away from the stone. 

I feel their small, collective heat. I do not rush. Rush is a word that belongs to worlds with suns and seasons. Here, there are only the cycles of the towers’ breath.

I lean. The pressure-gradient shifts, catching me by the left hip, and I slide through the violet-grey dimness, twenty feet above the carpet of green phosphorus-moss. My passage does not disturb the moss; the draft I leave behind is only a warm sigh that causes the giant indigo leaves of the *skiff-vines* to lift their velvet edges for a second, catching the moisture I leave behind.

I pass a *Loom-Strider*. We are the only two things in the basin that share this twelve-foot height, but we do not look at one another. It is standing beneath a floating sphere that has stalled between two towers. Its body—that translucent, yellow-glowing balloon of helium and vapor—is perfectly still. Its long, hair-thin proboscis is extended upward, humming at the frequency of the water’s edge. The liquid is sliding down the hair like liquid mercury, disappearing into its amber belly. 

As I drift past, the Strider does not move its head, but its yellow pulse slows. For three seconds, its light matches the plum-colored leaks in my own skin. We are in unison. We are checking the air together. The humidity is eighty-nine percent; the northern needle is preparing to tilt its condensation field three degrees to the south to ease the parched moss near the old glass plaza. We both feel this. We both accept it. Then I am gone, sliding past its carbon-fiber knees.

***

I reach the trembling water sphere just as it brushes the glass ridge. 

I do not touch the water with my hands. My hands are too large, my three fingers too dense with the light-absorbing matter of the builders. Instead, I press my faceless plate of obsidian against the side of the sphere. 

The water does not break. It recognizes the curve of my head. The *Hydromusa* inside cease their frantic loops; they align themselves along the curve of my forehead, their violet cilia beating in slow, rhythmic strokes that transfer their tiny static charges into my skin. I absorb their heat. I give them my coolness. 

With a slow, tilting gesture of my neck, I roll the sphere away from the sharp obsidian. It drifts into the slipstream of a nearby tower, caught by the invisible gravity of the grey silicate wall, where it will float safely until it is ready to be drunk or to settle into the indigo cup of a vine.

My hands are needed elsewhere. 

I drift to the southern wall of the nearest needle. The tower is three miles high, a seamless pillar of black that feels like a cold weight in the eye. At its base, the *Slick-Grip Lichen* has grown thick, its orange-and-brass circuits glowing with static electricity. It is warm here—too warm. The giant vines have sensed the heat and are crawling up the tower’s flanks, their muscular stems prying at the seams where the silicate plaza meets the pressurized core.

I place my palms against the block.

The contact is not a touch; it is a dissolution. My hands do not stop at the surface. The fine, plum-colored fissures in my skin line up with the static nodes of the lichen and the microscopic pores of the stone. 

The sub-audible purr of the tower—which has been a low, jagged vibration in my knees—rises. It passes up through my joints, through my chest-sac, and into my throat. 

*Hhhhum-tse.*

The sound does not travel through the air. There is no wind to carry it. Instead, it travels through the stone of the basin floor, through the roots of the skiff-vines, and into the soles of the other Keepers miles away. 

It is a syllable of release. 

At the base of the tower, the giant indigo leaves tremble. The muscular vines, which had been clutching the black stone with a terrifying, dry enthusiasm, relax. They do not die—they cannot die in this garden of passive engines—but they peel back their velvet tips, sliding down the tower’s skin with a wet, heavy rustle. They retreat thirty feet, settling into the green moss, leaving the orange circuits of the lichen clean and clear to receive the running water ropes.

I do not take my hands away. 

I will stand here, pressed against the seamless needle, for three days while the sky remains the color of a bruised plum. I will feel the *Silt-Sinks* gliding below me, polishing the raw silicate into dark mirrors that reflect nothing but the violet glow of my side-fissures. I will feel the earth deep below—the young, hot basalt—trying to stretch its limbs, and I will feed the tower the frequencies it needs to heal the cracks before they reach the surface.

They call us the Keepers, but we do not own this place, nor do we rule it. We are simply the parts of the machine that have been given joints. We are the soft, dark thread that keeps the earth’s raw heat from shattering the builders’ quiet math, forever listening to the long, wet breath of a world that is content to never see the sun.
