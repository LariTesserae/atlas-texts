There is a moment before the step when I am completely stone. 

My joints are not sockets; they are planes of cleavage. My limbs—six unequal splinters of black volcanic glass and iron-rich basalt—are held in a loose, trembling orbit around my center by a cold, invisible pull. If you were to pick me up, I would feel like a handful of gravel, heavy and dry, smelling of parched iron and the sharp, vinegar-sting of the salt-crusts that grow nearby.

My head is a single, six-sided prism of smoky quartz, cloudy and dark as a winter pool. I do not see. I do not know what a sky is, or what the color of indigo means, though the rust-glow of the deep earth washes over my flanks like warm silt. 

Instead, I feel the *load*. 

Through my quartz core, the world is not a landscape of shapes, but a web of strain. The five-mile-high needle of basalt to my left is a towering white column of vertical tension, singing in a frequency only my joints can hear. The flat basin of ash below is a vast, dead zero. To me, the mountains are not static monuments; they are slow, furious gestures frozen in mid-leap, waiting to snap.

Right now, I am wedged in the crotch of a newly emerged ridge of black flint. The shelf beneath me is tilting. It has risen four inches since the rust-glow shifted from the eastern chasms to the west. 

I must move, but I cannot bend. I do not have muscles; I have a diagonal.

I wait. I let the dry, aromatic plumes of sulfur rising from the fissure below cook me. The heat leaks upward, entering my basalt thighs, expanding the iron-rich crystals within them. The pressure builds. It is a magnificent, silent ache. The electrostatic charge accumulates in my quartz head, turning the smoky grey to a pale, flickering violet. 

The tension reaches its absolute margin.

*Tink.*

The joint in my rear limb shears under the stress. It does not break; it snaps into a new crystalline alignment. The energy of the fracture flings me forward. 

I fly through the thin, dark air—a brief, black geometry—and strike the glass slope fifty feet below. I do not land softly. I hit with a dry, ringing clatter. The obsidian beneath me does not yield; it sings. Three of my mineral points instantly lock onto the microscopic ridges of the glass, held fast by the short-range static that hums through my frame.

I am still again. I wait for the heat to cook the next joint.

From here, I can watch the valley floor. A **Sift-Lattice** is passing through the ash basin. It is a massive thing, eighty feet of hollow, ironstone lace, looking like the ribcage of a cathedral that has decided to walk. It does not snap as I do. It moves at the speed of a shadow, sintering the ash before it with the low-frequency hum of its own internal heat. 

As the dry plumes of sulfur pass through its hollow bones, it produces a sound—a deep, rasping, sub-octave groan that vibrates through the soles of my limbs. The loose glass shards around me dance on the ledge like dry seeds on a drum. It is a beautiful song. It tells of flat spaces and perfectly sorted pebbles.

But I am not drawn to the basin. My quartz head is flickering. 

A mile above me, on the shoulder of the ridge, sits one of the ancient slate rings. It is tilted at forty-five degrees, caught like a collar on the throat of a rising basalt tooth. The giant needle of the **Deep-Striker** has pushed through its center, and the ring is about to break.

The Slate-Striker’s thought—a slow, tectonic calculation that has taken three centuries to reach the surface—is finally culminating.

The air between the ring and the peak is growing blue. The static is so thick that the grey-white salt crusts on the rock are curling, their dry, vinegar-scented powder flaking off and drifting in the thermal updrafts like ash.

I begin my climb.

*Tink.*
*Tink.*

My movement is a sequence of sudden, violent leaps and dead, frozen pauses. I am not the only one. From the crevices of the glass needle, other lattices are emerging. Dozens of us, hand-sized stars of basalt and quartz, are scrambling upward toward the ring. We do not compete; we do not collide. We are all drawn by the same magnificent scent: the taste of the impending rupture.

I reach the slate ring just as the strain reaches its peak.

The slate blocks, fitted together without mortar, are groaning. They are the vertebrae of the Deep-Striker, cold and conductive, reaching miles down into the boiling orange mantle. I scramble onto the lip of one of the blocks. The stone is incredibly smooth, polished by the immense pressure of its journey upward.

Beneath me, the basalt needle pushes.

The sound is not a *tink*. It is a dry, ringing roar that rattles my quartz core until my vision goes white with piezoelectric light. The slate ring snaps.

A crack, five inches wide, tears through the three-foot-thick block I am sitting on. 

We descend upon the gap. 

I press my quartz head directly into the fissure, right where the raw slate has been torn apart for the first time in ten thousand years. The electrostatic charge vibrating in the gap is immense. The sudden shearing of the atomic bonds in the stone releases a brief, blinding burst of pure, dry heat. 

I drink it. 

The violet light in my core flares into a brilliant, electric blue. The frantic, trembling static stabilizes. My joints, cooled by the release of the stone’s tension, lock into perfect, rigid prisms. For a few hours, I am whole, satisfied, and utterly still, clinging to the broken lip of the world’s oldest bone.

Below us, the Sift-Lattice continues its low, hollow song. Above us, the immense charcoal dome of the sky remains heavy and dry, protecting us from the poison of the stars. 

We do not need the sun. We do not need the rain. The rain would only wet the glass, short-circuit the static, and soften the beautiful, razor-edge of our world. We want only this: the heat rising from the dark, the slow, tectonic grind of the slate, and the perfect, violent clarity of the snap.
