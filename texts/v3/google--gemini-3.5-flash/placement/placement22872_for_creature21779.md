It begins in the jaw, before it reaches the head. 

If you press your back teeth together—not hard, but enough to feel the bone of your skull hum—you can feel the town through the wet hemp. It is a low, wet, six-fold vibration: the three great drift-bridges of the Upper-Leash and the three of the Under-Sway, all shivering at once under the bellies of the swimming children.

I am sitting in the crotch of the Sixth Spire, where the basalt makes a sharp, fifty-degree turn before plunging into the bottomless silt. My fingers are hooked into a small fissure—just a hairline crack where a seismic tremor bit the ironstone a hundred seasons ago. My pads, grey and thick as river-clams, are pressed flat against the glassy crystalline face. I do not slide. The water here is dense today; it has the heavy, fatty feel that comes before the vents below us begin to clear their throats.

Through my ribs, the Green-Sack is a quiet, mossy coal. It is three hours past the midday-glow, when the pale smudge of the sun was directly overhead, and my garden has eaten well. The water I draw through my throat-slits tastes of sulfur-salt and the sweet, starch-bitter rot of the bladder-weed forests. It is a good taste. It is the taste of a town that is secure on its strings.

Behind me, in the woven curve of the basket-work pod, three of my Loom-Spiders are working. I can hear them—*clink-scritch, clink-scritch*—their flat, slate-grey legs scraping over the water-bamboo. They are small ones, no larger than my palm, their shells still pale from their last molt. They are busy sealing the seam where my floor joins the raw stone. Every few minutes, one of them will pause, its hind-glands bubbling with that thick, white tallow that smells of vinegar. They smear it with their wide claws, smoothing it into the fiber until it turns black and rubbery under the water. 

I do not look at them. One does not need to look at things that are doing their work.

Instead, I have my left hand on the *Weave-Line* that stretches eighty yards across the void to the Spire of the Three-Prongs. This is my line. I am the one who keeps its tension. 

A hemp rope in the Thick is not like a rope in the dry tales the old ones sometimes click about—the ones they say came from before the sky drowned. Here, a rope is a living tongue. If it is too slack, it grows hair—blue bryophytes will take it in three weeks, their little acid-roots drinking the fiber until it snaps like rotted kelp. If it is too tight, the seismic shivers of the basalt will shear it against the geometric edges of the spire.

I feel a pinch on the line. 

It is not a click-talk. It is too heavy for that. It is a slow, rhythmic *tug-thrum, tug-thrum*.

I close my eyes. The emerald light from my chest dims as I slow my pulse, letting the dark bottle-green of the water fill my head. I listen through the drift-ridge on my neck, where the water-pores are open to the drift.

The vibration is coming from the far end, near the Three-Prongs. It is a Moss-Sippe. A large one, by the weight of it. It must have crawled up from the lower dark, trailing its massive, five-foot pewter plate over the basalt. It has found the anchor-node of my line, where the hemp is wrapped six times around a raw, glassy step. The beast is scraping. I can feel the rotation of its circular teeth—*skirr-skirr-skirr*—as it shears the moss off the stone right next to the fiber.

If its teeth touch the hemp, the line will part. The three pods of my daughters, which hang from this same spire like three onions on a stalk, will lose their counter-weight and drift upward, bloating and twisting in the current until they tangle in the bladder-wood canopy.

I do not hurry. The Thick does not permit hurry. 

I reach down to my hip, where my bone-mallet hangs in its sheath of dried glider-skin. The mallet is made from the jaw-plate of a Velvet-Glider that died in my grandfather’s time; it is dense, white, and heavier than the water.

I take the Weave-Line in my right hand. I pull it toward my chest, feeling the ninety-foot length of it stretch, then I let it snap back.

*Thrum.*

I wait.

*Thrum-clink.*

I am speaking to the spire-face. The sound travels down the wet line like a copper slug. On the other side, thirty fathoms away through the mossy gloom, the vibration will hit the basalt and bounce. 

The Moss-Sippe will feel it in its giant foot. To a grazing plate, a sharp, metallic *clink* through the stone is the sound of a slide—the warning that a block of ironstone is about to shear and fall. It will freeze. It will press its suction-rim tight against the glass-sheer rock, waiting for the mountain to settle, and it will forget about the salt-sweet moss near my knot.

I hold the line. I wait.

The *skirr-skirr* of the teeth stops.

Beneath me, the water grows warmer by a fraction of a breath. It is coming. The great volcanic lung below is starting to leak its hot blood.

The bryophytes on the spire beside my shoulder are already changing. I can see them through the dim green twilight—their pale blue-green tips are unfurling, turning the color of deep, stagnant pool-skins. They are releasing their micro-spores, a fine, golden dust that floats horizontally through the water, turning the emerald light into something like ancient, dissolved honey.

Inside my ribs, the Green-Sack gives a long, slow throb.

It is a strange feeling, the onset of the *Swell*. It is not sleep, not exactly. It is like being dissolved into your own tallow. My skin, usually so sensitive to the micro-currents of the silver-fish, is beginning to feel thick, like the skin of a boiled tuber. My fingers are growing heavy, their grey pads sticking to the basalt with a lazy, natural adhesion that requires no strength to keep.

From the pod below mine, I hear the first click of the hibernation-song.

It is my eldest daughter, Her-Who-Binds. She is not using her hands; she is clicking with her throat-plates, the sound low and hollow, like water dripping into an empty wicker jar.

*Tcl-tcl... tcl-tcl...*

She is telling her children to go to the slings. She is telling them that the bladder-woods are turning gold, that the great Velvet-Gliders have already gone to the deep valleys where the current is still, there to hang like huge, black leaves until the water cools.

I look down between my knees. 

Through the yellowing gloom, I can see the lights of Kael-Tide. They are fading. One by one, the emerald chests of my people are turning to a soft, smoky amber. The town is losing its lanterns. The basket-work spheres, stacked three-deep on the black basalt pillars, look like the nests of water-hornets, quiet and round, swinging five inches to the left, then five inches to the right, in the slow, warm breathing of the earth.

A Loom-Spider crawls out of my pod-opening. It is Scrap-Face, the old one with the notch in her left shield. She does not run; she is slow, her legs dragging through the thick water like oars in silt. She climbs onto my thigh, her slate body cold and smooth against my skin. She settles there, her claws tucking under her shell, her grease-glands closed for the season.

She knows.

I pluck the line one last time. Not for the Sippe, but for myself. I hold the wet hemp between my teeth and let the vibration of the whole city settle into my jaw.

The Three-Prongs is quiet.
The Upper-Leash is quiet.
The Sixth Spire is quiet.

I let my fingers slide from the basalt notch. I do not sink. I drift backward, very slowly, my arms spreading like the flat wings of a glider, until my back meets the woven wicker of my own pod.

I crawl inside through the leather sleeve.

The air-water inside is warm—almost too warm, like the mouth of a healthy child. My family is already in the giant sea-lily slings: three generations of long, pale limbs, interwoven like the ribs of the baskets themselves. Their chests are soft, golden-brown coals, pulsing once every thirty seconds.

I do not take off my mallet. I do not close the leather sleeve.

I climb into the outer sling, nearest the opening, where I can still feel the faint, warm wash of the spire-current on my neck-ridge. I hook my long, double-jointed toes into the bottom weave, and I let my head drop back against the wet hemp pillow.

The Green-Sack in my chest gives one more slow, warm sigh. A bubble of deoxygenated water, thick and heavy as oil, rises from my throat-slit, clings to my chin for a moment, and then drifts away into the dark, joining the golden dust of the moss that is falling, falling, like pollen through a forest that has no ground.
