If you were to search for me in this world of warm terracotta and silent ceilings, you would not find me among the high, twelve-foot walls where the shadow-shrouds are thickest, nor would you find me near the margins where the wax-ropes drip their heavy camphor. 

You would have to listen for a sound that is not the *click-click-shuck* of the porcelain beetles, nor the slow, hollow *tock* of the gourd-creatures. You would have to listen for the sound of a dry spade being drawn through dry salt. 

I am the one who carries the flint.

***

My body is older than the others. Where their skin has the smooth, matte surface of biscuit-fired pipe-clay, mine has begun to show the deep, fine craquelure of too many turnings. The lines run up my throat and cross my shoulders like a map of dry riverbeds. They are dark, almost violet, because I do not wear the shadows as shrouds; I use them differently.

My hands are long, five-jointed, and narrow. But where the other dwellers have nails of flat, tarnished bronze, my fingers are tipped with slivers of black obsidian. They are flaked to an edge so thin that they look blue-gray when held against the daylight. They are the only cold things in this entire courtyard, because they do not belong to the clay; they belong to the limestone that sits beneath.

And my face-dish—the smooth, concave circle where a face should be—is not whole. It has a single, clean vertical groove running down its center, like a channel cut by a scribe’s bone needle. When I stand under the white plaster ceiling, the light does not pool in my face as a perfect circle. It splits into two pale crescents that blink and shift as I tilt my head.

I am the Scorer. 

***

The walls of this world are of a certain thickness, built by hands that left their prints ten thousand years ago. But the walls are not dead. Across the three-foot terraces, where the clay has a high concentration of grog—those sharp, ground-up bits of older vessels—the masonry will sometimes begin to rise like yeast. It takes decades. A joint will widen; a brick will lose its sharp, clean edge and become soft-looking, like dough that has risen against a proofing cloth.

The other dwellers cannot hear this. They read the walls with their bronze fingers, but they read only what is already written. 

But I can hear the draft. 

Inside the hollow columns of our chests, we do not have hearts; we have a chimney-pull. When a wall begins to swell, the draft in the mortarless joints is choked. The passage of air becomes thin and high-pitched. When I hear that dry, whistling sigh change its tone, I walk to the swelling.

Yesterday—or perhaps a century ago, for the light here does not change—I found a swell in the southern terrace of the third common. The clay was the color of dried plums, warm and leather-hard. Through the gaps between the bricks, I could see the outline of a hand, the fingers long and folded, pressing outward from the interior of the wall.

Had I left it alone, the wall would have baked them. The dry, warm air of the courtyard would have set the clay while it was still trapped inside the courses, and they would have emerged only as a relief-carving, fused forever to the structure.

I knelt. My knees made a dry *crick*—the calcite is always trying to settle in my joints, to make me a wall—and I set my obsidian fingers to the seam.

I do not carve. The wall has already decided the shape of the one who is coming. I only release the tension. I slide my flint fingernails into the mortarless joints, tracing the contour of the rising shoulder, the curve of the smooth, featureless head. The raw clay behind the bricks is different from the clay we walk upon; it has the smell of a cellar that has been sealed since the first day—pale, heavy, and raw, though there is no wetness in it.

As I score, small curls of clay peel away from my flint. They fall to the terracotta tiles, where they instantly dry into small, white rings. The stoneware beetles gather these curls immediately, carrying them off to the waxy thickets to build their small, dome-like nests. 

By the third day, the new one was free. They slid from the wall, leaving behind a smooth, dark alcove that smelled of warm flour. They lay on the tiles, soft and unformed, their limbs slightly bent. I did not touch them again. The air did its work. Within three days, they were dry, starch-white, and silent. They arose, trailed their bronze fingers along the terrace to find their place, and did not look back at me. 

They cannot look back. They do not know who scored them.

***

To keep myself from setting—to keep these obsidian fingers nimble enough to release the others—I must tend to my own joints. 

The other dwellers collect the violet shadow-velvet to protect their skin from the unblinking sky. They wear them as turbans or heavy, draped cloaks. I do not wear them. 

Instead, I take the sharp-edged ribbons of shadow that have grown under the high walls and I carry them to the five-step limestone staircase. I lay them upon the third step. Then, using a round pestle of unglazed river-silt, I grind the shadow.

It does not crush into powder. Because it is a shadow, it resists. It breaks down into a heavy, violet-black grease that is warm and slightly fuzzy to the touch, like the skin of a dark plum. It smells of old metal and the bottom of a well.

I rub this grease into the fine craquelure of my wrists. I paint it into the joint of my neck, where the white clay has begun to stiffen, and into the creases of my five-jointed fingers. The grease does not sink in, but it coats the limestone-cleavage of my joints, allowing them to slide against one another without that dry, warning *scratch* that precedes the final hardening.

When I have finished, my hands and throat are stained a deep, violet-black, as though I have dipped them in the ink of some dry, ancient flower.

***

When the walls are quiet—when there is no draft whistling through a choked joint—I go to the loop.

I walk down the first step of the five-step staircase. I walk down the second, the third, the fourth. On the fifth step, I step off, and my foot lands on the first step again. 

I sit on the third step. The white limestone is different from the terracotta; it is cool, holding no bread-oven warmth, a reminder of the vast, silent basement upon which our world is balanced. 

I reach out behind me. Because the space is folded, my hand does not meet the empty air; it meets my own shoulder. I rest my hand there. I feel the grain of my own back. It is rougher than my front, still bearing the horizontal ribbing of the wall from which I emerged four thousand years ago, before I learned the use of flint.

I sit in this perfect, small circle of myself, resting my chin against my own shoulder. 

Soon, the hollow-horn comes. It is a beast of stitched red tiles, its neck a flared horn of dark clay. It knows I am here. It climbs the steps with its dry, clattering gait, its tiles clicking like loose shingles on a roof. 

It does not have eyes, but it find the loop. It thrusts its open, trumpet-like neck into the folded space behind my shoulder—which is also the space before my chest. 

Then, it breathes.

The whistle it blows is low, flat, and continuous. The sound does not travel outward into the common. It enters my shoulder, passes through my hollow chest—vibrating the draft-way inside my collarbones—and exit my front, entering the hollow-horn’s own flank. 

Through this vibration, I can feel the entire courtyard. I can feel the tension of every terracotta tile. I can feel if a scale has expanded by the width of a grain of salt, or if one of the low walls is leaning even a fraction of an inch toward the plaster sky.

We sit there—the beast, the stone, and I—measuring the geometry of our house. We stay for months, until the vibrations are smooth and the warm clay has settled back into its perfect, right-angled peace.

***

I know what my end will be. I will not return to the niche from which I came; that niche was filled by another long ago, and its bricks have grown together, seamless and cold.

One day, when the shadow-grease no longer keeps the calcite from my joints, my flint fingers will cease to bend. The obsidian will grow heavy, and my knees will lock into the limestone of the stairs. 

I will not fall. I will simply become the sixth step.

The others will descend. They will step off the bottom onto my head, looking down at their own heels, never knowing that the step beneath them was once the one who cut them free from the clay. And the hollow-horn will come, and it will press its warm, red-clay neck against my throat, and find the whistle still vibrating inside my chest, long after the draft has stopped.
