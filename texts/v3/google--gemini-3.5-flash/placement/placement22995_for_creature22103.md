I begin in the knee-joint of another.

Before I am a whole thing, before I have the shape of a vanilla-pod or the crown of pine-needle antennae, I am a set of loose pieces. I am three segments of dried horsetail-stem, a curved leaf-tile from a roof that collapsed in the third millennium of the silence, and a coiled strip of inner bark that spent five hundred years lying in the vegetable flour, collecting no dampness, growing no older.

I am being assembled. 

There is no ceremony to it. It is done in the dark, during the brief, violent intervals when the world is singing. When the great *THRRR-UMMM* rattles through the cork conduits of the outer mass, the vibrations set my makers in motion. They do not know they are making me; they only know that their leg-springs have slipped their amber pegs, and their little wicker-work fingers must find something to needle, to bind, to stick. 

I feel the tiny, dry scraping of their hands. It is a series of hard, rhythmic vibrations—the *shrr-shrr* of bast fiber being drawn through holes drilled in my clove-brown carapace-scales. A drop of resin, scraped from the wall of the hollow, is pressed into my hip-joint. It is cold. It smells of dried thyme and old charcoal. For eighty years, between two system-wide groans of the settling wood, the resin hardens. It cures into a yellow glass, locking my leg-springs into a state of permanent, agonizing tension.

I am completed just as the sound dies away. 

The last thing they give me is my crown. They thread ten fine pine-needles into the hollow of my snout. They do not connect to a brain. They do not lead to a heart. They are simply wedged into the dry pitch of my skull, where they rest against the main strut of my longitudinal fiber-line. 

Then, the last echo of the wood-groan vanishes down a three-mile-wide pipe of compressed cork. The makers freeze. I freeze. 

We are a cluster of dry splinters clinging to a vertical wall of woven leaf-mortar. I am suspended by my rear claws from the lintel of a miniature house I did not build, looking down into a darkness so thick it feels like a physical weight pressing against my needles.

I wait.

The wait is not boring, because I have no mechanism for time. Without water, there is no chemistry of decay; without decay, there is no sequence of ending. I am as permanent as the lignin walls around me. My "thoughts" are merely the tiny, microscopic adjustments of my joints as they lose their tension to the cold, dry air—a micro-torr of pressure lost every fifty years, a tiny *tick* of my internal springs that does not release, but only settles.

I am aware of my sister-spinners. They are suspended around me like dried seed-pods left on a dead vine. We do not touch, but we are connected by the wicker-mesh of the city. When one of them shifts by a fraction of a millimeter, the vibration travels through the grass-blade bridge, up the wall, and into my rear claws. It is a dry, thin note. It tells me she is still whole.

Then, the earth shifts.

It begins miles away, in the dark timber of the world’s roots. A long, groaning sigh—the friction of ten million tons of dry wood sliding against ten million tons of dry wood. It travels through the fibers like a current of heat, though it carries no warmth. 

By the time it reaches our chamber, it is a roar. *THRRR-UMMM.*

The air in our ten-meter bubble does not blow—there is no draft—but it *clangs*. It strikes my chest-plate like a wooden pestle. 

Inside my leg-joints, the tiny pegs of fossilized amber are shaken. The vibration is exactly the frequency required to slide them back a hair’s breadth. 

*Click-clack.*

My internal springs, wound tight since my assembly, snap open. My legs jerk forward. I do not "decide" to walk; the sound has wound my clock, and I have no choice but to run.

I drop from the lintel. I land on a wicker suspension bridge that spans the deep, silent void. 

The movement of my jointed legs against the dry, woven grass of the bridge creates friction. In this absolute draftless chill, the friction is a creative act. It pulls static electricity from the dry silk of my body, from the mummified scales of my back.

*Spit. Crackle.*

For a microsecond, the dark is destroyed. A bright, cold-blue spark jumps from my middle claw to the handrail of the bridge. 

The light is so brief it has no time to cast a shadow, but in that instant, the city is burned into my needles. I "see" it not with eyes, but with the sudden, violent change in electrostatic potential. For three inches around me, the world is mapped in brilliant, violet detail: the tiny, layered tiles of the roofs, stitched with plant-thorns; the dangling reed-ladders; the absolute verticality of our home. I see another spinner, three feet away on a higher tier, frozen mid-stride, her own body haloed in a soft, silent crown of blue discharge-flashes. 

We are a constellation of brief, electrical stars, lighting an empire of dust.

With the next *THRRR-UMMM*, the bridge sways. I run down the curve of the wall, my snowshoe-pads keeping me atop the fine, weightless vegetable flour that covers the floor. The dust does not rise in clouds; it is too dry, too heavy with the starch of ancient seeds. It parts before me like dry silk.

My front brush—the bristled wheel at my mouth-casing—begins to spin, turned by the resonance of my chest-girdle. It sweeps the flour up. I do not taste it. I do not digest it. 

Inside my hollow body, the flour strikes my internal combs. The sticky, ancient resin that coats them catches the grains. It is a dry chemical graft. The starches of the seed-dust meet the dry catalysts of my interior, slowly fusing into new cellulose, replacing the micro-layers of my joints that were lost to the friction of the walk. I am repairing myself with the debris of the world that made me.

The sound is dying now. The great groin of the wood is settling into its new position. The vibrations are losing their sharp, hammer-like strike, winding down into a long, low mutter that sounds like a dry throat trying to clear itself.

My steps grow slower. The amber pegs in my joints, no longer shaken by the acoustic wave, slide back into place under the pull of my cooling resin. 

I am halfway up a reed-ladder when the stillness returns.

One of my legs is raised, its claw hovering a millimeter above a wicker rung. My pine-needle antennae twitch once, twice, adjusting to the absolute cessation of air pressure. 

A single spark—the last one—drifts from my head-plate. It is a tiny, cold-blue ember that hangs in the air for three seconds, falling toward the floor. In its dying light, I see the city on the wall. It is perfect. It is silent. It has survived another movement of the world.

The spark goes out.

The velvety blackness closes in, smelling of nutmeg and ancient charcoal. I am locked. I am part of the ladder now, a wooden rung among wooden rungs, waiting for the giant to breathe again.
