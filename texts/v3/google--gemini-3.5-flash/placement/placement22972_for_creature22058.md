I would not be found upon the walls, nor in the high, mist-drowned crevices where the silt-weavers keep their neat piles of broken shell. I would not be among the silver-striders, whose lives are measured in the light, ticking clockwork of a few quiet seasons before they dissolve into the black pools.

No. To find me, you would have to go down to the very floor of the grotto, beneath the thousands of pale-spotted salamanders that pave the rocks. 

You would have to look beneath the first layer of slate-skins—those whose rubbery backs still catch the faint, blue-gray sky-sheen from the vertical cleft. You would have to look beneath the second layer, where the bodies are cool and dry of sky-light, smelling heavily of wet iron and cold lard. 

You would find me at the third layer. Pinned.

I am a slate-skin, but I am of the ancient, bottom-most shingle. My belly is pressed flat against a polished, kidney-shaped lobe of basalt that has not seen the twilight in ten thousand years. Above me, the weight of my brothers and sisters is an immense, comforting ceiling of cold, living marble. We do not jostle; we do not shift to escape the pressure. The weight is our security. It is back-to-belly, flank-to-flank, a vast, interlocking mattress of silent flesh that seals the basalt from the air.

At this depth, I have no need for eyes. My unblinking black pupils are pressed into the side of a decaying weaver’s rib, which has long since become as cold and hard as the peat. My world is not one of gray columns and shimmering water-globes. My world is a map of pure, physical registration—of vibrations that travel through the marrow of the mountain, of chemical tastes that seep down through the emerald-and-charcoal thatch above us.

I know the clock of this room by the pulses in my skin.

Every seventy seconds, my heart beats. It is a single, heavy *thump* in my chest, so slow that between each stroke, the blood in my capillaries almost forgets where it is going. It settles, chilling to the exact temperature of the wet basalt, before the next low muscle-shudder pushes it forward again.

Every ten minutes, the collective lung of our shingle expands. I do not breathe on my own; it is impossible under this weight. Instead, a wave of shallow, hydraulic expansion begins at the eastern edge of the pool and rolls through us. A hundred flanks rise together; a hundred ribs widen. My skin slides against the skin of the salamander above me—a brief, wet, lardy friction—and then we settle back down, pressing the air out in a single, hushed lisp that is lost in the thick liverwort carpet.

And then there is the hammer.

I feel the strike before I hear it. Because my belly is fused to the basalt, the vibration of the weaver’s anvil travels through the mountain’s bones. 

First, a tiny, needle-sharp prick of silver light—a vibration so clean it has a taste like zinc—passes through my lower jaw. This is the white quartz pebble striking the basalt table three yards away. 

A fraction of a second later, the sound itself arrives through the moss: a damp, muffled *thunk*, instantly swallowed by the green peat. 

*Clink-thunk.* 

I know when the shell has given way by the change in the vibration. The second strike is always softer, landing on the wet, yielding meat of the grease-coil. Then, the long silence returns.

Sometimes, the chemical taste of the world changes. A grease-coil, crawling on its own alkaline highway of mucus, will slide over a crack in the shingles directly above me. The mucus is thick and sour, slightly melting the ancient peat as it passes. Through my skin, I taste the copper-moss the snail has been eating on the high walls. I taste the mineral-milk of the ceiling-water. 

If the snail is small enough, and if it wanders down into the narrow crevice near my snout, I do not hunt it. Hunting belongs to the hot creatures of the lower forests—creatures with snapping jaws and burning blood. 

Instead, I simply let my lower jaw sink. 

It is a slow, hydraulic widening that takes nearly a minute. The space inside my mouth grows cold; the air-pressure in the crevice drops by a fraction. The snail, heavy and blind in its flat, tendon-colored shell, is drawn into my mouth by the light vacuum of the inhalation. 

I close my jaws. There is no crunch. I do not have teeth for breaking. The snail lies in my dark, wet throat, still tasting the copper-moss, while my stomach slowly—over the course of twenty days—begins to secrete the cold, iron-like acids that will dissolve its shell. 

During these three weeks, I do not move a single toe. My five-toed feet, with their tiny, black-clawed tips, remain hooked into the basalt pores, holding me to the mountain.

I have been here so long that the boundary between my skin and the stone is beginning to blur. 

Where my belly touches the kidney-lobe, the gray basalt is white with micro-veins of calcium—the lime from ten thousand years of dissolved snail shells that has seeped through the moss-strata. This plaster has begun to bind me. The skin of my abdomen is no longer soft; it has taken on the texture of wet slate, cold and mineral-hard, shot through with tiny, milk-blue veins that match the spots on my back. 

I am becoming the floor.

If a weaver were to climb down from its ledge—if its greasy, wool-white body were to descend the moss-ropes to find a lost quartz hammer—its long, blind snout-star might touch my back. It would taste the lard-scent of my skin, the iron-tang of my mucus. It would recognize me not as food, nor as an enemy, but as the ground. It would step upon my spine with its broad, leather-padded hand, using my body as a handhold to reach the pool, and I would feel the weight of its three-fingered grip as nothing more than a passing cloud—a brief, warm shadow that does not break my cold, everlasting sleep.

And when my heart finally forgets to take its seventy-second stroke—when the slow clockwork of my blood finally runs down to its last, quiet tick—I will not rise or move. 

The three layers of salamanders above me will keep me cold. They will press me down into the basalt, until my bones find the exact shape of the rock-hollows beneath. The silver-striders will skim over the mosses above my head; the youth will hatch in the deep gel-globes nearby, their feather-gills tasting the water that my decaying skin has softened. 

I will remain here, a gray shingle in the dark foundation of the world, while the water-lobes swell and fall, and the white stones break the gray shells, in the perfect, heavy, unbreathing peace of the cleft.
