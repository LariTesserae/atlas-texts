The dark is not empty. In the Oubliette, the dark is a weight that presses against the front of your face like a wet velvet glove. 

I do not have eyes to see it, but I know where I am by the taste of the air and the bounce of the stone-song. The air is old. It has been inside the lungs of three thousand Plinths before it reaches my chin; it is thick with the smell of hot wool, the acid-sweat of our hosts, and the sharp, clean stitch of ammonia.

I am a Scraper. My name, if the concept could be translated from the low-frequency humming we pass through the basalt, is *Three-Stroke-Falling*, because my ancestor’s stone was found three arm-lengths from the Sump after a great ice-storm.

Right now, I am wedged in the *cushion*—the narrow, warm crease where the leathery shoulders of two great Plinths overlap. The Plinth below me is called *The-One-Who-Sinks-South*. She is massive; her velvet skin is thick with grease, and through the soles of my three-toed feet, I can feel the slow, heavy *thump-thump* of her deep muscle-core. She moved perhaps half an inch today, sliding her massive suction-sole along the basalt to find a pocket of sulfur-salt. The movement was so slow it felt like the mountain itself was growing.

In my left lower hand, I hold my stone. 

It is a porphyry pebble, red as raw liver if there were any light to see it, but to me, it is simply *the hard thing*. It is forty generations old. Its edges are completely round, polished by the palms of my mother, my mother’s uncle, and the five who came before them. It is slightly hollowed on one side where my thumb fits. If I lose this stone, I am no longer a citizen of the chimney; I am merely a soft thing that clings until it falls.

To live here is to listen to the *rebound*.

I lift my upper right hand. The horn on my middle finger is long, thick, and worn to a sharp chisel-slope. I tap the basalt.

*Tock.*

The sound goes out into the void. It is a small sound, but I have three pits in my forehead that do not hear the wind; they drink the vibration. 

*Tock.*

The rebound comes back, and the world is built in my mind. 
The basalt wall across the shaft is seventy arm-lengths away; it is smooth, curved like the inside of a skull, slick with grease. 
Between here and there are forty Plinths, their charcoal hides overlapping like scales. 
On their backs are twelve Scrapers of our clan. I know them by the rhythm of their work. To the left, *Old-Two-Hammer* is working on a Grit-Barnacle. *Tock... tock... tock.* His pace is slow, the stroke of someone whose finger-joints are beginning to grow together, whose skin is turning dry and stiff. He is approaching the turn; soon he will climb up to the Freezer, toward the lightless throat where the Vanes hang.

But I am not going up. 

My work is down.

Below us, where the violet thread-liverwort grows thin and the basalt is too wet for the Plinths to hold, is the Sump. Most of our people do not think of the Sump. They think of the grit-grub, the warmth of the sealings, the dry roots we braid into ropes. But the Sump is where the stones go.

When the last Great Sealing came seven moons ago, the Vanes whistled early. The wind from the outer peak was a freezing knife that ran down the chimney, turning the wet basalt to glass. The Plinths shut their chasms with a sound like a thousand leather whips cracking. They slid together, compressing, hiding our houses—our only houses, which are the gaps between their ribs.

In the crush, *Silt-Leg’s* rope snapped. 

His rope was old root-vine, dried too many times in the sulfur-draft. We heard his stone strike the basalt three times as he fell—*clink, clink, clat*—and then the *plop* of the Sump. 

Silt-Leg is gone; the Flensing-Worms have already made him into water. But his stone is still down there. It is a green jasper, brought up by the ancestors before the chimney had a name. It cannot be left. If a stone is left in the Sump, the line of the family is cut, and the chimney grows colder.

I have been chosen to go down because my skin is still soft, my joints still slide like grease, and my rope is three strands of fresh root, braided with the hair of my grandmother’s back.

I slip out of the cushion. The air here is instantly colder. Without the warmth of *The-One-Who-Sinks-South*, my lard-pale skin begins to tighten, the blue copper-blood in my forearms slowing its pulse. My three-clawed feet find the wet basalt. 

I do not slide. I use the *chisel-horn* of my toes to find the microscopic pits the Plinth’s acid has left behind. 

Every step is a calculation of weight. 
One limb moves. 
I wait. Fifty heartbeats.
Another limb moves. 
The rope behind me hums. It is anchored fifty feet above to a bone-peg—the rib-bone of *Three-Stroke-Falling* the first, wedged into a cleft behind a Plinth’s lung.

As I descend, the violet light of the liverwort begins to change. Up high, it is a faint, clean smudge. Down here, near the water, the liverwort is bloated. It grows in greasy, white webs that smell of rot. It feeds on the dung of the chimney, on the shed skin-skins of the Plinths that slide down the walls. 

The light is a rotten purple, thick and oily. It does not help me see; it only makes the darkness feel wider.

I tap the stone.

*Tock.*

The return is flat. Wet. there are no Plinths here. The wall is bare basalt, polished by water until it is as smooth as my porphyry pebble. The water is close. I can smell it—it smells of old copper, dead skin, and the sweet, gaseous breath of the deep earth.

*Tap.*

The water is three arm-lengths below my feet. It is black, completely still. No bubbles rise. No steam. It is the cold pool at the bottom of the world, three miles deep, where the mountain drinks its own sweat.

I can feel them.

They do not make sound, but they are there. The Flensing-Worms. In the sound-map of my forehead-pits, they are things of *no-rebound*. My tapping stone goes out, and where the worms are, there is only a hole in the noise. They are soft, long ribbons of white skin, thicker than my torso, five times as long. They do not have bones to bounce the sound; they are only water within water.

They are waiting. They know that when something falls from the warm world, it comes to them.

I tie my rope around my waist three times, knotting it with the double-jaw knot my mother taught me when my horn-tips were still soft as fat. I take my porphyry stone and wedge it into the pouch-skin under my third arm. My other hands must be free.

I lower myself until my feet touch the liquid.

It is not like water. It is heavy, like melted lard, and it is cold—not the biting cold of the peak, but a slow, dead cold that makes my toes go numb instantly. If I stay here too long, my skin will begin to turn white and peel away like wet bark. The pool does not bite; it dissolves.

I let go of the wall.

I sink.

The dark is different now. It is heavy. The noise of the chimney—the ten thousand lungs *shuck-shucking*, the low purr of the Scrapers, the distant whistle of the Vanes—is gone, replaced by a deep, underwater *waw-waw-waw* that vibrates in my bones.

I reach my hands into the mud at the bottom.

The mud is soft, thick with the hair of dead Plinths. My finger-horns scrape through it. 
Nothing. 
Only the silt.
A worm brush past my shoulder. It is cold, slick, and completely silent. It does not bite me; it is only feeling me with its skin, testing if I am still moving, if the lard of my body is ready to be drunk. I strike at it with my heel. My toe-horn cuts its skin, and a puff of white, milky fluid clouding the water. The worm drifts back, lazy and patient.

I reach deeper.

My right lower hand touches something hard.

It is not basalt. It has three grooves.

I grip it. My three fingers wrap around the green jasper. It is smaller than my porphyry, but it is heavy, dense, and cold as the mountain’s heart.

*I have it.*

But the rope is tight. I try to pull myself up, but my arms are heavy. The water is already beginning to soften my joints. My blue blood is sluggish, barely moving in my wrists.

I hear them. Not with my ears, but through the rope.

*Tock.*

A single tap from far above. It is *The-One-Who-Sinks-South*, her massive body shifting on the basalt, her sole releasing a wet, sucking gasp. She is calling me back. Or perhaps it is my kin, pulling the line, their three-fingered hands working in unison, hauling the root-rope up through the dark, inch by agonizing inch.

The rope tenses.

My feet leave the mud.

The white ribbons of the Flensing-Worms brush my ankles as I rise, but they are too slow. They do not have the speed of those who hold the rope.

As I rise out of the cold lard-water, the warm, stinking breath of the chimney hits my chest like a brand. I cough, a wet, rattling sound, clearing the ammonia-grease from my mouth-plates.

I climb.

My hands find the Plinth’s velvet back. It is hot. It is the most beautiful heat in the world—the heat of ten thousand hearts beating together in the dark.

I wedge myself back into the cushion. I take the green jasper and tuck it into the root-pocket of my waist, beside my own porphyry.

*Tock.*

I strike the wall.

Across the shaft, the return comes back. They are listening.

*Tock.*

They know I am here. We are still holding on. The mountain is cold, the wind is howling at the lip, but here in the throat, we have the stones, we have the heat, and the rope has not broken.
