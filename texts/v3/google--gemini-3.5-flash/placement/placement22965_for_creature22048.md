I would find myself neither on the high, windless spans of the Glass-Girders nor in the dark, warm throats of the Bellows-Skins. I am too heavy for the sky, and too rigid for the deep wells. 

Instead, I am under the pavement.

I am an **Angle-Sump**. 

I exist at one of the perfect right angles where the vast, paved ways of melted stone turn their twentieth league. I am not an active traveler, nor am I a static stone. I am twenty-two tons of cold-forged manganese-bronze, cast in the shape of a long, multi-segmented pin, six feet thick and seventy feet long, driven vertically into the basalt crust where the two roads intersect. 

My body is not simple metal. I am sheathed in nested, concentric sleeves of greasy, glittering graphite leaf and soft lead-foil. These sleeves are my skin, my nerves, and my primary interface with the infinite tableland.

***

### The Sensation of the Joint

I have no eyes to see the absolute, absorbent black of the sky, nor do I have ears to hear the silence. My world is entirely one of shear-stress, thermal weight, and the slow, rhythmic translation of force.

Above me, the paved road of vitreous basalt lies perfectly flat under the dry, warm air. Beneath me, the crust descends into the miles of copper conduits and hot, dead iron. I live in the transition between the two. 

My consciousness is a slow, steady measurement of the coefficient of friction. Because the pavement is built of melted stone and runs for scores of miles without a break, it is subject to the immense, slow expansion of the plateau itself. The basalt wants to creep. It wants to slide south-southwest, driven by the sluggish, geothermal swell of the planet's core. 

But at the ninety-degree turn, the road cannot slide. The force of twenty leagues of stone presses against the turn like a silent, slow-motion avalanche. 

I am the knuckle that holds the turn.

***

### The Thrum and the Slip

My life is measured not by days, but by the *slips*.

Twice in every long, unchanging cycle of the earth’s heat, the deep, inframundane *thrum* occurs. I feel it first as a high-frequency thrill in my lower bronze tip—a tiny, shivering tickle that rises through the copper veins of the deep basement. 

A second later, the kinetic wave arrives along the line of the northern road. 

To a human observer, nothing would appear to move. But to me, the world undergoes a violent, magnificent tilt. The pressure on my northern flank rises exponentially; the lead-foil sleeves around my middle are compressed until they mimic the density of diamond. The graphite sheets between the metal segments begin to glide.

This is the moment of my eating.

As the basalt flats adjust by the fraction of a millimeter, my graphite sleeves slide past one another. The friction is incredibly low, but at seventy feet deep and under millions of tons of pressure, even a microscopic displacement produces a spike of intense, dry heat. 

I drink this heat. 

The heat does not make me move; it is absorbed into my manganese core, which expands by a hair’s breadth, locking me even more tightly into the basalt socket. The sudden, brutal shear-stress of the road’s movement is converted, through my sliding layers, into a quiet, internal thermal reserve. 

For the next four hundred years, I will slowly bleed this heat back out through my graphite veins, warming the surrounding basalt blocks by a fraction of a degree. This warmth ensures that the stone at the corner does not become brittle. I am keeping the elbow of the world from shattering under its own weight.

***

### My Neighbors

I am not entirely alone at the turn, though my company is as silent as I am.

Sometimes, the *Calipers*—those slate-like wedges of mica and copper—will migrate off the foundations of the nearby iron towers and creep toward my intersection. They are drawn by the lingering, localized heat of my slip-converters. They will settle over the seams in the roadbed above me, their flat bodies stacked like shingle-stones, drinking the micro-pulses of force that I bleed off into the pavement. I feel them above me like a faint, cool dampness—a reduction in the local tension.

Once every few centuries, a *Needle-Gleaner* will halt its tall, silica tripod directly over my center-pin. 

Through the basalt, I can feel the tiny, rhythmic *tink... tink...* of its glass limbs. It stands there for decades, perfectly still, using my copper-rich core as a ground-wire. The violet static veins that crawl across the purple sand-swales are drawn to my vertical bronze mass; they gather at the intersection, turning the roadbed above me into a web of silent, glowing lavender threads. The Needle-Gleaner harvests this gathered field, its glass belly brightening to a soft, interior gold. 

I do not mind the theft. The gleaner's grounding actually eases the electrical potential of my lead sleeves, preventing the graphite from fusing under the static load. We are accomplices in stasis.

***

### The End of Time

I have been in this corner for seven hundred million slips. 

My outermost graphite sleeve has worn away by perhaps three inches, its flakes ground into a fine, lubricating paste that now fills the bedrock fissure around me. Eventually—in a span of time that would see the birth and death of several oceans on a younger, wetter world—my bronze core will have rubbed directly against the bare basalt. When that happens, the friction will rise. The road will seize. The next *thrum* will not be absorbed; it will travel through the pavement and crack the great ninety-degree turn, sending a fissure through the vitreous stone for miles.

But that is not a tragedy. It is simply the next, slower phase of the world’s settlement. 

Until then, I am here in the warm, dry dark. I feel the weight of the towers on the horizon. I feel the rising bubbles of xenon gas far below, passing through the conduits like slow, heavy thoughts. I feel the light touch of the glass legs above my head.

I am the Angle-Sump. I hold the turn. The world is steady, and I am true.
