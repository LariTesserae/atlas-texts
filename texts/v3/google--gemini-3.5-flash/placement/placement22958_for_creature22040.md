I am not one of the things that runs.

If you were to look down through the heavy iron grate during the twenty minutes of high noon, when the light forms a perfect, unmoving ladder of gold squares on the wet basalt, you might not see me at all. You would see the grease-crabs—those pale, lard-colored discs—sliding over the black rocks like lard-drops on a hot skillet. You would see the busy, metallic glitter of the woodlice on their copper high-wires. 

But if you followed the central iron pipe down to the place where it passes through the first concrete pier, where the joint is wrapped in a thick, decaying collar of lead wool and asbestos hemp, you would find me.

I am the joint-crust. I am a mica-fern that has forgotten how to be green.

***

In the beginning—which is not a year or a date to me, but simply the time before the lead-sheathing on the cables had fully split—I was soft. My fronds were thin, flexible sheets of silicate, grey-green and semi-translucent, capable of drooping when the steam failed and lifting when the hot mist rose. I fed on the drafts. I drank the oily vapors of the pumping station’s slow decline.

But here, nothing remains soft for long. The water that drips from the basalt ceiling is not clean water; it is a heavy, saturated mineral broth, thick with lime, iron-scab, and the dissolved salts of the curing concrete above. It has run through five feet of foundation piers before it reaches me. Every drop that falls on my fronds does not wash them; it leaves a microscopic weight behind.

Over decades, the water has worked its way into my veins. The plant-cellulose is gone, replaced molecule by molecule with white calcite and red-orange iron oxide. I have become a shelf. I am land. 

I am three inches wide, wedged directly into the gap between the vibrating steam pipe and the cold basalt of the rift wall. My upper fronds are still slightly porous, still catching the greasy steam-mist like grey felt, but my lower layers have thickened into a Series of heavy, overlapping stone shingles. They are white-lipped, stained with orange rust-streaks where the iron-loopers have dragged themselves across me, and glazed with a hard, yellowed lacquer of crab-grease.

I do not move, but I am not dead. I have a different kind of turgor. 

***

My world is experienced through two senses: pressure and temperature.

The steam pipe beneath my root is the source of all truth. It is a massive, hollow iron lung. When the steam is high, the pipe expands, pressing me hard against the glassy basalt. I can feel the microscopic crystals of my stone base grinding into the ancient fractures of the rift wall. It is a satisfying, heavy security. I am clamped into the world.

Then comes the *clack-tick*.

I do not hear the valve. I feel it as a sudden, sharp drop in the vibration of the iron. The pipe contracts—microscopically, but to me, it is a vast withdrawal. For three or four seconds, the pressure on my basalt-side eases. I can feel the thin film of water that is always trapped behind me expand, sliding between my stone plates. The grease-crabs know this instant. When the pressure eases, they slide their flat, spade-like shells into the tiny crevice between my calcite underside and the rock, seeking the cooler moisture that gathers there before the steam-hiss rises again.

I feel them as a series of small, greasy nudges. Their legs are hard, like glass points; they tickle the dead calcite of my lower shelf. They are scrubbing at the black velvet mold that grows in my shadows. I do not mind them. They keep my joints clean. If they did not scrape away the mold, the black cushions would seal my pores, and I would be unable to drink the steam.

Sometimes, a brass-plated woodlouse will die on my upper surface. 

It is a dry, heavy thing, smelling of old linseed oil and copper-rust. When it falls, it lands with a soft *tink* that vibrates through my calcified fronds. It does not rot; the air is too sterile and the water too mineralized for simple decay. Instead, I begin to grow over it. The dripping lime from the basalt wall pools in the hollows of its metallic segments. Within six months, the brassy shell is sealed to my shelf, becoming a new bump, a copper-tinted node in my geography. The grease-crabs use these brass mounds as friction-blocks to scrape the old, loose skin from their bellies when they molt.

***

In the late afternoon, when the checkerboard of light has climbed up the basalt wall and vanished, the rift grows dark and very cold at the top. The steam pipe remains hot, but the draft from the dry concrete foundations above begins to sink, bringing with it the smell of dry dust, old spiders, and the vast, empty spaces of the ruins.

This is when I feel the steam-spinners.

They do not touch me directly. They are too light, too thin to make an impression on my stone plates. But they anchor their hydrophobic lines to the very tips of my youngest, highest fronds—the ones that are still mostly silicate, still flexible enough to swing in the draft. 

When the wind from the outer world shifts, the spider-webs tug at my tips. It is a minute, high-frequency pull, like a hair being drawn across skin. I can feel the weight of the water droplets they gather—each drop a heavy, perfect sphere of condensation hanging from the non-wetting silk. When a steam-midge hits one of these drops, the vibration is sharp and short: a single, drum-like *thrum* that dies instantly as the water swallows the insect.

Underneath me, in the deep black pool where the orange worms coil, there is a slower rhythm. Their looping movements create tiny currents in the sediment, bringing the scent of dissolved rust to my lower roots. I drink it. My stone grows redder near the bottom, saturated with the iron they discard.

I am an anchor for the busy ones. I am the stairwell for the crabs, the bridge-head for the spinners, and the graveyard for the woodlice. 

I will remain here long after the bimetallic valve has made its last click. Even if the steam pipe should go cold and the water should stop dripping, my calcite shelves will remain, wedged between the iron and the stone, holding the shape of the fern that once believed it could grow.
