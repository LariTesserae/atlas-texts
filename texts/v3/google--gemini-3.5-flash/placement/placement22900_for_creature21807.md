It begins not with a name, nor with a thought, but with a tension. 

There is a thin, tough skin that lies over the still water of the calcium basin. I am suspended upon it. To the eyes of an outsider—though there are no eyes here that see as an outsider does—I would look like four hair-thin wires of glass, bent at elegant angles, resting their tips on a sheet of black ice. 

The ice is not cold; it is the temperature of a dying fever, though compared to the heavy, sweltering air of the dome, it feels like a cool thumb pressed against an eyelid. Under my front feet, the surface tension of the water does not break. It sags. It forms four tiny, shallow dimples in the liquid, like heavy oil under pressure. Through these dimples, I hold the pool.

For a long time, I think I am only the spider. I am the Ripple-Watcher, light and wire-thin, waiting for the shudder of a drowning gnat. 

But if I try to lift my rear legs, I find they do not belong to me anymore. 

Behind me, where the limestone rim of the basin meets the damp, black loam of the grotto floor, my body has ceased to be a separate thing. My four hind legs are no longer translucent. They have turned the flat, chalky white of root-fibers. They are wrapped in a fine, glowing sleeve of lace—the Weave. 

The white mycelium has grown up through my joints, not with the violent hunger of a parasite, but with the slow, searching intimacy of a vine finding a trellis. It has filled my hollow segments with its own damp humours. It has stitched me to the stone. I cannot leave this pool; I can barely turn my head. I have been traded. In exchange for the food the Weave brings me through my back legs—the sweet, dissolved essence of fallen fern-fronds and rotten wood—I have given the Weave my senses.

I am the translator. I am the ear the earth has pressed against the water.

***

In the warm twilight, the world comes to me as a series of concentric circles, expanding and colliding.

A droplet letting go of a stalactite forty feet above is not a sound; it is a sudden, sharp *pinch* on the surface of the basin, a column of force that strikes the water and sends a ring of shivering rings outward to die against my front legs. I count them: *one, two, three, four.* The fifth ripple is always eaten by the moss at the edge.

Further away, at the basalt terrace, the Slate-Backs are working. I feel them through the limestone bedrock beneath my basin. Their travel is a low, heavy tectonic grinding—the grate of basalt on basalt as they clear the channels. To me, it feels like a deep, rhythmic itch in the stones, a slow hum that rises through my anchored legs and vibrates the water from below, making the tiny calcium crystals at the bottom of my pool dance like salt.

Sometimes, one of the Amber-Wells will come to sit on the very lip of my basin, just inches from my face. 

The frog is so clear I can see the blue-grey shadow of its heart beating behind its ribs. When it speaks, it does not make a sound, but its amber throat-sac swells. It is like a small coal being blown upon in the dark. The pulse of its light does not touch my eyes—for my eyes are only tiny black beads that tell the difference between the green light and the dark—but I feel the *warmth* of the light. The amber glow has a faint, distinct thermal weight. It warms the skin of the water directly beneath the frog’s chin, causing a microscopic convection current. The water swells toward the heat. I feel that tiny, warm lift. I pass it down my white, tethered legs into the Weave: *The light-singers are gathered at the third well. They are pulsing at three-second intervals.*

The Weave receives this. Deep in the loam, the white threads shift, directing more nutrients toward the roots of the monocots near the third well, anticipating the waste the frogs will leave behind. 

An engine, ticking in the dark. And I am one of its valves.

***

As the hours drift—or perhaps the days, for there are no days here, only the rising and falling of the dampness—I find that my mind does not stay within my blue-glass body. 

Because I am stitched to the Weave, and the Weave is stitched to everything else, my identity has begun to leak out through my back legs. It is difficult to tell where the spider ends and the fungus begins. 

When a leaf falls from a strangling fig across the chamber, I do not just hear the impact of its landing. I feel the instant, microscopic prickle of the white threads rising to meet it. I feel the hunger of the mycelium as it secretes its clear, digestive acids, dissolving the leaf’s veins, turning the tough green skin into a sweet grey milk. I taste the leaf through a million invisible mouths three hundred yards away, while my spider-eyes still watch the emerald light shaft in my own small corner.

And when the great light comes—the brief, warm moment when the sun far above passes over the jagged fissure—the world ceases to be about the ground at all.

From my pool, I feel the air pressure in the dome drop. The air becomes thin and buoyant. The Felt-Wings, sleeping like dry leaves in the high roots, begin to stir. I can feel their awakening as a sudden, vast absence of weight in the canopy. 

Then, they let go.

They do not fly; they drift. Hundreds of them, rising into the green cylinder of light like ashes from a cold hearth. The velvet down on their wings absorbs the heat of the light, lifting them higher and higher toward the crack in the earth. For a brief hour, the silence of the dome is filled with the *touch* of air. The rising of so many bodies creates a draft—a tiny, cool wind that sweeps across the floor of the grotto.

For the first time, the surface of my basin is not still. It is ruffled by the wind. 

The ripples come from everywhere at once, chaotic, beautiful, crossing and recrossing one another until the water looks like a sheet of hammered silver. My front legs twitch with the sheer volume of the news. The world is moving. The world is rising.

Beneath me, the Weave hums with a deep, quiet static, drinking in the rain of dust and scales that falls from the courting moths above.

Then, the sun passes. The emerald light goes dim and cool again. The Felt-Wings drift back down, their brief lives near their end, their eggs already laid in the decaying logs. The draft dies.

The water of my basin settles. The silver turns back into black, liquid ice.

I adjust my four front legs, feeling the surface tension tighten and hold. I am still here. My body is small, and my legs are thin, but I can feel the whole dome sleeping in the dark. I can feel the Slate-Backs scraping the stone, the Ghost-Lungs writing their silver maps on the marble, and the Weave, vast and patient, digesting it all.

I wait for the next ripple. I have nowhere else to go.
