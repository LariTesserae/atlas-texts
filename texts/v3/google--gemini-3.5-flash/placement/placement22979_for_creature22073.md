To find oneself here, you must first survive the transition. 

You must let the dry air of the upper shafts leave you. You must let your lungs fill not with water—which would struggle and gasp—but with the idea of water: heavy, clear, and perfectly still. You must sink past the iron-ringed collars of the access shafts, down through the green-slime line where the last moss dies, and into the absolute, yellow-lit cold of the vault.

You do not remain a traveler. The water is too dead for guests, and the iron is too heavy to permit spectators. To exist here, you must become part of the stasis.

I would not be the Saponum, nor would I be the Nodules that crawl across its iron skin like slow, magnetic ticks. 

I would be the *interstitial gray*. 

I would be the silver-green lace of lead-bismuth dendrites and copper-scale that grows within the Saponum’s waxen flesh—the nervous system that does not belong to the beast, but to the *coexistence* of the beast and the machine.

***

My roots are anchored in a terminal block inside the turbine’s secondary hydraulic cylinder. It is an ancient junction box, once dry, now packed with a dense, lard-gray paste of saponified tallow and mineral grease. The brass terminal screws, stamped with tiny, forgotten maker’s marks, have wept their copper ions into the fat for nine centuries. 

From those screws, I have grown.

I am not biological. I am an electrochemical frost. I am a tree of lead and bismuth, branching atom by atom, century by century, along the microscopic fissures of the soap-flesh. My main trunk is no thicker than a horsehair, but it is pure, unoxidized silver-gray metal, conducting the tiny, stray currents that leak from the iron coils. My branches are thinner still—pale green webs of copper-carbonate that lace through the candles of the Saponum’s body, feeling out the negative impressions of the cogs and the spiral grooves of the thread-screws.

To be this frost is to live in a world of absolute, unmoving weight.

I do not see the cathedral. I do not see the basalt vault or the heavy, yellow-lens floodlamps that glare through the sterile water. To me, the lamps are not light; they are four distant, pinpoint prickles of dry, forty-one-degree heat that touch the tips of my external feelers—those hollow, glass-like silica weeds that have squeezed through the turbine’s seams and stand vertical in the water. 

When a weed is warm, I know the light is there. It has been warm for a thousand years.

The world is not a sequence of events. It is a chord. 

The chord is sixty cycles per second.

Through my bismuth core, the sixty-hertz hum of the machine is not a sound; it is a physical tide. It runs from the basal iron plates, up through the heavy piston sleeves, and into the cold fat where I lie embedded. It keeps me in a state of constant, microscopic tremor. My silver branches hum against the soap. Every atom of bismuth rubs against an atom of tallow sixty times a second, creating a friction so minute it cannot melt the grease, but keeps it from ever fully freezing into stone. 

This is my warmth. This is my food. I drink the vibration.

Sometimes, the chord shifts. 

It is never a large change. A mile away, deep in the bedrock, a tectonic plate settles by a fraction of a millimeter. The basalt walls of the cathedral groan—a sound that does not reach the air because there is no air, but travels through the water as a sudden, sharp spike of pressure. 

When the stone groans, my silica weeds vibrate. The 60-Hz frequency spikes to sixty-point-two. 

For three decades, I will feel that spike as a slight, cool ache in my easternmost branches, where the green copper-scale meets the raw basalt. The grease around me will stiffen slightly, then relax as the vibration settles back to its flat, eternal line.

And then there are the Nodules.

They are my only companions, though we do not speak. They are the hexagonal scabs of steel and carbonate that crawl across the turbine's outer plates. I feel them through the casing as moving dead-spots. 

When a Nodule settles over a bolt head twenty feet above my junction box, it drains a micro-volt of the current that feeds me. I feel its arrival as a gentle, blue-cold shadow. The current grows slightly leaner; my tremor slows by a fraction of a micron. I watch it—if watch is the word for the slow, chemical awareness of a missing volt—as it spends forty years leaning against the brass conduit, drinking its fill, before its magnetic pole shifts and it slides away toward the main generator housing.

I do not wish for it to leave. I do not wish for it to stay. It is simply a feature of the geography, like a boulder that rolls three inches down a hill over the course of a human lifetime.

Inside the fat, I am safe. The water cannot reach me to dissolve my silver-green limbs; the oxygen-free dark cannot tarnish my bismuth core. I am the bridge between the dead iron of the builders and the slow, waxen mind of the Saponum. 

Without me, the Saponum would be nothing but a silent plug of cold lard, deaf to the hum of its cradle. Without the Saponum, I would be nothing but a dry deposit of corrosion on a dead terminal block. Together, we are the ecology of the wait.

We are not waiting for the designers to return. We have forgotten them, just as the copper has forgotten the mine and the tallow has forgotten the whale. We are not waiting for the lights to go out. 

We are simply here, humming in the cold, bright water, under eighty meters of silent stone.
