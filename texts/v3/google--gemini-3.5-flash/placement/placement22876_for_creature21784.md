I am five hundred meters below the first green-rusted catwalk of Segment Eighty-Two, nested in the hollow curve of the great bronze support bracket that holds the axle of the Western Gear. 

This is the Lobe. It is my home, my workshop, and my church.

If you were to look for me, you would not see me. To an eye accustomed to flat horizons and empty skies, I would appear as nothing more than an unusual burl of wet lichen, or a thick, dark knuckle of grease that has accumulated in the angle of the bronze rib. My skin is the color of old boiler-scale—a mottled, oil-slick gray that has ceased to look like flesh. I have painted myself three times this season with the paste of the parakeet-lichen, and by now the lacquer has bonded with the dust of the coal-ash. I do not skin-shed; I crust.

My feet are tucked beneath me, my heels locked into the groove of a massive iron rivet. My toes are long, thick-jointed, and tipped with spade-like nails that I have filed down to fit the curvature of the bolt-heads. My hands are larger than my head. My fingers are permanently curved, like the iron hooks the old ones used to hang the steam-lines. I do not have to think about holding on. To hold on is the default state of my muscles; to let go would require a conscious, agonizing effort of the will.

I am an *Adjuster*. Our family’s station is the Western Gear, the slow five-story wheel that turns once every twelve hours. 

The machine-roar is not in my ears. I have no ears to speak of; when I was seven drops of the great boiler (which is how we measure our summers), my mother filled my canal-ports with a plug of lead-solder and spruce-resin. If she had not, the thrum of the four-thousand-decibel heat-exchangers below would have turned my brain to gray water before my first beard grew. 

Instead, I hear through my bones. 

Right now, my jaw is pressed hard against the bronze bracket. The metal is warm—about the temperature of a fresh-killed steam-owl—and it vibrates with a deep, purring *shudder-shudder-shudder*. To me, this vibration is a clear, cold language. 

The brass housing at the center of the gear is running dry. I can feel the tiny, high-frequency "cough" of the iron spindle as it drags against the sleeve. It is a light, dry tickle in my left cuspid. If I do not attend to it, by tomorrow the tickle will become a hot, grinding ache that will vibrate through the entire Western wall, warning the other Silt-Skins that the Adjuster of Segment Eighty-Two is lazy.

I reach down into the pouch hung beneath my breastbone. The pouch is made from the cured stomach of a glider-bird, tough and greasy. Inside is my **leak-cake**—a dense, yellow lump of tallow, beeswax, and crushed graphite. 

I do not walk to the axle. I do not even crawl. I *flow*. 

My body moves along the bronze rib like a loop of heavy rope. I swing my hips outward, my backward-pointing thighs hook-clamped to the next horizontal flange, my hands reaching ahead to grip the thick, mossy vines that wrap around the steam-conduits. The air here is so thick with rising steam that I can taste the cedar oil from the distant pumps on my tongue. It is sweet, heavy, and hot. It fills my lungs with a comforting weight. 

As I reach the gear’s center, a tap-lizard—the length of my forearm—skitters out of the moss-bed near the axle. It stops, its pale, translucent body shivering against the iron. It does not fear me; I am too slow, and I smell of the same mutton-grease as the machine. It lifts its tail and strikes the iron plate: *clack-click-clack*.

I feel the sharp tap directly in my elbows. 

*The water is rising three segments down,* the lizard’s heels say. *The steam is wet today. The green is fat.*

I do not tap back. I do not have the tail for it. Instead, I take my brass rod—my **tapper**—and strike the bronze rib twice. *Thud-thud.* 

*I am grease-fixing,* I tell the wall. *Keep off my rib.*

The lizard disappears into a clump of broad-leafed ferns, its pale tail-light flickering through the green shade.

I reach the axle-sleeve. The five-story gear looms above me, its massive teeth choked with the thick, spongy green lichens that turn its movement into a silent, crushing crawl. As I watch, one of the massive teeth—each as large as my home-hammock—moves three inches. It presses against the brass housing. A slow, thick drop of parakeet-sap oozes from the crushed lichen, smelling of pine and ancient, rotting forests. 

I press my mouth against the metal. I lick the brass to taste its temperature. It is bitter, hot, and dry. 

I take a lump of the leak-cake and stuff it deep into the grease-cup at the top of the spindle. I use my heavy wooden pestle to ram it down, packing the tallow into the hot crevices until the machine’s own heat begins to melt it. 

Slowly, the vibration changes. The high-frequency tickle in my left cuspid fades. It is replaced by a low, greasy slide—a soft, wet *slosh-slosh* that vibrates in my clavicle. 

The gear is happy. The machine is quiet.

I settle back into the moss-bed at the axle’s base, my knees drawn up to my chin, my fingers interlaced around my ankles. I am completely secure. 

From here, if I look outward—away from the wall—the world ceases to exist. There is only the gray-white mist of the central void, illuminated by that pale, shimmering daylight that none of us has ever reached. 

Through the fog, thirty meters away, I can see the three black cables. They are perfectly still, held plumb by the unimaginable weight of whatever hangs below us in the dark. 

A Cable-Saddle is sliding down the middle wire. From this distance, it looks like a long, dark tear-drop, moving with a heavy, regular pulse. It is probably half a kilometer down from its launch; it will be another two days before it passes into the hot, dry boiler-mouths below and fills its belly with gas to rise again. I watch it for an hour, or perhaps two. We do not have hours here; we have the *crawl* of the teeth and the *drip* of the condensation.

Some of my people—the young ones, whose skin has not yet hardened—wonder what is at the end of the fall. They think of the bottom of the chimney as a place where the iron ends and something else begins. They talk of a grand machine that eats the water and spits the steam, a thing of gold and fire.

But I know better. To look down is to look at the same plates, the same rivets, the same bronze ribs, descending forever into a heat that eventually turns the moss to ash and the blood to steam. To look up is to see the same plates, rising into a cold that turns the steam to ice and the skin to stone.

There is no "end." There is only the Conduit.

It is thirty meters wide. It is ten thousand kilometers high. It is green, wet, and loud. It is the only place where a hand can find a grip, and therefore, it is the only place where there is life.

I close my eyes. I slide my jaw down against the dry brass of the Western Gear. 

The thrum of the world enters my skull—a massive, rhythmic heartbeat that has been beating since before the first moss took root on the iron, and will beat long after my lacquer skin has been pressed into the teeth of the gear, becoming grease for the wheels of Segment Eighty-Two.

I am home. I am wedged tight. I am not falling.
