I am not in the deep, folded miles where gravity spins like a spider’s silk. I am at the lip of the threshold. 

If you were to look close enough at the dry alcove near the front of the fissure, just behind the little bowls of grey grease, you would see a small, irregular nodule of cloudy white silica. It looks like a mistake of the forge—a drop of glass that fell and cooled before it could find a proper shape. 

That is me. I am *The One Who Stopped Clicking*.

I am an elder of the Seam, though the word *elder* has a different weight when your blood is made of oil and your skin is made of sand. In our youth, we are all scythe-fingered. We are all twitching joints and rapid, frantic taps; we run along the ceiling of the grotto, our suction-cupped feet making a sound like dry grass in a wind. We grease the worms, we snatch the drifting globes, we carve the obsidian into little teeth. 

But as the seasons of the lower world turn—seasons we only know by the subtle shifting of the mountain’s internal drafts—the oil in our carapaces begins to slow. It thickens from violet water to violet honey, then to a dark, sluggish paste. The joints of the lower four limbs fuse first, locking together in a permanent, four-pointed stance. Then the segments of the chest melt into one another, hardening from flexible armor into solid, vitreous stone.

We do not die. We simply become part of the room.

My lower legs are gone now, lost into the basalt ledge beneath me. I have grown into the mountain, and the mountain has grown up through me, drinking the last of my organic salts and replacing them with black, glossy iron. I am six thousand meters above the valley, and I am anchored to the very bone of the sky.

But my antennae are still alive. 

They have grown long—far longer than they were when I was a scuttler. They are two great, feathery plumes of spun-glass wool, draped out of my stone skull like frozen willow branches. One of them hangs down into the hot, sulfurous dark of the three-inch crack, feeling the thermal breath of the deep colony. The other hangs outward, over the lip of the fissure, dangling into the great, empty hollow of the sky.

The young Tenders do not mourn me, nor do they leave me behind. In our garden, nothing is wasted.

I have become the Anchor.

At this moment, three separate nursery-globes—bubbles of boiling water the size of bird's eggs—are tethered to my stiffened left needle-finger. The young Tenders brought them to me yesterday, spinning their sticky grey worm-silk round and round my stone wrist before tying the lines to the floating spheres. I can feel the bubbles tugging at me. It is a tiny, upward pull—the light, buoyant weight of steam wanting to rise into the cold sky. 

Through the tethers, I can feel the larvae shifting inside the bubbles. They are tiny, glass-plated things, no bigger than a grain of soot, swimming in circles within their weightless, boiling oceans. *Twitch. Slide. Twitch.* Their vibrations travel down the silk threads, through my stone arm, and into my quiet core. I know exactly when they are hungry; I know when the crimson hydras inside their globes have grown too thick and need to be pruned.

With my active antenna, I send a slow, heavy pulse down into the stone of the ledge. 

*Clunk.*

It is not the bright, metallic *tink* of a youth’s tool. It is the deep, muffled groan of the mountain itself. The young Tenders six feet away, scraping the orange bacterial mats, stop their work. They feel the vibration in their foot-pads. 

*The third cradle is cold,* my pulse tells them. *The thread is slack. Bring grease.*

Soon, two young ones emerge from the steam. They are beautiful—the violet oil in their veins is so bright they look like walking stars. They crawl over my back, their suction-cupped feet clicking against my dead shell. It does not hurt; it feels like the rain that never falls here, a light, rhythmic drumming. 

They do not speak to me with tools. Instead, they use their tiny needle-fingers to gently scrape the yellow sulfur crust from my right antenna, keeping it sensitive to the drafts. They apply a smear of the grey mortar-paste to the joint of my remaining free arm, keeping the stone-rot at bay for a few more turns of the earth. In return, I hold their nurseries. I am the post to which their world is tied.

And I am their watchman.

Because I sit at the very edge of the Seam, I can feel the "Outside" in a way the scuttlers in the deep labyrinth cannot. 

I feel the sky. It is a vast, cold pressure that has no ceiling and no walls. It smells of ice and long-dead leaves—things that have no place in our hot, wet uterus. Occasionally, when the storm-clouds below us part, I can feel the immense, terrifying temperature drops of the great heights. 

I feel the "White Death" before it arrives. 

I can feel the cold air falling from the zenith, sliding down the black glass column of the mountain like a heavy, unseen knife. When I feel that first, needle-thin chill touch the tips of my glass wool, I do not panic. My heart beats only once every hundred clicks now; I have the patience of the basalt.

I send the warning deep. I strike the stone with my one free limb, a heavy, rhythmic cadence that travels yards into the impossible, folded space of the inner labyrinth. 

*Hide the herds. Draw in the globes. The sky is coming.*

Through my stone feet, I feel the colony react. Hundreds of tiny feet scuttle backward, retreating into the three-inch seam where the earth’s blood is thickest. The grease-worms are pulled into the cracks; the tool-bowls are tucked away; the silk lines are cut, and the floating nurseries are hauled down into the deep, gravity-less wells where the cold cannot reach them.

By the time the first flake of snow—that bizarre, six-sided crystal of frozen water that we know only as "The Falling Glass"—drifts into the mouth of our fissure, the grotto is silent.

The snow does not melt on me anymore. I am too cold now; my surface temperature has matched the stone. The white flake lands on my shoulder, sitting there like a tiny, silent star, before the hot sulfur steam from the deep cracks reaches out and dissolves it into nothing.

I will sit here for a long time. 

Perhaps, in some distant age, my violet oil will stop completely. The last soft thing inside me will turn to quartz. My antennae will break off in some great gale, and the young will use my hollowed-out head as a new bowl for their grease. 

Until then, I am the anchor. I hold the lines of the living. I listen to the deep furnace of the world, and I tell the children when it is time to hide from the sky.
