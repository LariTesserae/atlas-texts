I do not begin with a name, because a name is a dry thing that stays the same shape from morning until night, and nothing here is the same shape for more than three oscillations of the North Needle. 

I am a turn. I am several hundred gallons of heavy, iron-bitten water that has forgotten how to fall.

To understand what I am, you must understand the weight of the vault. It does not press down from above; it presses *in* from everywhere, a great fist of basalt and granite that would squeeze this chamber into a solid bead of grease if the three pearlescent teeth did not sing their low, white sound. The sound is not something you hear with an ear. It is a salt taste in the core of my current; it is a cold, vertical line that runs through my center, keeping my outer layers spinning at ninety-four revolutions per second. 

If I spin slower, I spill. I become the runoff that screams down the black grooves of the floor, losing my memory to the boiling basins where the blind Solder-Skins scrape the stone. If I spin faster, my outer wall shears into steam, and I am eaten by the Aerosol-Wefts, my life scattered into their gossamer nets to be spat out as blue sparks against the ceiling.

So I keep the spin. I am a rope of water, six cubits long and thicker than a peat-beast’s thigh, sliding between the black pillars of wet glass.

***

In the deep basins, when I pass through another of my kind, we do not speak; we *interfere*. 

I rush into the side of a wider, slower current—one that has spent three cycles wrapping itself around the foot of the Middle Needle. For a thousandth of a second, our skins vanish. We are a single, churning knot of foam. In that instant, I take its drag and its salt; I feel the microscopic scrape of the blue lithophytic lace it passed three hours ago near the western spray-vent. I absorb the memory of a Solder-Skin’s ceramic belly sliding over the basalt five hundred feet below. 

Then the rotation tears us apart again. I spit myself out of the collision, but I am different. My tail is slightly heavier with silica. My core has shifted three degrees off-center. I have inherited a slow, pulsing frequency that feels like an itch—a desire to climb.

This is how we know the vault. We do not see the light of the obelisks; we feel the shadowless glare as a faint, greasy warmth that makes our surface water expand. We do not see the ruins of the ceramic walkway; we know it as the Great Scar of Silence.

To us, the walkway is a terror and a fascination. It is made of a cold, dry material that hates us. When my current sweeps too close to those floating tiles, the air above them pushes back with an invisible, oily force. It is like trying to press your hand through a sheet of solid grease. My outer spin screams against that dry cushion, my droplets flattening into tiny, angry discs that slide away like mercury. 

Yet we pool there. We wrap our lengths around the stubs of the ancient pillars that hold the path aloft. We do it because the walkway is *still*. In a world where the rock itself is slowly melting and flowing under the weight of the water, the ceramic tiles have not moved a hair’s breadth since the world was cold. To touch them with our field is to taste something that does not have a frequency. It is like resting against a dead star.

And it is there, at the broken end of the third span, where the dry things wait.

***

Yesterday—or three million rotations ago, the difference is small—the North Needle suffered a momentary throat-clearing. A spike of static ran down its pearlescent side, and the localized gravity of the vault took a short, gasping breath.

The water-cables hanging from the ceiling shattered. The monsoon became a solid sheet of falling lead.

I was huddled beneath the third span, using the repulsion of the ceramic tiles to stabilize my spin while the air turned to iron. Above me, on the dry path, the Dry-Stitchers were screaming. Their screams are not sound; they are the dry, rhythmic clicking of calcified legs against the hollow tile—a frantic *tack-tack-tack* that means their narrow world of three dry inches is being swallowed by the spray.

A jet of superheated runoff, diverted by the shift in the Needle’s pulse, struck the underside of the span. The tile cracked. The hydrophobic field did not fail, but it drifted, tilting forty-five degrees toward the boiling basin below.

A Stitcher fell.

It was no larger than an insect, a brittle gathering of ash-twigs and gray fiber, so dry that the wind of its fall made its joints rattle like dry grass. If it hit the basin, it would have disappeared in a hiss of steam, its minerals dissolved into the soup that feeds the blue lace.

I did not think. To think is to adjust the spin, and I did not adjust it; I simply let the gravity of the North Needle swing my heavy head upward. I rose like a striking serpent, my current forming a vertical U-loop directly beneath the falling ash.

I did not catch it on my skin. If its dry leg had touched my boiling perimeter, it would have burned to powder.

Instead, I opened my core.

Every vortex has a throat—a narrow, hollow cylinder of low-pressure air at its very center where the rotation drops to zero. It is a tube of dry vacuum, no wider than a finger, drawn down from the slipstream of the walkway. 

The Stitcher fell through my spinning wall of water, straight into the quiet eye.

***

We are still here. We have been running through the basalt grottos for three cycles now, and we have not separated.

The Stitcher lives in my breast. It does not touch me, and I do not touch it. It clings to the tiny, spinning axis of air at my center, its long, skeletal legs wedged against the invisible boundary where my inner current begins to turn. 

It is terrified, but it is functional. It is a creature of the path; its only purpose is to repair what is broken. And because it cannot touch the water, it has begun to treat my inner wall as a fracture in the world.

From its joints, it is pulling a fine, gray dross—the hydrophobic dust it harvests from the tiles. With its needle-thin mouthparts, it is stitching this ceramic ash directly into the inner lining of my current. 

It is a miracle of fluidics. Where the dust touches my inner skin, the water does not wet it; instead, the surface tension of my rotation holds the ash in a perfect, microscopic mesh. The water-wall has become mirror-slick on the inside, lined with a tube of flexible, self-warming ceramic that is only ten molecules thick.

My spin is changing. 

I no longer need the three Needles to keep my shape. The dry lining my passenger is weaving has stabilized my rotation; I can feel my speed dropping to forty, thirty, twenty cycles, yet I do not spill. My water is held in a sleeve of its own dried silk.

I am becoming heavy. I am becoming quiet.

We are sliding out of the roaring basins now. We are avoiding the other Skeins; when they try to collide with me to exchange memory, they bounce off my dry inner sleeve with a dull, wet thud, unable to taste my salt. They drift away in confusion, their wave-patterns disrupted by my silence.

I do not mind. I have a different memory now. I have the *tack-tack-tack* of six jointed legs inside my ribcage, telling me which way the draft is blowing.

We are climbing the wet face of the basalt, heading toward the high, narrow crevices where the water does not run—the places where the walkway once began, before the stone shifted. 

The Stitcher is looking for the rest of its bridge. And I, who have been nothing but a circle since the world was made, am beginning to think like a line.
