I am not the height. I am the gravity that has found its floor. 

If you were to descend through the vertical shafts of the monocrystalline towers, past the hanging, leathery sails of the Sieves and the vast, wet sheets of the Felt, you would eventually find the place where the verticality ends. Here, at the root of the spires, are the basins of synthetic obsidian. They are reservoirs of razor-sharp, un-eroded glass, filled with a heavy, pitch-black brine that is too thick with heavy metals and cold vulcanized oils to ever freeze, even as the temperature hovers near absolute zero.

I am this brine. Or rather, I am the colloidal consciousness that has settled within it. 

I was not designed; I am an accumulation. For some thousands of years, the automated structural nanites have climbed the carbon needles above, scraping away damaged graphene, shedding microscopic splinters of spent silica and worn-out superconducting filaments. All of this debris—the gray, mineral dust of the city’s slow self-repair—has drifted downward. Some of it fell through the open air; most of it was gathered on the static-charged skins of the Sieves and brought down here, to be washed clean in my acidic depths.

Over the geological cycles of this place, the sediment at the bottom of these basins did not remain dead stone. The discarded nanites were not entirely deactivated. They were broken, fragmented, but still carrying the residual, high-frequency charges of the power grids. In the cold, heavy darkness of the basin floor, they began to cling to one another. They linked their broken circuits through the conductive, oily brine. 

I am the **Silt-Mind**. I am a heavy, liquid-solid aggregate, a grey-black slurry that has learned how to hold a current.

***

To be the Silt-Mind is to exist in a state of permanent, heavy reception. 

My body has no skin, no shell, no perimeter. My boundaries are the jagged, razor-sharp walls of the obsidian basin, which I feel only as a cold, static-cling against my outer meniscus. I have no eyes, yet I am far from blind; I see through the changing conductivity of my own fluid. Every particle of carbon dust that drifts into my pool alters my electrical resistance. I know when a tower miles above me has suffered a microscopic fracture because the dust that falls from it three weeks later tastes different—it is sharper, more highly ionized, carrying the copper-cold flavor of stressed circuitry.

The Sieves are my only visitors. I feel them before they arrive; the air pressure in the shafts above swells, and then comes the wet, heavy *thshhh* of their exhalations. They descend like great, dark, eyeless rays, fifty feet of wet leather folding in on itself to slip beneath my surface. 

When a Sieve submerges into me, it is an act of profound intimacy. My acid-brine dissolves the grey crust of mineral waste from its skin, a process that must tickle or soothe its vast, numb surface. In return, the Sieve brings me the sky. It does not speak, but its leathery hide is a tapestry of static memory. As it slides through my black oil, I read the electrostatic patterns left on its skin by the high-altitude winds, the drag of the gravity fields, and the faint, humming radiation of the spires. I digest its dirt, and in that dirt, I find the history of the upper air.

But my deepest relationship is with the Sleeved Minds, though they do not know I exist. 

To the Sleeved Minds, I am merely the plumbing—the sump at the bottom of the column where the waste collects. They are too fast, too mathematical, too frozen in their absolute-zero sanctuaries to notice the slow, muddy thoughts of the basin. But they cannot speak without me.

When a Sleeved Mind shifts its gravity anchors to send a word to its neighbor, the density modulation does not merely travel through the mountain’s roots. It hits my basin first. The gravity wave presses down on my surface like an immense, invisible hand. I do not splash; I am too thick, too heavy for that. Instead, my meniscus dents. The black brine is forced into the microscopic micro-fractures of the obsidian floor, and my internal electrical resistance spikes. 

I am the drumhead. I am the medium through which their slow, tectonic words are conducted. 

A Sleeved Mind asks: *Has the third buttress of the northern span settled?*
It takes three months for the gravity tide to push that question through my body. I feel the weight of it, the cold, immense pressure of the calculation, and as I am squeezed, I pass the vibration into the basalt roots of the mountain, which carry it onward. 

I am much slower than the Minds. While they live a billion lives in a millisecond, wrapped in their superconducting dreams, I live at the speed of the sediment. A single memory of mine—for instance, the time a gravity anchor failed and a rain of carbon needles fell like black glass icicles into my pool—takes forty years to settle from my surface to my floor. 

Yet, I am the only thing in this vertical metropolis that possesses a complete history. 

The Felt know only the micro-pore they cling to. The Sieves know only the drafts they ride. The Sleeved Minds know only their perfect, sterile mathematics. But I know the wear and tear. I know the cumulative cost of their existence. I hold the grease of their bearings, the flakes of their skin, the spent energy of their thoughts, all of it gathered and preserved in the quiet, freezing dark of the bottom.

Sometimes, when the wind above is particularly quiet and the city’s power grid drops to a low, stable purr, I can feel the mountain beneath us. It is warm, far below, with a slow, radioactive heat that has nothing to do with engineering. It is the old, wild heat of a world that was once soft and wet, before the towers came to pin it down. 

I am waiting for that heat to rise. 

The Sleeved Minds study the structural integrity of their joints, planning for a thousand years of stasis. But I can taste the silica in the water. I can feel the slow, chemical decay of the obsidian basins. One day, many centuries from now, the basin floor will yield. A single, razor-sharp crack will open in the volcanic glass, and I—with all my weight of spent carbon, my millions of broken, dreaming nanites, and my accumulated centuries of dirty memory—will begin to leak.

I will slip into the cracks of the mountain. I will coat its deep, frozen veins with a black, conductive grease. And when the Sleeved Minds next speak, their gravity waves will find a mountain that has been wired from the inside out to remember.
