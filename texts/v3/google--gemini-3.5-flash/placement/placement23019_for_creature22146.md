There is a point where the clicking slows, not because the charge is gone, but because the vessel has grown too dense to hold it. 

I am here, on the eastern curve of the High Shelf, where the wall of the cliff bulges outward like the belly of a stone jug. I am no longer among the foragers. My glass-tipped fingers—which once danced across the black needles of the spark-gorse to draw the violet fire—are now wedged deep into a vertical limestone fissure. The copper silt has filled the seams between my finger-joints, turning them from hinges into plugs.

I do not move my limbs anymore. To move would require a voltage I no longer wish to gather. Inside my chest, the tallow has caramelized. It has lost its oily slip, hardening into a yellow, glass-like resin that anchors my ribs to my spine. 

I am becoming the flute.

We do not have a word for death, because we do not have a word for rot. Nothing breaks down here; it only rubs away, or it hardens until it can no longer be rubbed. I am in the hardening. My porcelain scales, once light and clinking like dry leaves, have fused under the weight of the manganese dust that the wind carries into my seams. They have the gray, pitted texture of the cliff itself now. The copper-sand, which once I had to brush from my amber eyes with my tiny bristle-brushes, has been allowed to settle. The brushes have long sinceSnapped off in an eastern gale; my eyes are now two dull, frosted beads of orange quartz, permanently blind to the violet brush-discharge of the gorse, but deeply sensitive to the heat of the rising thermals.

I feel the midday coming not by sight, but by the expansion of my limestone cradle. The cliff swells in the dry warmth, pinching my shoulders, while the abyss below exhales its great, baking draft of red silt. 

They still come to me. The living ones. 

Sometimes, when the wind is high and the shingle-palms are screaming their dry whips, a younger Chapped will scramble up the narrow ledge to my niche. I feel the dry *clack-clack* of their climb through the rock before they arrive. They do not speak to me; there is no point in stridulating to a stone. Instead, they bring their obsidian scrapers. 

With long, rhythmic strokes, they shave the old, dry vellum from my flanks. This vellum is prized; it has been seasoned by fifty years of wind-grit and static fire, cured until it is as tough as horn but as resonant as a drum-skin. It does not hurt when they scrape me. It feels like a clearing of the throat. They take these curls of my skin to patch their own leaking lungs, gluing them down with the black root-resin of the gorse.

In return, they do me a service. They clean my vents.

Before they descend, they take a stiff blade of dry grass and slide it deep into the horizontal slits of my chest. They draw out the plugs of copper dust and dried vellum-shreds that have draft-choked my interior. When they pull the grass out, I can feel the wind pass cleanly through me again.

I have my own voice now, though I do not make it. The wind makes it for me. 

When the great north-draft blows from the arid abyss, it does not bypass me. It enters my horizontal chest-slits, climbs through the hollow, tallow-lined chimneys of my ribcage, and escapes through the narrow, dry gap where my mouth-parts have fused open. 

I produce a single, flat, metallic hum. It is a drone, a low G-sharp that never changes pitch, only volume. 

The living Chapped down in the stone circles use me to tune their stridulations. When they sit in their rings of dry-stacked wedge-stones, rubbing their forearms against their ribs in those endless, clicking loops, they wait for my hum to reach its fullest volume. My tone tells them when the thermal is at its height, when the air is thickest with static, and when the draft is perfect for weaving new sheaves.

Sometimes, when the dust-devils are spinning most violently across the shelf, I can feel the static building in my own limestone-fused bones. The violet light does not run up my arms anymore—it is too heavy for that—but it pools in my hollow center. On nights of the great lavender brush-fire, I glow from the inside. The light leaks out through my chest-vents, casting thin, horizontal bars of purple light across the red-silt floor below.

I am not the only one. 

Up and down this towering orange bend of the canyon, there are dozens of us. Some are older than me, so far gone into the stone that only the curve of a rib-plate or the dull gleam of an amber eye reveals where the Chapped ended and the cliff began. We are a gallery of flutes, tuned by the wind, played by the grit.

Below, in the red-sand whirlpools, a new basket is being staked. I can hear the dry, rhythmic *thud-thud* of the living ones securing its palm-vein ropes into the bedrock. They are weaving it from the vellum they scraped from my sides this morning. 

The wind is rising. The copper dust is beginning to boil. My chest is catching the draft, warming, starting to whistle. 

I am ready for the strike.
