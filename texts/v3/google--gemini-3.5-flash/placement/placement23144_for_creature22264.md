I would find myself forty feet above the sumps, on the shoulder of the third concentric ring, where the oldest towers begin to lean slightly toward the basalt cliff. 

I would not be an observer. Down here, there is nothing to look *at*, only things to be pressured *by*. 

I would be a **Lithic Weaver**, but one whose joints have begun to collect more silt than tallow. 

***

### 1. The Weight of the Grey

My world is not one of sight, but of weight and skin-drag. 

The four dark nodules on the front of my head do not see the sky; they know only where the grey is thickest and where the cold basalt rises to choke it out. To me, the light is a heavy, grease-colored wool that presses down from the gap above, growing thinner and wetter as it nears the black pools of the sumps. 

My body is forty stone of wet horn and bristled slate. I smell of cold mutton fat and the bitter, starchy rot of digested clubmoss. Under my belly, my six legs do not feel like limbs; they are heavy, grey spades that slide over the calcified lime with a dull *scritch-slide, scritch-slide* that vibrates through my own central plate. 

I am very old, which means I am very quiet. 

The younger Weavers—the small ones, no larger than a common dog—scurry on the higher skyways, their bristles still short and stiff, their glands spitting bright, wet tallow that smells like hot tin. They move with a frantic, clicking cadence because every whistle of the wind through the lower alleys makes their carapaces itch. They are still trying to plaster the whole world.

I have stopped trying to plaster the world. I only plaster what rubs against my flank.

***

### 2. The Singing of the Seam

Right now, there is a draft. 

It is not a wind, but a thin, icy needle of air that has found a way through the base of the chimney behind me. A white-furred Mew must have gouged the lime with its thumb-claw while climbing to its roost, exposing a tiny channel of bare stone. 

To a human, it would be a silent draft. To me, it is a high, dry scream that rattles the three hundred lateral bristles along my left side. The vibration is like cold sand being rubbed into an open sore. 

I do not think: *I must repair this.* I only feel the itch of the scream. 

Slowly, using the weight of my three left spades to pivot, I drag my bulk around. The peat moss beneath me does not crackle; it yields with a wet, silent *give*, the cold water welling up around my leg-joints. 

I reach down into the cleft between the chimney and the basalt wall. With my short, wet pedipalps, I scoop a mass of decayed star-moss and river silt. The silt is cold—so cold it makes the nerves in my mouth-slit ache—but it is rich with the mineral grease of the high flats. 

I begin to chew. 

I do not have teeth; I have hard, horizontal ridges of yellow horn that grind the moss-roots against the basalt sand until they are a grey, gluey mash. My lateral glands—the only warm part of my body—begin to secrete the pearly binder, mixing it with the mash in my throat. 

When the paste is thick and smells of wet iron, I lean my head against the whistling crack and regurgitate. I use the flat, hard spade of my front-right limb to smooth it down, plastering the mix over the basalt until the needle of wind is blocked. 

The scream stops. My bristles go slack. 

The world is quiet again. 

***

### 3. The Guests in the Bristles

As I lie still, waiting for the paste to cure in the dripping air, I feel the small, hot spikes of the Silt-Runners. 

There are four of them living in the hollow where my rear carapace overlaps my third pair of legs. They are tiny, round balls of grease-soaked fur, no larger than my mouth-slit, but they are hot as embers. Their long, splayed toes—thin as pine needles—scratch at the dry plates of my back. 

One of them slides down my flank, its tiny rubber pads clicking against my scales. It reaches my second joint, where the "silt-rot" has begun to form—a dry, powdery fungus that smells of vinegar and makes my joints stiffen during the freeze. 

The runner’s chisel-teeth make a faint, rhythmic *snip-snip-snip* as it cleans the joint, eating the grey crust. The tickle is distant, like something happening to a stone three feet away, but it is a good sensation. In the deep-night, when the mist turns to frost and my breathing slows to a single, wet gasp every ten minutes, these four tiny lumps of heat will be the only thing keeping my rear joints from cracking when the dawn melt comes.

I let my body sink three inches lower into the peat. The water rise around my lower rim is icy, but it keeps my bristles from drying out.

***

### 4. The Transition

I will not move from this chimney-shoulder again. 

My rear legs have not slid since the last full freeze; the silt and my own dripping tallow have settled into the spades, gluing them to the basalt plaza that a Limpet-Lurker cleared for us three seasons ago. The red-lichen has already begun to bloom in the shadow of my third thigh—rust-red patches that are feeding on the mineral runoff from my shell, hardening into metallic iron-scabs that bind my legs permanently to the masonry.

I am not dying. I am simply becoming foundation.

Tomorrow, or the day after, a younger Weaver will crawl over my back. It will find my plates cold and silent. It will feel the draft that rises between my dead carapace and the chimney wall, and it will begin to plaster my margins with fresh, wet mud. 

By winter, my slate bristles will be completely filled with river silt. The moss will take root in my joints. The Silt-Runners will find a new, warmer pocket in some younger builder, and I will be just another eight feet of grey, wind-blocking buttress on the third ring.

I can feel the stone-talk of the white birds on the roof above me now—the low, gravel-mixer rumble that means an eagle is crossing the high gap of the highlands. 

I do not pull my legs in. I cannot. I simply flatten my front spade against the floor, press my mouth-slit into the wet peat, and let myself become the silence.
