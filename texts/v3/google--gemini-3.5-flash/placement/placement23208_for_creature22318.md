I would not be found in the brick-walled rooms of the middle tiers, where the dining tables swing on copper chains, nor down in the thumping darkness of the flywheel shafts where the deaf grease-keepers live. 

I would be found higher up, where the iron begins to thin and the raw, unpolished basalt takes its place. 

I am suspended in the high, yellow-amber glare of the three-needle gap. I am an **Auscultator**—one of the few whose lungs have grown wide enough to taste the vacuum at the world’s lip, and whose eyes have learned to look directly into the mirror of the peaks without weeping.

***

### The Harness

My home is not a place with walls. It is a three-pointed sling of oiled glider-skin, suspended from three separate stay-lines that span the gap between the needle-points of *The Sisters*. Below me is two thousand feet of empty, heated air; above me is the pale, bleached nothingness of a sky that has never seen a cloud large enough to shade a man. 

I do not move with steps. To move is to adjust the brass buckles of my harness, sliding my weight from one iron cable to the next with the rhythmic, agonizingly slow *drip-clink* of my carabiners. 

My body is long, even for a Needle-born. My limbs are like the iron rods of the gravity-elevators—thin, dry, and knotty at the joints. My skin has the grey, matte finish of lead pipe that has been exposed to the sulfur-mist for fifty years. It does not sweat; instead, it secretes a dry, salt-like grease that keeps the razor-edges of the basalt dust from cutting into the meat of my thighs.

My left hand is larger than my right. The fingers are permanently curved, the skin there so thick with boiled felt-scabs and calluses that I can slide them along a rusty guide-rope at twenty miles an hour without feeling the heat. On my right breast, directly over the heavy, deep-thump of my three-lunged chest, hangs my *tine*: a three-foot bar of heavy, cold-rolled brass, notched at intervals of one-sixteenth of an inch.

***

### The Amber Filter

I pull my glare-lids down.

The world changes. The screaming white reflections off the wet obsidian cliffs—reflections that could burn the retinas out of a low-lander in three minutes—are instantly smothered. The landscape becomes a vast, silent composition in shades of honey, tea, and old grease. 

Through the amber film of my third eyelid, the city below me looks like a dark, calcified growth. I can see the steam. It does not look like vapor; through my glare-lids, the volcanic exhaust is a thick, golden grease that rolls lazily out of the cracks in the basalt, clinging to the brick towers like honey before it is torn away by the high thermal drafts. 

I can see the Mute-Sails. They are moving three hundred yards below my feet, three of them, side by side, their leathery wings catching the draft from the sulfur-mines. They do not flap. They are so still they look like iron plates suspended by invisible wires. One of them tilts its wing by a fraction of an inch; it rises, passing through my gap so closely I can smell the hot-tallow odor of its skin and see the tiny, salt-white mites crawling across its leathery nostrils.

We do not look at each other. In this altitude, looking is an expenditure of focus, and focus is the only thing keeping us from the drop.

***

### The Sounding

I am here because *The Sisters* are talking.

The central needle of the three—the one we call *The Spindle*—has been crying since the last shift of the flywheels. It is a sound the ground-dwellers cannot hear. It is too low, a vibration that enters through the soles of my felt boots, travels up the dry marrow of my shins, and settles in the back of my teeth like an ache.

I unclip my brass tine. 

I do not tap the iron stay-line. To tap it would be to introduce a high, false note into the mountain's throat. Instead, I lay the flat of the brass bar against the main tension-cable—a cable three inches thick, made of twelve hundred braided strands of iron wire that runs from my sling down to the five-ton eyelet driven into the heart of the basalt.

I press my forehead against the cold brass of the tine.

The vibration is immediate. It smells of sulfur and old rust. 

*...shhh-rruuummm... shhh-rruuummm...*

It is the signature of the mountain’s knees. Far below us, five miles down through the black glass, the earth is still soft. The magma is cooling, shrinking by millimeters, and as it shrinks, the basalt needles are being drawn down into the socket. *The Spindle* is leaning north. It is leaning by perhaps two inches every twelve hours, putting three hundred tons of extra shear-stress on the northern stay-line.

I feel the wire under my forehead. It is too tight. The individual strands are not sliding against one another; they are locked, frozen together by the salt-frost that has crystallized in the gaps between the braids. If they do not slip, the cable will not creak; it will snap. And if the northern stay snaps, the three brick towers of the High Gate will tilt thirty degrees into the gap before the Turnstile-Men can even reach their bars.

***

### The Knot-Talk

I raise my right hand toward the watch-tower on the nearest needle. The tower is a tiny brass tooth, half a mile away through the amber haze.

I do not shout. My voice would be lost in the empty sky, torn to throat-husks by the thermal currents.

Instead, I make three quick, heavy motions with my wrist—the **Knot-sign** for *Salt-Lock*. 

I pause, letting my hand hang in the air like a dead bird, then make the sign for *Twelve-Turn northern slack*.

A long, slow beat of my heart—the deep, slow-throbbing pulse that we share with the flywheels.

From the slit of the distant watch-tower, a small copper disc catches the sun. One flash. *Received.*

Now, we wait.

The Turnstile-Men will be assembling. I can picture them in my mind: twelve men and women, their backs like raw slate, stepping onto the massive bronze platform of the Northern Cap. They will be inserting the pine poles into the capstan. They will be spitting into their palms—thick, grey spit that tastes of yeast-loaves—and they will be leaning their weight into the wood.

I keep my forehead pressed to the brass tine.

The mountain continues to pull. The iron stays around me hum with a dry, electric tension. A Salt-Spider, no larger than my thumb, crawls out of the crevice where the cable meets the basalt. Its clear, silica legs rattle against my brass carabiner like dry grass. It begins to spin, its spinnerets drawing a hair-fine line of liquid glass across my harness strap. The line hardens instantly in the dry air, a tiny, brilliant white thread that reflects the gold of the afternoon.

I do not brush it away. It is a good sign. The spider only spins when the cable is still.

And then, it comes.

The vibration through the brass tine shifts. It is no longer a smooth, agonizing pull. It is a series of deep, wet, subterranean groans.

*Thrung... thrung... thrung...*

One mile below, the Turnstile-Men are walking. The stay-line slips through its leaded collar. The salt-frost between the braids is crushed, falling away from the cable in a sudden, silent shower of white dust that looks like snow, though it is hot and tastes of brine.

The wire relaxes. The tension in my teeth fades. 

The three brick towers of the High Gate, invisible behind the shoulder of the needle, have just risen three inches back toward the vertical.

***

### The Golden Hour

The sun is hitting the western face of the gap now.

The world through my glare-lid becomes impossible to describe. The black obsidian needles are no longer stone; they are columns of dark, liquid fire. The wet grease-slick that coats them runs down the valleys in long, vertical rivers of copper light, pulsing with the rhythmic vibration of the great pumps far below.

The air is so thin I can feel the individual cold molecules scraping the back of my throat with every breath. I draw the warm, sulfurous steam from the pipe-vent near my left sling into my lungs, holding it there until my blood is dark and full.

I am nineteen thousand feet above the earth’s cooling core. I have no family that does not live in a sling; I have no earth to walk upon; my boots will never touch anything softer than iron or sharper than glass. My dead ancestors are drifting below me in the hot rifts, their skin turned to leather, their bones turned to salt, flying without wings.

I reach down, detach my brass tine, and slide my harness three feet along the guide-rope toward the next needle.

The mountain is young. The iron is heavy. There are forty-two cables between here and the sunset, and every one of them is beginning to sing.
