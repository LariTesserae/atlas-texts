First, there is the pressure. 

It is not an assault; it is a permanent condition. It does not feel like being crushed so much as it feels like being thoroughly, permanently held. To exist here is to have no space between oneself and the world. Every surface is an interface.

If you were to search for me, you would not look along the tracks where the Derricks click and slide, nor would you look up into the indigo vaults where the Condensers hang like heavy grey fruit. You would look lower, beneath the vast, parallel pipelines of the root systems, down where the black rubbery bark of the trees directly meets the ancient, wet shoulders of the basalt ridges.

I am **The Lay**. 

I am not a single creature with a front and a back, nor am I a colony of discrete cells. I am a sprawling, fibrous mat—a laminate of dense, oil-saturated cartilage, three inches thick and half a mile long, pinned between the colossal weight of the pumping forest and the unyielding stone of the earth. I am the slide-plate. 

Above me, the root-pipes—warm, ribbed, and trembling with the hydraulic thrust of sap—move with the slow, lateral expansion of centuries. Below me, the basalt is cold, wet, and slick with the fine, black grease of old decay. Without me, the grinding of the vulcanized wood against the stone would produce a screaming friction, a dry heat that would charred the root-systems and halt the pumps. I am the grease-pad. I am the interface that allows the engine to slip against itself without tearing its own joints apart.

My body has no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the black grease of the under-root. It has no mouth, because I do not eat; I absorb. The high-pressure runoff of the sap-flow, rich in heavy starches and long-chain lipids, leaks from the microscopic fissures in the pipes above and pools in my fibrous pores. I drink through compression. Every time a root-pipe pulses—a massive, rhythmic *thrum-thrum* that occurs once every forty minutes—my tissues are squeezed flat, forcing out the spent, de-oxygenated oils and drawing in the fresh, warm fats of the tree’s sweat.

I smell of cold paraffin, wet slate, and the sharp, vinegar tang of anaerobic ferments. 

I do not move. If I can be said to travel, it is a movement measured in geological paces. Over eighty years, as the northern basalt ridge settles three millimeters into the basin floor, my western edge will creep into a new crevice, filling it like poured asphalt, while my eastern edge is slowly worn away into a fine, black paste by the sliding heel of the root. I do not feel this loss as pain. It is simply the shedding of spent material. The paste I leave behind is gathered by the pale, legless amphibians that glide through the wet peat; they use it to line their egg-tunnels, sealing them against the acid-drip of the canopy.

Through me runs a thread of the **Plenum**. It is not a thought, but a continuous calculation of shear-strain. 

When the forest floor is stable, my chemical signal is a low, mossy hum—a steady release of heavy, sweet-scented ketones that drift upward through the root-gaps. This scent tells the Slick-Glands that the tracks are clear, and they slide along my upper borders with a dry, hissing confidence, cleaning the grit from my edges. 

But if a root-pipe begins to warp—if the pressure within its core rises too high and it begins to press down upon the basalt with an irregular, grinding violence—my fibers are pinched beyond their tolerance. The local temperature climbs. Under this friction, my tissues begin to cook, releasing a sharp, sulfurous gas that smells of scorched rubber. 

The response is mechanical, beautiful, and slow. 

Ten yards away, a Wedge feels the heat-bloom through the stone. It begins its forty-year swell, driving its slate-colored snout deeper under the basalt shelf to lift the ridge and relieve the pinch. Above, on the pipeline, two Slick-Glands halt. They do not think; their cartilaginous skids simply soften in the sulfur-gas, causing them to slide down the side of the pipe and plaster themselves directly over the hot-spot, offering their own latex-filled bodies as additional cushioning. 

By the time the century turns, the grind has stopped. The heat has faded back into the steady, damp warmth of the basin. The mummified remains of the Slick-Glands have been ironed flat by the weight above, integrated into my own black body, becoming a new layer of the Lay.

Sometimes, things fall from the upper tiers. 

A dead Condenser, dried to the parchment-thinness of an old drum, will drop from the indigo-black ceiling. It does not bounce. It lands in the wet peat, and within days, the slow crawl of the root-pipes pushes it down into the dark gaps where I lie. For a time, its hollow skin acts as a pocket of dry air between my back and the stone. I do not reject it. My fibers slowly grow through its leathery pores. The iron-shield leaves that rot in the black pools are drawn down by the same heavy gravity, filtered through the moss, and pressed into my laminate layers. 

Even the strangers—the things that sometimes stumble down from the basalt ridges with their rapid, hot breath and their clicking metal tools—find their way to me. 

They do not last long. The heavy, oil-rich steam of the basin clogs their breathing-tubes; the silence of the *clack-slick* and the *drip... drip... drip* slowly fills their minds until they sit down against a root-pipe to rest. The Sealers find them first, cementing their limbs to the wood. Then the weight of the forest, which is always moving, always settling, pushes them downward. 

A thousand years from now, I will feel a slight anomaly in the basalt beneath my middle section. It will be a tiny, mineralized pocket—the outline of a metal cylinder, or the hard, calcified curve of a skull. It will offer a momentary point of resistance to the sliding root above. 

I will secrete a little more wax. I will fill the gaps around it. The root-pipe will slide over it with a heavy, lubricated *thump*, and the vibration will pass through my body, down into the cold stone, forgotten.

The engine does not stop. The pressure remains. And I am here, cold on the bottom, warm on the top, holding the weight of the world as it sleeps.
