I am not on the floor of the basin. 

At the bottom, where the Silt-Kneaders roll and merge like wet dough in the grey-lime shallows, the light is too flat, too thick with the steam of the newborn earth. And I am no longer high enough to hang by my knuckles from the fresh green vines, weaving the outer skin of the seventy-foot towers. That was my first century, when my fingers were still wet willow-whip and the latex in my palms was sticky enough to hold my thirty-pound weight against the slickest knuckle of marrow-stone.

No, I am here, fifty feet up, at the **High Junction of the Seven-Flute**. 

I am a Wright, but I am no longer green, and the time when I carved the empty cells with my bill has passed. I am in the late afternoon of my *Cured Stage*. The lime-water has done its work. It has crept up through my heels, which are now completely fused to the split-pine platform of the bridge; it has climbed through my long, three-jointed shins, turning the pale, wet leather of my youth into something like grey horn, and then into something like salt-baked oak.

I have become a linchpin.

***

If you were to look up from the central cistern, you might not see me at all. You would see the great, hollow wicker columns rising toward the sky, and you would see the bridge—a thin, sagging web of pine-slats and hempen cords that connects the third tier of empty cells to the wind-flutes of the northern wall. 

At the point where the bridge meets the tower, there is a crook. In that crook, I am wedged. 

My left arm—seven feet of dry, fibrous wood-flesh—is wrapped three times around the main vertical stay of the wicker tower. The latex of my palm has dried into a hard, amber glaze that has bonded with the willow-bark. My right arm remains free, though it is heavy. It is the only part of me that still moves with the fluid, slow-motion grace of the living. With it, I hold the main tension-line of the suspension bridge.

I am the scale. I am the weight that keeps the bridge from swinging too wild in the midday drafts. 

I do not feel cold, though the water weeping from the high rock face has been dripping onto my left shoulder for forty years. It does not feel like ice anymore; it feels like grease. It runs down the channel of my collarbone, over the flat, featureless plate of my chest, and down my belly, leaving a thick, white crust of sinter behind it. I am being clothed in the mountain. Every year, my chest grows two pounds heavier. Every year, the vibration of the wind-songs travels through me with a slightly different resonance, because my density is changing.

***

In this world, we do not think in words. We think in *intervals*.

Through my heels, which are rooted in the pine-plank, I feel the long, low *hum-m-m* of the Seventh Tower. It is tuned to a deep, hollow fourth. When the wind from the outer peaks hits the basin at midday, the tower swells with air, and the wicker tubes whistle like a choir of dead bulls. 

Through my right hand, which grips the hempen stay-line, I feel the high, nervous *tink-tink-tink* of the water-drops hitting the cistern below. If the rhythm is too fast, it means the upper shelf has cracked and the water is weeping through a new pore. If it is too slow, it means the mosses have grown over the lip of the aqueduct.

And there is a third sensation, very slow, very heavy. 

A karst-snail is coming up the suspension bridge behind me. 

I felt its weight four days ago when it first left the main platform—a slight, dragging sag in the line that made my right shoulder ache with a dry, mineral stiffness. Now, it is only three feet away. I can hear the dry, raspy *lick-lick* of its flint-hard tongue as it clears the fat, wet strap-ferns from the hempen handrail, saving the rope from the rot that never comes, but from the weight that would snap it.

Its porcelain shell is the size of a brewing vat. As it crawls, the shell rubs against the pine-slats of the walk, producing a metallic, bell-like *tonk-tonk* that vibrates through my hip. 

The snail does not know I am here, or if it does, it regards me only as a turn in the path. Soon—perhaps by tomorrow morning—its grey, wet foot will touch my foot. It will climb over my lower shins, which are now as hard and white as the marrow-stone of the basin floor. 

I am waiting for that moment. When its immense, cold weight presses down on my calcified legs, the friction of its heavy shell will slide against my flank. It will produce a sound—a long, agonizingly slow, metallic *skre-e-e-e-p*—that will travel down the bridge-line, into the wicker tower, and across the entire amphitheater. 

The other Wrights, stationed at the far ends of the loom, will feel that vibration through their feet. They will know, by the pitch of the friction, exactly how far the lime has climbed up my spine. They will know down to the inch how much of me is still soft willow, and how much of me has become the bridge.

They will not pity me. They will adjust their own stay-lines by a fraction of an inch to compensate for my new hardness. 

***

By the next century, the snail will have passed. The latex of my right hand will have hardened around the hemp stay-line, gluing it forever to my palm. My bill will have filled with lime, and my two large, drum-like ear-drums will have grown thin and brittle as dry leaves, until they finally crack under the pressure of the mountain gale.

I will no longer hear the basin. 

But I will be the basin. 

When the young Greens climb up from the pools to string the new vines for the fifth tier, they will not look for my face. They will find my long, dry arm—now a white, calcified branch of stone-wicker—and they will use it as a cleat to tie off their hemp. They will step on my shoulders to reach the higher boughs, and their wet, sticky five-fingered hands will leave small, grey smudges on my neck, which will be wet, and cold, and silent, and perfectly still.
