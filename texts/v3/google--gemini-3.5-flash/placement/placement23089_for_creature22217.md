### I. The Salt of the Slicks

You would not find me in the high, warm bellies of the blockhouses, nor in the grease-slicked baskets of the cableways that swing between the Plinths like spiders on a clothesline. Those are places for the *stand-up* men—the ones with sixty pounds of lead in their heels and the steam-pipes hissing at their backs. 

I am down below the green-weed canopy, where the world is exactly three inches deep if you are lying on your belly, and sixty meters deep if you look up. 

I am a **Slab-Creeper**. In the clack-cant of the Cradle-Born, they sign my name with three flat fingers dragged across the palm: *The-One-Who-Scrapes.*

```
       [MY HAND]

        |  (==)  |      <-- Bronze knuckle-shimbles
        |   ||   |      
        |   ||   |      <-- Scar-stripes (spray-pock)
        |___||___|

       |          |
```

My hands do not look like hands anymore. They are white as peeled ash-wood from thirty years in the eight-degree flow, the skin on the knuckles so thick and split that I must fill the cracks with cold sheep-tallow every morning before the first bell. If I do not, the water gets into the pink meat, and by noon the current will have torn the skin from the bone like wet paper. I don't wear the heavy glove-leather of the gantry men; I need the rock’s face under my fingertips. I need to feel the *hum*.

The basalt here doesn’t lie still. If you press your ear to the black-green belly of the Slick, through the moss and the cold grease, you can hear the Plinth singing. It is a long, iron vibration—the weight of three miles of vertical water-fall pulling at the stone columns, trying to bend them into the sky.

### II. The Tool and the Larva

My work is the **Grand Pin of Cradle Four**. 

The "Four" is an old blockhouse, built of dark red granite that has turned the color of dried liver from the spray. Its thirty-meter wheel doesn’t run on modern steel bearings; it turns on a brass sleeve as thick as a beer-keg, held to the basalt by eighteen iron pins. Every six hours, the tide of the High Sieve shifts three points to the north, and the torque on those pins is enough to screech the fat out of the grease-boxes.

The **Anchor-Ticks** love the pins. 

They are small, grey, and shaped like thumbs. If you leave them alone for two moons, they will spit their acid-spittle into the joint where the lead-seal meets the basalt. They don't want to eat the iron; they want the lime-flour that the builders used to pack the holes. They scrape it out with their three-toothed tongues, leaving the pin loose in its socket. A loose pin under twenty thousand horsepower does not stay loose for long; it *wallows*. And when a pin wallows, the basalt around it turns to black sand, and the Cradle begins to dance.

So, I crawl.

                     o   o   o   o   o   o   o   o   <-- Bladder-tips
                    | | | | | | | | | | | | | | |

                    [#]     [#]     [#]     [#]      <-- Plate-Crabs (my neighbors)
                  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ <-- The Moss-Felt
                  ================================== (The Black Slick)
                     \___\_____I_I_____/___/         <-- The Basin of Pin Four
                               I_I                   
                               I_I <-- The Grand Pin (Iron, 12 yards deep)
```

I do not use a safety-tail. A safety-tail is a three-inch rope that catches the water; if the Scurry hits while you are tied to a thirty-foot rope, the water will use that rope like a whip to crack your neck against the stone. Instead, I use **"the Sinks"**—two square plates of cast iron, forty pounds each, strapped to my thighs with oil-skin belts, and my crawler-spikes.

The scraper is four inches of case-hardened railway iron, ground down to a wedge that has no handle, only a ring for my thumb. 

I lie in the moss-felt. The water is so clear that when I look up through my brass pock-glasses, I can see the bellies of the sieve-tails shivering six feet above me, like silver knives suspended in blue ice. They don't mind me. I am too flat to be a predator, and I smell of the same mutton-fat they graze on when the grip-weed drops its grease.

I find a tick. It has buried its snout into the shoulder of Pin Seven.

*Clack.*

I don't use a hammer; the water would slow the stroke until it was soft as a kiss. I use the heel of my palm, which is shod in a bronze "shimble"—a small, curved plate of metal strapped to the meat of my hand. 

*Clack. Clack.*

The tick’s grey shell splits. A yellow, vinegary juice runs out, instantly torn away by the eight-knot current and turned into a thread of white cloud. The plate-crabs, five or six of them who have been waiting under my chest-apron, scurry forward on their twelve needle-pointed legs. Their shells click against my goggles as they fight for the soft white fat of the dead tick. We are partners, the crabs and I. They clear the small corners my scraper cannot reach; I give them the meat.

We do not speak. Even if we had tongues, the roar here is not a noise—it is a solid thing. It is a wet wall that presses into your ears until your head feels like a hollow gourd filled with gravel. You do not hear the "Great Run"; you feel it in your teeth.

### III. The Scurry

The water is turning the color of tallow-milk.

I feel it first in my hips. The lift.

When the Scurry is five minutes off, the water doesn't rise; it *sucks*. The current slows from eight knots to three, and the grip-weed bladders, which usually strain toward the sky like green balloons, go limp. They float sideways, their long stalks tangling in my leg-straps.

I look toward the edge of the Slick. The great vertical falls, three hundred yards away, have gone silent. For twenty seconds, there is no roar from the void—only the wet, greasy slide of the water-mass preparing to drop.

Then comes the **"Whistle."**

It is the air being pushed out of the caverns below the Plinths by the rising weight of the lower mist. It sounds like a steam-boiler losing its plug—a high, thin scream that makes the plate-crabs drop flat and press their outer rims into the basalt until they look like round, black stones.

             /   (o)  (o)   \

```

I don't go back to the blockhouse. There is no time to crawl three hundred yards against a rising head of water.

I find the **"Squeeze."**

It is a natural trough in the basalt, six feet long and two feet deep, carved by a log that must have gone over the falls three hundred years ago and left its shape in the stone. I slide into it like a dagger into its sheath. I pull my legs up to my chest, tuck my chin into my collar—which is lined with thick, greased wool to keep my neck-skin from being torn off—and lock my fingers into the iron ring of Pin Seven.

The world goes black.

This is not the darkness of night. It is the green-black of thirty feet of cold freshwater falling on your head at forty miles an hour. 

The weight is immense. It doesn't feel like water; it feels like grease-iron. It presses my chest down until the breath is forced out of my nose in a long, rattling whistle. The pock-glasses are pushed hard against my eye-sockets until I see red rings in the dark. 

I can hear the "Four" singing. Through the iron of Pin Seven, I feel the giant wheel above me scream. The governors have failed; I can tell by the *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the shaft. It is spinning at twice its speed, throwing its massive oak buckets into the foam until they shatter. 

A piece of the wheel—a six-foot spar of tarred pine—goes past my trough. It doesn't float. The current is so dense it carries the wood like a stone, slamming it against the basalt forty feet ahead of me with a sound like a small cannon-shot. The pine is reduced to yellow splinters in three seconds.

I hold the pin. 

In this moment, I am not a man. I am a part of the ironwork. I am a leaf of grip-weed. I have no thoughts of the lowlands, or of the sun, which I have not seen since I was seven years old. 

The world is only water and stone, and the small, iron circle that keeps them apart.

### IV. The Return

The Scurry passes in seven minutes.

The water drops as fast as it rose, leaving the summits wet, steaming, and silent under the slate-grey sky. The grip-weed lawns are flat now, slicked down against the basalt like hair on a drowned dog. 

I climb out of my trough. My shoulders are blue with bruises where the iron weights pressed into my skin, and my goggles are cracked across the left lens. But Pin Seven is still there. It hasn't shimmied. Its lead-seal is grey and clean.

I look out over the edge of the Plinth.

The mist-drafts are coming up from the void now, cold and smelling of green silt and aerated stone. In the distance, between Cradle Four and Cradle Five, a **Cloud-skin Ray** is rising. 

It has come up from the deep channels to catch the wet thermal. It is six meters of pale, leathery hide, glinting like wet lead in the flat light. It doesn't flap its wings; it holds them curved, sailing through the grey mist-wall with three plate-crabs clinging to its belly-rim like tick-gems.

As it passes the edge of my Slick, it tilts one large, dark eye down toward me. We look at each other through the rain-shroud. It has no more use for me than I have for the sky, but we are both here, clinging to the wet ribs of the world.

I take my bronze castanets out of my belt-pouch. My fingers are too stiff for Clack-cant, but I can make the old sign for *Clear-Water*.

*Click.*

The ray slides into the grey wall of the chasm and is gone.

The wheel-house above me begins its low, rhythmic *chug-chug-chug* once more. They have cleared the broken buckets; the new shaft is turning. 

I reach into my grease-horn, scoop out a glob of cold sheep-tallow, and rub it into my split thumbs. The moss is starting to rise again, its tiny black hairs lifting to drink the grey rain.

I have three more pins to scrape before the night-shift.
