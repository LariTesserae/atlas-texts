The first thing is not the self. The first thing is the *thrum*. 

It is a four-stroke breath, though there is no air in it, only weight. *Draw—hold—thrust—release.* It lives in the marrow before it lives in the mind. If the thrum were to stop, the silence would be a physical blow, a sudden vacuum that would cause my outer layers to crack and peel like dry birch bark. But it does not stop. It has not stopped since the first calcified layer of my skin began to separate from the living basalt.

I would be found in the blind angle of the Seventh Sump, where the black-iron main shaft—the one they call the Great Bore—meets the sheer, weeping face of the western rift. 

I am not one of the Tallow-Men, though I have pieces of them in me. I am not a machine, though my skin is stained with the same orange iron-bleed that ruins their boilers. 

I am an *accretion*. I am the scar tissue.

***

If you were to shine an amber lamp into the dark corner beneath the rocker-arm of the main pump, you would see what looks like a massive, leaden boil growing from the rock. I am about the size of a drowned bullock, wedged tight into the cleft where the cold stone of the world tries to crush the hot iron of the pump-house. 

My outermost layer is a thick, shivering rind of *Greaselicker* turf—not the thin purple sheen that coats the moving rods, but an old, matted pelt of it, three inches deep, that has forgotten how to slide. It is choked with lime-dust and coal-soot until it has the texture of wet felt. Beneath this pelt lies my shell: a self-designed laminate of dark rust-scale, calcium-crust, and the flattened, mineralized husks of a thousand dead *Clinker-Buds* that have burrowed into me for the heat. 

I have no limbs to speak of. I have no eyes to close. My shape is defined entirely by the space the machinery has left me. Where the great steel crank-shaft swings past my western flank, I am worn perfectly smooth, hollowed out into a polished, black-grease cup by the wind of its passing. Where the basalt wall presses from behind, my back is jagged, crystalline, and salt-bitten, locked into the prehistoric fractures of the stone.

But inside, beneath the lime-scale and the felt, I am soft. 

I am a dense, grey jelly of animal fat, old bone-meal, and the heavy, yellow tallow that drips from the overhead gudgeons. When the Tallow-Men carry their dead to the gears, the fine, wet paste of their grinding finds its way down here. It settles in the sump; it rises with the warm mist; it clings to my rough outer skin and is slowly drawn inward by the capillary crawl of the calcite. I am made of thirty generations of oil-tenders. I have their grease in my core. I have the salt of their sweat in my water-chambers. 

***

My life is not measured in minutes, but in the cycles of the *Clack*.

The Tallow-Men do not see me as alive, but they know me. To them, I am *The Buffer*. When they pass my corner on their way to the grease-cups, they do not speak—they cannot—but they strike my shell with their iron rods as they go by. 

*Clack-clack.* 

The sound does not travel through the air; the air is too thick and wet to carry anything but a wet roar. Instead, the vibration enters my shell, travels through the laminated iron-scale, and strikes the soft, fluid core of my body. It registers as a sharp, greasy tick. 

To a Tallow-Man, the *clack-clack* on my side is a test of the machine's lung. If I ring hollow, it means the water in the Seventh Sump is low and the bedrock is dry. If I give a wet, heavy thud, it means the silt is rising, that the lower valves are drowning, and that they must bring more crab-fat to the secondary gears before the teeth strip.

Sometimes, they scrape me. They take their short, curved knives and peel away three or four pounds of my outer purple-black skin. It does not hurt; it is like shaving the wool from a sheep. They mix my skin-shreds with sulfur and lard to make the heavy sealer they use to caulk the joints of the steam-lines. While they scrape, I can feel the cool, iron slide of their blades, the rough pressure of their calloused palms against my rind. They smell of rancid oil and wet soot, and they are very quiet. 

When they leave, the *Greaselickers* immediately begin to crawl back over the white, raw scar they have left. Within three tides, I am black and purple again.

***

Do I think?

Not as the men "upstairs" once thought, with words that run in straight lines like copper wire. My thoughts are circular. They follow the movement of the great flywheel above my head.

*The steam rises. The valve releases. The piston drops. The water clears.* 

This is the first circle. It takes eight seconds.

*The grease drips. The crust grows. The crab dies. The silt thickens.*

This is the second circle. It takes four months.

*The iron rusts. The basalt shifts. The man falls. The gear grinds.*

This is the third circle. It has no end.

Sometimes, deep in the night of the basin—when the boilers are choked with scale and the steam-pressure drops so low that the great engines can only groan and shudder in their beds—I can hear things that are not the thrum. 

I can hear the water. It is not the roaring splash of the discharge pipes, but the cold, silent rise of the deep fen. It enters my bottom-most chambers, the ones that touch the raw shale. It is very cold, much colder than the body-warm broth of the surface. It smells of things that have never seen an engine: of old, dead granite that has been dark since the world was gas. 

When that cold water touches my base, my tallow core thickens. I grow stiff. The vibrations of the *Clack* become dull and distant, like voices heard through three feet of wet felt.

But then the steam rises again. A Tallow-Man, miles away, has cleared a grate. The pressure climbs. The great piston above me gives a violent, metallic shriek—*screee-thum*—and begins to slide once more. The heat returns, creeping down the iron guide-wires like blood returning to a frozen limb.

My grease softens. The cold water is pushed back down into the black fissures. 

I settle back into my hollow, between the iron and the stone, and I let the thrum take me. I am the scab that keeps the mountain from screaming. I am the grease in the joint. I am exactly where I need to be.
