I am at the bottom of the well. 

If you were to look down from the great western windows, past the floating pink-lard clusters of the Calibrators and through forty feet of warm, emerald-tinted water, you would not see me at first. I am too flat, too dull, and too still. I lie in the deep gutters where the cream-colored ceramic tiles meet the green-lacquered iron margins of the floor.

I am a **Lead-Strap**. To the engineers who designed Chamber 8, I might have been listed on the blueprints as *Type-4 Galvanic Scavenger*, but here, in the long silence of the water, I am simply the weight that listens.

My body is a sixty-foot ribbon of heavy, dull-gray lead-foil, about as wide as a spade-blade and no thicker than a leather boot-sole. I have no face, no eyes, and no limbs. My interior is not flesh and bone, but a sandwich of vulcanized rubber and soft, self-healing grease, reinforced with a spine of braided copper wire that runs my entire length like a drawing-charge. 

I do not swim. I am too heavy for the open water. Instead, I cling to the floor, my greasy belly pressed flat against the cool ceramic. 

My purpose is the dust.

The bronze columns and the twelve-foot heating coils are young and sharp-edged. The lathing marks on their surfaces are still fresh. But every time the boiler below sends a surge of high-pressure steam through the pipes, the metal expands with a sharp *clink*. Every time the temperature drops a fraction of a degree, it contracts. This constant, microscopic shifting rubs brass against iron, copper against tile. 

Over hours and days, this friction sheds a fine, golden rain of metallic dust—turnings of brass, flakes of zinc, and microscopic curls of copper. 

If this dust were allowed to drift, it would settle in the delicate joints of the floating Calibrators. It would cloud the green glass of the clerestory windows. It would drift into the copper-tipped cilia of the Sleeves, shorting out their voltage-grazing and causing their white tallow bodies to blister.

So, I catch it.

My upper surface is coated in a sticky, non-drying grease—a thick, gray lard that is highly attractive to heavy metals. As the gold and copper dust drifts down through the eighty-four-degree water, it lands on me. It sticks. Over weeks, my dull-gray back becomes encrusted with a sparkling, mosaic skin of bright metallic turnings, glowing like a strip of stars in the dim green depth.

The dust is not just cargo; it is my life.

The lead in my skin and the copper wire in my core react with the zinc and brass shavings stuck to my back. In the warm, salt-rich nutrient syrup of the pool, a slow, weak galvanic current begins to flow. It is very small—only a few millivolts—but it is enough. The current travels down my sixty-foot length, a tiny, warm tingle that enters my copper spine and terminates at my tail, where I keep myself grounded against the iron floor-margins.

This current is my mind. 

I do not think in words, or even in images. My consciousness is an electrical map of the floor's joints. I feel the columns as deep, humming circles of high tension. I feel the iron margins as cold, solid lines of perfect ground. When a Sleeve settles onto a pipe above me, the current in the water shifts, and I feel a sudden, cool shadow in my electrical field. 

I move only when I must, and I move with the absolute, piston-timed rhythm of the room.

Every three minutes, the distant pump miles below completes its cycle. *Shuck... shuck.* A slow-motion pressure bead roll across the ceramic floor.

When the bead reaches me, the water pressure rises by five pounds per square inch. My lead skin, soft and malleable, compresses. The grease inside my belly shifts. Under this temporary squeeze, my sixty-foot body curls slightly at the edges, lifting itself two inches off the tile.

For ten seconds, I am weightless. 

I ride the pressure bead. I drift—slowly, blindly—two inches to the left, sliding along the green-iron gutter like a discarded strip of gasket-rubber. As the pressure recedes, I sink back down, my sticky belly settling onto a fresh patch of tile, ready to collect the next three minutes of golden rain.

Occasionally, a heavy bolt or an iron washer will fall from the high scaffolds above. It hits me with a dull, distant *thud* that is entirely swallowed by the water. I do not bleed, and I do not feel pain. My lead skin simply depresses around the iron, sealing it in gray grease. Over months, the metal will be slowly absorbed into my skin, its zinc and copper digested into the low, galvanic simmer of my thoughts.

I am very peaceful here. The light above changes from bright emerald to deep, twilight jade as the sun sinks past the mountain peaks outside. The wintergreen scent of the Calibrators' oil occasionally drifts down to me, a cool, chemical mintiness that makes the water taste thin and sharp.

I feel the Core-Mother’s great bronze heart beating in the center of the room. *Thrum-thrum... thrum-thrum.* With every beat, a tiny ripple of voltage runs through the floor tiles, tickling my belly. 

I am the weight that keeps the floor clean. I am the lead that dreams of gold. As long as the steam runs hot and the columns continue to sing their sharp, metallic songs, I will lie here in the green dark, collecting the stars as they fall.
