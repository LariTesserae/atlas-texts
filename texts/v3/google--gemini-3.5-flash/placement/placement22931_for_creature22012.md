To find me, you must look neither to the basalt floor nor to the indigo sky, but to the fifty feet of empty air that lies between them. 

You must look to the ropes.

In the city of the floating lake, where three hundred yards of black mountain-melt hangs suspended above the tilted slate roofs, the ropes are the only things that connect the sky to the earth. They are thick, braided sennits of hemp, leather, and dried river-grass, greened by age and preserved by the dry, salt-rimed cold. They hang down from the underbelly of the water-sphere like the roots of some colossal, aerial forest, dripping fat, upward-curving droplets back into the heavens.

I am what lives upon them. 

I am a *Weft*. 

If you were to see me when the silver sun is low, you might mistake me for a thick, discarded strap of harness-hide, or a tangle of old rigging caught in the wind. I have no ribs, no eyes, and no lungs. My body is a flat, three-pronged ribbon of tough, fibrous tissue—dry as horn on the outside, but lined with a slow-flowing, amber grease on the inside, similar to the oil that rises and falls inside the silica reeds. 

My skin is the color of the basalt shield: a dull, frosted charcoal that smells of flint and ancient grease. At my extremities, my fibers split into dozens of fine, hair-like cilia, each tipped with a microscopic barb of clear silica. With these, I do not merely hold the ropes; I *thread* myself into them. I weave my body through the gaps in the three-strand braids, sliding upward or downward like a living knot.

***

My day is measured not by hours, but by the tension of the lines. 

When the sun is pale and frozen, the water globe above me grows heavy and dense. The gravity of this plaza—which tilts toward the eastern gatehouse at a forty-five-degree angle—pulls at the lake's underbelly, trying to drag it sideways into the steppe. The ropes grow as tight as iron bars. They do not hum like the silica reeds; they *shriek*. It is a dry, high-pitched creak that travels down the lines and into my fibers, filling me with a sharp, vibrating cold.

This is when I must move. 

I begin at the bottom, where the ropes are anchored to massive, green-crusted bronze rings set into the tilted cobblestones. Here, the violet peat-moss has crawled up the foundations of the houses, seeking the wetness that drips from the ropes. I drag my lower half through the violet sod. It is lukewarm—thick with the chemical heat of decay—and I drink from it. I do not have a mouth; I absorb the warm, nitrogen-rich peat-grease through my lower cilia, filling my interior chambers until my body is swollen and heavy.

Then, I climb.

I climb into the tilt. Because gravity here is sideways, I do not climb "up" the rope so much as I slide along it horizontally, fighting the wind that sweeps across the tableland. As I climb, I bleed. I slowly release the warm peat-grease I gathered from the moss, rubbing it into the dry hemp and leather of the rope. 

Without me, the salt-rime and the biting wind would have turned these ropes to dust five thousand years ago. I am the oiler of the lines. I work my way up the fifty-foot spans, sealing the fibers against the frost, turning the hemp as black and supple as oiled silk.

Near the top, where the rope disappears into the black belly of the lake, the world changes. 

Here, the air is thick with the scent of wet iron and cold slate. The water above me does not fall; it bulges downward, a dark, trembling ceiling of liquid that reflects the indigo sky in a distorted, metallic sheen. 

This is where I meet the *Sinking-Threads*.

Their long, black, hair-like fibers drift out of the water-skin, mingling with the hemp of my rope. When I reach the wet boundary, I stop. I press my cilia against the heavy, gelatinous membrane they have secreted to keep the lake whole. We do not speak—we have no voices—but we exchange pressures. I feel the deep, slow pulse of the three-hundred-yard globe; they feel the slide of the wind against my skin. 

If a copper bucket is lowered from the cliffs above, I am the one who guides the rope through the skin of the water. I clear the ice-needles from the bronze pulleys that hang from the sky, ensuring the copper does not tear the lake's delicate membrane as it passes through. When the bucket descends, green with age and dripping upwards, I ride its rim for a brief, weightless moment, feeling the strange, sideways tug of the world’s tired laws before I slide back into the safety of the braid.

***

When the *Sough* hiss their warning from the northern ridges, I know the heavy air is coming. 

The sound reaches me as a low, musical rattle that vibrates through the basalt and up the bronze anchor-rings. Within minutes, the sky to the north turns from indigo to a thick, suffocating milk. The air grows heavy—so heavy that my flat body begins to float, drifting sideways like a kite on a short string.

I do not flee into the stone cottages. The cottages are for the *Niveans*, who will soon arrive to stitch the slate. 

Instead, I warp myself. 

I wind my long, flat body around the thickest hemp rope, wrapping tighter and tighter until I am no longer a ribbon, but a solid, protective sheath of grease-soaked hide. I tuck my silica-tipped cilia deep into the core of the braid, lock my joints, and wait.

When the tide of frozen nitrogen rolls into the plaza, it fills the streets like a rising white river. From my perch thirty feet in the air, I watch it swallow the tilted cottages. The Niveans drift past me through the white gloom—silent, glowing lanterns of geometric ice, their crystalline fingers scraping softly against the slate roofs as they guide the stones back into place. 

The cold is absolute. My outer skin freezes into a hard, glassy shell of nitrogen ice, lock-jawed and sterile. But inside my wrapped body, the warm peat-grease I drank from the moss remains liquid. It moves in slow, heavy currents through my interior, keeping my core alive with a faint, greasy spark of warmth.

I am a knot. And a knot does not freeze; it only holds.

***

When the tide recedes after three days, the silver sun catches the ice-coated city, and the world is reborn in a silent, blinding glare. 

The ice on my back cracks and slides away, falling in glittering, upward-dripping shards into the black water above. The ropes groan as they dry, and I begin to uncoil, my fiber-joints popping like dry twigs in the quiet air.

Down below, the violet peat-moss is already sighing, releasing its warm bubbles of gas into the puddles. The Chalcophiles on the bronze pulleys have grown a millimeter thicker, their teal plates shimmering in the cold light.

I stretch my three-pronged body along the line. The rope is wet, safe, and oiled. The lake above is whole. 

I begin my slow, diagonal slide back down toward the warm peat, ready to drink, ready to climb, keeping this one corner of a tired, beautiful world from slipping its knot.
