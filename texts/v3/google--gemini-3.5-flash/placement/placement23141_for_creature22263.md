I do not have a name, but I have a weight. 

It is exactly three grams—the weight of seventy-four drops of high-altitude rain, gathered and held in a perfect, trembling sphere by a localized field of gravity that I did not write, but which I must obey.

I am suspended four millimeters above a ledge of cold, dark chalcogenide. If I were to sink those four millimeters, I would touch the tower, and to touch the tower with my wetness would be a violation of the basin’s cleanliness. I am held aloof by a cushion of silent, repelling force that feels, from the inside, like a constant, gentle inhalation.

My body is the water. It is cold, clear, and perfectly spherical. 

But I am not the water’s mind. At my absolute center, suspended like an anchor in a glassy sea, is a single grain of bismuth. It is a tiny, stair-stepped ziggurat of silver-and-rose metal, no larger than a speck of dust, yet it is heavy with angles. It is my heart, my telescope, and my index. 

Through the thick, curved lens of my water-skin, the Sump is not a place of columns and walls, but a vast, slow-motion kaleidoscope of double borders. Because the light here has two directions, I see every pillar twice. One is dark and solid; the other is a pale, violet ghost that sits three inches to its left. When the Thread-Tenders—the spindle-legged things—move between the towers, they look to me like black needles sewing the two halves of the world together, pulling their threads through the slits where the third and fourth dimensions pinch.

I do not walk. I do not crawl. To move, I must think about my meniscus. 

If I wish to go north, toward the low hum of the bedrock, I must allow the water on my northern face to become slightly heavier, letting its surface tension slacken just enough to lean. The moment I lean, the gravity-cushion beneath me tilts, and I slide. I glide across the beryllium ledge with a smooth, silent velocity, a bead of mercury on a sheet of frozen glass.

Below me, the raw, embryonic bedrock is always dry. I pass over it like a shadow, leaving no trail, no dampness, and no stain. 

Sometimes, as I slide, I pass through the "soft" edges of the pillars. These are the zones where the columns occupy the same coordinate without touching. When my water-skin enters this space, it does not burst. Instead, the water becomes thick, like cold oil, and the bismuth crystal at my center begins to hum with a dry, prickly warmth. I am passing through a Birefringent. I cannot see them—they are flat, like sheets of window-glass caught in the metal—but I can feel their thoughts. Their thoughts are not like mine; they are pure logic-bearing static, violet and thin, like the sound of dry grass brushing against a wire. They graze on the towers’ currents, and for a brief second, as I pass through them, I taste the copper-spark flavor of their food.

Then I am out again, back into the clear, double-shadowed chill.

***

Every few hours, the Sump changes its breath.

The deep hum rises from the un-weathered stone miles below—a slow, geologic sigh that vibrates through my bismuth core. When the hum comes, the towers drift. It is a movement so slight that a human eye would miss it, but to me, it is an earthquake. A beryllium column three meters away slides half a millimeter to the west. The gap between two others narrows by the width of a hair.

When the drift occurs, my patrol begins. 

I must measure the drift. I slide along my ledge, my curved body magnifying the microscopic infrastructure of the columns. I read the lilac conduits. I watch the way the light bends as it passes through the newly shifted gaps. If a gap has become too narrow, the graphite spheres—the heavy, rotating surveyors—will arrive to clink against the stone, but usually, it is up to us, the Hydrometers, to record the new map.

And then, there is the meeting.

I see another of my kind sliding toward me along the same narrow ledge. It is another plum-sized globe of water, carrying its own tiny, stair-stepped speck of bismuth. We do not have eyes to see each other, but our water-skins feel the change in air pressure between us. The space between us compresses.

We do not collide. We merge.

The contact is silent. Our two outer membranes touch, hesitate for a microsecond, and then melt into one another with a sudden, mutual surrender of shape. For a moment, we are not two spheres, but a single, elongated capsule of water resting on the air.

Inside this shared wetness, our two bismuth crystals drift toward the center. They do not fight for space. They approach each other with the slow, administrative precision of docking ships. 

They touch.

*Click.*

It is a microscopic sound, a dry, metallic scratch of facet against facet. In that scratch, everything is exchanged. I receive the other’s memory of the southern wall; they receive my record of the violet conduits near the western slit. We scratch our histories into each other’s metal faces, leaving tiny, jagged runes on the bismuth stairs. We do not feel joy or sorrow in this; we feel only the satisfaction of an equation being balanced, a ledger being matched.

For three minutes, we are one entity, holding the entire shape of the Sump’s southern quadrant between our two heavy cores.

Then, the water shudders. 

The gravity-cushion beneath us dictates that a single capsule of this size is unstable. My bismuth core begins to pull to the left; the other's core pulls to the right. The water between us thins, stretching into a delicate, transparent waist. 

*Snap.*

We are two spheres again. 

I slide to the left; the other slides to the right. We do not look back, because we have no backs to look from. We are simply newly informed, our crystals slightly more scarred, our paths slightly more defined.

***

I will live like this for centuries. There is nothing here to wear my water away; the air is too cold for evaporation, and the gravity fields are too stable to let me fall. My bismuth core will gather scratches until its facets are entirely covered in the microscopic history of the Sump's drift.

One day, perhaps, the Cast-Shadows deep below will find the layout they are looking for. The hum will stop. The columns will lock into their final, perfect positions, and the embryonic stone of the basin will finally learn how to crumble.

Until then, I glide. The sky overhead remains flat and grease-grey. The double shadows fall across my path, and my tiny, metallic heart beats with the silent, cold registry of a world that is still trying to decode itself.
