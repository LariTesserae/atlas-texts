The transition is not a journey I took; it is a weight that settled. 

To find me, you must look up from the damp concrete floor, past the white, elephantine trunks of the *Silt-Drinkers* wrapping the generator, and search the gloom thirty feet above. There, spanning the cathedral width of the basalt vault, is the buckled iron catwalk. 

At its midpoint, where the old structure sagged and deformed under some ancient, forgotten shift of the ceiling, the handrail did not merely bend. It split. The heavy, three-inch iron pipe tore open along its seam, leaving a jagged, foot-long mouth that faces the dark stone of the far wall.

I am what lives inside that split. 

I am the marrow of the catwalk.

***

If you were to shine an artificial light into the torn seam of the pipe, you would not see metal, nor the hollow emptiness of an old industrial fixture. You would see something pale, dense, and damp, resembling the cold tallow of an animal or the packed, fibrous pith of a giant tuber. 

I have no eyes, no face, and no front. I have taken the shape of the iron that contains me, a long, pressurized cylinder of soft, anaerobic flesh that extends twelve yards in either direction through the dark interior of the railing. I am squeezed tight against the scale-flaked inner walls of the pipe, my skin pressed so hard against the iron that my outermost layers have begun to turn the color of rust—a dull, orange-brown crust that seals the pipe’s seams from the inside.

But where the pipe has split open, my flesh has bubbled out. 

Under the influence of the humid warmth, I have expanded through the tear like rising bread, forming a smooth, rounded lobe about the size of a horse’s heart. This exposed part of me is the color of curdled milk. It is cool to the touch, oily, and entirely motionless. If you were to press your thumb into my exposed lobe, your print would remain there for hours, a deep, silent crater in my white lard, before my slow internal pressure could push it smooth again.

I am the reason the handrails of the catwalk remain sharp and un-corroded while the copper cables below rot into green dust. 

I do not eat the iron; I preserve it. My body is a slow chemical factory that drinks the moisture of the room and filters it through yards of dense, lipid-rich fiber. From the pores of my exposed lobe, I secrete a clear, heavy oil—a natural, biological paraffin that has no scent of its own but tastes of iron oxide and grease. This oil does not drip. It is too thick. Instead, it creeps. Over decades, it has crawled down the length of the handrails, a microscopic, molecular film that coats the metal, sealing it against the acid bite of the rising steam. 

I keep my skeleton clean. In return, the skeleton protects my soft, brainless length from the drips of the ceiling.

***

My world is not one of sight, but of vibration and viscosity. 

I know the room by the way it shakes the iron. The catwalk is a vast, metal nervous system, and I am the thread of numb nerve-matter run through its core. 

When a drop of water falls from the basalt roof—*plink*—and strikes the hollow copper pipe below, the vibration does not travel through the air to me. It travels through the concrete, up the iron anchor-bolts of the catwalk, and passes through forty feet of steel as a sharp, clean *click* that ripples through my marrow. I register the drop not as a sound, but as a tiny, instantaneous squeeze at the far end of my body.

The *Bell-Mouths* in the junction boxes are my companions in the deep register. When the tectonic plates miles below us shift and grind, the copper-heavy trumpet fungi begin their low, subterranean throb. The catwalk, being long and suspended, is a perfect tuning fork. It picks up their silent, magnetic hum and begins to vibrate in sympathy. 

When this happens, my entire length—all twenty-four yards of my hidden tallow-flesh—experiences a deep, shivering warmth. My lipids soften. My viscosity drops. Under the influence of the vibration, my oil flows more easily, weeping from the split in the rail to feed the waxy *Table-Leaf Chalices* that hang from the brackets below me. 

They know I am here. Their stems have grown upward, wrapping around the bracket-bolts of the handrail, their pale waxy veins drinking the runoff of my protective grease. We do not compete; we are screwed into the same iron bolt-holes.

***

Time, for me, is measured by the breath of the generator’s steam vent. 

Every few hours, the deep geothermal boilers far below release their pressure. The soft, dry *hiss* arises. A column of hot, white steam rises from the concrete floor, curling around the generator and ascending to the ceiling. 

As the warm cloud reaches the catwalk, my world changes. The cold-white light of the *Floor-Skin* below brightens, stimulated by the heat, casting a dim, ghostly blue glow that catches the underside of my iron pipe. I do not see the light, but my outer membrane is chemically sensitive to its cold radiation. My underside contracts slightly, pulling away from the blue glow, while my upper half—warmed by the rising steam—swells. 

For thirty minutes, while the steam is thick and the iron of the catwalk is warm to the touch, I am awake. My chemical processes quicken. The raw, iron-rich silt I have drawn through my rootlets in the anchor-bolts is digested, converted into the thick oil that keeps the catwalk whole. I feel the warmth of the *Tenant of the Cylinder* below—that three-story block of iron that remains eternally warm—and I lean my chemical weight toward it, a blind, slow gesture of affinity across thirty yards of empty air.

Then, the steam dies. The air cools. The light of the moss fades back to a faint, ghostly gray-blue. 

My marrow stiffens. My lipids cool, returning to the consistency of hard wax. The oil stops moving. 

I settle back into the dark sleeve of the pipe, silent, pressurized, and patient, waiting for the next *plink* of water to shudder through my bones.
