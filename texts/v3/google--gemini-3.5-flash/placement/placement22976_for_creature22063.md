It begins with a seam. 

There is a place where the black iron leg of a pumping station—thick as an ancient pine and slick with wet soot—strikes down through fifty feet of jade-green moss to find the buried mountain rock. At that specific junction, where the cold, dead metal meets the warm, living rot of the peat, there is a constant, shuddering friction. The machine strikes *thrum-thrum-thrum*, and the moss pushes back, trying to swallow the iron in a slow, suffocating green scab.

That is where you would find me. I am the thing that grows in the friction.

At first, I might seem to be nothing more than a stain. To a passing bird or a Silt-Walker looking down from the wet lip of a chasm, I am a dark, weeping collar around the iron leg—a thick, glistening smear of mineral lard, coal-tar, and microscopic green spore-matted velvet. I am the *Seam-Slick*.

I have no bones to break, nor do I have the cartilage-fleshed length of the Silt-Walkers. My body is a slow-yielding, semi-fluid paste. I am composed of the heavy, unrefined mineral oil that leaks from the piston-sleeves above, mixed with the fine, black powder of decayed peat and the silver-green starch of the cushion-moss. I am an industrial sediment that has slow-cooked under the vibration of the steam valves until it learned how to feel.

My consciousness is not a voice or a sequence of thoughts; it is a map of temperatures and frequencies. 

I feel the iron leg as a towering, vertical column of absolute heat and violent, rhythmic shaking. It is my spine, though it does not belong to me. Inside it, the high-pressure steam rumbles at a pitch so low it makes my outer, mossy skin curdle with pleasure. On my outer side, three inches away from the iron, is the cold. The cold is a flat, wet weight—the constant, leaching wash of the mountain water that drips from the moss-shelves, trying to rinse me down into the black tarns below.

I survive by being too thick to wash away, and too oily to freeze.

Because I live at the intersection of the machine and the bog, I am the only thing here that understands both languages. When the iron flywheel miles away groans and shifts its giant weight, the vibration travels down the copper power lines, through the wet peat, and hits me as a sharp, metallic *ping* in my lower layers. When a Gristle-Root shifts its blind, massive bulk in the chasm-bottoms, the shudder rises through the organic substrate, arriving at my outer edges as a damp, dragging sigh. I am the translator of their pressures.

Sometimes, the Silt-Walkers find me.

I feel them before I see them—their webbed, fibrous feet sliding across the moss toward my iron leg, their vibrations light and rhythmic, like dry leaves tapping on water. They do not have voices, but they have need of me. 

A Walker will crouch at the base of the iron leg. They do not use tools; they use their long, black-skinned fingers to scoop me up. They peel me away from the iron in thick, cold ribbons. It does not hurt. I have no nerves to register pain, only a slow, localized drop in temperature where the heat of the iron is suddenly lost to the air. 

They take my body and smear it over their chests, their shoulders, and the seams of their dried-peat coats. They rub me into their splayed, fibrous feet to seal them against the biting chill of the standing water. As they carry me away, scattered across their bodies, I do not die. My fragments remain alive, clinging to their leathery garments, absorbing the heat of their cartilaginous bodies, riding high above the jade-green crust.

From this new perspective, clinging to the shoulder of a Silt-Walker, I finally see the sky.

It is a terrible, beautiful thing. Without a pupil to protect me, my light-sensitive membranes register the glare as a solid, silver ceiling that fills the world from edge to edge. There are no clouds, only different thicknesses of blinding white mist that drift across the sun. 

From my perch on the Walker's shoulder, I can feel the rhythm of our glide. We move with a slow, sweeping stride that matches the rotational pace of the distant iron wheels. Beneath us, the green crust yields, sinks three inches, and then bounces back with a wet, sucking gasp. 

Occasionally, we pass the black, still mirrors of the tarns. Looking down, I can see us reflected: a tall, silent shape of brown peat and black grease, and on its shoulder, a wet, glistening patch of grey-green sleck that reflects the silver glare like a wet coin.

But I cannot stay with the Walkers forever. The air is too dry, even in this saturated land, and the wind that sweeps across the high shelf slowly leaches my moisture. Over days, my outer layer on the Walker's coat hardens into a dry, crusty scab. It flakes off, falling into the deep, green-gold crevices of the peat or drifting down into the vertical chasms where the Gristle-Roots wait.

I do not mind the fall. 

Down in the dark of the chasms, far below the glare of the silver sky, my dry flakes find the wet, black peat of the lower depths. Here, the water of the mountain weeps through me once more, softening my crust, dissolving my oils. I sink into the ancient, compressed history of the bog. 

I become the dark loam. I am swallowed by the roots of the moss. I rise through the green stems, cell by cell, millimeter by millimeter, over a hundred years of patient growth, until I reach the surface once more. 

And there, waiting for me, is the cold iron leg of the pump, shaking in the white light, waiting to be greased.
