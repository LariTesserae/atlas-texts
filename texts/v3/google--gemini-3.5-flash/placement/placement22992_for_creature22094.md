The world does not come to me as light, for I have no eyes to be broken by the white core above. It comes as pressure, as temperature, and, above all, as the great, roaring chord of the spires. 

I am a creature of thirty-two segments, three feet of cold, dense tallow wedged into the vertical crevice of a spire we call the *Three-Tongued-Singer*. 

To my left, three inches away, is the sister spire, its face wet and cool. To my right is the dark, dry basalt of my home-seam. I am held here by my twelve pairs of lateral ribs—mineral-hard, copper-bearing spurs that grow from my flanks like the teeth of a comb. When I contract my midsection, the ribs on my left draw up; when I expand, they bite into the porous tufa, pushing me an inch higher. 

*Clack-click. Clack-click.*

The basin of my skull is a broad, slightly concave plate of horn-silica, polished by centuries of wind and the gentle, abrasive touch of drifting water. It is my map. The wind matches the shape of the stone; as it enters the hollow vents of the spires, it translates the canyon into a language of pitches. 

A high, thin whistle at eighty-five hundred hertz tells me there is a crack two body-lengths above me, narrow enough to freeze my tallow if I venture into its draft. A deep, bone-rattling thrum—the great forty-cycle bass that vibrates through my rear segments—is the mouth of the Eastern Abyss, dropping down into the blue-shadowed dark where the air is heavy and silent.

Below me, the water is climbing. 

I feel its approach as a tightening of the air. It does not fall; it climbs the spires in thick, upward-bound sheets, guided by the fine, vertical grain of the silica. It moves over my hydrophobic flanks like a cool, sliding hand, unable to wet me, yet lifting some of my weight as it goes. 

Beside me, a drifting sphere of water the size of a human skull misses my horn-plate by a finger’s width. It makes no sound as it floats, but my silica plate registers the tiny, high-frequency tick of the Core-Mite at its center. The mite is steering, leaning its copper-pyrite weight against the inner wall of its bubble, riding the wind-vibrations toward the wet face of the adjacent tower. I feel the dull *clack* as the sphere hits the basalt, the water instantly soaking into the white crust of the tufa, the mite scrambling into a wet pore to lay its heavy, metallic eggs.

I am climbing toward the Bridge of the Three-Tongued-Singer. 

It is my task, as it was the task of my segment-parents before they petrified, to keep the crossing secure. The bridge is a single slab of split slate, gray and ancient, wedged between our spire and the neighboring *Hollow-Crown*. The wind here is fierce; it tries to slide the slate from its seats, to throw it down into the dark where it would shatter against the roots of the world.

To prevent this, I carry the glass-wool.

I have spent three cycles of the sun-core dangling my copper hooks into the quiet cold of the eastern abyss. Down there, where the light is only a memory of blue, the Glass-Weavers drift. They are long, black ribbons of obsidian, weightless in the lazy gravity. As my hook brushed their domain, they left behind their vitreous spittle—strands of glass finer than the hair of a Core-Mite, yet stronger than the copper plates of my own ribs. I have reeled it in, pound by pound, winding it around my middle segments until I am thick and heavy with the shimmering, metallic wool.

Now, I reach the bridge-niche. 

It is a dry, beehive-shaped hollow carved into the wet basalt. Here, the wind is muted to a soft, wet hiss. On the floor of the niche lies the property of my line: three heavy wedges of green jadeite and my copper scraper, its edge turned aqua-blue by the trace-acids of the condensation. 

I crawl into the niche. My waxy skin, smelling of mineral fat and old sulfur, drags against the grease-smudged stone of the ceiling. These smudges are the markers of my ancestors. They were made when the old ones scraped the excess tallow from their own flanks, kneaded it into cakes, and set them alight in the stone basins to soften the winter-frost. I can smell them still—a faint, ancient grease-odor, preserved in the dry, dead air of the pocket.

I do not light a lamp today. I do not need the yellow heat. 

Instead, I use my copper scraper to peel a thin, ruined layer of silica-crust from my third and fourth segments. The crust has grown thick over the last fifty winters, making my joints stiff and dry. The scraper goes *scritch-scritch* against my skin, peeling away the white, brittle scales. I do not feel pain; I feel only a clean, cool relief as the fresh, yellow tallow beneath is exposed to the air. I collect the peelings and press them into the corner of the niche; they will make fuel for whoever climbs here when my own segments have ceased to bend.

With my jadeite wedge, I secure the end of a glass thread to the slate bridge. 

The glass is cold—colder than the stone. I climb out of the niche, exposing my front segments to the raw wind of the gap. The wind screams here, a glassy, piercing note that vibrates through my silica plate like a needle of ice. I take the glass thread in my mouth-slit, loop it around the basalt tooth of the spire, and run it back to the slate. 

I weave. I do not use hands; I have none. I use the rhythmic, alternating contractions of my segments, threading the glass through the natural fractures of the stone, tightening it with the weight of my own body. 

*Clack-click. Clack-click.*

The copper-hard ribs bite. The glass thread hums as I pull it taut—a beautiful, high *E-sharp* that tells me the tension is perfect. It is a new string for the great instrument of the plateau. When the next storm-wind rushes through the labyrinth, this thread will not break; it will sing, adding its own glassy whistle to the choral roar of the spires.

As I work, the sun-core reaches its zenith. 

The heat is sudden, hard, and clean. It hits the tips of the spires above me, and the permanent transit begins its daily acceleration. The upward-climbing sheets of water reach the summits and begin to evaporate in a furious, silent hiss. The air is thick with the smell of wet opal. 

I look upward—not with eyes, but with the heat-receptors along my flank. The summits of the spires are glowing. They are white-hot, swelling with the layers of young silica left behind by the dying water. 

One day, forty winters from now, the crown of the *Three-Tongued-Singer* will swell so far that it will touch the crown of the *Hollow-Crown*. They will fuse. The gap between them will close, and my bridge will no longer be needed. The wind will lose this particular sixty-cycle whistle, and a new, lower note will be born.

I feel the stiffness in my tail segments. It is not the cold of the draft. It is the silica. 

It has begun to travel inward, replacing the oil of my tallow with the slow, eternal weight of quartz. My last four segments no longer bend; they have become gray, hard, and hollowed by the wind. 

I am not afraid of this. 

When my ribs can no longer climb, when the scraper can no longer find soft tallow beneath my crust, I will make my last transit. I will climb to the very top, where the spires are kissing. I will wedge myself face-up into the final gap, right where the water rises to meet the sun. 

The streams will flow over me. The opal will bloom in my mouth-slit and fill my hollowed segments. I will become the mortar. I will become the stone.

But today, the bridge is yet loose. The wind is still calling for a higher cord. 

I draw my head-plate back, contract my wax-flesh, and press my ribs into the basalt. I reach for another strand of glass, and climb.
