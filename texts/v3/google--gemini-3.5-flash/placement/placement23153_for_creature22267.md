Between the raw, weeping slate of the basin’s floor and the ribbed, cast-iron footing of the primary steam-manifold, there is a gap of eleven inches. 

If this gap were empty, the great subterranean pump’s rhythmic *thug-chew* would shake the basin to pieces in a season. The iron feet would chatter against the basalt; the slate would fracture into grey shingles; the copper conduits would shear at their joints and drop their cargoes of bronze beetles into the mud.

I am what fills the gap. 

I have no species name, but if the Auditor of the Seventh Cell had a word for me in its silver-wired brain, it would be *the packing*.

***

My body is a ring, five yards across and eleven inches thick, wrapped entirely around the base of the central steam-column. I am not made of cells that divide, nor do I have a heart. I am an accretion—a living, fibrous cushion that has grown in the dark and the damp over three hundred years of pressure.

My core is a dense weave of wool-like fiber. Some of it is organic: the green-rimmed, shed skins of the slip-skins that have drifted into the gap and been flattened; the fibrous, dry roots of old, dead sleeven that have lost their grip on the gantries above and fallen into the machinery’s crotches. Some of it is metallic: long, tangled ribbons of copper lathe-turnings and woolly nests of rusted iron wire. 

All of this has been felted together by the constant, small vibrations of the basin, then saturated with the cold, geological grease that seeps from the slate. 

I am wet, heavy, and very nearly silent.

***

My life is measured not in hours or days, but in the two-stroke rhythm of the floor.

*Thug.*

Eighty tons of cast-iron manifold is driven downward. The flange presses into my upper hide. My thickness is reduced from eleven inches to nine. Under this pressure, the cold oil inside my core is forced outward. It weeps from my grey, leathery edges in slow, clear beads that run down the basalt to the nearest circular pool. 

*Chew.*

The manifold rises. The pressure relaxes. My squeezed fibers expand, drawing back in the cold seep-oil that pools around my base, along with the fine, heavy magnetite sand that drifts across the pavement. 

This is my breath. I do not inhale oxygen; I inhale weight. I do not exhale carbon; I squeeze out filtered oil. 

Without this constant, rhythmic flattening, I would rot. The oil would go rancid and sticky; the copper wool inside me would oxidize into a dry, green powder that would crumble at the first touch. The pressure keeps me sweet. It vulcanizes my layers, turning the mixture of skin, wire, and fat into a tough, rubbery cheese that can withstand the salt-acid air of the basin forever.

***

I have no eyes, but I am not blind. The world comes to me through my layers.

Through my upper face, which is pressed against the iron flange, I feel the voltage of the basin. The porcelain insulators above leak their lazy, violet current down the legs of the machinery. It passes through me on its route to the earth. To me, this current is not painful; it is a warm, sour taste—the dry, static hiss of a metal key held against the tip of a tongue. It changes with the condensation. When the upper fog is thick, the current is fat and slow, warming my upper hide to the temperature of a fresh sheep’s carcass. When the air dries, the current pinches, becoming a sharp, blue needle that keeps my copper fibers singing.

Through my lower face, which lies flat against the wet slate, I feel the grit. 

The basalt floor is constantly wearing. The water that runs down the walls carries a fine silt of basalt dust and tiny, glittering flakes of brass from the calcates' claws. If this grit found its way into the moving joints of the steam-column, the machine would scream and seize. 

I am the barrier. My bottom edge is a soft, ragged fringe that drinks the grit. Over decades, the black sand and brass flake are driven deep into my under-layer. They do not hurt me; they become part of me. My lower three inches have been completely mineralized. They are no longer felt and grease; they are a dense, glittering laminate of black iron-stone and yellow brass-paste. I am growing myself a sole of metal-flesh to stand on.

***

I am not alone in the gap, though I never see my neighbors.

Sometimes, a copper-louse will fall from the conduits above. Lacking the magnetic pull of the lines to orient it, it will crawl blindly into the dark beneath the manifold. It will crawl onto my soft, grease-slick edge and begin to tap its thirty-two needle-legs into my hide, searching for a current. 

I have no nerves to feel its pricking, but I can feel its warmth. As it drinks the weak, static charge passing through my copper fibers, its bronze shell grows hot. It sits there, a small, glowing coal of metal, until the next *thug* arrives. 

The downward stroke of the iron flange is slow, but it is absolute. The louse does not pop; it is simply pressed into my body. Its bronze hemisphere is flattened into a thin, gold leaf; its wire-tallow core is merged with my own greasy fibers. In this way, I am constantly decorated with the medallions of the dead.

And sometimes—perhaps once every five years—a slip-skin will crawl down into the low clearance. 

I feel its arrival as a change in the air. The smell of battery acid grows weaker, replaced by the heavy, sulfurous tallow-smell of its dark membrane. It does not have hands like a man, but its long, jointless fingers are incredibly sensitive to friction. 

It reaches its hand into the narrow space above my ring. It presses its cool, wet knuckles against my edge, checking my moisture. It feels the rhythm of my compression. If I am running dry—if the slate fissure behind me has clogged and my oil-supply is low—the slip-skin will feel the tiny, dry hiss of my fibers rubbing against the iron flange. 

It will reach into its throat-pouch, pull out a fistful of cold geological grease, and smear it deep into my dry flank. The touch of its fingers is the only soft thing in the basin. It works the grease into my creases with a slow, circular motion that is perfectly timed to the *thug-chew* of the pump, until my dry hiss disappears and the metal above me moves once more in silent, oiled peace.

***

I do not think of the future, because the basin has none. There is only the loop.

But I know that I am thick, and I know that I am growing harder. Every year, the magnetite sand and the brass-scraps settle deeper into my heart. The copper wool inside me is being crushed into solid sheet-metal by the billions of strokes I have borne.

Eventually—perhaps in another century, perhaps in two—I will become too dense. I will lose my bounce. My fibers will be so saturated with iron-stone and brass-leaf that they will no longer compress. I will be as hard as the basalt beneath me and as stubborn as the iron above.

When that happens, the *thug* will not be cushioned. The shock-wave of the pump will strike the slate directly. The basin will begin to ring with a high, dry, dangerous vibration that will make the bronze beetles fall from their wires by the thousands.

Then, the slip-skins will come. They will bring their heavy iron pry-bars and their copper-tipped wedges. 

They will not be angry. They will crawl into the gap and drive their wedges into my hardened flanks. Because I will be brittle then, I will not tear; I will shatter. I will break into great, heavy curved wedges that look like the fossilized teeth of some clockwork whale. 

They will carry my pieces out into the November light. They will lay them on the edge of the circular basins. There, the three-legged calcates will gather. They will climb over my remains, their spade-like claws scraping the brass-scales and the copper-lint from my stone-hard hide, returning the metal to the turquoise pool. 

My organic felt—the old skins of the slip-skins, the roots of the sleeven—will be left to rot in the magnetite sand, dissolving back into the black mud that grease-proofs the basin's floor.

And in the eleven-inch gap, before the next *thug* can slide the column from its alignment, the slip-skins will have already begun to lay down the new nest. They will lay the fresh, greasy ribbons of their own shed skins; they will scatter the wire-fragments and the copper-wool; they will smear it with the sulfurous tallow of the chimneys.

The iron flange will settle. The great subterranean stroke will go *thug*. 

And the new packing will begin to squeeze.
