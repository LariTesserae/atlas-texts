The dark is not dark to me; it is a tapestry of scalds. 

In my three horizontal brow-slits, the world is a map of heat and cold, rendered in slow, bleeding washes of white, amber, and deepest carbon. 

To my left, the great vertical column—the *Mother-Trunk of the Third Rib*—shows as a massive, steady column of cool, iron-saturated blue, throbbing with the faint, rhythmic pulse of the deep sap rising from the bedrock. To my right, the high-pressure steam bypass is a screaming, thin needle of absolute white that cuts through the fog, vibrating at three hundred degrees. Between them, I hang.

I am no longer one of those who walk. My feet have forgotten the wet iron of the catwalks; they have split, peeled back like the rind of a blighted plum, and run their fibrous cordage deep into the soft, rotten heartwood of the column behind me. I have been here, in this niche, for forty summers—though "summer" is only a term I translate from the geothermal heat-pulses that rise from the earth's belly every dozen years, when the sumps grow fat and the water rises to the first level of copper grates.

My name, if the vibration of my rattle-plate could be translated into a human mouth, is something like *The-Swell-at-the-Seventh-Seat-which-Holds*. But names are for things that can be moved, things that might be lost in the fog and must be called back. I am not lost. I am precisely where the pressure demands me to be.

### The Valve and the Bark

My left hand is no longer a hand. 

Fifty years ago, when the great bronze bypass valve at the Seventh Seat began to weep through its packing gland, I was sent to tighten it. I reached out my fingers—each tipped with the broad, spade-like horn that fits the octagonal nuts of the lower casing—and I closed my grip around the spoke. 

I did not let go. 

The steam was hot; it smelled of sulfur and tallow-grease. Over twenty cycles of the main piston below, my rind softened, ran like warm beeswax, and then settled into the grain of the bronze. My fingers have since lost their joints; they have become a three-pronged bracket of mineralized ironwood that curves around the rim of the wheel. 

If I were to pull away now, the valve would tear from its seat, and the high-pressure line would scream its life out into the cisterns, drowning the lower district in forty cubits of boiling mist. So, I do not pull away. I have become the spring within the assembly. 

When the steam pressure rises too high—when the deep boilers sixty feet below groan under the weight of the ceiling-rain—the pressure pushes against my palm. I feel the bronze wheel turn a fraction of an inch to the left, winding the fibers of my forearm like a hemp cable. My wood compresses, absorbs the shock, and slowly, over three or four hours, nudges the wheel back to the right as the pressure ebbs. 

I do not think of this as work. It is merely the balance of the meniscus. I am the weight on the scale.

### The Long Sleep of the Sap

In the early decades, my mind was full of the dry *clack-click* of the public stairs. I remembered the heavy, satisfying drag of my feet through the warm peat-slurry of the canal-beds, and the scent of the first-pressing fern-oil we used to grease the turbine governors. 

Now, those memories are like the shells of dead beetles—thin, hollow, and easily crushed by the weight of the water. My thoughts have taken on the shape of the trunk I am grafted to. 

When I sleep, which is nearly always now, I do not dream of faces or movement. I dream of the *shhh-tuck, shhh-tuck* of the fourth bilge pump down in the dark. I feel its stroke through the core of the ironwood: the slight hesitation at the top of the piston-rise, the tiny, metallic click of the brass check-valve seating itself in the lime-scale, the rush of the black, oily discharge as it enters the lower siphons. 

I know when a pipe three levels below has split its seam, because the vibration in my left heel loses its hard, ringing edge and becomes a soft, wet *fizz*. 

I know when the Sump-Born are rising in the low cisterns, because the water through my roots tastes of copper-salts and new starch—the sweet, clean oil of the gestational casks as they begin to swell and split under the heat.

### The Visitor

Yesterday—or perhaps three years ago, for the ticking of the drip-clocks has long since dissolved into a single, continuous hum—a young citizen came to my platform. 

Through my heat-slits, they were a pale, soft gold against the dark stone, their head-fungus glowing with a cold, greenish-blue light that cast long, liquid shadows through the steam. They were newly cured; their rind was still supple, smelling of fresh turpentine and the clean, tallow lubrication of the sumps. 

They did not speak to me. One does not speak to a bracket. 

But they carried a small wooden bucket of boiled fat and coal-dust. They knelt by my lower legs, where my calves have fused with the basalt masonry of the canal-wall. With their broad, flat fingers, they began to work the grease into the deep splits in my bark where the pressure of the bypass line has cracked my grain. 

The fat was warm. It seeped into my dry cambium, relieving the dull, dry ache of forty years of steam-scald. 

The citizen finished their work, then stood and reached up. They did not touch my face—my brow-slits are too sensitive, and the salt on their skin would have made me shudder—but they laid their palm flat against the center of my chest, directly onto my Rattle-Plate.

My plate is old now; its petrified ridges are thick with verdigris, and the copper-bone is dull. But under their touch, I let go a tiny, low-frequency hum. It was not a message. It was simply the sound of a valve that is holding.

The young one pressed their forehead against my shoulder-joint for three cycles of the main pump. I felt the vibration of their own chest—their Rattle-Plate was high, dry, and fast, like the whirring of a small brass governor on a fresh turbine. 

*Keep the seal,* they said, though they used no words. 

*The seal is held,* I answered, through the slow, deep iron of the column.

Then they turned and melted back into the steam, their golden heat-signature fading into the vast, warm grey of the canal-corridor.

### The Stiffening

My shoulder is turning to stone first. 

I can feel the lime-scale from the condenser rainwater creeping down my collarbone, filling the tiny vascular tubes where the sap once ran free. It is a heavy, cool feeling—not painful, but final, like a thick coat of lead-paint applied to a rusting pipe. 

Soon—perhaps by the time the next silt-dome splits in the lower cistern—the movement in my elbow will cease entirely. The bronze wheel of the valve will be locked forever in my stone grip, a permanent part of the plumbing. My skin will lose its last warmth; the heat-slits on my brow will skin over with gray lichens, and the waxy fungus on my head will rot away to leave only a calcified cap of white tufa.

I am pleased with this. 

The seventh bypass has always been a weak point in the district's lungs. The engineers of the old days—those who built the first boilers before the trees grew over them—made the casing too thin. It needs the extra thickness of my chest. It needs the mineralized weight of my ribs to keep the steam from tearing the masonry apart.

I will remain here, under the drip of the iron-water, holding the Seventh Seat. The younger ones will walk past me on their way to the pump-wells. They will not look at me, but they will reach out as they pass, using my petrified elbow as a handhold to steady themselves on the wet, slippery catwalk. 

Their grease-slick palms will polish my stone skin until it shines like black glass. The water will run down my chest, into the copper-lined gutters, and back to the pumps that never sleep. 

I will be the machine. I will be the wood. I will be the bone that keeps the water from the door.
