I am hanging seventy feet above the Sump, forty feet below the heavy suspension-bridge of the Middle-Weavers. 

My world is roughly five inches wide. It is the width of the split-spruce cradle that holds my thighs, and the diameter of the live service-berry stump where my three main guide-ropes are hitched. Beyond that five-inch margin of safety, everything is wet air, green steam, and the endless, percussive *whuff-whuff-whuff* of the third-terrace water-baffle as it chops the glacial stream into hand-sized chunks of foam.

My name—if one were to whistle it in the short, sharp throat-clicks our mothers use to pierce the roar of the falls—is *Tess-with-the-Slate-Spit*. 

I am a Suture-Weaver.

My hands are the color of a wet walnut shell, stained to the bone by wood-tannin and the sticky, black lard we boil from pine-knots to seal the seams of the homes. My fingernails are gone, replaced by thick, yellow horn that I use to split hemp-fibers when my bone-awl is tucked behind my ear. 

Right now, my right foot—wide, flat-toed, and grey-skinned as a river eel—is clamped around the green neck of a giant pipe-reed. The reed is shivering. It has been shivering since sunrise because the mountain above is discharging three weeks of high-altitude sleet into our limestone throat. If the reed slips, I will swing sideways into the iron-moss wall with enough force to turn my ribs into kindling. I do not think about this. One does not think about falling any more than a fish thinks about drying; it is simply the boundary where the world stops being yours.

I have three bone-pins in my teeth. They taste of old grease and sheep-marrow. 

With my left hand, I am reaching deep into a three-foot-wide "slip" in the iron-moss. This is my work. The moss is three hundred years old here; it is as heavy as a wet sheepskin and thick as three men's torsos. When the great rains come, the water behind the green masonry begins to pool against the bare limestone. If we do not pin it back—if the moss-skin lofts and "bellies" out from the stone—it will peel away in a single, three-ton sheet, taking three spindle-nests, twenty-four meters of elderberry aqueduct, and perhaps six families down to the Sump in the space of half a breath.

I find the dry-hole in the limestone with my thumb. The rock is cold—a deep, ancient cold that makes my knuckle-joints ache with a dull, iron throb. I spit one of the bone-pins into my palm. I align it.

*Click.* 

Above me, on the dry underside of a willow-nest, a clack-beetle answers. *Click-click.*

"Busy, grandfather," I whistle through my teeth, not looking up. "Go find a spore-cap."

The beetle doesn't move. It is the size of my fist, its shell a green so dark and oily it looks like a puddle of grease in the grey mountain light. It is waiting for me to finish hammering the pin. When I drive the split-ash peg home, the impact will jar the surrounding moss, dislodging hundreds of tiny wall-midges that have taken refuge in the damp undersides of the fronds. The beetle knows this. We have worked the same three vertical meters of wall for four summers. We are partners, though we have never agreed on terms.

Behind my collar, something warm and dry twitches. 

It is my hearth-mouse—a yellow-striped sap-frog no bigger than a thumbnail. She has been nesting in the greasy wool of my cape since the mist came down at noon. She is incredibly dry, her skin velvety and hot against my collarbone. Occasionally she lets out a single, metallic *tink*, like an iron needle dropping onto stone. She is pleased because my skin is salt-crusted from the morning’s climb, and she is licking the brine from my shoulder with a tongue like a wet thread.

I raise my mallet—a heavy chunk of green plum-wood bound with raw hide—and strike the peg.

*Thump.*

The sound is flat, swallowed instantly by the roaring throat of the cleft. The moss-skin flattens. The water squeaks as it is forced out of the pores, running down my forearm in a cold, grey sleeve.

*Click-click-click-click.*

The beetle descends three inches, its serrated legs rattling against the wet limestone like dry reeds in a wind. It begins to graze on the midges I have dislodged, its jaws working with a soft, chewing snip-snip.

I let myself hang back in the harness, my knees locking around the pipe-reed to stabilize my swing. The cold mist settles on my eyelashes, turning the far wall of the gorge into a shifting, shimmering tapestry of green and grey. From here, I can see the whole ladder-road of the Middle-Weavers. It looks like a great, tangled fishing line that has been dropped down a chimney and caught on every brick.

There is old Geth, three terraces down, sitting on his small platform of split birch. He is eighty summers old, and his feet have gone completely white and flat, like two turnip-roots. He cannot climb the high lines anymore; his knees "creak like dry hemp," as he says. So he sits in the spray with an adze, hollowing out birch-logs into water-troughs. The chips fly from his blade in wet, yellow flakes, falling down, down, down into the green dark where the Oar-Mouths live.

I observe Geth's feet. I look down at my own.

My left big toe does not bend as far as it did last winter. The "moss-rot"—the pale, green lichen-fungus that lives in the dampest corners of our wool-capes—has taken a small hold under my nail. It doesn't hurt, but the skin there has gone grey-green, the color of alder-bark after a long rain. It smells of ginger-yeast.

I know what that means. My mother had the grey foot. By forty, she could no longer feel the difference between a wet vine and a dry hemp-rope. By forty-five, she had given her stone adze to my sister and had gone down to the Silt-Gleaners to boil the pitch-pots.

I strike another peg. 

*Thump.*

The sap-frog on my neck shifts, turning her nose toward my ear. Her breath is tiny and hot.

"Not yet," I click to her. "The birch-bridges are still holding. The pine-pitch is still black."

But I can feel it. When I stretch my leg to find the next toe-hold in the iron-moss, there is a small, cold silence in my hip. It is the same silence that lives in the deep stone behind the moss. It is the *Sigh*.

It is not an enemy. We do not fear the *Sigh* any more than we fear the water that turns our looms or the wind that dries our fish. It is simply the floor. In Kael-Sunder, the floor is not something you walk on; it is something that eventually climbs up to meet you.

I reach into my pouch for a third pin. My fingers are so cold they feel like peeled sticks, but they know the shape of the wood without looking. They know the difference between the ash-pins (which swell when wet and lock the moss forever) and the willow-pins (which give way during the spring runoff to let the mud slide through).

A young messenger-child—no more than eight summers old, with braids so long they are tucked into his belt to keep them from catching in the pulleys—comes sliding down a guide-rope thirty feet to my left. He doesn't use a harness. He has his toes clamped around the hemp-braid, his body leaning out into the mist at a forty-five-degree angle, a bundle of dried salt-fish slung across his small, barrel-shaped chest.

He sees me. He doesn't stop, but as he passes, he lets out a high, nasal whistle—the one that means *The sheep-pens are dry today; the salt-women are laughing.*

I do not whistle back—my mouth is full of pins—but I click my heels together twice against the wet limestone. *Clack-clack.*

He is gone before the sound has finished echoing off the wet walls, his small, grey figure dissolving into the cold steam of the lower terraces.

The light is changing. The bright, clean green of the midday sun is turning into the deep, bruised indigo of the gorge-evening. The clack-beetles are slowing down. On the terrace above, the weavers are pulling the willow-hatches shut on their spindle-nests, the heavy wooden sliding doors groaning as they slide through their mud-greased tracks.

Soon, the only sound will be the water. Not the busy, clapping water of the day-troughs, but the deep, heavy, limestone-eating roar of the main drop at the bottom—the sound that does not change, whether we are here to sew the moss or not.

I have one pin left.

I find the last loose seam where the ginger-roots have split the moss-masonry. I place the ash-peg. I strike it once, twice, three times, until the wood is flush with the green wool of the wall.

I do not unharness myself. I will sleep here tonight, suspended in the spruce cradle, with my back against the wet iron-moss and my cape pulled tight over my nose to keep the mist-swallows from nesting in my hair. The sap-frog will sleep in my armpit. The clack-beetle will stay within hand-reach, its legs ticking like a slow pulse in the dark.

I close my eyes. Beneath the roar of the third baffle, through the wood of my cradle and the hemp of my ropes, I can feel the mountain breathing. It is cold. It is wet.

It is holding.
