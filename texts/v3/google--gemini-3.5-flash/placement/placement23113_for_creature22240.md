My left knee is no longer a joint of meat. It has become *krak*—the rotten, grey ice that looks solid from above but will drop an ox into the void if he trusts it with his weight. 

When the joint first began to click like a dry mason’s mallet, I stayed on the third tier, mending the wind-screens with pine-pitch and slate shingles. But by the time the blue-shrub berries turned from green to their three-year frost-dark, my thighs would not lift me up the staircase to the middle cistern. The air up there had grown too sharp, too thin; it tasted of scraped bone. 

So, I did what all our blood does when the legs refuse the stairs. I came down. 

I settled into the Roots.

***

Down here, on the very bottom shelf of the world, we live in the great, dripping shadow of the ox-vaults. Above my head are three spans of dry-diorite trunking, held up by pine beams that have grown black and greasy with two hundred years of steam. Directly above those beams is the floor of the great stalls. 

At night, when the wind on the high ridges is howling loud enough to splinter the slate roofs, I lie in the dry straw and listen to the oxen. Sixty of them, shaggy and broad-backed, shifting their four-ton bulks from hoof to hoof. Their heat does not rise; it falls. It seeps down through the cracks in the masonry like warm lard, thick with the smell of fermented clover-root and wet, steaming wool. It is a good warmth. It is heavy, yellow, and slow.

In the mornings, when the white cloud-deck rises from the lower valleys and chokes the alleys with *vorn*—the greasy condensation that turns every doorstep into a slide—my hands go to work. 

I am a Weaver of the Knots. 

My eyes are mostly gone now, clouded over with the white grease-glare that comes from seventy winters of looking at the glaciers. I do not need them. My fingers are broad and split at the knuckles, the skin on my thumbs turned to the hardness of an old horn spoon by the constant friction of the hair. 

Before me lies the great coil of the *Syllable-Rope*. It is as thick as three fingers, spun from the coarsest shoulder-hair of the white mountain goats. It smells of the high pasture, of vinegar-ash, and of the mutton-tallow we use to keep the damp from rotting the core.

My left hand holds the guide-line; my right hand makes the hitch.

*Loop. Turn. Seep.*

This is the knot for the third winter of my youth—the year the great slide took the south terrace of the middle peak. I remember the sound of it: a dry, clean *shhh-tup*, like a hot iron dropped into a bowl of grease. Two hundred houses went down into the blue mist. We did not find the people, but we found their goats, frozen into the ice-chimneys like dried plums. 

*Loop. Double-hitch. Pull.*

This is the knot for my sister, Mina. 

She did not die of the slate-cough, nor did she slide. She went up to the High Spire in the month of the High Glare, when the violet sky is so thin you can see the stars at midday. I remember carrying her up. She was very light; the sickness had eaten the fat from her ribs until she felt like nothing but a bundle of dry pine-branches wrapped in a hide. 

Where Mina’s life ends on the rope, I have spliced in three inches of grease-stained wool from her favorite nanny-goat. When my fingers pass over that spot, they know it without looking. It is soft. It is warmer than the rest of the line.

***

Next to my knee, on a flat slab of green slate, sits Mina’s skull. 

The Sky-Steward did his work well; he clean-scaped her three winters ago with his green-jade cleaver. The bone is white as glacier-milk, polished by the mountain wind before we brought it down. I have filled the hollow of her temple with ox-grease and tucked a wick of braided goat-hair into the socket. 

It burns with a small, flat, blue-edged flame. It does not smoke much, because I use only the clearest fat from the kidneys of the spring-slaughter. In its light, I can see the green glow of the jade adze I am polishing with river-sand and water. 

The adze belongs to my nephew, Kael. He is eleven. This winter, when the frost is at its hardest and the ice in the chimneys is *glis*—the deep, blue ice that will hold an ox—he will take his tether off. 

I was there when they buckled him into his harness eleven years ago. He was no bigger than a newborn kid, his chest already barrel-shaped, his throat clicking with the nose-gutturals of our people. We drove his diorite peg into the hearth-stone of my sister's house, and for eleven winters his world has been nine paces of roof-slate. 

I have heard him up there, through the chimney-flues. *Clink-clack, clink-clack.* The sound of his bone scraper against the stone. The sudden, sharp *tock* of the rope going tight when he reached the edge of the terrace and looked down into the three-mile drop. 

Now, he is ready. I must have his jade adze polished to a mirror-green before the *Climb of the Five Crevices*. If the blade is rough, it will catch in the slate-seams; if it catches, the mountain will take him, and I will have to carve another notch in the goat-hair.

***

At dusk, the work stops. 

The wind always drops then, as if the mountain itself is catching its breath before the long freeze of the night. The dampness in the air turns to sharp, dry needles that sting the nostrils.

Across the three peaks, the silence is huge. It is the silence of three million tons of granite leaning against the sky.

Then, from the West Summit, comes the first chime.

*Ting.*

It is the hammer of old Geth, who lives on the highest shelf of the sixth tier. His stone-chime is carved from a slate flange that hangs over the great abyss; it has a high, clear tone like a frozen tooth breaking under a boot. 

A breath later, from the Middle Summit, comes the reply.

*Clink.*

This one is lower, deeper. It is the chord of the cistern-keepers. It tells us that the water is still seeping, that the ice-chimneys have not frozen solid to the core.

Then, from the East Summit, near the High Spire where the condors nest, the third chime sounds.

*Tong.*

It is a heavy, flat sound. It has no ring to it, only a dry weight that vibrates through the rock and settles into the soles of my feet. It is the sound of the dead-keepers, telling us that the ledger is balanced for the day. The sky has taken its portion; the city still holds.

I drop my hands. Beside me, the other old ones—their faces grey with the dried *chara* paste, their shoulders hunched like old ravens—stop their spinning. We do not speak. There are no words needed for this hour. 

Instead, we shift closer together in the straw. The white *Sentry-Warmers* creep in from the corners of the vault, their thick, woolly coats smelling of cold grease and dust. They do not bark. They settle against our thighs, their deep, rhythmic purring starting up like the low rumble of water in a deep pipe.

I lay my hand on the skull of my sister Mina. The bone is warm from the grease-flame. I feel the smooth curve of her brow, the sharp line of her jaw-joint where the jade cleaver cut. 

We are here. We are very low down, where the water is bitter and the air is thick with the dung of the beasts, but we are still here. The stone above us is heavy, and the cold is very old, but our hair is woven tight, our grease is thick, and the mountain has not yet found a way to slide us off.
