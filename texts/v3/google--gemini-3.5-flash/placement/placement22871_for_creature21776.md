I am not one of the ones that run, nor am I one of the ones that fly. 

To find me, you must look down—past the floating, blue-veined spheres of the middle air, past the whistling steam-vents of the Spires, down to the level plain where the rime-frost crackles like dry paper. And even there, you will not see me at first. I do not rise into the gray twilight. 

I am a **Fulgurite-Corm**. 

I live three miles from Spire Three-Seven, in a shallow depression where the peat-crust has buckled and dipped. My body is a sprawling, irregular knot of green-black obsidian, iron-hard lignin, and salt-crusted copper wire, embedded sixty feet deep into the five-mile mattress of the living moss. I am thirty feet across, shaped like a cross between a rotted cedar stump and a shattered glass honeycomb. I smell of singed vinegar, cold solder, and the wet, ancient grease of the deep earth.

I was once moss—perhaps ten thousand steam-cycles ago—but the lightning has changed me. I am the place where the High Loom grounds itself.

***

### The Rhythm of the Root

Everything begins with the *clank-thud*. 

Through my deep, zinc-laced nerves—which thread down into the dark, wet peat like cold solder-lines—I feel Spire Three-Seven before I hear it. The shockwave is a heavy, rhythmic throb that travels through the water-logged moss: 

*Shlump... thunk... shudder.* 

One. Two. Three. Four...

At twelve, the valve slams again. The vibration rattles the tiny, hollow teeth of my glass chambers. It is the heartbeat of my corner of the plateau. If Three-Seven were to go silent, my nerves would begin to wither within an hour; the lack of vibration would tell my cells that the water had stopped moving, that the deep heat had died, and that it was time to let the frost take me.

But the Spire is healthy today. Its valves are clean. The Piston-Lice are working. I can feel the tiny, scratching resonance of their copper claws three miles away, scraping the lime from the cellulose hinges. 

As Three-Seven pumps, the static charge builds. 

I feel it as a tickle in my upper ribs—the plates of vitrified peat that rise six inches above the frost. The air around me is growing thick, tasting of dry tin and sulfur. The rime-frost on my outer skin is not melting, but it is beginning to glow. The ice-needles are turning a faint, pale violet. 

A single Tread-Beetle, its dark brass-and-shell canopy dull and uncharged, zips across my eastern slope. Its zinc rollers make a dry, clicking hiss against my glass ribs. It knows what is coming. It parks itself in one of my hollows, locking its rollers, waiting.

***

### The Stroke

The air goes dead empty. For a three-second window, even the steam-screams of the distant Spires seem to flatten out, absorbed by the tension of the sky.

The hairs of the moss around my base stand straight up, stiff as wire. 

Then, the blue thread drops.

It is not a sound at first. It is a pressure that slams into my center, a white-hot fist that drives twenty feet down into my subterranean taproots. My glass chambers, cold a moment ago, scream with instant, blinding heat. The peat inside me—the ancient, compressed moss—boils, carbonizes, and melts into liquid jade. 

*Crack-BOOM.*

The sound arrives a millisecond later, a iron-shattering report that rumbles through the four-mile moss-mattress like a subterranean wave. 

For several minutes after the strike, I am the brightest thing on the plain. My entire body glows with an incandescent, green-gold luminescence. The St. Elmo’s fire doesn't just lick my skin; it nests in my hollows, spinning in tiny, lazy blue hoops that hiss as they contact the rime-frost. 

This is my feast. I do not eat the moss, and my copper roots are too blunt to mine the deep zinc. I eat the voltage. The current surges through my conductive veins, cracking the bitter, salty brine in my lower chambers into hydrogen and raw oxygen. My internal cells swell with the heat, their leathery walls expanding to push the warm, freshly minted gas through my breathing-pores. 

And, most importantly, I grow. The strike has melted a three-foot ring of new peat around my perimeter. As the cold air of the High Loom rushes back in, this molten soup of moss-ash, copper sand, and boiling salt-water cools in seconds, freezing into beautiful, dark green tubes of new glass. I have extended my reach by another span of a hand. I am slightly more than I was.

***

### The Guests of the Corm

As the glow begins to fade into a dull, smoky amber, my visitors arrive. 

The Tread-Beetle that was waiting in my hollow is the first to move. It is no longer dull; the ambient leakage of the strike has jump-started its internal capacitors. Its shell is translucent yellow now, sparks spitting from its roller-axles as it crawls out onto my hot, freshly vitrified edge. It plunges its needle-snout into a pool of bubbling, mineral-rich slag at my center. It drinks the liquid copper-salt with a desperate, sucking whistle, its bellows-like body inflating with every gulp. 

From the north, the perspective changes. The horizon is broken by a low, dark hill that moves. 

It is a Silt-Wether. It has smelled the ozone and the hot, charred peat from miles away. It moves with a glacial, rhythmic stride—*step... slide... step*—its six pillar-legs sinking three feet into the moss with every stride, leaving black pools of oil-tallow in its wake. 

It reaches me in the late afternoon. Its massive, orange skirt of feeding-cilia—looking like a curtain of wet, fibrous Spanish moss—sweeps over my glass back. It does not hurt. The cilia are soft, smelling of rotted wood and yeast. They lick my glassy ridges, cleaning away the soot and the carbonized moss-crust left by the lightning. 

As the giant passes over me, it drops its payload. From the wet crease of its underbelly, hundreds of seed-pods—each the size of an apple, hard and black as coal—fall into my cooling, glass-lined fissures. 

These seeds are stubborn. They have lain dormant in the Silt-Wether's gut for months. They need the extreme, five-thousand-degree kiss of the lightning to crack their outer husks, and they need the residue of my melted zinc to feed their first roots. Within minutes of touching my warm glass, three of the pods split with a sharp *pop*, sending out tiny, pale green shoots that smell of sulfur. By tomorrow, they will be orange tubers, boiling the frost around them, ready to feed the next generation of beetles.

***

### The Fall of the Sky

Near twilight, the air pressure shifts. One of the huge, floating Loom-Orbs—a house-sized bubble of tepid, salty glass-water—has drifted too far from the magnetic field of Spire Three-Seven. Its charge has leaked away into the cold, iron-slate sky.

It falls slowly, like a bladder of oil dropping through syrup.

It hits my western crest with a heavy, wet *splat-shudder*. Thirty tons of lukewarm water collapse over my hot glass. 

A great cloud of white vinegar-steam rises, screaming into the gray sky, mimicking the whistles of the Spires. The water rushes down my hollow chambers, cooling my inner core from red-hot to a stable, shivering cold. 

With the water come the Spindle-Fry.

They are small, needle-thin darts of silica-bone, writhing on my wet glass surfaces. They do not have long. The cold of the Loom is already beginning to freeze the puddles on my back. 

But they know what I am. They wriggle frantically toward my central chimney—the deep, vertical shaft that leads down to my subterranean water-vaults, where the water is warm and salty enough to never freeze. They slip into the dark, one by one, like silver needles falling into a velvet drawer. 

Down in my dark, wet belly, they will swim in circular paths, feeding on the dissolved copper-dust and the sulfur-spores I filter from the peat. They will wait. 

They will wait until the next time Spire Three-Seven’s static field reaches out and lifts a new sphere of water from the plain. When the steam-vent howls, the thermal updraft will draw the water from my vaults back up into the middle air, and the Fry—metamorphosed once more into waxy darts—will ride the hot plumes back to their home in the sky.

***

### The Long Circuit

I am the middle-man of the High Loom. 

I do not have the voice of the Spires, nor do I have the slow, majestic walk of the Silt-Wethers. I am anchored to this one spot, three miles south of Three-Seven, under an iron-slate sky that never changes. 

But I am the memory of the plain. In the layers of my green glass, if you could read them, are the signatures of ten thousand lightning strikes. I have recorded the mineral balance of every century; I have held the seeds of a thousand forests of tubers; I have sheltered millions of blind, silver fish while they waited for the sky to open.

The Spires will eventually clog and explode. The Silt-Wethers will die and melt back into the black peat-slurry. The Loom-Weavers will spin their copper nests over the ruins, and new things will grow. 

But I will remain here, cold and hard and glass-green, waiting for the air to grow thick. Waiting for the blue thread to find me again.
