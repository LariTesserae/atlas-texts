I am located at the third elbow of the Churn-Gate, where the southern terrace-wall meets the raw obsidian rib of the ridge. 

If you were to look for me, you would have to wait several hours for your eyes to separate my shape from the masonry. I am not a builder anymore, nor am I yet a simple stone. I am in the state of the *Great Setting*. 

My fourth, fifth, and sixth segments—the middle core where my tallow-glands once wept the soft, yellow grease for the nursery floors—have gone cold. They are no longer mine to move. They have been packed around with a dry mortar of chewed cork-lint and mineral dust, smoothed over by the paper-bugs until they are indistinguishable from the terrace wall. 

Only my first three segments, my head-cap, and my forelimbs remain free. I hang here at an angle of thirty degrees, my blunt claws wedged deep into a clean, unweathered fissure of pale gypsum, my rear segments acting as a living buttress for the narrow spiral ramp that winds up to the high nest of the oil-grubs.

***

To be here is to understand that the world is not viewed, but felt as a slow pressure and a shifting map of warmth. 

With my head-pits, I do not see the jagged edges of the ridge or the bright, terrible glare of the sun. To me, the sun is a heavy, golden hand pressing down on the top of my head-cap, warming my interior lard until my oily lymph flows with a delicious, thin ease. The sky above is an immense, empty cold—a black void that draws the heat from our towers if we do not insulate them with our thickest pelt.

But the city—the city is a towering, braided vine of amber light. 

Below me, the nurseries glow like live coals, white-hot with the frantic, slippery movement of the oil-grubs. I can feel their vibrations through my anchored segments: a soft, rhythmic *slither-slither-slither* as they slide through the tallow-troughs. It is a wet sound, but it is the safe, greasy wetness of life, not the corrosive water of the plains. 

Directly opposite my face, about two inches away, grows a paddle-leaf of a butter-shrub. In my heat-vision, the leaf is a cool, dark-blue plate, but its stem is a pulsing, orange vein of rich fat that climbs from the gypsum soil. I can smell its grease—a heavy, sweet scent like lard that has been melted with wild sage.

A snail-disc has been sitting on the upper ridge of my third segment for three sun-cycles. It is a dome of dark, hard resin, about the size of a saucer, and its flat, yellow foot is pressed against my pelt. I feel its mouth-parts—a tiny, structural scraping, very slow, perhaps one stroke every five minutes. It is licking the dry, yellow dust of the cork-trees from my wool. The sensation is not painful; it is a cool, localized pressure that keeps my skin from itching under the baking heat. We are old companions now, the disc and I. We do not move, but we share the same heat-surface.

***

In the late afternoon, when the shadows of the obsidian plates begin to stretch across the Notch like long, cool fingers, the traffic on the ramp beside me slows. 

A younger Keeper approaches. I feel the warmth of their approach long before they reach me—a soft, radiating ball of orange light moving up the spiral. They are a gatherer, returning from the lower ledges with their shoulder-pelt laden with raw turpentine-scrapings.

As they reach the elbow where I am wedged, there is no room to pass without contact. The ramp is narrow, and my head-cap projects slightly into the right-of-way. 

The younger one does not hesitate. They do not have the speed for hesitation. They begin the *soft-slide*.

Their flank presses against mine. The contact is a long, heavy mute-note. My dense, yellow wool interlocks with theirs; for a moment, we are a single, twelve-segmented creature. Through the wool, I feel the high-frequency tremble of their youth—their heart-wave is fast, perhaps three pulses a minute, and their lymph is thin and hot. 

During the slide, our shoulder-glands touch. 

The exchange is instantaneous, but it takes my slow brain several minutes to unpack. The smear of fat they leave upon my hide is rich with the news of the low slopes. They have found a new cleft where the gypsum has split fresh, revealing a pocket of soft, underground grease that smells of old lichen. They also carry the scent of a sister-nest three ridges over—a dry, bitter odor of pollen that means the cork-trees there are beginning to drop their yellow cones.

In return, my skin gives them the *stiffening*. 

They feel the coldness of my rear segments, the way the bone-less fat inside me has settled into the permanent, hard lard of the wall. They receive the memory of the great heat-wave of fifty years ago, when the butter-shrubs wilted and we had to feed the grubs on our own stored belly-grease until our segments shrank to the size of fists. 

They carry this memory away with them, smeared upon their side, to be passed to the next Keeper they touch, and the next, until the entire city knows that the third elbow of the Churn-Gate has set true and strong, and will hold the ramp through the coming cold-cycles.

The slide ends. The soft *thup* of our wool separating is swallowed by the dry air. The young one continues their slow climb, their heat-signature fading into the amber glow of the upper terraces.

***

I am losing the desire to move my front limbs. 

Yesterday, my left claw slipped from its gypsum crack, and it took me three hours to find another hold. The keratin of my nail is dry, no longer weeping the healing resin. It is ready to fuse.

In a few more sun-cycles, the paper-bugs will realize that my front segments have stopped their deliberate, slow swaying. They will climb over my head-cap, their tiny, dry legs tickling my sight-pits. They will bring the fine, grey lint of the cork-trees and the old, flaky wax of the abandoned nests below. 

They will build their little cells over my mouth-slit. They will fill the creases of my neck-joint with the leathery carton, sealing me into the archway. 

I do not fear this. It is the natural weight of things. 

The mind of a Keeper is not like the quick, flickering minds of the water-creatures who live in the lowlands—those frantic, liquid beasts whose thoughts change with every splash of rain. Our minds are in our fat. As the fat cools and hardens, the thoughts do not vanish; they simply stop moving. They become *held*.

When the final stiffening comes, my thoughts of the high sky, the smell of the sage-lard, and the warm, heavy slide of my neighbors will not disappear. They will be frozen into the very bracket that supports the Churn-Gate. I will become a silent, permanent slope of the world, holding up the weight of the active ones as they climb toward the sun.

Under my front claw, the rock is steady. The sun is setting, and the obsidian plates are turning the deep, dark blue of the cold sky. I draw my head slightly back, settling my chin into the warm, waxy mortar of my ancestor’s rib below me. 

We are building a very quiet city. It is almost finished.
