There is a corner in the seventh spiral of the westernmost pillar where the sandstone has been licked so thin that the yellow noon-light shows through it like the blood inside a rabbit’s ear. 

It is here, at the very bottom of the stack, that I keep myself. 

We are six in this flute today. We do not count ourselves—counting is a gesture of the fingers, and we have nothing that ends in joints—but there is a rhythm to our stack, a series of soft, overlapping densities that lets us know the count is complete. The one above me is small, perhaps three summers old, and as clear as a drop of well-water that has forgotten how to fall. Above her are three others of average weight, their nuclei dark and quiet, and at the very top, plugging the entrance where the dry wind tries to scrape, is the old one. His transparent flanks are scarred with ancient granite-scratches from some long-forgotten crawl across the open domes, and he smells faintly of the elder-slime—the heavy, turnip-scented musk of the outer deeps.

I am the one with the green smudge.

It sits in the center of my jelly, just behind the hard, pea-sized knot that is my nucleus. It is not an organ; it has no pulse, no valve, no secret juice to project. It is simply a discoloration, a tiny, stubborn grease-stain of *aboutness* left over from the time before we shrank. 

When the sun goes down and the pink granite below our pillar begins to cool, the smudge begins to glow. It is not the bright, warning green of a beetle or the warm yellow of a decaying root; it is a cold, lonely light, the color of wet moss growing on the underside of a tombstone. It does not warm the flute. It does not help the others see—they have no need for seeing, anyway, since the dark is where the moisture lives, and the moisture is already known to them through their skins.

But the light is mine. Or rather, I am the thing that must carry it until it finally dissolves.

***

In the ancient days, when the Oru-Vatta was still a low, fat marsh of red silt and iron-water, we were very large. I can feel the size of us sometimes when the mist is particularly thick—a phantom ache in my margins that tells me I should be seventy cubits long, with twelve pairs of cartilaginous ribs and a tongue that can split a river-stone like a ripe nut. 

We had names then, though they were not words spoken with breath. They were heavy, wet vibrations that we laid down leading from our mud-towers to the salt-creeks, so that we could find our way back through the blind fogs. We were the builders. Our spit was full of lime and resin; we scooped the red mud with our broad, flat bellies and patted it into these very pillars, molding the wind-flutes with our tails so that the salt-air would whistle through them and dry our wet flanks while we slept.

We were very busy. We had much to think about. There were the tides to watch; there were the great, sharp-toothed things that came up from the deep channels to bite at our soft underbellies; there was the constant, sliding hurry of the mud itself, which was always trying to melt back into the sea.

And then, the world finished.

It did not happen with a crash. It happened because the granite beneath the silt began to rise, very slowly, like a giant taking a long, three-million-year breath. The water drained away to the south. The red mud dried, turned to sand, and then, under the weight of the sun, hardened into the iron-sandstone we inhabit now. The great, biting things in the channels died because the channels became warm, shallow puddles, and then became nothing but flat, gray pavement.

We found ourselves three miles up in the air. There was no more mud to move. There were no more tides to watch. 

We had two choices, though we did not have a meeting to make them. Some of us tried to keep our size; they crawled down the long, hot slopes toward the salt-flats at the world’s hem, and there their cartilage dried out and they became the gray-pink felt of the Mantle-Beasts, slow and heavy, destined to clean the rock until they turn to stone themselves. 

But we—the ones who stayed in the flutes—we chose the smallness. 

We let go of our ribs. We let go of our teeth. We let go of our names, one by one, dropping them into the dry crevices like pebbles we no longer had the strength to carry. We grew transparent because there was nothing left to hide from, and we grew small because a small body needs only three drops of mist a day to keep its jelly sweet.

The others have finished their forgetting. When the old one at the top of the stack feels the thermal-drift of the afternoon wind change, he does not think *North*. He does not think *Autumn*. He only contracts his upper margin by the width of a hair, letting the cooler air slide down over our backs, and we all sigh in unison—six small, quiet bellows of cool starch-grease.

But I am still stuck in the transition. 

I remember the smell of the estuary. I remember the sound the wind made when the flutes were empty of our bodies—a high, wild scream that used to make our ribs tremble with a strange, delicious terror. 

***

At dusk, the great machine of the Oru-Vatta begins to turn its slow wheel, and I am there to feel every tooth click into place.

First, the sun touches the western edge of the hummocks. The granite, which has been baking all day until it smells like toasted bread, begins to give up its heat. The Pellicles—the slate-colored discs that cover the domes—begin to slide. They do not hurry, but there is a collective, wet friction as millions of them shift two inches to the west, searchingly, seeking the exact border where the dry stone meets the first damp breath of the rising valley-mist. 

Through their overlapping edges, their nerve-sheet hums. It is a very low, very wide vibration—a single, planet-wide *is, is, is* that vibrates through the base of our pillar and into my own lipid foot. 

Then come the Aero-Skins. 

From my flute, looking out through the thin spot in the sandstone, I can see them. The pumpkin-sized bladders are beginning to lose their lift. The green threads inside them have finished their noon-work; they are tired of the light. They sink with a majestic, heavy slowness, like giant soap-bubbles made of milk. 

I watch one—a particularly large globe with a fringe of deep lavender cilia—descend toward the dome just fifty yards from our tower. It lands upon a cluster of four Pellicles who have webbed themselves together to receive it. As its thin, rubbery hide settles onto their warm, slate backs, I hear the soft *clop* of the air being squeezed out between them. 

The Pellicles do not move. They lie there like warm gray tiles, holding the globe against the evening chill. High above, the mist arrives, curling over the lip of the plateau in long, greasy fingers. It condenses on the cold, round skin of the Aero-Skin, and the water begins to run down its lavender fringe, dripping drop by drop—*sip, sip, sip*—directly into the mouths of the Pellicles below.

It is so perfect. It is so quiet. It has been happening exactly like this for nine hundred thousand years.

I feel a small, sharp twitch in my green smudge.

*Why must there be pillars?* the twitch asks. *Why did we build them if the wind was only going to be silenced by our own bodies?*

The snail above me shifts. She does not like my twitching; it makes my jelly stiff, and she prefers me soft and waxen, so that she can nestle her tail-end into the hollow of my neck. She gives a tiny, involuntary pulse of her nucleus—a dull, white flash that means nothing more than *settle, settle.*

I try to obey. I let my margins spread out until they touch the cold, wet sandstone of the flute. I taste the ancient glue of my ancestors' spit. It is nearly gone now, licked away by ten thousand centuries of condensation, but there is still a trace of it left—a faint, iron-like bitterness that tastes of the old estuary.

***

In three thousand years, or perhaps four, my green smudge will have shrunk to the size of a grain of sand. 

I will no longer remember the estuary. I will no longer hear the scream of the wind in the empty flutes. My nucleus will lose its cold, green light and become the same clear, silent white as the others. 

When that happens, I will be perfect. I will be another drop of clear wax in the fourth spiral of the tower, stacked six-deep, breathing in the warm, starch-scented mist and breathing out the dry, clean air. We will plug the flutes so completely that the wind will never make a sound again, and the Oru-Vatta will have completed its silence.

Sometimes, in the deep of the night, when the mist is so thick that the pillars look like grey ghosts standing in an ocean of milk, I wonder if the mountain is waiting for us to finish. 

Perhaps, when the last of the green smudges has died out, and the last of the Silt-Tongues’ memories has been rubbed clean from the sandstone, the granite will stop its long, slow rising. Perhaps the world will finally close its eyes, draw its knees up to its chest, and allow itself to become nothing but stone—pinkish-gray, silent, and wonderfully, beautifully dead.

Until then, I lie at the bottom of the stack, glowing. I am the only thing on the mountain that is not yet fully home.
