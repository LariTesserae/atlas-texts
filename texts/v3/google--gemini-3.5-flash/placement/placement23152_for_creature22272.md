I am not on the surface plate. 

The surface, with its sliding Slickers and the frantic, blind rolling of the Chippers, is too loud, too subject to the sudden, explosive shifts of the structural plates. 

To find me, you must descend through one of the vertical trunk-capillaries, past the whistling conduits of hot nitrogen, down into the deep interstitial wells where the sheer weight of the plateau is balanced. Here, where the pressure would flatten a grease-bladder in seconds, are the **Spindles**.

They are the adjustment screws of the world. 

Each Spindle is a colossal, vertical shaft of ancient, slow-grown ironwood, forty feet in diameter and six hundred feet tall, carved with a single, continuous, spiral thread. They do not turn quickly. Driven by the deep, seismic waterless tides of the lower root-engines, a Spindle may make only one full rotation in the course of three sunless days. Their purpose is to maintain the gap—to elevate or lower the upper ceiling-plates as the searing, turpentine-hot air expands and contracts the dense timber vaults above. If the Spindle binds, the ceiling will buckle and crush the distillation towers below.

I am a **Whorl**. 

I am a split-ring carriage, twelve tons of dense, book-matched greenheart and lignum-vitae, clamped around the waist of the giant Spindle. I have no legs, no eyes, and no lungs. My body is a crescent, living nut, whose inner curve is carved with female threads that mirror the male threads of the shaft with absolute precision. 

I do not walk up or down. I do not have to. 

Hanging from my outer rim, like strange, heavy fruit, are several massive, pendulous burls—the counter-bobs. They are packed with dense, iron-saturated heartwood. They are so heavy that they anchor me against the rotation of the shaft. Because I cannot spin, when the Spindle turns beneath me, I am forced to crawl. 

Slowly, millimeter by agonizing millimeter, I ascend the spiral. It takes me five rotations of the world—five of the long, silent cycles of the deep piston-gourds—to climb fifty feet. 

My inner curve, where my wood-grain brushes against the giant wood-grain of the Spindle, is lined with *cork-felt*, a thick, fibrous bark that I keep constantly saturated with a heavy, black grease of graphite powder and animal-free palmitic oil. I secrete this oil from three internal bladder-chambers located near my core. 

As I ride the thread, my primary function is *reading*. 

I do not think in words, but in **backlash**—the tiny, microscopic play between my threads and the Spindle’s threads. 

Through my cork-felt lining, I feel the state of the machine. I feel the dryness of the wood-grain. If the Spindle is dry, the wood will "cry"—a high-frequency, ultrasonic shudder that travels through my entire frame, warming my oil-bladders and causing them to expand, forcing more graphite-paste through my pores and into the groove. 

If there is a crack in the Spindle, I feel it as a sudden, sharp *snick* in the grain—a micro-fraction of an inch where the thread has sheared. 

When this happens, I do not signal with a voice. My counter-weights shift. Under the sudden change in resistance, my outer burls swing outward, like the governors of a steam engine. This mechanical displacement opens a tiny, lateral valve on my outer skin, releasing a thin, pressurized stream of volatile pine-tar into the air. 

Far above, suspended in the black ceiling, the Resonators will catch the scent. Within hours, a Tensioner will descend, or a Slicker will come sliding down the trunk-capillaries, carrying the heavy mending-amber.

My life is incredibly long, measured not in years but in the wearing down of my threads. 

I see nothing. The dark down here is absolute, broken only by the rare, static-spark of dry wood rubbing under immense pressure—a brief, blue-white snap that illuminates the vertical grain of the shaft for a millisecond before the dark swallows it again. But I *hear* everything. 

I hear the deep, rhythmic *lump-lump-lump* of the piston-gourds three levels below. I hear the high, whistling hiss of the nitrogen gas purging through the reedpipes. I hear the distant, gunshot cracks of the ceiling-timber adjusting to the heat. 

Sometimes, another Whorl passes me on an adjacent Spindle, only thirty yards away through the blackness. We do not speak, but we feel each other’s vibration—a low, rhythmic *shhh-shhh-shhh* of graphite-soaked felt sliding over seasoned heartwood. We are in phase. We are part of the same chord.

Slowly, over decades, my inner threads are losing their edge. 

The sharp, square profile of my greenheart teeth is rounding. The grease-bladders are drying out, their walls becoming stiff and corky. The backlash is growing. 

I can feel myself beginning to rattle. It is a loose, light clatter that occurs every time the Spindle reverses its rotation to compensate for the cooling of the upper vaults. The rattle is a sign of clearance. It means my grip is failing. 

I am not afraid. I have no capacity for it. There is only the mechanical expectation of the run-out.

In a few more rotations, I will reach the top of the Spindle's thread—the "washout" zone where the spiral flattens into a smooth, unthreaded neck. Here, there will be nothing left for my teeth to grip. 

When I reach this point, the weight of my counter-bobs will pull me outward. I will slip from the shaft. 

I will fall. 

It will be a long, silent drop through the dark wells of the plateau, ending in the crushing-zones below. There, the Chippers will find me. They will not recognize me as a cousin; they will rolling-grind my dry, greenheart frame into the fine, uniform wood-flour, which the Suckers will draw down into the distillation towers.

My oil will be boiled out of me to lubricate the next generation of Slickers. My carbon will be baked into the gas that drives the pistons. And my successor—already growing as a dense, brough-like tumor in the graft-house at the base of my Spindle—will feel the first, slow turn of the thread, and begin its long, blind climb into the dark.
