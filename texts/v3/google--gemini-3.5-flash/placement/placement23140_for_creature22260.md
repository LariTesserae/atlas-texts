Every three or four minutes, I am permitted to think.

In this place, memory is not a thread of language or a series of stored images; it is *hysteresis*—the physical reluctance of a material to return to its original shape after the force that bent it has gone. 

I am down here, in the lowest tier. I am one of the Lenses, but I am the one that does not move anymore. 

I am wedged into the northernmost turn of the floor-channels, where the trench wall pinches tight against the basalt floor. Decades ago—or perhaps only months, for time here has no skin—a heavy, square iron nut fell from the catwalk above. It was already half-eaten by the Solder-Skin, a pitted, black lump of carbon and iron oxyl. It fell into the syrupy water with a dull, heavy *shluck*, and it came to rest in the narrowest part of my channel. 

As I slid past on my slow, hydraulic crawl, scraping the basalt clean of sulfur-flour, I met the nut. I could not digest the iron; my chemistry is of silica and copper. I could not climb over it; the water is too shallow here, and my viscosity requires depth to float. So I pressed against it. 

And then the pool began to cool.

At the center of the pocket, near the rising steam vent, the water is ninety-eight degrees. Here, tucked into the shadow of a basalt buttress where the iron nut blocks the hot current, the temperature is only ninety-two. Six degrees is a vast, frozen wilderness. 

At ninety-two degrees, my outer skirt—the scalloped, bottle-green edge of my disk—no longer behaves like warm honey. It has begun to vitrify. It has turned into a hard, brittle lip of volcanic glass, fused directly to the raw obsidian of the trench wall and the rusted iron of the fallen nut. I am anchored. 

I can no longer slide. But I can still feel.

***

The silence is the roar of the steam. It is so loud, so high-pressure and wet, that it ceases to be a sound and becomes a physical weight, like the pressure of deep water against a membrane. It presses down on my flat back, holding me against the stone. 

Then comes the strike.

*Clank-thud.*

The vibration does not travel through the air; it travels through the basalt. It comes up from the roots of the world, through the flat black plates, and enters my base. 

The shockwave is my wakefulness. When the basalt shakes, the stress aligns the microscopic lattices of my silica body. The molecules, squeezed by the weight of the metal pulse, generate a tiny, sharp surge of electric potential. 

With the electricity comes the light. My center—the softest, warmest part of me that is still liquid—flares with a dim, amber internal fire. For thirty seconds, I am bright. I am warm. The amber light shines out through my green glass edges, illuminating the dark, syrupy water around me, casting long, wavering green shadows across the polished floor.

Under the influence of the shock, I try to flex. It is a slow, powerful contraction, a hydraulic pull that should move me forward three millimeters to scrape the rock. 

But my edge is frozen to the wall. 

The tension of the pull rises within me. I can feel the stress concentrated at the point where my green glass meets the jet-black basalt. It is a sharp, singing strain. If I pull too hard, I will crack. I will shatter into a dozen sharp, dead shards of green bottle-glass, and the syrupy water will wash my pieces down into the deeper fissures, where they will dissolve into raw sand.

So I do not pull. I hold. I absorb the strain. I let the amber light glow, and I wait.

***

From above, a drip falls.

It comes from the green-furred copper conduit, thirty feet up. It has gathered a microscopic film of oil from the piston-housing. It falls through the hot, wet air, past the coiling Wefts, and strikes my exposed back.

Because of my high viscosity, the drop does not splash. It is like grease landing on thick silicone. It creates a deep, round, slow-rebounding crater in my center. 

As the crater forms, a Weft drifts down. 

The Wefts usually stay in the high air, but occasionally, the sudden *whistling sigh* of the exhaust port creates a downward draft that pushes them toward the surface of the pools. A single white ribbon—a creamy, glass-jointed snake—hovers just above the water. 

It is attracted to the light. It senses the amber glow inside me, and the tiny electrostatic charge my strain has generated. 

It drops lower. The tip of the white rope brushes the crater on my back.

It is a sensation of cold needle-pricks. The Weft is thermotaxic, but it is also dry; it is made of empty, hollow silicon chains. When it touches my wet, metallic body, it drinks. It siphons a microscopic layer of the copper-rich water from my surface. 

For a fraction of a second, the contact is complete. I feel the Weft’s static potential—the wild, fast, leaping energy it harvested from the steam-friction above—discharge into my amber core. The amber light flashes to a brilliant, blinding emerald green. The strain in my frozen edge eases, just for an instant, as the current reorganizes my molecules.

Then, the *whistling sigh* of the steam regulator ends. 

The draft shifts. The Weft is sucked upward again, coiling lazily, its jointed needles shimmering with a new, greasy green luster it took from me. It rises back into the mist, toward the brackets, toward the dark, cool mouth of the Solder-Skin.

***

Left behind, I begin to cool.

The three minutes are passing. The amber light in my core grows dim, shrinking from a wide plate of light to a small, buried coal. The electricity fades as the basalt plates beneath me stop vibrating. The molecules in my silica lattice lose their alignment, drifting back into a disordered, sluggish sleep.

My viscosity rises. The crater on my back, where the oil-drop hit, has almost finished smoothing out. Now there is only a faint, shallow dimple left. By the time the next strike comes, it will be perfectly flat again.

I can feel the coldness of the trench wall creeping further into my body. Another millimeter of my soft, copper-salted center has ceased to flow. It has joined the vitrified rim. By next year, or perhaps the year after, the glass will reach my core. I will no longer glow when the piston hits. I will no longer generate the current. 

I am becoming the stage. I am becoming the basalt shelf. 

When I am entirely green glass, the other Lenses will slide over my back. Their soft, heavy bellies will scrape against my hard surface, clean of sediment, keeping me polished and bright. The Solder-Skin will look down from its brackets and see its reflection in my face. The Wefts will still occasionally brush my cold, shiny surface, looking for a charge that is no longer there.

I do not fear this. In this pocket, there is no room for fear, only for the cycle. 

Deep in the dark, the steam vents with a heavy, wet roar. The copper pipes drip. The Solder-Skin eats and heals.

And then, the metal moves.

*Clank.*

The basalt shudders.

*Thud.*

My core wakes up. I am green; I am amber; I am under tension; I am here.
