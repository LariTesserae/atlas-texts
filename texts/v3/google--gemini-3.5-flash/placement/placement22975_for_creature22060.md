My eyes are closed against the rising gold, but I am not blind. 

Through the amber film of my inner eyelids, the Saddle is a world of warm, glowing honey. The sharp edges of the lilac granite domes are softened into orange curves; the canyon drop-off is not a terrifying void, but a thick, luminous sea of copper mist. 

I am sitting on the third-tier terrace of the House of the Grey Swift, my legs tucked beneath my barrel chest. My feet, bound in the tough, boiled leather of swift-nests, are wedged into the smooth depression of a sandstone step—a groove worn down by four hundred years of heels that moved exactly like mine: slow, flat-footed, and sliding.

I am a **Tuner of the Stack**.

In the lower world, they might call me a carpenter or a mason, but here, those words have too much iron in them. There is nothing to hammer here; there is only what we can wedge, bind, and grease. My tool is not a mallet, but a long, curved scraper of mountain-swift bone, its edge worn thin as a dry leaf, and a small leather pouch filled with shale shims and paraffin-wax.

Behind me, I hear the *shrr-clack, shrr-clack* of my sister’s scraper. She is working on our uncle, who lies face down on the warm stone of the upper roof. The sound is dry and rhythmic, like a locust cleaning its wings. With each stroke, she peels away the pink, clay-like paste of the silt-wash, taking with it the old, wind-poured grease and the fine grey grit of his daily labor at the wax-press. The smell of it drifts down to me—warm paraffin, the sharp tang of iron-dust, and the heavy, sweet scent of cedar-sap that leaks from our skin when the sun is thickest.

I click my tongue against the roof of my mouth—*tlk-tlk*—a small, flat sound that does not travel past the next two houses. 

From the crevice beneath my left thigh, a slate-blue lizard responds. *Tlk-tlk-tlk.* 

It is a clicker, reassuring me that the sandstone block I am sitting on is stable. If the clicker had scurried or remained silent, I would have known that the vertical weight of the two houses above us had shifted, or that the old lintels were drying too fast. 

We live in a house made of our own history. To my left, the lintel over our family’s grain-jar is my great-uncle, *Oru*. He was a heavy man in life, broad-shouldered and quiet, and his body cured exceptionally well in the Deep Flue eighty years ago. The wind dried him to the density of seasoned oak. He is a deep, polished mahogany color now, his long-fingered hands still holding the copper brackets that keep the door-frame from buckling under the weight of the fifth tier. 

When the wind comes up from the salt-desert—that low, immense drone that sounds like a wet finger circling the rim of an earthenware jar—I can put my ear against Oru’s dry shoulder and hear the draft. 

If he hums in a high sibilant, the stone below him is settling. 
If he is silent, the house is in tune. 

Today, there is a rattle. It is not Oru, but the third-tier floor-beam—a long, straight trunk of tallow-wood that has grown parched. The wax-vine cords that bind it to the cliff-anchor have stretched under the heat of the noon sun. 

I lean forward, moving with the sluggish grace of a shadow on a sundial. To move fast in this air is to waste the moisture we keep locked so deep inside our grease-varnished skin. My lungs, broad as bellows inside my wide ribs, draw in the dry, warm air without a sound.

I reach into my pouch, pull out a lump of the thick, pale wax-reed syrup—so solid it must be kneaded between my copper-stained fingers until it softens—and a wedge of lilac granite. 

I slide the wedge into the gap where the timber meets the sandstone wall. I do not use force. I simply lean my weight—the heavy, dense weight of a body that has never known the lightness of water—against the stone. The joint grows tight. I smear the warm wax-sap into the seam, sealing it against the wind that would otherwise whistle through the crack and drive the lizards from their nests.

The rattle stops. 

The house settles back into its natural chord: the dry rustle of the wax-vine curtains above, the metallic whistling of the swifts diving through the copper smog, and the steady, low *clack-clack* of the slate-blue lizards hunting the oil-flies in the dark spaces between the blocks.

I open my amber eyelids. For a moment, the world is too bright—a blinding, glittering haze of pink silt and yellow sun—before my eyes adjust, and the lilac shoulders of the mountains return to their true, heavy shape. 

Below me, five miles down, the white salt-floor of the world is shimmering, a flat and silent nothingness. It looks cold from here. It looks thin.

I turn my back to the drop-off and climb the sandstone steps toward the smell of the scrape-wash, my soft-wrapped feet making less sound than the dust that settles behind me. I am home. I am part of the stack. And one day, when my skin has ceased to yield its cedar-oil and my lungs have taken their last dry draft, I too will take my place in these walls, holding up the terrace for those who have yet to learn the language of the stone.
