The first thing is not the light, though the light is a flat, heavy weight that presses down upon my upper edge. 

The first thing is the grip.

I am held. I am always held. To the left, a wall of cold, wet calcium carbonate; to the right, another. The gap between them is exactly three inches wide, and I am exactly three inches thick. I do not have skin as the soft things of the lower valleys might have it; I have a flank of slate-like shingles, overlapping like the teeth of a giant file. When I am still, the stone of the columns bites into these shingles, and we are one thing: a static, icy sandwich of mineral and cartilage.

But I am not still. I cannot be still, because the water will not let me. 

Below me, miles deep in the black basalt groin of the world, the sea is screaming. It is being squeezed by the weight of mountains, and its only escape is up through the capillaries of these pillars. I feel that massive, hydraulic heave in the very center of my grindle—the long, vertical cord of gristly marrow that runs the length of my thirty-foot height. 

The pressure enters me from the front. My leading edge—the wet, blunt prow that faces the dark interior of the maze—is porous. It is full of small, hair-lined vents that drink the cold, spitting mist. I draw the water in. I do not drink it for thirst; I drink it for leverage. 

Inside my gristly core, the chambers contract. *Heave.* The water is compressed, forced backward through my internal channels, and jetted out behind me through the trailing vents. 

*Slide.*

It is a movement of inches. With every pulse of my internal pumps, my slate scales grind against the flanks of the columns. The sound is a wet, shattering rasp—a dry scrape that occurs entirely under water. I cannot hear it, because there is no air here that is not already roaring with the high, whistling hiss of the column-tops, but I can feel it. It is a harsh, clean vibration that starts in my outer shingles and runs inward, shaking the lime-mud from my pores.

I am a Shale, but I do not think of myself by that name. I am simply the friction that keeps the world wet.

If I did not slide, the water would build its monuments too fast. The pillars are greedy; they sweat a mineral-thick grease that wants to harden, to grow fat, to touch the neighboring pillar and fuse into a silent, solid block of white deadness. My purpose—if a thing that is half-stone can have a purpose—is to be the wedge. I file away the wet, soft sinter before it can petrify. I am the tooth that keeps the throat of the maze open.

My world is divided into three zones of touch and temperature.

The first is the Sky-Edge. It is only three inches wide and perhaps six feet long—the very top of my head, which rises just slightly above the shoulders of the pillars into the open, blinding glare. This part of me is not grey or gristly; it is tough, dry, and stained a deep, scorched ochre. It is where the silver compounds from the water have settled and cooked under the flat, white dome of the sky. The Sky-Edge is always hot to the touch—not with the heat of fire, but with the dry, stinging heat of concentrated light. It is my only sense of the outer world. Through the Sky-Edge, I feel the passing of the great, white noon, which is no different from the great, white midnight except that the glare is slightly more vertical.

Below the Sky-Edge is the Grey. This is the middle twenty feet of my body, wedged in the perpetual, misty twilight of the columns. Here, the water is always sliding down my flanks in a thin, unbroken sheet. It is cold—so cold that it makes my gristle stiffen—but it is rich with the taste of raw iron and sulfur. I do not have eyes, but I know the shape of the columns by the way they taste as I scrape them. This column to my left is sweet with lime; the one to my right is bitter with silica, hard enough to dull my scales.

The third zone is the Foot. This is the bottom three feet of my length, where the light does not reach at all. It is a place of absolute blackness and heavy, sluggish currents. Down there, my cartilage is soft, white, and tallowy. The Foot is where the Nummulae live. 

As I slide, the grey dust of my scraping—the ground-up lime and silica—drifts down into the dark basins. I can feel the Nummulae down there, clustered around my Foot like flat, heavy coins. They do not move for me; they are too slow. I slide over them, my soft grease-flesh dragging across their concentric, vibrating rings. They do not mind. They are licking the dust from my flanks. Their tiny, hydraulic bodies hum in sympathy with the deep drone of the columns, and that humming enters my Foot as a gentle, rhythmic tickle—the only soft thing in this hard world.

Lately, though, the movement is changing.

It is not a sudden thing. Time on the shelf of the world does not have seasons; it has only the slow deposition of layers. But over the last few hundred miles of slide, I have felt the iron entering my marrow.

My pumps—the small, wet chambers that flex to draw the water in—are losing their rubbery stretch. They are tasting more of the stone. The water I drink is no longer just passing through me; it is leaving its bones behind in my gristle. 

My middle zone, the Grey, is growing heavy. The shingles on my left side have stopped flexing. They have fused together into a single, rigid sheet of dull, silver-grey calcite. When I pump my water now, I do not slide three inches; I slide perhaps one. The scrape is no longer a smooth, wet hiss; it is a notched, shuddering jerk. 

*Hold.*
*Pump.*
*Jerk.*

I am becoming what I fight. 

I can feel the columns waiting for me. They do not want to destroy me; they want to include me. The pillar to my right—the hard, silica-rich one with the bitter taste—has a long, shallow groove that matches the curve of my stiffened flank exactly. It has been waiting for me to reach this point. 

In a few more miles—perhaps a century, perhaps two—the hydraulic pulse in my core will grow too weak to overcome the friction of my own calcified shingles. I will stop in one of these narrow slots. The water will continue to flow over my Sky-Edge, down through my dead vents, and across my frozen scales. 

My gristle will turn to sinter. My silver-scorched edge will become a hard, ochre ridge on the side of the lane.

I do not fear this. The Shales do not have room for fear; we are too narrow. Instead, there is a strange, heavy satisfaction in the stiffening. 

Even now, as my movement slows, I can feel how my presence changes the voice of the forest. The water that rushes past my rigid flanks is squeezed into a tighter, cleaner channel. The columns above me are beginning to sing a different note—a higher, clearer whistle that vibrates through my marrow with a wild, crystalline intensity. 

I am no longer just the file that keeps the flute clean. 

I am beginning to hear the song I am about to become.
