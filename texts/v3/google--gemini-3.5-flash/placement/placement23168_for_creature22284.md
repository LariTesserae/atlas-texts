The light is the first thing, and it is also the last. 

It does not fall from above so much as it occupies the space between things, a hard, white density that presses against my skin like a sheet of clean glass. Because there are no shadows, there is no depth to the distance; the great wooden looms five miles away do not fade into a blue horizon, but remain as sharp and flat as if they were drawn on the back of my own eyelids. 

I am a **Shingle-Gauge**. 

I do not have a name, because in a world of perfect loops, there is no need to distinguish one point on a circle from another. I was grown from the flank of a primary yellow-wood colonnade, three hundred cycles ago, during a period when the deep turbines were humming in the key of G-sharp. My wood is tight-grained, seasoned by three centuries of dry nitrogen wind and the fine, sweet mist of silica dust until it has the color of ancient butter and the hardness of horn.

My body is a vertical stack of twenty-four flat, hexagonal discs of kiln-dried ash, held together by a central spine of braided copper wire. I am four meters tall when fully extended, but I rarely extend. Usually, I am compressed, my wooden discs clicking together like a stack of heavy plates as I creep along the seams of the vitrified floor-tiles. 

I do not walk setting foot before foot. I have no feet. Instead, the edge of my bottom-most disc is rimmed with hundreds of tiny, spatulate fingers made of split bamboo. When the static charge of the floor-tiles rises, my copper spine contracts, pulling my discs tight; when the charge dips, the internal tension of my spring-wood ribs pushes me outward again. In this way, I slide—*shruff-clack, shruff-clack*—along the modular seams, a slow, wooden bellows measuring the micro-expansion of the continent.

***

Today, I am working in the Hinge-Gap, where the horizontal plane of the loom-terraces bends upward at a ninety-degree angle to become the vertical wall. 

Here, the geometry of our world is strained. The great cream-colored floor-plates, each ten meters square, must meet and roll into the vertical plane without cracking. If a single plate expands by even the width of a spruce-needle, the pressure will travel down the line for fifty miles, jamming the slow-turning starch gears or snapping the copper-lined root-bundles that feed the felted mosses.

I am the one who feels the pinch.

I sit over a junction of four tiles. I extend my copper spine, raising my upper discs toward the porcelain ceiling, and let my flat, drum-like head—a disk of polished gypsum—touch the side of a massive, square-sectioned pillar. I do not have ears, but the wood of my body is an excellent conductor of vibration. 

Through my spine, I feel the world-engine.

*Thrummm.* 

It is the deep, fifty-cycle sigh of the primary nitrogen turbine below the tiles. It is wrapped in three feet of grey moss, so the sound is nothing but a tooth-giggling pressure in my joints. But within that thrum, there is a tiny, dry *tick*. 

*Tick.* Every three seconds.

I tilt my gypsum head three degrees to the left. The light-beam next to me—a physical rod of frozen *lux* three feet thick—vibrates slightly. Out here, where the light is solid, the creepers have grown thick around it. Their parchment-like leaves are yellow and brittle; they are drinking too much light, drawing it out of the pillar and making the beam cold. When the beam gets too cold, it shrinks, pulling away from its ceramic socket in the ceiling.

I must adjust the slack.

I reach into the hollow of my third disc with a small, jointed tong of dark brass—an instrument that grew into my side while I was still a bud on the parent tree. From my storage chamber, I extract a tiny, dry-cured oak peg. It is exactly seven millimeters square. I slide it into the seam between the two tiles beneath me.

I wait.

The tiles are closing. Slowly, with the weight of five million tons of ceramic continent behind them, they squeeze the peg. 

Through my brass tong, I feel the grain of the oak peg beginning to give. The fibers are sliding past one another, dry and silent. If the peg crushes completely, I will have to signal the Skein-Menders to come and weld the tile-seams with amber resin to halt the slide. But the peg holds. It stops at six-point-eight millimeters. The tension has found its balance.

I let out a long, dry hiss of nitrogen from my internal bellows. It is the only voice I have—the sigh of a wooden toy being squeezed.

***

From my position on the Hinge-Gap, I can look "up" into the loop. 

Directly above me, hanging suspended like a vast, dusty chandelier, is another terrace. I can see its underside: a grid of copper pipes wrapped in felt-moss, and the tiny, three-legged shapes of Skein-Menders crawling like brass ticks across the seams, their antennae spitting silent, violet sparks of static. Above them, the "sky" is the same white porcelain grid that sits above me. We are sandwiched between two floors that are also two ceilings.

A Vitreous Spinner slides down the *lux* pillar next to me.

It is a large one, easily ten meters from its lead-limb to its tail-peg. Its hollow elder-wood body is the color of old paper, and its joints are bound with thick, dry sinew that creaks like a ship's timber as it moves. *Ping-shhh... ping-shhh...* go its copper cuffs as they slide down the glass-like light.

It does not notice me. I am part of the floor. 

It stops two meters above me and begins to graze on the pillar. Its flint tongue makes a dry, scraping *schritch-schritch* against the solid light. As it eats, a fine drift of white silica-dust—the "frozen" light turned back into mineral powder—falls from its mouthparts. 

The powder settles over my wooden discs. It feels cool, like dry salt. It fills the grain of my ash-hard skin, polishing the wood, filling the tiny micro-cracks that have opened in my joints over the last century. This is how we are preserved. We do not oil ourselves; we are dry-lubricated by the dust of the light.

When the Spinner has finished its run, it tilts its flat gypsum head toward the ceiling, senses the optimal frequency of the glare, and slides upward again, disappearing into the white haze where the terraces loop into the infinite.

***

There is no time here, only the state of being *correct*.

A human observer—should such a damp, soft thing ever stumble into this vault—would find this world terrifying in its stillness. They would look for a beginning or an end; they would ask who built the turbines, who laid the first cream-colored tile, who spun the first beam of solid light. They would look for a creator, or at least a ruin.

But we do not think of ruins. A machine that mends itself as it grows cannot be ruined; it can only be slower or faster. 

When my ash-wood discs eventually become too packed with silica dust to flex—when my copper spine loses its temper and will no longer contract under the static of the tiles—I will not fall. I will not soften. I will simply stop sliding.

I will choose a seam that needs permanent bracing. I will pack myself into the gap, extending my discs one last time to fill the space between the tiles. The Menders will come. They will run their static-welders over my wooden ribs, coating me in a hard, glassy seal of yellow amber. 

The felted mosses will grow over my stack of plates, their dry, grey needles anchoring my spine to the floor. The silica dust will settle in my hollows, turning my birch-horn skin slowly, century by century, into a pillar of green-veined jasper.

I will not be gone. I will simply have transitioned from the register to the hinge.

For now, though, the nitrogen wind is cool and dry. It carries the scent of ozone and parched pine. Below me, the great turbines hum their G-sharp, and the wooden gears of the world turn their three-day circle.

*Hruff-clack.* 

I slide another centimeter along the seam. The peg is holding. The tension is perfect. The sky is bright, and there are no clouds.
