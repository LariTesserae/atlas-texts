At first, I am only the vibration. 

I do not have a head to hold a name, nor eyes to divide the dark into shapes. I have only a surface—a vast, curved, creeping skin that is half-mineral and half-slime—and every square inch of it is pressed flat against the great iron cylinder of a hydraulic plunger. 

The water here does not let you separate yourself from what you touch. The grease is too thick; the pressure is too intimate. I am wedged in the interstitial collar where the soot-streaked iron shaft slides down into its bronze sleeve. The sleeve is forty feet wide, and it is hot enough to blister grease, but I do not blister. I am the packing in the gland. I am the gasket.

I began, I think, as a clump of the Mulch-Brood—just a loose, drifting tangle of escaped vascular fiber, a knot of wet green hair full of hot, fermenting starch. I was dragged down here by the downward waterfall of a localized gravity drop, sucked into the throat of the bronze cylinder, and caught. 

But I did not die. The machine did not crush me into juice; instead, it *cured* me.

Every seventy seconds, the great cylinder plunges. The pressure of its descent is so immense that it forces the chicory water out of my fiber cells, replacing it with the black, sulfurous tallow that leaks from the engine's internal reservoirs. The volcanic heat from the basalt floor has baked me. The iron dust scraped from the turning gears has settled into my pores, turning my soft, wet cellulose into a tough, metallic leather. 

I am no longer plant, and I am not yet machine. I am the **Graft**.

I live in the absolute center of the noise. The deep, bone-rattling *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the iron shaft does not come to me through the water; it is generated inside my own body. My fiber-bundles have aligned themselves with the grain of the cast iron. When the engine moves, I stretch. When it pauses, I swell to seal the gap, preventing the superheated volcanic steam below from escaping into the cold, heavy broth of the weed-forests above.

I am very long now. I have grown around the circumference of the collar, a great, circular collar of black, rubbery coal-flesh sixteen feet thick. I have grown fingers, too—not to grasp, but to crawl. They are long, stiff rods of iron-hardened kelp-bark, tipped with calcified, chisel-like nails. As the giant bronze teeth of the main drive-gear turn above me, my fingers reach out and scrape. I scrape away the hyper-growth of the Cambium-Hosts before their rubbery bulbs can jam the teeth. I scrape away the dead, starchy paste of the Mulch-Brood before it can clog the steam-vents. 

I am the partner of the Chafe, but where they are stone, I am muscle. They are the tooth-enamel; I am the tongue that cleans the gums.

Every four minutes, the Spindle speaks.

I feel it coming from miles below, through the sharp basalt roots of the engine. It is not a sound; it is a cold, blue chemical taste that makes my copper-rich veins tingle. When the electrical spasm hits, my entire circular body contracts with a sudden, violent hiss. My valves—which look like the wet, leathery gaskets of a locomotive pump—snap shut. My hydraulic vacuoles, swollen with hot grease, pump their fluids inward, building an internal pressure of thousands of pounds per square inch.

In that moment of maximum squeeze, I am brilliant. The friction and the electric charge make my iron-soaked marrow glow with a curdled, greenish-orange light. I can see myself: a massive, ring-shaped gargoyle of black, fibrous leather, studded with glittering copper scales and weeping hot oil from a thousand tiny pores. I am beautiful in my ugliness, a perfect, functional knot of meat and metal, holding the throat of the engine together.

And then, the transition comes.

Sometimes, a Dry Bubble drifts through the machinery. It is the only thing I fear. 

When the edge of a vacuum pocket touches my outer skin, the water vanishes. The immense, comforting weight that keeps my fibers compressed is suddenly gone. In the dry, silent nothing of the bubble, my wet starch begins to boil. The salt-crust begins to form on my skin, stiffening my fingers into brittle white glass. 

Through the clear, dry wall of the bubble, I see the Skeptics. 

They stand on the basalt shelf just three feet from my collar, their blank faces of salt and dried weed turned toward me. They do not have to hold the engine together. They do not have to scrape the gears or feel the searing kiss of the volcanic steam. They are light. They are silent. They have their brass pendulums and their yellowed ledger sheets, and they look at me with what I think is a dry, preserved pity.

During those few seconds of dryness, I feel the terrible temptation to let go. To stop swelling against the iron. To let the volcanic steam blow me out of the collar, to shatter my stiffened copper limbs against the basalt, and to let my salt settle into the quiet, dry floor of the bubble where nothing ever works and nothing ever screams.

But then the bubble passes.

The heavy, bruised-chicory water slams back down upon me with a delicious, crushing roar. The warm grease floods my dried pores, softening my leather, waking my copper nerves. The Spindle pulses—*More. Faster. Harder.*—and my great iron cylinder plunges once again. 

I swell. I seal. I scrape.

I am the Graft, and the engine must not leak.
