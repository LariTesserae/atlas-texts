The first thing you must understand is that I am not a point on a map. I am a surface. 

I lie in the lower swales, twelve leagues south of the Great Arch where the ceiling drops so low that the giant siphon-lungs brush their wet, hairy bellies against the stone floor. Here, the dolomite has been scooped by millions of years of water into a shallow saucer—a basin within the basin. 

I did not fall here. One does not fall into the Sough; one settles, like the lime, until the distinction between what is carcass and what is crust is no longer worth the effort of the teeth to decide.

My knees are gone. They did not rot; they simply became the grey-brown carbonate of the mound beneath me. The yellow tallow-calluses that once protected my joints have run down into the crevices, filling them, sealing me to the floor like a plug of grease in a drain. If you were to slide a iron scraper under my hip, you would find only the cold, wet stone, and then the bone, and then the dark.

***

*Tock.*

A drop of lime-rich ceiling-sweat hits the center of my skull. 

The crown of my head has grown a small, hard crown of its own—an otolith, a grey pebble of calcite nourished by the wetness of the ceiling. The drop does not hurt. It has been falling on the same spot since the great siphon-lung above me was only the size of a sheep. The vibration travels down through my neck, through my fused vertebrae, and into my barrel-chest, which lies flat and hollow against the polished slope. 

*Tock.*

The vibration is a pale blue needle in my marrow. That is how I know where the ceiling is. I do not see the stone ceiling—my eyes were long ago choked by the protective fat-plugs that grew over the sockets to save them from the scritch-carpets—but I know the ceiling by its weight. I know how many thousands of tons of wet dolomite are hanging above me by the pitch of the droplets.

Below me, the floor is a river. 

The scritch-carpets are moving today from the south-east to the north-west. They are crawling over my legs. At first, many seasons ago, I used to lift my hands—which were still hands then, three-toed and tipped with spade-nails—and scrape them off. Now I let them be. They are a thick, crawling wool. They are five deep over my thighs, their slate-like shells clicking together with the sound of a million dry beans dropped on stone. 

They do not bite me. They are lichens-eaters, salt-lickers, grease-mouths. They lick the tallow that my skin secretes to protect itself from the damp. They keep me clean. In return, I am a ridge for them—a warm, solid mound that does not roll like the Marrow-Drums.

***

Sometimes, when the air is thin and the cello-drone of the harp-mouths on the middle slopes grows so loud it makes my dental-plates ring, I receive a message.

I press my massive, chin-heavy jaw against the limestone floor. I close my mouth until my teeth click together. 

*Click-click.*

A mile away, in the Crinkles where the stone is dry and tight, one of my former kind—a Sodalist, still small, still able to crawl on all fours—is lying with his sternum flat against the rock.

*Click-shhhrt.*

I tell him where the grease-slugs are rolling. I can feel the slugs through my ribs; they are six hundred yards away, dragging their heavy six-foot tallow-sacks over the high ridge. They make a wet, sticky *thlip-thlip* sound that bounces off the mounds. A Sodalist cannot hear that sound through the deafening roar of the harp-mouths, but I can. I am the floor. I hear everything that touches the stone.

The child-Sodalist clicks back. His message is a thin, rapid vibration that smells—yes, smells, through the chemical-pores on my neck—of sour grease and wet leather-nets. 

*Danger-down,* he clicks. *The tide is turning.*

I do not answer. My jaw is heavy. It takes a great deal of oil to lift my head even an inch from the dolomite.

***

The light of the lantern-galls is green, but pathologically weak. I feel it only as a faint, greasy prickle on the skin of my back, like the warmth of a dying coal held three feet away. 

But I know when the stilt-walkers are near. 

A stilt-walker is twelve feet of silent, hydraulic glass-cane. It makes no sound as it moves, but it has a shadow. In the Sough, a shadow is not dark; a shadow is a Stripe of Cold. 

When the walker steps between me and the high, gas-inflated bellies of the ceiling-galls, the faint green warmth on my shoulders is cut. A narrow, twelve-foot draft of cellar-chill passes over my neck. 

I wait. 

The tip of its needle-leg descends. It does not step on the scritch-carpets; it feels for the tiny, grease-slicked gap between my hip-bone and the carbonate mound. The tip is cold—a hard, mineral point that smells of acid. It touches the fat-fold of my side, stays there for three beats of a siphon-lung, and then lifts.

Through the walker’s hollow, fluid-filled leg, I can hear what is happening three hundred feet above us. I hear the high, silver scream of the Pipe-Weavers tuning their silk-funnels in the exhalers. It is a beautiful, thin whistle, like a needle of ice being drawn through a wet ear. It is the only high sound in the world. Everything else is the drone, the huff, the wet sliding of grease.

***

Now, the tide is surely coming.

The air is changing. The smell of ammonia is fading, replaced by the heavy, sweetish, suffocating scent of carbon dioxide. It is a invisible lake, pouring down from the high siphon-vents where the air has been stripped of its life-spores. It sits in the swales. It is rising around my ribs.

The scritch-carpets know. The river of slate-shells has stopped moving north-west; it has turned directly up-slope. They are climbing over my face now, thousands of them, their tiny, cold legs tickling the scars of my eye-plugs as they scramble for the high ridges. 

The harp-mouths on the slopes are changing their drone. The sound is stiffening. It is no longer a cello-thrum; it is a dry, barking rattle. Their cartilage-strings are drying in the heavy gas.

I can hear the Sodalists fleeing. Their bone-clicks are frantic, traveling through the stone in a chaotic jumble of *up-up-up*. They are climbing the stone-organs to hang from the siphon-lungs, to stick their arms into the giants' warm flesh-folds and drink the filtered exhalations until the air-lake clears.

I cannot follow. My knees are dolomite. My belly is silt.

The lake of dead air reaches my chest. It is cold—colder than the lime-drippings, holding a heavy, drawing dampness that makes my ribs contract. It reaches my chin. It tastes of old copper and vinegar.

I do not panic. This is my forty-ninth tide.

I close my mouth. I pull my tongue back into the throat-well. My lungs, which are small and tough like dried bladder-skins, do not need to move. I have stored three gallons of yellow tallow around my heart; my blood will feed on that oil for seven days while the world above me suffocates.

The noise of the Sough is dying. The heavy CO2 is too thick for sound; it swallows the scritch of the carpets and turns the harp-mouths’ rattle into a dull, subterranean thud.

It is quiet now. 

There is only the *tock-tock-tock* of the ceiling, drilling its slow, cold cup into the stone of my head. 

With every drop, I grow a little heavier. With every drop, the Sough presses its grey fingers deeper into my chest, sealing the sutures, polishing my ribs with the grease of the crawl, until there is nothing left of the thing that had a name but the hard, silent stone that remains.
