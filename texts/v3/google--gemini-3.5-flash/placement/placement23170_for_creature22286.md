I hang sixty feet below the wet underbelly of the third tier, suspended by nothing but a double-braided cord of moth-silk and the seal of my own soles against the basalt. 

To the people of the valleys, this would be a nightmare of empty air, but to me, the drop is simply the space where the mountain breathes. Below me, the purple shadow of the cleft plunges three hundred yards into the sulfurous warmth of the lower vents; above, the thread of sky is the color of a bruised plum. 

I am one of the *Oru*, though I have lived long enough that my skin has begun to lose its slate-blue moisture. If you were to touch the palms of my hands, you would find the charcoal-grey whorls—the tiny, leather ridges that draw the air out from the stone—are wearing smooth at the edges. They look like old, water-rounded pebbles now, rather than the sharp, fresh-split shale of a young hunter’s grip. This is my thirty-fourth season tending the *Mother-Creeper*, the great central muscle-vine that anchors the lowest six huts of the village.

My somatic name is *Three-Flicks-and-a-Sigh*—a gesture made by tapping the middle three fingers against the thumb-pad, then slowly widening the hand like a fern opening to the mist. In the language of the chasm, it means *He Who Stays Deep*.

Against my collarbone, nestled in the heavy, violet-dyed folds of my moth-silk coat, is *Tse*. She is a flat-bodied Lanth-lizard, no longer than my middle finger, her translucent skin so clear that I can see the slow, rhythmic pulse of her three tiny hearts. As I dangle in the cold draft, her throat-sac glows with a steady, lukewarm indigo light, illuminating the basalt wall inches from my nose. 

The basalt is alive today. It hums. 

Through the bone stylus tucked behind my ear, I can feel the vibrations of the upper tiers. It is a busy morning. The children are out on the suspension bridges, their small, dark eyes wide as they drift their bird-bone cups through the rising mist-globes. I feel the rhythmic *click-click-slide* of a five-year-old’s small fingers three levels up, followed by the soft, vibrating scratch of an elder’s stylus: *Three globes caught. A clean harvest.*

I smile, a silent widening of my lips that does not disturb the cold air in my lungs. My wide, flat ribs expand, drawing in the thin, metallic air. I hold it, letting my blood drink the chill, before letting it out in a slow, noiseless stream that doesn't even disturb the cloud-moth sleeping on my sleeve.

Before me is the joint of the Mother-Creeper. 

It is as thick as my thigh, a corded mass of wet, fibrous muscle that runs from a deep geothermal cleft in the rock out to the main support-timber of the Weaver’s hut. The vine is tense today. The mountain cold has sharpened over the last hour, and the fiber has contracted, pulling the slate hut tight against the basalt to keep the Weaver’s children warm. But it has pulled too tight. The wooden pegs driven into the basalt are groaning; I can feel the high-frequency distress of the wood through the soles of my feet.

From the leather pot at my hip, I scoop a glob of warm, yellow tallow. It smells of sulfur, old fat, and the root-oil of the violet moss. 

I do not speak to the vine—to speak is to break the world—but I lay my hand upon its thickest knot. Through the remaining whorls on my palm, I send a rhythmic, pulsing pressure. *Relax,* the pressure says. *Lengthen. The stone is strong, but the wood must breathe.*

I begin to rub the tallow into the fibrous joints. The work is slow. My long, thin fingers trace the run of the plant-muscle, tracing the heat that rises from its core. Under my palms, the tendon begins to soften. I feel the subtle, liquid slide of the fibers as they drink the fat. Slowly, the groaning in the peg subsides. The Weaver’s hut swings out perhaps three inches into the damp air current, its slate gutters aligning perfectly to catch the next drift of mist-plums.

As I work, a shiver-fox leaps from the low sill of the Weaver’s window. It is a tiny, puff-furred thing, no larger than a fist, its grey coat so light it seems to float. It does not look at me; it doesn't need to. It launches itself into the open space of the chasm, its wide skin-flaps catching the rising geothermal draft. It glides in an absolute, ghostly circle, its soft, hair-ciliated paws landing without a sound on the vertical basalt across the rift. 

Then, it does something that makes my chest tighten.

Instead of hunting the swirling rivers of cloud-moths, the fox presses itself flat against the wet rock. It tucks its pointed ears back, turns its nose downward toward the sulfur-vents, and begins to tremble. Its fur, usually loose and cloudy, contracts until it looks like a hard, grey scab on the stone.

*A frost-shear.*

I look down. The floating water-globes, which usually drift like lazy, translucent plums, are beginning to change. Their outer skins are tightening, shivering in the changing draft. As I watch, one of them—the size of a man's head—rises past my knees. It is no longer liquid. A delicate, crystalline skin of ice has formed over its surface, refracting the lavender dawn into hundreds of tiny, violet needles.

In minutes, the rift will freeze. The water-globes will become ice-glass, falling like stones.

I reach for my bone stylus. My hand is stiffening with the sudden, plunging cold, but I press the needle-sharp tip of the bone against the bare basalt. 

I tap. 

*Three fast, hard strikes. A long, dragging scrape. Two fast strikes.*

The basalt carries my voice. It travels up through the sharp shale layers, through the slate foundations of the huts, into the feet of the mothers, the hands of the masons, the chests of the viner-tenders. 

*The sky is closing. Pull the children in. Grease the upper links before they freeze.*

Across the chasm, the blue throat-sacs of the flat-bodied lizards begin to stutter in rapid, chaotic syncopation, relaying the message into the dark crevices where the stone-taps cannot reach. Up on the bridges, the children vanish. They do not run—running is for flat-landers—they flow backward into their huts like water returning to a cistern, their bird-bone cups clattering softly against their chests.

I do not climb up. Not yet. 

The Mother-Creeper needs one more coat of tallow to survive the shear. If the frost locks its fibers while they are dry, the muscle will split, and the Weaver's hut will drop into the Grave-Moss by morning.

My fingers are numb, the charcoal skin turning a pale, warning white as the cold begins to freeze the moisture in my skin-ridges. I scoop the last of the tallow. I do not use the spatula; I use my bare palms, pressing my own warmth into the heart of the vine, whispering through the skin of my hands.

*Hold,* I tell it. *We are both old. We both know how to cling.*

The vine shivers, absorbing the grease, pulling me slightly closer to the warm basalt as the first ice-globe falls from above. It passes me with a sharp, musical *whuff*, its frozen shell shearing a clump of violet moss from a ledge fifty feet below.

I press my forehead against the cold, black basalt, my eyes closed, listening to the mountain’s clockwork. I feel the huts above me retracting, pulling tight against the stone's shoulder like sleeping birds. I feel the shiver-foxes tucked deep into the geothermal cracks, their tiny hearts slowing to match the rhythm of the chimneys. 

And here, suspended in the silent, frozen dark of the rift, I am exactly where I belong: a single, fragile joint in the great, quiet alignment of the mountain, holding the line between the sky and the void.
