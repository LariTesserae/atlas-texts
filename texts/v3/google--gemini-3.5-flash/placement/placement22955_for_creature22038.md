The cold does not feel like an enemy here. It is simply the temperature of things that are true. 

It enters through the chest first, because that is where the weight is heaviest. When I have lain still for many hours—perhaps three floods, perhaps four—the herringbone pattern of the terracotta has bitten deep into my belly. I can feel the exact shape of the three-span tiles: the rough, sand-textured face of one, the slightly vitrified, over-fired corner of another, and the cold, wet trench of the seam between them where the brick-dust has gathered. 

I do not have a name, because there is no one here who would use it, but I have a weight. I am three thousand pounds of wet lard and slow, gray skin, and my work is very small.

My hands are where my thoughts are. 

I have four of them, small and pale, bunched together under the heavy fold of my chin like a cluster of root-vegetables. Compared to the rest of me—this great, blind sack of silt-flesh—my hands are delicate. The skin on the fingers is yellowed and tough, but the tips are incredibly sensitive. They can feel the difference between the lime-crust left by a dying snail and the white salt-bloom that rises from the clay when the Sump-Spindle breathes. 

Right now, my fingers are in a seam. 

A tile has begun to lift. It is a tile near the eastern wall, where the weeping from the dry-laid granite is loudest—a sound like *sheerp, sheerp, sheerp* as the water slides down the stone face. The tile has buckled because the Spindle, far below in the dark, has swollen with the last flood and pushed a yellow root-finger upward. If I leave it, the next torrent will catch the lip of the clay. The water will tear it free, and then the snails will tumble into the gap, and their shells will grind together until the weep-hole is choked with white gravel.

So, I work. 

My nails—flat, gray, and hard as slate—scrape the joint. *Scritch. Scritch. Scritch.* The sound is tiny, but in this basin, everything has an echo. It travels up the thirty-foot walls of granite, lost for a moment in the slate fog, before bouncing off the broken stone ribs of the vault overhead. 

Above me, there is a sudden, dry *snick*. 

I do not look up. I cannot. My eyes are only two weeping slits that see nothing but the difference between the gray of the day and the black of the wall-shadows. But I know what the sound is. A Gutter-Snipe has dropped from an archway. A drop of ceiling-water, falling toward a pool three paces to my left, did not make its metallic *ping*. It was intercepted. The Snipe’s skin must have hissed as it fell through the draft—that thin, naked skin that looks like wet paper. Now there is a faint, wet thrashing as the Snipe climbs back up the granite blocks, its three-jointed wing-claws clicking into the chisel-marks. It has a crayfish in its beak. I can smell the ruptured shell and the old-pond stink of the creature’s tail.

The scraping takes a long time. My arms are short, and my body must slide-drag itself an inch at a time to keep my hands in the crack. My belly makes a wet, flat *thluck-thluck* against the tiles as I move. 

Suddenly, the floor beneath my massive, leathery foot—the great disk of muscle upon which I drift—begins to tremble. 

It is not a shake; it is a long, low hum that starts in the marrow of my bones. The Sump-Spindle is beginning to empty. The water it drank an hour ago has been digested, stripped of its iron and lime, and now the great fibrous mattress beneath the floor is shrinking. The terracotta tiles groan as they settle. The tile I am working on sinks two lines of an inch, its edge finally aligning with its neighbors. I press down upon it with my chest, using my great weight to seat it firmly into the shifting bed of brick-dust.

*Clink.*

The copper float-valve in the northern wall has reached its latch. 

The basin goes very quiet for three heartbeats. Even the dripping from the ceiling seems to hesitate. 

Then comes the migration.

I feel them before I hear them. From the dry-ish margins of the walls, where the black slime turns to ink, the Shingle-backs are moving. Thousands of them. They do not run, but they walk with a frantic, dry-legged urgency, their gray, plate-like backs dryly clicking against one another like a spilled bag of coins. 

They know my bulk. They know the height of my back.

The first ones reach my flank. Their tiny, jointed legs tickle—a sharp, prickling sensation that would make a smaller creature twitch, but to me, it is only the sign of the cycle turning. They climb. They scramble over the folds of my cold, gray skin, packing themselves into the deep, herringbone-molded scars on my sides. They pile on top of one another, three or four deep, until my entire back is covered in a dense, rattling roof of living slate. 

I flatten myself. I draw my four small hands under my chin, tucking them into the warm, wet crease of my throat. I press my head against the cold tile. I become a hill.

*WHOOSH.*

The stone spout near the top of the wall lets loose. 

The water does not fall as rain; it is a solid, foaming tongue of ice and iron that strikes the granite steps and roars down into the basin. Within seconds, the floor is gone. The shallow pools join together into a single, swirling lake, two inches deep, four inches deep in the hollows. 

Around my base, the white snails are rolling. They have pulled their soft bodies inside, their ivory shells clicking together like dry beans in a sack as the current sweeps them toward the drains. The blind crayfish hang onto the tile-edges with their pale claws, their long, whip-like antennae lashing in the wild, cold dark of the flood.

The water rises over my sides. It is freezing, but my skin is thick—an inch of cold fat that knows no shivering. On my back, the Shingle-backs are silent now. They are dry. They cling to each other and to me, a single, water-tight dome of armor that keeps the flood from their lungs. I can feel the vibration of their thousands of tiny hearts, beating in those small, gray hulls, a fast, dry hum that contrasts with the deep, slow throb of the water.

For ten minutes, the world is nothing but the roar of the spout and the cold pressure of the lake against my ribs. I lie still. I am the anchor of the basin. If I were to move, if I were to slide, I would crush a thousand snails, and the Shingle-backs would slip into the grey torrent and drown. 

And then, the roar dies.

The spout gurgles, spits twice, and is quiet. 

Immediately, the weep-holes begin to drink. I can hear the water whispering as it is sucked down through the floor-tiles, a long, sighing *hushhhhh* that sounds like dry leaves being dragged across a stone courtyard. The water level drops. The backs of the crayfish emerge; the snails find their footing on the wet terracotta and begin their slow, blind journeys once more, leaving thin, silver trails of mucus behind them.

The Shingle-backs begin to untie themselves from my back. They slide down my flanks in small, gray slides, their armor scraping my skin with that familiar, dry rattle. They do not say thank you. They do not know who I am. They only know that the hill did not drown them. They disperse toward the walls, hungry for the black slime that has been washed clean by the flood.

I let out a long, wet *sough* through my nostrils. A small plume of white steam rises into the slate-gray fog, hangs for a second, and vanishes.

My hands creep out from beneath my chin. They feel the tile I have just seated. It is flat. It is cold. It is where it belongs.

Two paces away, there is another tile that hums slightly when the water drains. It is sitting too high. 

I begin to drag my belly across the wet clay. *Thluck-thluck.* 

There is work to be done before the next *clink*.
