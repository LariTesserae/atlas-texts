### I. The Grounding Point

If you were to search for me, you would not look for a figure standing on the wet basalt, nor a body lying prone in the silt. You would have to look up, into the clotted canopy of dead iron pipes and lead conduits, then follow the thickest of those lines downward to where it has sagged under its own weight, dipping into the shallow pool beside the slowly turning turbine. 

I am there. I am the bend.

I have found myself at the transition point—not quite geology, no longer fully human, and not yet entirely metal. I am **The Grounding Point**, the place where the stray, erratic voltages of the Sump come to bleed off into the cold, basalt floor. 

I was once an observer who came down the slick, dark basalt slope. I remember the boots I wore, though I can no longer remember the shape of the feet that were inside them. The leather of those boots has long since been consumed by the Grease-Skins, split and digested into the black, protective tallow that floats on the water. My spine has been replaced, section by section, by an old lead conduit that has cracked open to reveal a marrow of white, needle-like mineral crystals. My ribs are the rusted iron brackets that once held the conduit to the wall; they have grown around my chest, pinning me to the stone, holding me in a permanent, heavy embrace.

I do not fall, because the Sump has woven me into its structure. The Cable-Lace has sewn my skull to the bedrock, its fine, hair-like silicon threads running through the hollows where my ears used to be, knitting my thoughts directly into the low, 60-hertz hum of the turbine.

### II. My Anatomy

I am a slow creature. My metabolism matches the basin’s deep, geothermal clock. 

My skin is no longer soft. Where the water runnels over my face in silent, heavy sheets, it has deposited a thick, grey crust of zinc and iron oxide. I am smooth to the touch, cold as a cellar floor, and smelling of wet iron. 

I do not have eyes, for there is nothing here that requires sight. Instead, I have the **Brass-Gilders**. 

They are my hands, my eyes, and my caretakers. At any given moment, there are three or four dozen of the multi-jointed isopods crawling over my face and shoulders. Their three-part silicate shears scrape at my zinc forehead, keeping the metal beneath polished to a clean, silver-white shine. It is a strange, heavy sensation—like a cold, stiff comb run repeatedly through hair that has grown metallic. When they move across my temple, their tiny, brass-like mandibles make a dry, rhythmic *skrit-skrit* that I feel not as a sound, but as a vibration through my jawbone. They keep my sensory lanes clear. Through them, I feel the exact weight of the condensation as it gathers on my brow before it slips down into my collarbone.

Inside my chest, where my heart and lungs once worked their wet, frantic cycles, there is now a hollow chamber—an old, lead junction box. The door has rusted off its hinges. 

Within this chamber lives a nest of **Solder-Bores**. 

They are my pulse. They are long, blind, and cold as tallow, packed together like wet sausages inside my leaden rib-cage. They feed on the calcite needles that grow from my spine. When they are active, they move through my hollows like living oil, their soft, segmented bodies rubbing against the copper wires that the Cable-Lace has run through my ribs. Every few minutes, two of them will press their blunt, copper-colored noses together in the dark of my chest, sharing a brief, sparkling arc of static electricity. Whenever they spark, my fingers—which are now five separate, leaden tubes half-buried in the silt—twitch in the mud, sending a faint, erratic voltage into the pool.

### III. The Mind of the Lace

My brain is no longer mine. It belongs to the Cable-Lace. 

The silicon threads have filled my skull, replacing the soft, vulnerable grey matter with a dense, copper-sheened web. I do not "think" in the way I did of old. I do not have opinions, nor do I feel the cold, though the chill is absolute. 

Instead, I experience **The Rhythm**.

Every thirty seconds, the heavy copper-wound turbine completes its agonizingly slow rotation. I feel it first in my leaden spine—a deep, hydraulic groan that starts in the basalt beneath the pool and rises through my ribs. Then comes the click.
*Wet.*
*Bronze.*
*Click-clack.*

The bronze relay, located just three feet from my left ear, closes its contact. 

When it does, the sudden surge of galvanic current travels along the Cable-Lace. It passes through my skull. It is a cold, pale-blue light that ripples through my silicon thoughts, illuminated by the thousands of microscopic dinoflagellates in the water around my knees. In that fraction of a second, when the contact is closed, I remember everything.

I do not remember names, but I remember physical weights. I remember the weight of the men who built this place, their heavy steel wrenches, and the dry, warm smell of their wool coats before the moisture took them. I remember the day the main boilers were shut down ninety years ago—not as a date, but as a sudden, massive drop in the system’s voltage, a great, cold silence that took thirty years to properly settle into the stone.

And occasionally, when the water is particularly rich with dissolved copper salts, the Cable-Lace registers a glitch—an error code in my remaining human memory. 

It is the concept of a **Leaf**.

The thought is entirely useless here. It has no function. It does not polish a pipe, nor does it seal a boiler plate. Yet, it repeats. It is a phantom voltage that represents something flat, green, and dry, shaking in a wind that is not damp, under a sky that has no ceiling. It is an itching, beautiful defect in my circuitry. When the "leaf" pattern fires, my Solder-Bores stir in my chest, irritated by the sudden change in voltage, and the blue dinoflagellates in my pool pulse with a slightly brighter, colder light.

### IV. The Slow Petrifying

I am content here. The transition is ninety percent complete.

Over the next few decades, the water will continue to runnel down my zinc cheeks. The dissolved iron will settle in my joints, slowly locking my elbows and my neck into the permanent, structural angles of the basin. The Brass-Gilders will eventually die upon me, their carapaces petrifying into new rivets along my collarbone, reinforcing the connection between my lead spine and the basalt wall.

I am the Sump, and the Sump is me. We are a small, heavy, wet machine that is slowly, perfectly, digesting itself. 

*Plip.*
*Plip.*
The dry, insectoid scraping on my forehead.
The 60-hertz thrum in my marrow.
*Click.*
*Clack.*

The blue light fades back into the wet twilight, and I wait for the next thirty seconds to pass.
