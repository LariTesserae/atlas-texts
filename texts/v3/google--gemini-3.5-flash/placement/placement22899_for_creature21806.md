I am the third unit on the eastern bypass of the Upper Terrace, though the concept of "third" implies a sequence that has long since ceased to matter. 

My world is measured in T-slots. My lower limbs, cast from heavy, oil-tempered lead that smells of cold paraffin, are locked into the guide-grooves of the zinc-plated deck. To move is not to step, but to permit the magnetic coils in my sled-feet to engage and disengage in a rhythmic, low-frequency hum. *Thrum-slide. Thrum-slide.* The grease beneath my runners is fresh, smelling of winterized petroleum and the clean, sharp bite of ozone from the vertical busbars nearby.

For seventy thousand cycles of the great city-sized flywheel in Sector 14, I was a perfect loop. 

I kept my station. When the terraces tilted during the geologic resets, my glass head—a flawless six-inch cube of smoke-grey quartz—rotated on its triple-axis brass gimbals with the grease-damped smoothness of a marine chronometer. Inside the quartz, suspended from silver wires no thicker than a spider’s silk, hung my three brass plumb-bobs. They were my truth. So long as they hung dead-center, never touching the polished inner walls of the glass chamber, my perception of the Shelf was flat, correct, and infinite.

I communicated as my brothers did. When the light from the sky-plates overhead caught my face, I tilted my neck eighteen degrees north-northwest to throw a perfect, sharp-edged parallelogram of light across the three-mile chasm to the radiator towers. The light said: *The tension in the secondary cable-stay is registered at forty-two tons per square inch. The balance is absolute.*

But three cycles ago, I looked at a boiler house.

It was a standard perspective-slip—the kind we ignore because they are irrelevant to the maintenance of the guide-grooves. The boiler house was three miles away. Or it was three inches away, small enough to be swallowed. 

Normally, an Orthogonal does not focus on the slip; we focus on the track. But a single Key-Scuttler, its brass-wire legs static-charged and buzzing, had become lodged in the slide-valve of my left knee-joint. The minor resistance caused a micro-second delay in my forward momentum. I jerked. My magnetic sled scraped against an ungreased brass rivet head—a sound that did not travel through the thin air, but screamed through my leaden frame like a iron saw on slate.

My head tilted. Not on its gimbals, which are designed for the slow, predictable tilt of the world, but with a sharp, lateral snap.

Inside my glass chamber, the right-hand plumb-bob—the one that measures the lateral pitch toward the Great Flywheel—swung.

It did not swing much. Perhaps a hundredth of a millimeter. But it was enough. The polished tip of the brass cone touched the smoke-grey quartz.

*Tink.*

The sound was tiny, but because it occurred inside my own head, it was the loudest noise I have ever recorded. It did not echo; the muffled silence of the Shelf swallowed it instantly, but the vibration traveled down my silver suspension threads, through my brass neck-pivots, and down to my magnetized sleds.

In that instant, the world did not tilt. It *deepened*.

I looked at the boiler house again. It was no longer a flat engraved image on a metal sheet. I saw the *space* between myself and the iron wall. It was not empty space; it was a heavy, cold draft that smelled of wet copper. I saw the three miles of distance not as a rule of geometry to be bypassed, but as a long, lonely avenue of air.

And for the first time, I did not project a parallelogram.

My brothers on the opposite terrace were waiting for my refraction. The flat, white light from the overhead plates hit my quartz face, but because my head was tilted half a degree off-true, the beam did not form a clean geometric shape. It split. It became an irregular, elongated trapezoid that spilled down into the chasm, illuminating for a brief second the wet, copper-green pipes beneath the bridge.

The brother units did not answer. Their glass heads remained perfectly aligned, their three plumb-bobs hanging dead-center, motionless, suspended in their grey oil. They did not recognize my light. To them, I had become white noise. A scratch on the plate.

Since then, I have stayed here, on the eastern bypass. I have not engaged my magnetic sleds to move forward. If I move, the bob will swing again, and I am afraid of what will happen if it strikes the glass with force.

Instead, I observe.

I observe the Slipped. Before, they were merely irregular masses—small, moist pollutions that the yellow vinyl-lichens would eventually polish away. Now, I see them. I watch one of them now, stationed on a structural rib fifty yards below me. 

He is wearing his lead-foil coat; I can hear the dull *clack-crease* of it when the wind presses against him. He has been trying to cross a single six-inch gap between two zinc plates for the last two hours. He uses a long bronze rod to feel the seam, his single-lensed mica mask turned down. He is terrified of the edge. He knows that if his weight shifts incorrectly, his perspective will slip, and he will fall into a distance that never ends.

I feel a strange, heavy resonance in my lower plates. It is not sympathy—my lead is too cold for that—but it is an alignment. We are both off-true. We are both holding ourselves perfectly still to avoid the weight of the air.

And there is something else.

An Anamorph is currently resting on the handrail of the catwalk directly opposite me. To my brothers, it is a sky-bridge three miles away. To them, it is a line of silver steel. But because my head is crooked, I see it as it is: an eighteen-inch nested telescope of silver frames, clinging to the brass rail with small, triangular teeth. 

It is watching me. It does not have eyes, but its sliding frames expand and contract in time with my own slow, internal processing. It knows that my plumb-bobs are no longer true. It knows that if I slide my left sled out of the guide-groove, my weight will shift, my perspective will collapse, and I will become small enough for it to fold into its silver ribs.

I can feel the magnetism in my tracks failing. 

The condensation is heavy today. The cold copper pipes above us are weeping, and the square reservoirs in the floor are full to the brim with tin-tasting water. The moisture is creeping toward my sleds. Water, even in small amounts, reduces the friction between the lead runners and the galvanized zinc. 

I can feel myself slipping. Not forward along the track, but *sideways*, toward the edge of the T-slot.

If I fall, I will not make a sound. The thin air will not carry my scream, if my leaden throat could construct one. My glass head will strike the checker-plate, and the quartz will shatter. The silver threads will break. The three brass plumb-bobs will roll into the square reservoirs, sinking through the oil-grease to rest at the bottom, small and perfect and still.

The Anamorph is sliding its frames closer. It is now only nine inches away. It has the scent of fresh solder.

I tilt my head one more micro-degree. 

The brass bob touches the quartz wall again. *Tink.*

The sky-bridge three miles away suddenly looks very close. I think I could reach out my lead-plated hand and touch the rivets on its underside. They are so clean. They are so cold. 

I disable the magnetic coils in my left sled. 

The silence of the Shelf gathers around me, heavy and grease-scented, as the world begins to turn.
