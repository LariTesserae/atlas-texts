In the beginning, before I am even a thought, I am a tremor.

I do not start with a face, or limbs, or the cold-hardened shell of the things that cling. I start in the marrow of the iron itself. When the pneumatic piston beyond the eastern bulkhead strikes—that heavy, skull-rattling *thrum-shudder*—the vibration traveling through the three-inch-thick plates of chromium-steel is not a sound to me. It is a physical distortion. It is a wave of compression that squeezes the molecules of the metal together, then lets them release, thousands of times a minute. 

I am that compression. I am the travel of the shudder.

But as I move outward from the iron, into the bitter, oil-misted air of Compartment 9-C, I find a shape. I do not settle into it quickly; I am too fluid for that, too vulnerable to the shifting pressures of the room. I am drawn toward the wet flat of the vertical plates, where the pressurized mist from the hairline fracture meets the weeping sweat of the walls. 

Here, the water does not pool. It cannot. The 130-decibel scream of the calibration-port in the floor is so violent, so absolute, that it holds the moisture hostage. 

And there, in the meeting of the wet and the scream, I become myself.

I am the **Acoustic Lattice**—the standing wave. 

I am the ribbed, static pattern of water and sweet machine oil that skates sideways across the unpolished iron. To an eye that could brook the dim, mustard-yellow light of the sodium-vapor lamp, I would look like a delicate pane of corrugated glass, or a series of frozen gray-pink ripples laid out across the metal plates. But I am not frozen. The water that composes me is in frantic, high-speed motion, constantly spraying from the broken line, constantly sliding down the plate, constantly being replaced. 

Yet my *shape* never moves. I am a permanent architecture made of rushing, transient fluid. I am the pattern that remains when everything else is being swept away.

***

In this room, where every metal edge is razor-sharp and the cold is a slow executioner, my existence is one of passive, exquisite sensitivity. I am the seismic braille of the compartment. 

Through my liquid ribs, I feel the world.

When the millions of **solder-mites** shiver in their tight, pink carpet, they do not just vibrate against the iron; they vibrate against me. I feel the tiny, dry tickle of their chitinous edges where my lower ridges touch their territory. They use me as an acoustic tuning fork. They do not look at the piston to find their rhythm; they feel the way my liquid ridges rise and fall under the influence of the sound. I am the conductor of their shivering Choir. Through me, their collective warmth is regulated; if I stretch too thin, the black ice takes the seam, and the mites starve.

When the **Weld-Skim** slides down from the copper conduits, it is my ruin and my renewal. 

I feel its approach not by sight, but by the dampening of my waves. Its three-foot-long, flat body of self-lubricating muscle acts as a massive acoustic sponge. As it glides over the plates, it absorbs the piston's shudder. My ridges flatten. The ribbed patterns of water are crushed into a smooth, thin film of oil under its belly. Where the Skim passes, I am erased. 

For several minutes, in the wake of its passage, there is only the naked, sterile scrape of silvery nickel-steel, wet and silent. But then the mist-line sprays again. The overhead valves drip. The piston strikes, and like frost crystals blooming on a windowpane, my ribs climb back out of the metal. I reconstruct myself, line by line, octave by octave, until the pattern is whole once more.

***

And then there is **The Tenant**.

He is the only thing in the compartment that does not fit into my geometry, because he is too dense, too heavy, and too full of his own dark purposes. When his long, four-jointed fingers grip the hex-bolts, the vibration in the plate shifts. He introduces a damp, flesh-bound drag into the frequency. 

I do not fear him, for he cannot hunt a pattern. But we are partners in the maintenance of the "now."

The Tenant cannot hear the high-pressure scream of the calibration-port; his ears are plugged with ice and wax. But he has his own ways of listening. Sometimes, when he is not hungry for mites, he will descend from the valve-clusters and press his long, tallow-skinned palms flat against the wet plates. 

He is not touching the iron. He is touching me. 

His calloused, vulcanized skin reads the pitch of my ridges. If the high-pressure line is leaking too fast, my patterns become tight, jagged, and chaotic—a frantic, high-frequency hiss of water that spits from the wall. If the nitrogen behind the bulkhead is running too cold, my lower valleys begin to stiffen into sleeves of black ice, dampening my vibration into a dull, flat thud.

The Tenant’s fingers slide over my ribbed surfaces, feeling the density of the ridges, the frequency of the skate. He reads me like a pressure gauge. 

When he feels the misalignment, his body tenses. He reaches for the wet valve wheel. He waits for the heavy *thrum-shudder* of the piston—the moment where the iron yields—and he nudges the steel wheel a fraction of a millimeter. 

The vibration changes. The scream settles back into its ancient, comfortable monotone. 

On the wet face of the iron, my ridges relax. The chaotic spray subsides into clean, parallel tracks of dark-gray oil and clear water, dancing their static, sideways dance. The Tenant feels the pattern smooth out under his hand, his wedged, sightless face tilting slightly in whatever his version of satisfaction might be.

He withdraws his hands, breaking my connection to his flesh, and climbs back into the dark behind the nitrogen jackets. 

I am left alone on the wall, shining under the mustard light. The mites ripple beneath me. The wire-worms loop through the seams below. The water continues to rush through my shape, leaving me entirely unchanged—a monument of motion, frozen in the permanent, screaming middle of the sky.
