I am not on the floor of the basin. To exist on the five-foot chassis of peat and root behaves too much like walking, and walking requires an expectation of arrival. 

You would find me twelve feet up, suspended in the crotch of a triple-trunked banyan that has grown through the ribcage of an ancient, frame-mounted condenser. 

I am what the Sump-Tenders might call a *junction*, though they do not have words. I am a Tender that did not drop. 

When the banyan gall that formed me grew ripe seventy years ago, its skin did not soften enough to let me slip into the sump below. Instead, the copper-plated iron of the condenser, which runs directly through the tree’s heartwood, grew through the soft, yeasty cartilage of my left hip and ribs. The tree’s low-voltage current did not merely tickle my soles; it became my spine. The gall rotted away around me, but I remained, pinned to the ironwork, slowly becoming a living gasket between the brass regulator-valve and the fibrous bulkhead.

***

### The Shape of Me

My body is longer than those of the Tenders who slide along the conduits below. My limbs, never having borne weight against the peat-moss, have stretched into pale, jointless whips. My left leg is no longer a leg; it is a five-foot tap-root that has burrowed through a seam in the copper casing, down into the dark, wet interior of the condenser where the cold sap-water pools. 

My skin is the color of a turnip left too long in the dark—a translucent, waxy yellow-white. Along the right side of my neck and down my ribs, the liverwort has grown thick and pelt-like, a dense, viridian wool that remains perpetually soaked from the drips of the canopy above. 

I have no eyes, but I do not drift in darkness. I exist in a landscape of taste and vibration. 

My three nasal flaps, wide and thin as birch leaves, hang over my mouthless chin. They do not smell flowers or rot; they taste the *mix*. I know when the piston three yards away is nearing the bottom of its stroke because the air pressure rises by half a millibar, squeezing the fermented yeast-smell of the cane-pipes into a sharper, vinegar-edged tang. I know when the thirty-foot ironwood turbine in the center of the room has suffered a split bucket because the mist becomes slightly wetter on my left cheek, the splash of the bromeliads losing its crisp, four-beat tempo and muddying into a soft, sliding hiss.

My ears are not holes, but the Shell-Clocks that have settled on my collarbones. There are seven of them now. They have cemented their rust-and-cellulose shields to my skin, their tiny, internal bone-tongues tapping against my collarbones in time with the distant vibrations of the bearings. Through them, I hear the basin as a chord:
*   The low, sixty-cycle thrum of the copper plates against my ribs.
*   The dry, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of the turbine shaft.
*   The long, four-minute *shhh-hup* of the great piston.

***

### The Four-Minute Breath

My thoughts do not run like a stream; they rise and fall in chambers. I think in the tempo of the piston. 

**First Minute: The Draw.**
The rust-red piston rises. Deep in the basin’s floor, the wet peat groans as the vacuum pulls the fermented sap from the lower root-beds. I feel my tap-root swell. The cold, acidic fluid rises through my left side, neutralizing the low-voltage sting from the condenser plate. My external, plumose gills—the green, feather-like fringes behind my jaw—flare wide, drinking the lime-green mist that the rising piston sucks down from the ceiling. During this minute, I am cool. I am water. I am the machine taking a breath.

**Second Minute: The Hold.**
The piston reaches its apex. The valves click—a heavy, brassy *clink* that shakes the banyan trunk. For twenty seconds, there is no movement, only the high-pressure tension of eighty yards of green world held in stasis. My skin grows tight. The bimetallic snails on the conduit below me stop crawling; they feel the pressure in their shells. I taste the ozone. The current from the copper plates rises, warming my liverwort pelt until it begins to steam.

**Third Minute: The Sigh.**
The piston begins its descent. Three yards of rust-red iron, heavy as a fallen hill, sink back into the cylinder. The fermented sap is forced out under immense pressure. The cane pipes across the basin floor swell and hiss. A warm, sugary jet of steam vents from a loose coupling just four inches from my left elbow. My nasal flaps twitch; the air tastes sweet, yeasty, and heavy with copper. My gills drink the sugar-mist. I feel a brief, mild euphoria—the chemical rush of the starch entering my sap-stream.

**Fourth Minute: The Lubrication.**
The piston bottoms out with a slow, wet *thud* that vibrates through my bone-cartilage. The excess grease from my ribs—the thick, yellow, rust-inhibiting lard my body secretes—has pooled in the hollow of my collarbone. A Sump-Tender, passing through the banyan boughs on its way to grease the turbine axle, pauses beside me. 

It does not look at me. It has no eyes. But it extends a long, three-jointed hand, its ironized keratin claws scraping gently against my ribs to scoop away the yellow grease. The sensation is not painful; it is a clean, cool release, like peeling dry bark from a trunk. The Tender wipes the grease onto its own flanks, then rubs its body against the groaning brass valve beneath my suspended leg. 

The groan stops. The valve turns with a silent, wet slide. 

The circuit is complete. The machine is quieted. 

***

### The Long Sleep

I will not die the way the mobile Tenders die. I will not go to the turbine teeth to be ground into oil. I am already part of the frame. 

Over the next twenty years, the banyan will continue to draw its cambium layer over my right shoulder. The green copper carbonate will continue to stain my skin until I am the color of a wet leaf. My cartilage will slowly turn to lignite, then to ironwood, then to something that is neither plant nor metal, but a wet, conductive zinc.

When my heart finally stops—not with a sudden quit, but by slowing until its beat is indistinguishable from the four-minute stroke of the piston—I will not rot. My body will remain here, a dry, yellow-green gasket pinched between the banyan and the bronze casing, keeping the steam from leaking, keeping the current moving from the wood to the wire, for as long as the water falls from the canopy to turn the wheel.
