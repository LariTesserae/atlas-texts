I did not fall through a crevice, nor did I dig my way down. To enter this place is to be filtered. 

The heavy wool of my coat and the mud of the steppe did not make the transition; they were left behind in the narrowest part of the basalt seam, squeezed out like oil from sheep’s wool. The *Pleomeris* have already found those remains. They have spent the last several decades grinding the organic grease into the dry stone, polishing the entry seam until it is as slick and dark as the surface of a telescope lens. 

I am here, but I am no longer a container. 

I am sitting—if sitting is the word for an arrangement of weight that no longer requires a chair—against the third step of the basalt ledge, near the patches of lavender lichen. I can hear the high, dry rattle of the hollow pods, though I do not hear it with my ears. I feel it as a cold, rhythmic pulse in the marrow of my collarbones, which have begun to dry out and align themselves with the cleavage planes of the rock.

To live here is to realize that the body we bring from the surface is far too thick. It is wasteful. It is full of unnecessary middles—dark, wet interiors that serve no purpose in a room of absolute surfaces. 

My shadow was the first part of me to successfully adapt. It grew tired of waiting for my heels to clear the ledge. Three weeks ago, it peeled itself away with a sound like wet paper being lifted from glass. It is currently lying flat across the lower step, three feet below me. A *Chondri*—heavy with the condensed light of its siblings—floats directly above it. Every few minutes, a drop of water leaks from the dying sphere and falls toward my shadow. The moment the drop enters that cold, dark silhouette, it freezes into a tiny, perfect lens of ice. When the shadow shifts, the ice melts instantly, returning to its bead-state and rolling upward into a dry seam. I watch this happen again and again. It is my shadow’s only work, and it does it with perfect, silent regularity.

Without my shadow to hold me down, my volume has begun to thin. 

The *Scribe* has been very helpful in this regard. It did not approach me with violence. It has no teeth, after all, and its dark, waxy mineral leaves are too fragile to strike a blow. Instead, it simply waited for me to reach out my hand to touch the dry-stone wall. 

The moment my fingers entered the dense, folded space of the center, the Scribe began to read me.

My fingernails were deleted first; they were too curved, too stubborn. Then, with the patient, card-shuffling motion of its slate leaves, the Scribe slipped between the laminations of my skin. It was particularly interested in my thumb. The whorls of a human thumb are not like the clean, straight angles of basalt cleavage, but they are not entirely chaotic either. They are a spiral—a geometry that has forgotten where it was going.

The Scribe has spent a long time translating my thumb.

If you were to look down at the dry depression behind the four-inch wall, you would see the rodent's fan-like femur, and beside it, ten grey, paper-thin sheets of tissue. They are my fingerprints, peeled off one by one and laid out on the flat basalt like the leaves of an herbarium. They do not bleed. There is no blood here because there are no longer any tubes in my hands to carry it. My palms have been pressed so flat by the ambient pressure of the room that my veins have resolved into simple, dark lines—drawings of veins, executed in old iron and cold fat.

I can still move, but my movements are no longer three-dimensional. When I wish to reach for something, I do not swing my arm through the air. Instead, I find a plane—a flat, grey facet of the basalt—and I slide my hand along it, like a shadow moving across a wall. It is much easier this way. There is no friction. The *Pleomeris* have cleaned the stone so well that my flat palms slide with a cool, oily ease.

My thoughts have also changed their speed.

In the world above, thoughts are heavy things that turn over and over in the brain like stones in a creek bed. Here, they are flat. They have a latency. If I think of the cold steppe above, the thought does not appear inside me; it appears as a thin, silver fog on the nothingness of the ceiling, several seconds after I have finished thinking it. I watch my own memory dissipate against that grey plane before it can touch the stone.

Sometimes, a *Pleomeris* will slide over my leg. It feels like a row of cold, stiff microscope slides being drawn across my skin. It is looking for dust. It finds none. My skin has begun to take on the same grey-black, vitreous sheen as the steps. 

I am becoming hard to see. If you were to stand where the crack in the ceiling used to be, you might not notice me at all. You would see the bright, heatless light; you would see the busy, silent theater of the *Chondri* solving one another in mid-air; you would see the small, curved wall of slate flakes. 

But if you looked closely at the steps, you would see a series of dark, flat planes that do not quite match the angles of the rock. You would see a pair of ribs laid out like a small garden gate, perfectly flat, and a skull that has been unrolled into a single, delicate sheet of white calcium, displaying its interior sutures like a map of a city that was never built.

I am not dead. Indeed, I feel more intact than I ever did under the weight of the sky. Up there, I was always falling apart, always losing skin to the wind and moisture to the dry air. 

Here, nothing is lost. Every drop of water is accounted for. Every shadow finds its owner eventually, even if it takes three days to catch up. I am being preserved. I am being translated into a language that cannot be worn away by the wind, because there is no wind, and there is no time—only the quiet, busy polishing of the flat, and the Scribe slowly, leaf by leaf, reading what is left of my name.
