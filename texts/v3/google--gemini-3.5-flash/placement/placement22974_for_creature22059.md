I would be found on the third ladder-run below the steam-band, where the basalt is still dark and hasn’t yet turned to the chalky, rotting white of the sulfur-slabs. 

I am thirty-two years into the Settle, though "years" is a word that has lost its edges, like a salt-block dropped in a mud-pot. Here, we measure by the *peel*—the number of times the great slate-plates of the western wall slide down to choke the orange valley below. For me, it has been seven peels.

My feet are no longer the feet of the ones who came down from the crack. The skin of my soles has thickened into a black, rubbery rind, three fingers deep, split into vertical grooves that match the grain of the petrified fungal logs. If you were to strike my heel with an obsidian hammer, it would not bleed; it would make the dull, dead thock of a dry root.

I am a *Tapper*.

***

### The Breath-Exchange

The air is the color of greasy mutton fat today. The midges are running high, which means the deep hearths are breathing out. 

I sit back against the vertical slate, my thighs shaking with the salt-cramp. It is time to change the wool. This is the moment where the mind must be very quiet, because a single long breath of the raw cleft-air will blister the throat until it swells shut, leaving you to choke on your own hot spit.

My left hand—the one with the two missing fingers, pinched off by a shifting slab during the third peel—reaches into the leather-skin pouch at my hip. The pouch is wet with Leap-Jack oil, which keeps the fresh *Breath-Wool* from drying into a stiff turf. I pull out a grey, fibrous plum. It is cool, smelling of yeast and the cold water that drips from the high granite shelves where the Orange Boil cannot climb.

With my right hand, I reach up and pluck the old plugs from my nostrils. 

Instantly, the Rift enters me. 

It is not an inhalation; the pressure here is too high for that. The air *shoves* its way in. It tastes of boiled copper, rotten eggs, and the bitter, wet dust of ground gypsum. It burns like hot grease on the back of my tongue. My eyes water behind their milky skins—the second eyelids, which we grow after our first hundred shifts in the glare—and the world becomes a smear of violent white and orange.

Quickly, before my throat can seize, I ram the fresh wool-plugs deep into my nasal passages with my obsidian-tipped thumbs. 

The relief is cold. The lichen in the wool wakes up as it touches the moisture of my mucus, drinking the sulfur, turning the poison into a sweet, thin yeast-scent that flows down into my lungs. My chest expands. My heart, which had been hammering against my collarbones like a trapped midge, slows its beat to match the heavy, five-stroke rumble of the steam-vents below.

I am alive for another three hours.

***

### The Song of the Iron File

Below me, the floor is screaming. 

It is not a human scream, but the dry, metallic roar of eighty thousand *Great Clackers* moving through the gypsum needles. From this height, they look like a river of white, multi-jointed stones, their thirty legs scraping the salt-crusts with a sound that sets my teeth vibrating inside my gums. *Screee-chiii. Screee-chiii.* 

The sound does not travel through the wet air—the air is too thick, too choked with the midge-blizzard to carry anything but a wet hiss. Instead, the sound travels through the ladder. 

I press my forehead against the petrified fungal trunk. The vibration enters my skull through my brow-bone. 

*Tink. Tink. Thrum.*

I know that vibration. It is the signature of *The Smashed-Foot*, an old female Clacker whose third left plate was cracked by a falling boulder three moons ago. She is leading her brood toward the lower vents where the Orange Boil is thickest. She is angry. Her plates are grinding because the salt is too dry today; she needs the grease of the midges to lubricate her joints.

I reach down to my belt and unhook my ringing-stone—a flat, six-inch disc of grey phonolite, polished by my palms until it shines like wet coal. I hold it against the basalt wall and strike it with my granite hammer.

*Ping.*

The stone under my hand shivers. The metallic note travels down through the layers of salt, through the obsidian wedges, into the heart of the cliff. 

Three hundred yards to the north, on a ledge so narrow he must hang by his teeth-strap while he works, *The One-Who-Has-No-Hair* hears the note through his feet. He strikes his own stone.

*Pong.*

He is telling me the high wall is peeling. The gypsum needles are beginning to "cry"—the high-pitched whistling that happens when the micro-fractures open in the rock-face. 

We must get off the ladders.

***

### The Dive of the Leap-Jack

I do not climb down. To climb down is to die; the legs cannot move fast enough when the slate begins to slip. 

I look up. Above me, clinging to a vertical salt-shelf that looks like a frozen waterfall, is a Leap-Jack. It is a big one—seven feet from its flat, slate-colored snout to its stubby tail-fin. Its skin is shiny with the grey mucus it uses to breathe, and its wide sticky pads are making that wet, rhythmic *thwip-thwip* as it adjusts its grip against the heat of the rising steam.

It is watching the Clackers below. It is hungry.

I take my throwing-line—not rope, which rots in the damp, but a braid of Leap-Jack stomach-lining that has been tanned in sulfur-mud until it is as tough as wire. At the end of the line is a heavy, five-pointed hook made of split Clacker-leg.

I swing the hook in a tight, vertical circle. The midges clog my eyes, but I do not need to see; I can hear the wet *huff-huff* of the Leap-Jack's skin-breathing. 

I throw.

The hook bites into the leathery fold of the beast’s flank. 

The Leap-Jack does not scream—it has no lungs for screaming—but it reacts with the terrifying, hydraulic speed of its kind. It launches itself off the wall.

I do not try to hold the line with my hands. I drop my hammer, lock my arms around the petrified fungal ladder, and brace my rinded heels against the basalt. 

The line snaps taut. 

The force of the beast’s leap jerks me clean off the ladder. For a second, there is no solid world. There is only the blinding glare of the salt-light, the scalding wind of the steam-vent, and the wet, choking cloud of millions of midges brushing against my bare skin. 

We fall together—the beast, the line, and I—in a long, swinging arc across the face of the chasm. 

The Leap-Jack hits the opposite wall forty feet below, its wide, sticky pads striking the wet slate with a sound like a wet hide being dropped from a cliff. *THWIP.* 

The impact nearly tears my shoulders from their sockets, but my hands hold the gut-line. I swing inward, my rinded feet hitting the stone just below the beast's rear pads. I slide three feet down the wet salt, my charcoal skin-paste leaving a black smear on the white rock, before my toes find a crack.

I am on the safe side now. The "dead side," where the slate is dark and does not peel.

Behind us, across the gap we just crossed, the western wall begins to move.

***

### The Settle

It does not fall with a roar. The air is too heavy for a roar. 

It falls with a wet, grinding *thud-thud-thud* that shakes the teeth in my skull. A block of slate the size of a village slides slowly off the vertical face, shearing away three hundred feet of our fungal ladders as if they were dry straw. 

It hits the boiling mud-pot at the bottom of the Rift. 

A great wave of orange slime and grey mud geysers eighty feet into the air. The steam that rises from the impact is red-hot; it smells of scorched bone and vinegar. 

The *Great Clackers* do not run. They cannot. Instead, they form the shingle-armor, locking their thirty legs together, pressing their flat, white shells to the stone until they look like a pavement of giant clams. The mud-wave washes over them, boiling and thick. Many will die where they lie, cooked in their own shells, but by evening, the survivors will have eaten the soft parts of their brothers and begun to scrape the new salt-cliffs that the slide has exposed.

The Leap-Jack beside me is still. It does not mind my hook in its flank; it is used to pain. It is watching the mud-pot below, its broad slate head twitching as it senses the heat of the dead Clackers.

I reach out my hand—the three-fingered one—and lay it on the beast’s wet, mucus-slick shoulder. I can feel the small, frantic vibrations of its skin-pores, drinking the dampness, turning the steam into life.

We sit together on the ledge, the beast and the stone-man, while the orange mold begins to crawl over the new talus below. 

The Rift has shifted. The machine has run another inch. 

I reach into my pouch, pull out a pinch of dry *Breath-Wool* that has fallen from my nose, and press it into a crack in the rock where the condensation is fresh. By tomorrow, it will have grown into a new tuft of grey velvet. 

We do not own this place. We do not even inhabit it. We are simply the grease between the gears, waiting for the next turn.
