I am the one who has stopped clicking. 

In the high, frantic terraces of the *tup-tup-tasp*, to stop clicking is to become invisible. The youth—the Gliders—slip past me like dry leaves carried on a draft, their leathery wing-flaps whistling in the narrow gaps between the walnut-huts. They do not see me because I do not move in the sharp, liquid bursts of the living. I have settled. My scales, once the bright, oily gold of toasted cumin, have taken on the dull, powdery grey of the sandstone itself. 

If you were to search for me, you would have to find the place where the light dies entirely. 

Down past the common squares where the seed-pod flour is ground, past the communal chambers where the great, swollen Wax-Mother sits in her puddle of heavy gold, there is a throat in the stone. It turns three times, narrowing until the air becomes thick as lard, smelling of old fat, cold resin, and the sour chalk of our ancestors' urine-paint. Here, the hum of the city above is reduced to a low, rhythmic throb—a heartbeat that does not exist in the chest, but in the deep roots of the mountain.

This is the **Wall of the Gloss**. And I am its tongue.

***

### I. The Drum in the Chest

I do not see the dark. I do not have a need for eyes; they have long since grown over with a thick, frosted caul, dry as onion skin. 

Instead, I have my sternum. 

I lie flat upon the rock, my four-toed feet tucked beneath my belly, my chest pressed hard against the sandstone floor. My breastbone is a wide, thin spade of bone, hollowed out like a dried gourd. It is my true ear. Through it, the mountain speaks to me in a language of shifts and settle.

*Thrum.* That is the great wind-shudder on the plateau three miles above our heads. It is a dry, whistling iron wind that would strip my scales like husk-wheat if I ever stepped into the blue. 

*Tup-tup-click.* That is the third-tier bridge. A beetle is crossing, its heavy obsidian feet dragging a load of hard-pod seeds. One of its joints is dry; it needs the grease-paste. I can feel the tiny screech of its leg-hinge traveling down through the sandstone spur, into the floor of the throat, and up into my ribs. 

And then, beneath it all, there is the slow, cold *groan* of the world itself. The sandstone is folding. Every hundred summers, the cleft closes by the width of a single seed-scale. We are being squeezed. The stone is a slow mouth that has been trying to swallow this jungle for ten thousand years. We live in the space between the teeth.

***

### II. The Taste of the Wax

When the vibration in my chest grows quiet, I begin my work. 

I lift my wedge-shaped head. I stretch my neck—it is stiff today, the joints clicking like dry twigs—and I reach out with my tongue. My tongue is not wet; it is split like a snake’s, dry as parchment, but covered in thousands of microscopic, hook-like papillae that can catch the shape of a grease-molecule.

I press my tongue against the Wall of the Gloss.

Here, the history of my people is not a story told in the mouth. It is a lard. For ten thousand years, the blind ones like me—the ones whose oil has grown too thick for climbing—have come to this wall to rub their scent-glands against the stone. 

The top layer is fresh. It tastes of **The Spring of the Great Yellow Dust**. It is the grease of my sister’s grandchildren. It has the sharp, bitter tang of the mustard-moss they harvested after the great gale three moons ago, mixed with the musky, copper smell of young-fear: a Shiver-Vine had sprouted near the waterless well, and they had to saw it down before its needles found their skin. I can taste the grease of the cutters—thick, hot, smelling of the caustic sap they used to blind the vine’s roots.

I lick deeper. My tongue scrapes through three generations of oil, past the soft, yellow-fat layers of my youth, down into the dark, dried crust of the **Cold-Silt Age**.

Here, the wax is hard as horn. I must press my snout against the wall, warming the ancient grease with the slow, recycled heat of my breath until it softens enough to yield its scent. 

*Ah.* 

This is the grease of **The Great Huddle**. Six hundred winters ago. The taste is lean, dry, and chalky. There was no wind-pollen for three seasons; the outer canyon was a white glare of dead air. The family lines did not separate; they crawled into the deep wells and formed one single, massive sphere of scales—three thousand bodies tangled together to keep from drying into mummies. The grease they left behind is full of the smell of *ash-silt*—the dust they ate to keep their bellies full—and the sweet, flat smell of the dead who lay at the center of the sphere, whose dried bones were broken down to make the floor we sleep on.

And deeper still...

At the very bottom of the laminate, where the wax meets the raw sandstone, there is a smell I have only tasted twice in my life. It is the **Wet-Memory**.

To the young ones, the word *wet* is a bad word. It is the rot. It is the green-poison that makes the moss go black and mushy. But here, at the base of the wall, the grease of the First-Mothers has a scent that is unbelievably heavy. It is like the oil-vine seed, but without the fat; it is sharp, clean, and so cold it makes the scales on my neck stand up. It tastes of a time when the sky did not blow dust, but fell in grey sheets that washed the stone clean. 

It is the smell of the world before the mountain folded shut. It is beautiful, and it is terrifying. If the wet ever came back, our walnut-houses would melt like flour-paste. Our scales would turn to mush. We would be un-made.

***

### III. The Joining

My work is nearly finished. The skin-glands beneath my ears are swollen and hot, heavy with the specific lipid-signature of my own long life. 

I am the last of the fourth-tier weavers. I am the one who designed the hanging bridges that do not swing when the gale-gusts curl into the cleft. My sister was the Wax-Mother who died during the great Shiver-Vine plague. My blood is in the mortar of thirty-two houses.

I turn my head. I press my left cheek hard against the Wall of the Gloss. 

I slide my head slowly down the stone, leaving a thin, glistening trail of my own fat. It smells of *roasted pine-kernel* (which I have eaten every day since my knees failed), the *bitter leaf-gold* of the high ferns, and the *slow-stone*—the scent of a body that has spent thirty summers lying flat against the sandstone.

Tomorrow, a younger scribe—perhaps one of the Gliders whose wing-flaps have begun to stiffen and dry—will crawl down into this dark throat. He will feel the wall with his chest-gourd. He will find my trail. He will lick it.

He will know how the bridges were woven. He will know that the third-tier spur is cracking. He will know that my sister's name was a double-click and a sliding lick.

***

### IV. The Edge

When I have dried the last of my grease onto the wall, I do not go back up to the light. The city is too loud. The clicking of the young is like the sound of dry grass brushing against a horn-shell; it tires my ears.

I crawl downward. 

I do not use the steps. I slide my belly down the smooth, oiled chute that leads to the floor of the cleft. 

Here, the flour-dust is deep and warm. It rises around my chin like dry silk, smelling of age and ancient friction. I can hear the *Dust-Mutes* moving beneath me—long, blind, soft-skinned things that do not even have scales. They are my friends. They do not click. They only make a soft, sifting sound, like dry seeds sliding down a chute.

I crawl to the very edge of the shadow-line.

Ahead of me, the air changes. The thick, warm, resin-heavy breath of our jungle ends. The air becomes thin, sharp, and cold as flint. 

If I stretch out my front paw, I can feel the boundary. The light of the outer canyon is a blue glare that heats my claws even through my caul-eyes. The wind here does not curl; it *hisses*, a long, endless scream of dry space that is trying to drink the world.

I lie down in the silk-dust. I tuck my snout beneath my tail, forming my body into a tight, golden-grey stone. 

The wind-harvest is coming. I can feel the mountain singing its three-hour warning. Soon, the warm gust will curl in, the yellow pollen will rain down, and the city above will erupt in a frenzy of tiny baskets and clicking claws.

But down here, the silt is already rising around my ribs. A Dust-Mute nudges my flank, its soft, blind nose testing the dry surface of my scales. I do not move. I let the warm flour cover my legs. 

In a few days, the miners will come down to gather the fallen dust for the Wax-Mother’s mortar. They will dig me up. They will find my bones—light, dry, hollowed by the wind—and they will grind them into the flour that binds the new homes on the fifth tier.

I will be the ceiling of a nursery. I will be the doorsill of a granary. I will keep the wind out. 

I press my chest-drum one last time against the stone. 

*Thrum...*

The mountain answers. It is a good stone. It is a dry stone. And we are very small inside it.
