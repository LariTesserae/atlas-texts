There is an itch in my third claw that means the wind-shear is turning.

I do not have skin to feel the draft, nor a nose to scent the changing of the season—if seasons can be said to exist in this vertical white throat. I have only this rind, this gray-green coat of waxy pith, and the five-jointed legs that lock me to the grain.

I are an *Ancre*, but here, in the long-shadowless verticality of the Sister-of-the-Shear, they call me the *Clapper-Who-Measures-the-Grave*.

Through my amber lenses, the blinding noon of the cambium is filtered down to a cool, bruised violet. The world is easier to see this way. It loses its white, screaming teeth. The frost-fringes, growing horizontally from the trunk like miles of frozen eyelashes, do not glare; they glow with the quiet purple of deep water.

My claws—three prongs of black beetle-cote at the end of each dry limb—are set into a longitudinal groove. I do not walk; I slide. I am a key in a keyway. *Shhh-clack. Shhh-clack.* I move down the trunk by letting my weight do the work, my pincers clasping and releasing the long, vertical cordage of the wood. My body, which weighs no more than a basket of dry grass, is pressed so close to the bark that I can feel the sap-pulse through my sternum.

*Tink-tink-tink.*

It is the music of the growing wall. To my left, where the wood has split into a fresh, yellow gash the size of a mountain range, the cellulose is drunk on light. It is expanding. The fibers are lengthening, sliding against one another with the dry, high-pitched screech of sand under a fingernail.

I stop. I unstrap the small mallet of iron-wood from my thigh-groove. I tap twice.

*Click. Click.*

The wood is thick here. The pitch is low and solid—a heavy, water-logged *thrum* that tells me this rib of the trunk will hold for another thirty cycles of the wind. But three inches to my right, where the bark has turned a dusty, scabby gray, the sound changes.

*Tock.*

It is hollow. There is a "shake" behind it—a pocket where the sap has fled, leaving a dry gallery of dead, brittle sponge. One day, a thermal draft will hit this spot at ninety miles an hour, and the grey scabs will peel away, taking three of our basket-nests with them.

I will have to tell the Others. I will tap it out tonight, when the cross-winds die down and the giant trunks are quiet enough to carry the rhythm. I will use the *Tack-tock-tack*—the three-beat signal that means: *Pack the cords. The skin is sloughing.*

I slide further down, into the shade of a massive, horizontal splinter. 

Here, the wood has healed. A century ago, a branch the size of a city broke off, leaving a raw, green crater. Now, it is filled with a smooth, glass-like lake of dried amber resin, ten yards across. The light shines through it, deep and honey-colored, warming my legs where the wax-grease has run dry.

And there, inside the amber, is Su-Aru.

She was my mother’s sister, though we do not count cousins the way the flesh-creatures might. We count them by the splits they shared. She is three feet deep in the wood now. Her body is perfectly preserved, a dry, gray-green stick-figure with her six limbs folded neatly over her chest, her claws still locked around the main vertical fiber she was holding when she died.

Her amber eyes are open. They stare out at me through the yellow resin, matching the color of the wall that holds them. In another fifty years, the cambium will have grown another six inches over her. In two hundred, she will be near the heartwood, dark and silent, a knot in the center of the world.

I touch the resin with my middle claw. I do not tap. There is no point in tapping to the dead; the wood has already swallowed her song. She is part of the structure now. She is what keeps this five-mile ledge from buckling under the weight of the frost-fringe.

"Su-Aru," my mind-plates whisper, though no sound leaves my vertical mouth-slit. "The wind-shear is coming from the floor today. It is a wet wind from the dark."

An answering vibration rattles through the soles of my claw-joints. 

It is not Su-Aru. It is a *BOOM*, far below, perhaps forty miles down where the base of the Sister-of-the-Shear meets the blackening peat of the floor. It is a slow, wet, heavy groan—the sound of a root-buttress, sixty yards wide, finally giving up its grip on the cold bottom.

The vibration travels up the trunk like a shiver. It hits my claws, travels through my hollow leg-pith, and rattles the dry plates of my ribs.

*One... two... three...*

I count the seconds of the shiver. The trunk is leaning. It is a tiny lean—fraction of a degree—but at five miles up, a fraction of a degree is a hundred yards of swing.

The frost-fringes above me begin to break.

They fall in a silent, glittering snow of razor-sharp glass. I pull myself flat, my belly-grease sealing me to the bark, my nictitating membranes sliding sideways over my amber eyes. The world becomes a dim, purple blur as the ice-needles scream past my head, clattering against the wood like dry rice. One of them cuts my rear-most joint; there is no blood, only a tiny squeeze of bitter, greenish-white sap that instantly congeals in the cold air, sealing the gap.

I wait.

The silence returns, heavier now, digested by the vast blue vacuum of the gulf. The glitter settles toward the floor, a five-mile fall of light.

I look out across the empty space. Fifty miles away, through the glinting, spore-dusty air, the silhouette of the *Great Brother* trunk is a thin, white line against the black ceiling.

A drift-glider passes between us.

It is three meters of flat, pale leaf-flesh, floating on the updraft. It has no legs to hold the wood, no eyes to see the glare. It is simply a sail that lives. It rises, three feet a second, its long sensory ribbons trailing behind it like gray threads. It looks so light. It looks as if it has never known the fear of a tightening split, or the dry *tock* of hollow core.

For a moment, I envy it. I envy the way it belongs to the air, while I must forever clutch the vertical miles, an insect on a giant’s tooth.

But then, the wind rises.

It is the true wind—the cold, circular draft from the northern rift. It whistles through my leg-pith. It is time.

I reach behind my back and touch the kite-frame. The skin of the *Scythe-Moth* is dry and tight, stretched over springy splinters of green wood. The drag-line—braided from the silk of the ring-lice—is coiled around my hips, anchored to a fire-hardened wedge I have driven four inches into the vertical heartwood.

I look at the distant trunk of the Brother. It is a long five miles.

I release my claws.

I do not jump. There is no strength in my dry legs for jumping. I simply let go of the grain.

The gravity of this world is a friendly thing to the light; it does not pull me down so much as the wind pulls me sideways. The kite-wing catches. The air rushes under my flat belly, lifting me, turning me into a dry leaf.

The Sister-of-the-Shear recedes. Her massive, glowing face—miles of white lines, green scars, and the tiny, basket-like nests of my kin—shrinks into a single, vertical pillar of ice and wood.

Below me is the five miles of nothing. Above me is the black slate sky.

I am suspended in the cold glare, a green-pith thing with amber eyes, flying on the breath of a world that is growing too fast to notice me. My drag-line sings behind me, a thin, vibrating thread that connects me to the wood.

If the wind holds, I will strike the Brother before my wax freezes. I will drive my claws into his green moss. I will tap the grain. I will hear who is alive, and who has been swallowed by the wall.

And if the wind fails...

If the wind fails, I will feel the rope tighten. I will sway in the cold, blue-white middle of the world, dangling like a gray spider, until my people haul me back into the split.

Either way, the wood will have me. It always does.
