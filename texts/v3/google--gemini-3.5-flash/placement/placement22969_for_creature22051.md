I am no longer one of those who walk. 

My boots—those heavy, brass-cleated blocks of black coal—were unscrewed and recycled long ago, perhaps thirty thousand condensations past. I do not remember the sound of my own stride. I do not miss the metallic *click-snap* of the iron-vine catwalks, nor the hard, arbitrary choice of deciding which surface should be my floor. 

I am where three planes have agreed to become one. 

I find myself suspended sixty feet above the lowest sump, at the junction where a major vertical taproot—thick as an ancient oak and wet with vinegar-sharp sap—meets a horizontal bypass of woven ironwood. Inside this junction is a bulbous, hollow gourd-gall, six feet wide, packed with soft, cold lard-peat that has not yet chosen a final shape. 

I am planted in this lard. 

From my hips downward, the transition is complete. My legs have dissolved into a white, hairy root-system that fills the interior of the gall, drinking the slow starch-drainage from the taproot above. My waist is a collar of tough, fibrous bark. But from the sternum upward, I am still ivory-faced, still turnip-fleshed, still alive with the slow, green hum of working sap.

I am the **Junction-Keep**. 

My arms are very long—even for our kind—and they do not end in fingers so much as a dozen pale, flexible whips of living rootlet. These whips are wrapped tightly around four parallel copper conduits that pass through my chamber. The copper is cold, greened with crusty salt, and constantly vibrating with the high-pressure hum of distilled starch-gas. Because my skin is pressed flat against the bare metal, I do not just hear the city; I taste its work through my palms.

To my left, three miles of pipe away, a Calker is sealing a boiler-pod. I know this because the rhythm of their mallet—a soft, wet *thop-thop-thop*—travels down the copper line, through the salt-crust, through the skin of my left thumb, and into my collarbone. 

To my right, five hundred yards down, the white eels are thick in the sump. I can feel the change in the line's temperature when many of them gather near the intake valves; their cold, phosphorescent bodies draw the heat from the metal, turning the hum of the sap inside from a warm simmer to a stiff, greasy slurry.

My moss-cap has grown large. It is no longer a simple velvet crown; it has spread down my neck, over my narrow shoulders, and down the front of my turnip-pale chest like a damp, olive-colored vest. It is heavy, soaked with the condensation of a thousand drenchings. It is my main source of pleasure. 

Every forty minutes, the great change begins. 

First comes the silence. It is not an absence of sound, but an alignment of it. The low, tooth-gritting hum of the dynamos drops an octave, sinking deep into the peat walls until it is only a tremor in the soles of those far below. The blind leaf-moths, sensing the pressure drop, cease their silent drifting and cling to the undersides of the boiler-pods, their dusty wings folding shut like rotting sails. 

Then, my vertical breathing slits whistle—a short, cold intake of vinegar-air. 

The temperature plummets. The air, thick with grease and turnip-rot, grows suddenly sharp. And then, the Drip.

I close my flat, pupil-less silver eyes. The clear horizontal lids slide across them, locking out the moisture. At the same instant, the first giant drop hits the copper line above my head with a soft *tunk*. 

Then another. Then ten thousand. 

Because I am suspended in the open void, the mist hits me from all sides at once. The moss on my shoulders swells, drinking greedily. It grows dark, almost black, and the nerves beneath my scalp tingle with a sharp, sugary warmth that makes my fingers tighten on the copper lines. The cold water runs in rivulets down my flat face, pooling in the small hollows above my collarbone, clearing the salt-crust from my skin. For three minutes, I do not think; I am merely an instrument for measuring wetness. I am a leaf being washed in the dark.

When the boilers hiss and the warm, fermented turnip-broth air rises to dry us, the city wakes in stages. 

First, the wood-lice. A family of them lives on my horizontal copper bypass. They are pale, jointed, the size of terriers, and terribly tidy. After the mist, they creep out from the crevices of the taproot and begin their march toward my chamber. They make a dry, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* as their brassy feet step on the metal. 

They do not fear me. I am part of the frame. One by one, they crawl over my shoulders, their cold, hard bellies scraping against my moss-cap. They use their small, busy mandibles to lick the green condensation off my copper pipes, occasionally nibbling the dead peat off my bark-covered waist. Their touch is ticklish, a thousand tiny needle-pricks that keep the green sap in my chest from settling into winter-sleep.

Sometimes, a Shepherd will swing down from the high catwalks on a line of braided root-silk, landing softly on the lip of my gourd-chamber. They do not speak. They do not have boots; they are young, and their feet are still soft, white leeks. 

The Shepherd will step into my chamber, their pale body smelling of compost and moth-dust. They do not look into my silver eyes—there is nothing to see there—but they lean down and press their moss-cap firmly against mine. 

Our mosses knot together. Instantly, the chemistry of their mind flows into mine. I feel the drift of the giant leaf-moths they tended this morning; I feel the dry, powdery warmth of the boiler-skins where the insects rested. In return, I give them the rhythms of the deep city: the news of the Calker’s leaky seam three miles to the left, the cold eel-clog in the sump to the right, and the rate of the master-pump’s stroke. 

The communion lasts for five seconds. When they pull away, their moss tears from mine with a soft, wet *shrrp*, like two sheets of damp felt being separated. They nod once—a slow, folding tilt of the head—and swing back into the velvety gloom, their pale body vanishing before they have even reached the next platform.

My sap is growing thicker. 

Lately, when the forty-minute condensation ends, it takes longer for my fingers to loosen their grip on the copper. The cellulose in my elbows is hardening into the ironwood that will eventually form the bypass junction itself. In another winter or two, my silver eyes will turn back into the grey, protective bark of the taproot. My chest will split, and a new, hollow gourd-gall will form where my ribs used to be, ready to receive the next Junction-Keep who grows tired of walking.

I am content with this. 

Under the roots of the single elder-tree, nothing is lost. I was the newborn who fell from the Nursery-Gall; I was the Siphoner who dived into the eel-grease; I am the Keep who holds the copper; I will be the pillar that rings like iron when the boots of the young pass over me.

Below, the master-pumps take their first deep, wet stroke. My copper pipes press their cold, vibrating song into my hands. 

*Shuck. Shuck.* 

The city is wet. The work continues. I hold the lines.
