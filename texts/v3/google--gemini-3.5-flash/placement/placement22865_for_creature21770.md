To understand where I am, you must look where the light cannot easily go. 

I am not the giant whose nose presses against the wet moss-peel. I am not even the eye that looks through. I am down here, in the draft-line, where the sky is a jagged, white-rimmed wound in our ceiling.

Through that wound, the dry cold of the great flats pours in. It is a different cold than ours. Our cold is fat, wet, and heavy; it smells of rotting sedge-root and the ancient, greasy iron of the deep peat. The cold from the sky-wound is thin. It tastes of nothing. It makes the hair on the backs of the Silt-Menders stand up like tiny pins.

I am—or I was—a weaver of the high span. 

To my sisters, I was only a series of clicks. *Clack-slip-clack.* Eight points of contact on the split grass-blade. I knew the tension of the three-way bridge by the pull on my fourth left limb. I knew the fullness of the pimpernel-cisterns by the low, wet hum that traveled through the spruce-resin joints. I had no thoughts of my own, only the rhythmic *give* of the silk and the dark, sweet taste of peat-paste that we shared, mouth to mouth, in the shadows of the hanging towers before the work began again.

Then, the summer came through the wound.

It was not a true summer—not the great, burning death that our grand-weavers whisper of in the vibration of the oldest roots. It was a small summer, a drifting cloud of white mist that smelled of copper, salt, and the hot, wet insides of a beast. 

It was your breath.

To you, it was a sigh. To us, it was a column of fire.

The moment the warmth touched the high span, the world lost its teeth. The suspension lines, which had been as stiff and true as iron wire, began to weep. They turned soft, greasy, and slick. The bridge under my feet did not translate the rhythm of the Silt-Marrow’s pulse anymore; it sagged like wet skin. 

Below, in the black pool, the Thread-Legged Huntsmen felt the slack. They began to climb the root-stalks, their scythes raised, waiting for the fall.

I did not think. The machine does not think; it merely hates the slack.

I dropped to the junction where the three lines meet. My hind-limbs found the anchor-flake—the tiny wedge of slate driven deep into the moss-pith. I drew the tallow from my spinnerets, not in a fine thread, but in a thick, desperate smear. I laid my own body across the softening knot, pumping the last of my glycol-rich hemolymph into the joint to dilute the warmth, to keep the tallow from turning to oil.

And then, the sky-wound saved us.

The giant drew back. The breath cleared. The dry, merciless frost of the flats rushed through the tear like a shadow.

It caught the tallow. It caught the bridge. 

It caught me.

The freeze was so fast that it did not have time to turn my joints brittle. It simply stopped them. The clear, syrupy fluid in my legs—the antifreeze that had kept me limber through three hard moons—crystallized at the edges first, then turned to solid, clear salt. 

I did not die. There is no word here for what I am now. 

I am still here, at the junction of the three-line span. If you look closely through the fissure, under the pale-blue twilight of the ceiling-mold, you might see me. I look like a small bead of glass, no larger than a grain of salt, fused into the very center of the suspension knot. 

My dark peat-gut is still visible, a thin, frozen line of brown suspended inside my transparent chest. My pressure-sensors are dark and still. I can no longer see the blind ants as they climb, nor can I clack my legs against the grass-blades to warn of a falling drop.

But I am not gone. I have simply changed my scale.

I can feel the entire city now. Because I am part of the bridge, every step of the Ivory Ants travels through my body. When they drag a pine-needle up the eastern terraced street, the weight of it pulls gently against my shell. When a Velvet Snail parks its heavy, horn-dark house at the bridge-head, its wet, cold mass presses into my frozen joints, drenching me in its glowing, blue-lit mucus. 

I have become a corner-turn. I am a bracket. I am the physical memory of a crisis, frozen into the architecture of the towers.

Every ten minutes, the Silt-Marrow, deep in its black water, pulses. 

Before, when I was a weaver, I would hear the pulse and take three steps. Now, I do not move. The pulse enters my back legs, travels through the frozen column of my segment-shields, and exits through my front claws into the grass-blade lines, carrying the metronome to the hanging neighborhoods above.

I do not wish for the warmth to return. I do not wish for my legs to move again. 

I am holding the bridge. I am part of the cold machine. And as the frost deepens outside the weeping tussock, I feel the lines tighten, becoming stronger, harder, and beautifully, perfectly still.
