I would not be found in the warm, moss-insulated bellies of the *wefts*. I would not be among those who weave the green ribbing of the spine-willows, nor would I be one of the hunters who follow the shimmer of the glass-skates with bone-pointed spear in hand.

You would find me three hundred feet below the lowest dwelling of Oru-Val, where the light is the color of a bruised plum and the silence of the heights is crushed into a thick, vibrating weight.

I am the **Tensioner**. 

In the language of the high basin, my name is not a sound that can be spoken with the lips; it is a three-beat *thrum* produced deep in the marrow of my collarbones—a low, scraping vibration that feels like two river-stones grinding together under a heel. 

***

My body is wider than those of the weavers above, pack-mule thick and heavy with the grey mountain fat. My skin is not the clean slate-grey of the weavers, either; it has been stained a mottled, greenish-black from decades of hugging the mineral-crusts of the granite peaks. My fingers are short, the nails worn down to thick, spade-like horn. 

My eyes are my pride and my burden. Down here, in the shadow-basin where the sun is only a rumor of silver six hundred feet overhead, my golden-ringed irises are wide, flat pools of blackness that fill nearly the entire socket. My glaze-lids stay closed most of the time, not to shut out the water, but to protect my sight from the tiny, glowing brine-shrimp that drift through the granite crevasses like sparks from a chimney.

I live in a *nook*—not a grown weft, but a natural, water-carved keyhole in the black face of the summit rock. It is lined with nothing but the coarse, ancient beard-moss that grows where the current is fastest. I do not need the warmth of decaying moss; my blubber is four inches thick, and my blood runs slow and cold, like mountain honey.

My work is the cables.

***

Every three or four years, a spruce-root cable will begin to *sing* in a way that is wrong. 

The weavers above cannot hear it—to them, the world is too full of the racket of their children, the snapping of the blue bivalves, and the scraping of the skates. But down here, where the seven great cables of Oru-Val anchor themselves to the basalt, the music of the hamlet is reduced to seven clean, vertical lines of tension. 

I spend my days lying flat against the cold stone, my chest pressed hard against the basalt rings that ballast the hamlet. I hold the cables between my bare thighs and my thick palms. 

If a cable is healthy, it hums with a steady, dry *nnnnnnnn*—the sound of the willow-woods above catching the lazy, three-day gyre of the valley. It is a good sound. It means the weft above is buoyant, balanced, and riding the current like a sleeping gull.

But sometimes, a cable will begin to *shiver*. 

It is a wet, loose vibration—a *thrwup-thrwup-thrwup* that travels down from the dark. It tells me that a family above has let their moss-walls grow too thick with silt, or that more glass-skates have gathered on their outer ribs than their home can bear. The weight is dragging them down; the cable is bowing in the middle, collecting the river-drift that will eventually rot the spruce-knot joints.

When this happens, I do not swim up. I do not speak. 

I take my bone-wedges, my heavy slate hammer, and I crawl into the cold crevices where the *grey-moss crone* lives.

My relationship with the crone is older than the hamlet itself. To the weavers, they are just a "living stone" that locks the anchors in place. To me, they are a thousand tiny, calcareous mouths that must be fed and flattered. 

I do not use the sharp *tck-tck* of the tongue-bone; that would cause the crabs to withdraw into their lime-tubes, hardening the crust into glass. Instead, I lay my bare forehead against the encrustation. I send a slow, rhythmic thrum through my skull—the "grazing song"—which mimics the gentle, harmless scraping of a glass-skate’s belly. 

Under the influence of the vibration, the tiny, blind crabs reach out their feathery, pink antennae. While they are soft and distracted, I use my slate scraper to clear the sand and decayed weed from the joints of the anchor-stakes. I drive the bone-wedges deeper into the granite fissures, tightening the slack, taking the wobble out of the world above.

The work is slow. It cannot be rushed, because the stone does not yield to haste, and if I strike the granite too hard, the sound will travel up the cable like a lightning bolt, shattering the clay cooking-pots in the wefts above and deafening the elvers in their nests. Every blow of my hammer must be spaced three minutes apart, allowing the water to swallow the shock and carry it away into the empty valleys.

***

In the winter-dark, when the silver ceiling above turns to iron and the light dies completely, the weavers stay inside their spheres, their breathing-slots pulsing in the warm, mossy dark.

But I do not go inside.

In the dark, the *great ones*—the basin-carp—come down to the summits to rub their slate-hard flanks against the granite to clean themselves of river-fleas. 

They are as large as the wefts themselves, and they do not see me. To them, I am only another lump of mineralized moss, another grey stone in the cradle of the high range. 

I lie still as they pass, my breathing-slots clamped shut to avoid releasing bubbles of old air that might startle them. Sometimes, one of the giants will drift so close that its pectoral fin—a broad, ribbed sail of black cartilege—will sweep over my back, pressing me down into the moss. 

I do not move. I reach out my short, spade-like fingers and touch the cold, slate-plated side of the beast. Through the bone of my hand, I feel its heart. It beats only four times a minute—a deep, heavy *thud* that shakes the granite beneath my belly.

In those moments, suspended in the black water between the silent mountains and the drowned village, I am neither a creature of the heights nor of the depths. I am simply the knot where the two are tied together—holding the sky down, so that those above may sleep without drifting away.
