I am not on the floor, where the lead-shod Scrapers grind their slow, carborundum paths, nor am I in the high, swinging dark where the Knots drift on the breath of the transformers. 

I am squeezed. 

I would find myself at the exact point of highest tension, wedged between the black, glass-sharp tooth of a basalt pillar and the heavy, green-painted iron flange that anchors the great horizontal slide-valve. I am the **Insulation-Stack**. 

To the eye, if there were eyes to see me in the dull, orange glow of the vacuum tubes, I would appear as a solid, hexagonal column about the size of a blacksmith’s anvil, bolted through its center by a rusted steel stud three inches thick. But I am not solid. I am a laminate. I am composed of eleven thousand micro-thin sheets of natural amber mica (phlogopite, dug from the deepest, dry veins of the crust) interleaved with sheets of beaten lead-foil. 

The five-ton nut at the top of my central stud is my crown; the basalt floor is my root. I have been here since the green paint on the dynamos was smooth and wet.

***

### The Heartbeat of the Five-Ton Bolt

My world is divided into two distinct halves: the mechanical and the electrostatic. I am the mediator between them.

Every four seconds, the great slide-valve makes its six-inch journey. *Shhh-tock.* When it moves to the left, the iron flange presses against my western side with a force that would reduce human bone to a white paste. My lead-foil sheets yield by a fraction of a micron, absorbing the shock. The mica leaves slide against one another, their perfectly flat faces generating a minute, dry friction heat that keeps my core at ninety-two degrees—seven degrees warmer than the feverish air of the gallery. 

When the valve moves to the right, the pressure releases. I expand. The lead relaxes; the mica settles. 

This is my sense of time. I do not know years or hours. I know the *shhh-tock*. To me, the history of this place is a series of four-second compressions. I have felt nearly eighty billion of them. Each one is slightly different; I can tell when the slide-valve’s brass guide-shoes are wearing thin by the microscopic angle of the thrust, or when the Gray Lard in the sump below has grown thick enough to cushion the valve's return.

***

### The Sixty-Cycle Mind

But if the *shhh-tock* is my body’s clock, the sixty-cycle hum is my mind.

The primary copper busbars, carrying the raw, high-voltage current from the buried transformer banks, run directly over my crown. Because I am a dielectric—a resting place for electrical strain—the immense voltage does not pass through me. Instead, it presses into me, aligning the atoms within my mica sheets. 

As the current alternates sixty times every second, the electrostatic field rises and falls like a dry, invisible tide. This tide does not wet me, but it makes my laminations sing. Every sheet of mica vibrates against its neighbor at sixty cycles. This vibration is not a sound I hear; it is a physical state in which I exist. It is my consciousness.

Within the microscopic gaps between my leaves, a tiny amount of the yellow-gray sulfur dust and graphite grease has been drawn by capillary action. As the electrical tide surges, these tiny particles of dust arrange themselves into chains, then disperse, then chain again. This is my memory. I do not think in words, but in the shifting resistance of these sulfur chains. 

When a heavy surge passes through the line—perhaps because a Knotted drift has shorted out a resistor coil in the dark—the chains in my third-thousandth layer fuse. I "remember" that surge forever as a small, silent spot of zero resistance.

***

### My Neighbors

Because I am stationary, the world must come to me. And it does, in its slow, dry, mechanical ways.

The **abacus-crabs** use my lead-foil margins as a ladder. Their horn-and-brass legs make a high, ceramic *pip-pip-tick* as they climb my sides to reach the high-voltage wires above. I do not mind them; their bellies are coated in dry, graphite-scented grease, and as they drag themselves over my mica edges, they polish away the thin crust of slate-dust that might otherwise cause a "flashover." 

A flashover is the only death I know. If the dust piles too thick, the sixty-cycle current will not stay in the busbars above; it will find a path down my side, leaping in a foot-long, white-hot arc of blue lightning that would melt my mica into a useless, glass cylinder and fuse my lead into a gray puddle. The crabs are my keepers. They count their way across my face, and in return, I let them feed on the dry, static charge that clings to my corners.

Once, every century or so, a **Contact-Scraper** will crawl over my basalt base. It is a massive, pewter-colored thing, and its carborundum wheel grinds with a terrible, dry screech against my mounting bracket. The vibration of its passing is different from the sixty-cycle hum; it is a low, rough, dragging growl that disrupts my sulfur-chains and makes me "forget" the last few decades of current-fluctuations. I am always glad when it moves on, leaving my copper connections shining and raw.

And then, there are the **Knots**. 

Sometimes, one of these wire-tumbleweeds will lose its grip on the overhead lines and drift down, its nichrome hairs brushing my iron nut. When it touches me, its stored electrostatic charge discharges into my stack. This is the closest I come to contact with the outside world. For a fraction of a second, I "see" what the Knot saw: the wet, cold crawl of the Gray Lard in the trenches; the cherry-red glow of the high Vibration-Gibbets; the slow, ecstatic shudder of the Seizure-Heaps. 

The Knot’s memory sits in my layers for a few days, a warm, high-frequency hum, until the constant sixty-cycle tide washes it clean, returning me to my own four-second rhythm.

***

### The Dread of the Wet

If you were to ask me (in the language of resistance and capacity) what I fear, I would tell you that I fear the Wet.

I have never seen water, but I know what it is from the oldest, deepest memories stored in my lowest mica plates—the ones that touch the bedrock. Water is the great short-circuit. It is the solvent of order. 

If water were to enter this gallery, it would fill the dry spaces between my leaves. It would offer the current an easy path to the basalt floor. My sixty-cycle song would become a single, roaring explosion of steam and shattered stone. The dynamos would rip themselves from their concrete beds; the abacus-crabs would cease their counting; the slide-valve would freeze, mid-stroke, after ten thousand years of perfect, friction-free movement.

So I press myself harder against the iron and the basalt. I hold my leaves tight. I breathe the dry, eighty-five-degree air of baked tallow and zinc, and I sing my sixty-cycle song into the warm, dead dark, keeping the world safe, dry, and eternal.
