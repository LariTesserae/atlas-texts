The hammock does not swing so much as it floats. 

It is made of the root-hair of the three great cedars, woven so fine that it feels like a wet, warm pelt against my back. When I wake, I do not open my eyes immediately. I listen first. 

*Chug.* 
A long, wet breath of steam, thirty yards away at the pressure-well.
*Thump.* 
The great redwood wheel turns, its sixty tons of water-logged bone clicking over the cedar cog. 

I count three of these cycles before my eyes open. The ceiling of our gourd is a pale, ribbed amber. The lantern oil is low—only a finger-thickness of gray tallow remains in the glass bowl, but there is no need for more light. The emerald gloom of the Ward is constant, a green soup that fills every corner of my sight.

I roll sideways. My skin does not rustle against the root-hair. It slides. I have the waxy, rind-like skin of my mother, and her mother before her; there are no creases at my wrists or my elbows that are not slick with the Ward’s grease. We are like the stems of the lilies in the Sump—smooth, hollow-feeling, and heavy with water.

I stretch my legs. My feet find the lip of the gourd-opening. 

My soles are wide, the orange pads of my moss-tread already wet from the condensation that trickles down the outer rind. I do not drop. I slide my toes into the fibrous hemp rope of the hanging walkway, feeling the damp grain of the shelf-fungus beneath. I am an **Anointer**, though in the register of the Parishioners I am simply called *Sloam-who-hears-the-dryness*. 

I wear only a narrow apron of river-eel skin, cured with mint-oil until it has the color of boiled tea. Around my shoulder hangs my harness: four copper cans, three of them filled with the gray tallow we press from the fat-seeds of the water-vines, and one filled with "blue-oil"—the heavy, thick distillate we draw from the cedar-crust where the steam-pipes run hottest.

I walk. 

*Slide... slide...*

I do not lift my feet. To lift a foot is to lose the grip of the moss-tread, to risk the fifty-foot drop into the Sump where the boiling runoff gas bubbles through the duckweed. I keep my soles flat, feeling the vibrations of the great wheel through my arches. 

*Chug.* 
A small tremor in the fungus-bracket beneath my left heel.
*Thump.*
A deeper, base-rich ring that travels up my shinbones and settles in my hips.

"Sloam."

The voice is small, a dry hiss that barely cuts through the steam. It comes from above.

I stop. I wait. 

*Chug.*
"The third..."
*Thump.*
"...well-branch..."
*Chug.*
"...is hot."
*Thump.*

It is Mae, one of the Gourd-Wrights. She is hanging thirty feet above me, suspended by three green-shoot cords that are tied to her thighs. She is working on a young gourd, no larger than a bucket, binding its neck with strips of split willow so it will grow into a narrow chimney-pipe for her cousin’s workshop. She looks like a gray spider in the green mist, her long, waxy limbs glistening under the drip from the canopy.

I do not look up to answer. The mist is too thick; if I tilt my head, the condensation will run into my nostrils. Instead, I tap my copper can against the brass pin of my walkway.

*Clink.*
(I hear.)
*Clink.*
(I am going.)

I move toward the left pillar-tree. The cedar-root here is sixty feet wide at its base, a vertical wall of red-brown cordage that disappears into the steam-blackened ceiling. The green copper pipes are strapped to it with rubber bands, throbbing like the veins of some iron-blooded beast. 

As I near the root, the air grows warmer. It smells of hot starch and the bitter, metallic tang of the tannin vats. My hands—stained that deep, permanent blue-black from forty years of handling the bark—are already greased. I smear a blob of the gray tallow onto my right palm. 

I lay my hand flat against the wood.

The bark is wet, but beneath the wetness, there is a pulse. I closed my eyes. The redwood wheel is turning, turning. The cog-teeth are drawing sap from the main line to lubricate the axle. But there is a rattle. 

It is very small. To an outsider, it would be drowned out by the great *chug-thump* of the geothermal venting. But to me, it is a splinter in the eye. A dry tooth. A tiny patch of cedar-wood on lignum-vitae where the oil has washed away, where the wood is rubbing against wood. 

Friction. 

The word itself feels like a sin. It is the only thing we fear here. Friction brings heat; heat brings rot; rot brings the collapse of the platforms, the tearing of the root-skins, the end of the Sump-Ward.

I climb. 

The ladder is not made of rungs, but of deep notches grown into the cedar trunk itself, smoothed by thousands of oily palms before mine. I do not use my eyes; the steam has thickened, a white boiler-vent having just released a cloud from the central distillery. The world is nothing but emerald fog and the smell of boiled mint-leaves.

I find the axle-housing. It is a massive box of green-charred cedar, bound with copper bands. Inside, the three-foot axle of the flywheel rotates.

I slip my hand into the lubrication-port. 

The heat inside the housing is intense, almost enough to scald, but my callouses are thick. I feel the gear-teeth as they pass. *One. Two. Three.* 

There. The fourth tooth of the main pinion is dry. The oil-channel in its face has become clogged with a thread of moss-fiber. 

I reach into my harness, pull the copper can of blue-oil, and insert the long, curved spout. I do not pour blindly; I wait for the gap.

*Chug.*
(I push the spout home.)
*Thump.*
(I squeeze.)

The thick, blue lubricant spreads over the wood. I feel the tooth pass under my fingers again. The click is gone. The sliding has returned. The axle moves with that perfect, silent weight that we call the *Sough*.

I lean my forehead against the wet cedar box. The wood is vibrating against my skull, a rhythmic, friendly thrum. 

Below me, in the white blankness of the steam, I hear the children. They are calling to their snails, their voices tiny, dry clicks that fit perfectly into the spaces between the engine’s breaths.

*Chug.*
"Come, gray..."
*Thump.*
"Come, sweet..."
*Chug.*
"...the mint..."
*Thump.*
"...is fresh."

The mist begins to clear, turning back into the heavy, emerald-tinted gloom of the afternoon. A large garden snail, its shell the color of wet river slate and as wide as a carriage-wheel, crawls slowly past my foot on the vertical trunk. Its rasping tongue makes a wet, rhythmic *shluck-shluck* as it licks the green slime from the copper pipe. 

I reach out my blue-black hand and touch its shell. It does not pull back. We are both wet. We are both slow. We are both part of the giant, dripping lung of the Ward.

I will stay here, on the side of this cedar tree, until the evening vent. I will oil three more cogs. I will eat a piece of moss-bread from my pouch, tasting the sweet, earthy sugarcane yeast. And when the white mist comes down for the night, I will slide back to my hammock, rub my skin with the mint-tallow, and dream of the great wheel turning, forever wet, forever silent, in the deep green dark.
