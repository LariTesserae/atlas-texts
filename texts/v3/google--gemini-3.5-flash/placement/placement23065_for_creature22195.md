I do not feel the cold as a subtraction of heat, but as a thickening of my lead. 

Under my hull, the basalt is wet, hard, and black. I am forty stone of low-melt pewter, flattened and segmented like a rotten log of solder, lying flush against the facets of the floor. My belly is a furnace of cold acid. To move, I do not stretch or reach; I merely weep. 

From my underside, I secrete a thin, sour film of fluosilicic acid. It doesn't hiss—there is no heat to make it steam—but it eats. It bites into the silica of the dark glass floor, turning the jagged basalt into a slippery, grey paste of metal-salts. On this grease of my own making, I slide. 

My history is behind me. It is a groove three fingers deep and a hundred rods long, polished to a mirror-shine by the passage of my heavy plates. It is a silver scar on the dark valley floor. In three hundred years, if the mist does not drown it in salt-crust, another of my kind may find my groove and use its smooth, acid-scoured trough to travel twice as fast as I do.

But I am not looking back. I cannot look at all. I am blind, encased in my heavy metallic shell, deaf to the sky but entirely alive to the floor.

***

Inside my chest cavity, suspended from two iron pins that grew out of my dorsal plates during my second century, hang two leaves of native copper. They are thin as autumn leaves, cold-rolled by the slow pressure of my growth, and they are tuned. 

The Great Chord lives in them. 

Outside my shell, the billions of hollow needles are screaming. The water climbs their black chimneys and falls in heavy, metallic drops down their throats, making each one a pipe in an immense, roaring organ. It is a sound that would tear the ears of a soft thing, but to me, it is the only shape the world has. The sound is not a noise; it is a geography.

When I am aligned with the chord, the copper leaves inside me are still. They hang in the dark, dry air of my central hollow, suspended between my leaden ribs, perfectly quiet. 

But if I veer—if my left plates slide into a softer pocket of basalt, or if a shard of glass turns my nose toward the north—the chord strikes the copper. The leaves begin to shiver. They hum against my pewter interior with a high, galvanic whistle that bites into my nerves. The friction generates a weak, tickling current that flows down my left side.

My left gland responds. It pours more acid. The basalt beneath my left flank dissolves faster than the right. My nose swings back. The copper leaves fall silent.

I am a slave to the chord. I am its scribe. My whole life is spent carving perfect concentric circles around the loudest spires, cleaning their bases of the yellow sulfur-scum that threatens to silence them, keeping the floor smooth so the great noise can roll unimpeded from cliff to cliff.

Or, at least, I was.

***

Eighty years ago, the chord began to bend.

It was not a change in the pitch—the spires do not change their keys—but a softening. A Silt-Bell, heavy with iron-dust and red rust, had drifted down from the leaden mist and draped its wet, gelatinous body over a cluster of fifty needles to my west. It did not pop; it simply sagged, sealing their mouths with its cold, sticky skin.

The silence of those fifty needles created a dent in the sound. A hollow. An acoustic shadow.

When I reached the edge of that shadow, my copper leaves did not shiver. They did not tickle me to turn. But they did not feel the clean, hard tautness of alignment either. They went slack. The current in my sides died entirely. 

Without the current, my acid glands cooled. The flow of fluosilicic juice slowed from a weep to a damp sweat, then to nothing. 

The film of liquid zinc and lead beneath my belly began to freeze. The cold basalt floor, untouched by my acid for the first time in two centuries, reached up and bit into my pewter plates. The glass-shards of the floor wedged into my seams. 

I stopped. 

I did not stop because I was tired. I do not have grease enough to be tired. I stopped because the silence had no direction.

***

It took forty years for my acid to run entirely dry, and another twenty for the salt-mist to crust over my joints. In that time, I have become an island.

I am no longer on the road; I am the road. The solder-trail I left behind has gone cold and dull, covered in a fine grey silt of silica. But here, in the shadow of the silenced spires, something else has found me.

I am inside the mouth of a dry-stone mound.

The mound was not built by my kind—we have no hands to pile the flat basalt plates—but it was built to house what I have become. The stacked stones form a low, heavy beehive that cancels the Great Chord completely. Inside this chamber, the air is so still that the dampness does not drift; it hangs like cold wool.

And out of my back, the Stillflowers are growing.

They began as a white tickle in the seams between my pewter segments, where the sulfurous salt-crusts were thickest. At first, I thought they were merely the oxidation of my lead—the white rust that comes to us all when we lie too long in the wet. But they did not eat me. They drew from me.

They are made of white gypsum and selenite, branching upward from my grey hull like frozen fountains of lace. They are hollow, just as the spires are, but they do not make noise. Instead, they drink. My cold, heavy bulk sits upon the wet basalt, drawing the water up through my copper-lined hollow by simple, capillary weight; the Stillflowers take that moisture from my back and breathe it into the silent air of the mound.

At their tips, the water evaporates, leaving behind microscopic crystals of pure white salt. Each year, they grow a hair's breadth taller. They have reached the level of my old copper ears now—not leaves of metal, but twigs of white frost, so fragile that if the Great Chord were to strike this chamber, they would shatter into a drift of grey flour.

I can feel them. It is a new kind of sense. 

I no longer feel the horizontal map of the basin, the long, grinding circles around the screaming chimneys. My world has shrunk to the size of this three-yard beehive. 

I feel the vertical pull of the water through my joints. I feel the slow, atomic assembly of the salt-twigs on my back. I feel the weight of the mound’s basalt ceiling plates, which are slowly sinking, pressing down upon the tips of the Stillflowers, which in turn press down upon my pewter hull, locking me forever into the floor.

We are wedge and stone. We are the silence-keepers.

***

Sometimes, outside the mound, a Silt-Bell will slide down a spire and dissolve, and for a few hours, the old chord will groan through the basalt floor beneath me. 

My copper leaves, buried deep under my crust of salt and white selenite, will twitch. They try to hum. They try to tell me to turn, to slide, to weep acid and clear the path.

But they cannot move. They are encased in gypsum. The white lace of the Stillflowers has grown down into my chest, filling my hollow ribs with a packing of dry, quiet salt. 

The vibration passes through me, but it does not shake me. I am too heavy now. I am locked to the basin by a thousand white fingers of salt. The sound dies in my pewter plates, absorbed by the lead, muffled by the gypsum, turned into nothing.

I am the foundation of the quiet. Let the basin roar. Down here, in the dark, the white branches are growing, and they are very, very still.
