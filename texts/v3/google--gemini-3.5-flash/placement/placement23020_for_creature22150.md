My ribs do not expand outward when I breathe; they lift upward, a single, shallow rise of the breastbone that makes no sound. In the thin air of the saddle, the chest must behave like an bellows of cured leather—stiff-walled, economical, moving only the exact volume of air required to keep the blood cool and grey.

I am the inhabitant of the Southern Tower. If the sisters in the double-tower were to speak of me—which they have not done since the season the well-rope was replaced—they would refer to me simply as the *the southern sill*. 

My hands are the first things I see each morning. They lie on the ochre wool of my wrap like two dried lizards that have died in the sun. There is no pink in the nails, only the dull, translucent yellow of horn. They are very long, the knuckles thick and dry, but there is no tremor in them. When I press my thumb against the limestone wall of my sleeping loft, there is no give in the flesh; the skin has grown tough from eighty years of slide and friction against the slate.

I know every joint in my tower. I know which blocks of limestone were laid in the first century of the settlement—those at the base, which are dark and have begun to take on a greasy polish from the passage of hips and shoulders—and which were replaced after the great frost three generations ago. The newer stones are paler, their tool-marks still sharp enough to graze a careless knuckle.

My day begins before the light has cleared the eastern ridge, at the hour when the condensation is heaviest. 

I slide from the larch-plank platform. I do not use my shoes inside; the skin of my soles has a dry, tacking quality that grips the cold flagstones. I descend the spiral rise of the tower, my shoulder brushing the damp inner curve of the masonry. Here, in the dark of the ground floor, the smell is always the same: the sharp, vinegary scent of the grey liverwort that grows where the wall meets the floor, mixed with the flat, dead smell of the sheep-tallow basin.

I check the wick. It is a single, braided strand of our tight-woven wool, no thicker than a grass stem. The flame is a tiny blue bead, completely silent, producing neither smoke nor draft. It sits beneath the small iron pot. I do not look into the pot; I know by the sound of the condensation—the *click-click* of the heated water expanding against the iron—that it is at the correct temperature. I drop four grains of the parched barley into my palm. They are hard as river gravel. I put one into the side of my mouth, behind the back teeth, where it will soften over the next three hours before I chew it.

To open the larch door is to perform a small ceremony of gravity. The door is four inches thick, hung on hand-forged bronze pins that have worn slightly oval over the centuries. One does not pull; one leans. I place my shoulder against the timber, brace my flat feet against the joint of the threshold, and let my weight fall forward. The door grooves into the dry mountain air with a long, sibilant *husssss* as the wood clears the stone floor by a fraction of an inch.

Outside, the saddle is cold. The sky is the color of a wet slate that has been left to dry in the wind. 

I can see the others, though we do not look at each other. To look directly at another person in Alto-Vand is considered a waste of sight, an unnecessary movement of the neck. Instead, we use the periphery. I see the inhabitant of the Fourth Tower—she is smaller than I, her shoulders hunched so high against the draft that her head seems to emerge directly from her collarbones. She is already at work on the northern edge of her plaza, her horn wedge moving with a tiny, dry *scrape-scrape* as she coaxes a line of moss back into its seam.

My work is at the southern boundary, where the cliff drops away into the grey-green soup of the lower valleys.

The copper windlass sits in a niche carved into the living granite of the saddle. It is my charge. The green crust of copper carbonate on its gears is thick, like dried lichen, but the teeth are clean where they bite into one another. The rope—three thousand feet of braided horsehair and hemp, thick as my wrist—is coiled in the shadow of the winch-bed. It is stiff with tallow. 

Once every three days, I walk to the edge to inspect the drop. 

The lowlanders are down there, under the floor of white cloud that never quite reaches the saddle. I do not think of them as people; they are more like a species of heavy, wet forest animal that lives in the mud. The parched barley they send up has a thick, greasy taste that stays in the throat for days, smelling of woodsmoke and sheep dung. Their wool is coarse and greasy; when we weave it on our dry looms, we must wash our hands three times a day in cold well-water to rid ourselves of the yellow grease that clogs our skin.

But the rope must be tended. I take a block of cold mutton tallow, no larger than a plum, and rub it into the sections of the hemp that have run through the copper grommets. My hands become grey and slick with the fat, but the wind soon dries them again, turning the grease into a hard, protective glaze that smells of nothing.

I remember when the last keeper of this tower died. It was thirty years ago, during the season when the clouds did not clear the saddle for forty days. 

He had become very thin—thin even for us—so that when he breathed, his ribs made a dry, clicking sound like a beetle in a drawer. On the night his wick went out, the others came to his tower without being called. We did not speak. We wrapped him in his own ochre wrap, which was stiff with sixty years of his body's salt. 

He was so light. We carried him between us, four of us holding the corners of the wool, our soft soles whispering on the wet slate. The cloud was so thick we could not see our own knees. When we reached the windlass, we laid him in the wicker basket.

I remember the sound of the counterweights—the five granite cylinders—as they rose into the fog, their dry iron rings groaning against the bronze guide-rods. The basket went down into the white wet. It took half an hour for the rope to go slack.

In the morning, when the wind cleared the pass, I went to the windlass. The rope was dangling, light and free. I hauled it back up by myself, turning the copper wheel tooth by tooth, my narrow chest laboring in the cold air. When the basket cleared the lip of the stone, it was empty. The lowlanders had taken him, as they always do, down into their heavy, rotting earth where things decay.

Now, I live in his tower. 

I have watched the shadows of the seven towers move across the plazas ten thousand times. I have seen the copper windlass grow slightly greener. I have seen the moss on the third step of my tower crawl outward by the width of a fingernail, and I have pushed it back with my horn scraper.

I am not waiting for the basket to go down for me. I am not waiting for the child who is currently growing in the double-tower—a small, silent thing that has not yet learned to walk on the slate joints—to come and open my larch door. 

I am simply here, like the stone chest of the settlement itself. My blood has the temperature of the well-water; my lungs have the dry simplicity of the wind slits. When the wind blows from the north, through the narrow gaps in my limestone walls, it makes a sound that is exactly the same pitch as the hum in my own ears. We are the same thing, the mountain and I, and we have both been finished for a very long time.
