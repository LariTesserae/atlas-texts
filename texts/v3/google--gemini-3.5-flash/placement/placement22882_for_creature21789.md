The saffron-veil is always the first thing to settle. 

I do not remember what the sky looks like without its grease-gold filter. To see the world in its raw state—the blue that kills the eyes, the white flare that cooks the wetness out of a tongue in thirty heartbeat-spaces—is a punishment reserved for infants who have not yet grown their lashes, or for those whose cords have parted over the two-thousand-foot drop. 

For me, the world is three things: the yellow light, the dark charcoal of the dry-stone slate, and the grease.

I am the **Third-Anchor of the Seventh Cord-of-the-Weep**.

My body is short, even for a Vahri. My ribs are wide and barrelled like an oil-skin, housing lungs that must sift the thin, cold air for what little sweet spirit it holds. My skin is ancient. The violet-grey rind of snake-fat and hare-soot has not been scrubbed from my back in forty thaws; it has cured into me, forming three-inch scales across my shoulders that smell of cold mutton-fat and old moss. If you were to strike my shoulder with a bone-chilling mallet, it would make the same dull *thud* as the basalt under my feet.

We are starting to wake because the Pave is ending.

***

### The Waking

I can feel the Pave breaking through the soles of my feet before any of the others. 

During the Pave, the world is perfect and terrible. The salt-springs freeze into grease-yellow columns that do not drip. The glass-shield isopods die by the millions, their tiny bodies freezing into one solid, clicking sheet of ice that coats the paths like broken glass. In the Pave, we do not move. My Cord—the four of us—sleeps nested together like field-mice in the middle of our stack-hut. 

On my chest is the weight of seven silt-moths. They are the size of baking stones, their white hair sticking to my grease-scale. They are cold. Their shivering has stopped. When the Pave is deep, they enter a state so close to stone that you can stack them like shingles.

Then, the first *click*.

It is not a sound. The air here does not tolerate sounds. It is a vibration that rises through the three-ton foundation plate of our hut, through the dry-stone floor, through the calluses of my knees, and into the marrow of my pelvis. 

*Clack-click.*

A mile above us, at the crown of the notch, a hot vein of mineral salt has found a new way through the diorite. The oil-water has begun to weep again.

Against my ribs, I feel the twitch of the Cord. 

I am bound to three others by the gut-line. It is twelve feet of braided mountain-hare intestine, greased until it is supply-grey, looped three times around my waist and secured with a toggle made from my uncle’s left collarbone. 

To my left is **The-Two-Taps-on-the-Wrist**, whom we call **Kesta** when we must use throat-noises. She is young; her grease-rind is still pale violet, almost pink at the elbows where her skin is thin. She is our shingle-tosser. 

To my right is **The-Long-Slide**, the one who gathers the salt-crust from the wells. 

And behind me, dragging her weight like an anchor made of wet wool, is **The-Old-One-Who-Does-Not-Climb**. Her name on the hair-line is just a heavy, downward pull.

I reach out my hand. My fingers are broad and blunt, the nails worn down to flat plates of horn from sixty years of digging out frozen moss. I touch the moth on my breast. 

A tiny, high-frequency tremor. The moth’s heart has felt the weep. It begins to shiver. 

*We are moving.*

***

### The Slide

We crawl out of the stack-box on our stomachs, our bellies scraping the grease-skins that cover the floor. 

The air outside is a yellow fog. The mock-suns have risen—four of them today, linked by a burning cross of white light that my saffron-veil turns into a heavy, golden cage. The basalt walls of the notch are sweating. The oil-water doesn't run like creek-water; it slides down the stone in thick, skin-like sheets, bubbling where it meets the sub-zero wind.

I strap the clack-pads to my feet. They are carved from the broad, flat horn of a valley-goat, held to my toes with dried sinew. 

*Clack-shove. Clack-shove.*

The sound of the village waking is the sound of ten thousand bone plates sliding over wet rock. We do not look at each other’s faces. To look at a face is to waste the light. We look at the heels in front of us. We look at the stone.

Kesta is leading. She moves with the light, greasy speed of a lichen-snake. The gut-line between us goes taut, then slack, then taut again. I can read her path by the tension in my belly.
*   A sharp, upward jerk: *A fresh ice-lens is lifting a path-stone. Step wide.*
*   A slow, vibrating drag: *The isopods are thick here. Slide the feet; do not lift.*

I slide my feet. Below me, the glass-shield isopods are a dense, shifting carpet of pale gold. They are feeding on the mineral-grease that the spring leaves behind. Under my horn-soles, they click and scramble, their translucent shells refracting the yellow glare into tiny, darting needles of light. If I lift my feet, I will crush them, and their wet, salt-insides will make the basalt too slick for even our grease-skins to grip. So we slide. *Clack-shove. Clack-shove.*

We are heading to the **Runnel-of-the-Limb**.

It is twenty levels down, near the lip where the shelf drops off into the great grey sea of clouds. 

***

### The Greening

At the lowest runnel, the water is thickest. It smells of copper and dead volcanic chambers. This is where we keep **The-Three-Notches-behind-the-Knuckle**.

She was the fifth of our Cord until four thaws ago, when her lungs filled with grey silt and she stopped shivering. 

We find her where we left her, wedged into a deep, grooved trough where the heavy mineral-water runs in a steady, gelatinous fall. The water has done its work well. When I lay my bare hand against her thigh, it does not yield. It is no longer skin; it is something cold, heavy, and slick. The salt has replaced her fat with stone. Her knee is a lump of pale green jasper; her ribs are like frozen lard that has turned to glass.

She is almost cured.

Kesta reaches down. With her bone mallet, she taps the dead girl’s shoulder. 

*Clink.*

It is a clean, metallic sound. It is the register of dry rock. 

The-Old-One-Who-Does-Not-Climb emits a low, rolling vibration from her throat—the chest-growl that means *she is heavy enough.*

We set our shoulders to her. We are a Cord; we do not heave with our arms, we lean our collective weight against the line. I plant my broad, horn-shod feet into the wet basalt. Kesta hooks her fingers into the dead girl’s armpits—her arms are frozen in a semi-circle, as if she were still holding a basket of moths. 

With a wet, sucking sound, the stone-girl comes out of the runnel. She is sixteen stone of green-grey weight, cold as the heart of the mountain.

We do not weep for her. We have her. She is our new roof-lintel.

***

### The Stack

The Great Re-stacking has begun.

The suns are at their height, and the basalt notch is screaming in its quiet way. Behind the stack of huts, a massive column of ice—blue-black and thick as three men—is growing out of a cleft. It is pushing our winter-box three feet to the west. The slate shingles of our back wall are already cracking under the pressure, the dry-stone basalt groaning as the mountain-bones grope for room.

Our neighbors are already there. They do not help us, nor do we help them; we simply work in the same tight, greasy space, our shoulders rubbing as we strip our walls.

*Tac. Tac. Tac.*

We work in complete silence, save for the rhythmic hammering of the bone wedges. 

We dismantle the back wall shingle by shingle. The ice-tongue behind it is beautiful through the saffron-veil—a great, golden tooth of frozen salt-water that vibrates with the pressure of the spring behind it. It is hot to the touch—not with heat, but with the dry, burning cold that blistered my hands when I was a boy.

We do not fight the tooth. We hand the slate shingles down to Kesta, who stacks them by her flat-pads. 

We clear the space. We let the ice-tooth grow until it fills the space where our sleeping-nest was this morning. It stops, locked against a secondary column of grey diorite. It is stable. It will hold for at least six thaws.

"Now," the line tells me. A sharp, double-tug from the Old One.

We bring the stone-girl up. 

We lift her—Kesta at the knees, myself at the pelvis, the Old One holding the central cord-ring to brace us against the wind that howls up from the cloud-line. We lay her across the top of the two new slate pillars we have built on either side of the gold ice-tooth.

Her green-jasper thighs are flat and broad. They make a perfect lintel. 

We lay the roof-shingles directly over her chest. Her mineralized ribs will bear the weight of the five feet of wet slate above her. Her stone hands, curled upward, will catch the run-off from the upper huts and direct the water away from our entranceway, keeping our threshold dry.

By dusk, the work is done. 

The four suns are sinking into the grey floor of the clouds, turning the mist into a vast, flat sheet of violet iron. The Weep is slowing. The gel on the walls is thickening into those fat, blue-white knuckles of rime.

We crawl back into our new, smaller box. It is four feet narrower than it was this morning, but the roof is solid. The green-grey belly of our old Cord-mate forms the ceiling above our heads. 

The silt-moths are brought out from the leather bags. They are hungry. They crawl over our greased thighs, their white, woolly wings beginning to shiver in the dark. 

I lie down. The line around my waist is loose, but it is there. I can feel Kesta’s breathing through the hemp; I can feel the Old One’s slow, watery heartbeat through the floor-plates.

Above us, the wind screams through the dry slate, but the dead girl’s bones do not move. They hold. 

We shiver together in the yellow dark, waiting for the Pave to lock us in once more.
