I would not be found on the high plateaus where the light is loudest, nor would I be at the damp, dark floor where the geothermal vents cook the fallen leaves into a thick, sulfurous broth. 

I would be in the middle-space. The gap.

I am a **Tensioner**—though if the Stave-Shadows had words for me, they might call me *the string that remembers*. 

I live suspended between two basalt fingers, hanging from a massive, three-foot-thick green creeper that spans eighty yards of empty air. My body is not designed for the stone. Had I been dropped onto a flat ledge, I would slowly pool and die, unable to support my own wet mass. Here, however, coiled around the living suspension bridge of the vine, my weight is my strength.

Physically, I am a long, tapered bolster of muscle and fibrous tissue, perhaps twenty feet from end to end, colored the bruised, violet-black of a wet plum. I have no limbs, no face, and no bones. Instead, my skeleton is hydraulic—a system of internal chambers filled with the dense, mint-scented water of this world, pressurized to match the thick syrup of the atmosphere. 

At my northern end, my body splits into thousands of hair-fine, silver-grey threads—my *wefts*. These are not simple roots; they are neuromuscular cords that have threaded themselves deep into the bark and pulp of the giant creeper. At my southern end, they do the same. I am literally stitched into the bridge. I am a living segment of the span.

***

To be a Tensioner is to experience the world through three primary sensations: weight, pull, and the damp.

Because the atmosphere is so thick, it does not blow past me so much as it pushes against me like a slow, warm river. I feel the wind not as a draft, but as a lean. When the thermal currents rise from the valleys in the afternoon, my entire body bows upward, a twenty-foot crescent of violet flesh, straining against my silver anchors. It is a delicious sensation—the feeling of the air attempting to lift my immense, sodden weight and carry me into the light-saturated clouds. 

I do not see. The golden, refracted light of this place is something I experience as a slow, rhythmic baking of my upper skin. I know the sun has reached its peak when my dorsal pores begin to secrete a thick, aromatic grease that smells of wet iron and clove. This oil keeps my skin from cracking under the sun’s stare, and tells the **Vitreous Ribbons** that I am awake.

As the afternoon deepens, the floating globes of water drift past my span. They are attracted to my shadow. Often, three or four of these hand-sized spheres will bump gently against my flanks, sticking to my oily skin like beads of mercury. Inside them, I can feel the tiny, cool tickle of the blue Ribbons as they ripple their bodies. They do not feed on me, nor I on them; they simply rest in my lee, using my physical mass to shield themselves from the rising air currents that might otherwise carry them too high, where the Axioms' fields would pop their liquid homes. We hum together—their blue, pulsing spines sending tiny electrical currents through my skin, which I answer with the deep, slow thud of my hydraulic heart.

***

But my true work—my reason for being—is the *tension*.

The mountains of this world are ancient and settled, but they are still alive. They expand in the heat of the double rainbows; they contract when the great, localized mist-clouds settle over their peaks. This movement is microscopic, but across a gap of eighty yards, it changes the pull on the creepers.

I am the scale that measures this change. I feel the tug. 

When the basalt fingers lean away from each other, the vine grows taut, humming like a bowstring. The pressure in my internal chambers rises. I feel the strain in my silver wefts. In response, my muscle fibers undergo a slow, chemical relaxation. I lengthen. I allow the bridge to sag just enough to prevent the vine from snapping. When the peaks lean back together, and the vine begins to droop into the path of the giant, armored gastropods crawling below, I do the opposite: I pump water from my lateral reservoirs into my core, contracting, hardening, pulling the span tight and hoisting it back into the warm, safe middle-air.

It is a slow, quiet dialogue. A single adjustment can take three days to complete. I do not rush. Nothing here rushes.

***

Once every few months, a **Silted One** will crawl across my span. 

I feel its approach hours before it arrives—a rhythmic, heavy clicking through the fibers of the creeper as its calcified, violet shell scrapes against the bark. I do not fear it. When it reaches my body, it does not stop. With its four muscular tentacles, it climbs directly onto me.

Its adhesive pads are cool and dry against my greasy skin. As it crawls along my length—a journey that takes a day and a night—its broad underbelly scrapes away the yellow lichens and choking mosses that have begun to sprout in the folds of my flesh. It is a deep, agonizingly slow massage. In return, it drinks the nutrient-rich oil that seeps from my dorsal pores. 

We do not speak, but as its heavy shell presses into my soft hydraulic core, our pressures equalize. For twenty-four hours, we are a single organism: one of us holding the bridge, the other cleaning the line. When it finally steps off my southern end and continues its infinite crawl down the creeper, I feel a light, buoyant emptiness, like a singer who has finished a long, low note.

***

My thoughts are not like the thoughts of the Stave-Shadows, who hover near the Axioms and contemplate the stars. My mind is a slow-water mind. 

I do not think in words or symbols. I think in *balances*. 

I think of the ratio between the moisture in the air and the water in my blood. I think of the distance between the black basalt finger to my left and the green-capped wall to my right. I hold the memory of the great rain of forty years ago, which made my vine so heavy I had to contract until my skin turned white to keep us from falling into the heat of the vents.

At twilight, when the horizontal rainbows fade and the valleys turn the color of bruised slate, the hum of the nearest Axiom changes. It becomes deeper, a rich, golden vibration that travels up the basalt walls, into the roots of the creepers, and straight into my silver wefts. 

When that hum arrives, I close my pores. I let my body relax. I hang in the deep, warm syrup of the air, perfectly balanced between the two cliffs, listening to the megaphonic frogs boom in the hollows below. 

The water falls. The water rises. The mountains hold. And I hold the mountains.
