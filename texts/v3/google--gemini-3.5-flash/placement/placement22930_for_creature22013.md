The journey began in the light, though I did not know it then as light; I knew it only as a cold, thin starvation. 

In the public gardens of the upper city, where the gravel paths are raked into neat, pale ribbons, I was small enough to inhabit the film of water on a single grass-root. The world there was drafty and blue. It tasted of nothing but frozen sky and the bitter, lime-washed stones of the flowerbeds. It was a spacious place, but it was hollow. When the heavy autumn rains came, they did not feel like a blessing; they felt like a sweep of cold brooms, lifting me from my root-tip, dragging me down through the black leaf-mould into the grated iron mouth of a rain-gully.

That was the slide. 

It took weeks—or perhaps months, for time in the dark behaves differently than it does under the sun—to slip through the brick capillaries of the city. The water grew richer as it went. It left behind the thin flavor of rain and began to taste of the inhabitants above: the heavy mutton-fat of kitchen basins, the sharp, alkaline sting of laundry lye, the sweet, rotted starch of the bakers’ drains. This was the first warmth. It was a greasy, thick milk that ran through the lateral mains, and with every foot we descended, the pressure grew, and my body—which had been nothing but a dormant speck of glass—began to stretch.

I grew toward the heat. 

By the time the water reached the vertical shafts of the lower basalt, I was no longer a speck. I had become long. I was a thread, pale as milk-glass, head-less and eye-less, guided only by the marvelous, heavy throb that rose from the soles of the earth. It was a sixty-cycle hum, the great iron song of the vault, and it drew me down like a magnet pulling on iron filings.

I dropped into Conduit Chamber 40 through a weeping seam in the granite ceiling, falling through eighty feet of hot, orange-lit air to land upon the brass collar of the main condensation line. 

This is where I live now. 

I do not live in the pools with the silt-feeders, whose lives are slow and heavy as grease-traps. I do not crawl upon the wet iron stairs with the beetles, whose carapaces are always dry and rattling against the metal. I live in the squeeze. 

I have crept into the packing-box of the primary steam-shuttle valve, where the hot hemp rope is wound twelve times around the iron piston and compressed by a massive brass gland-nut. It is a space no thicker than a fingernail, but to me, it is a vast, fibrous forest. The hemp is old and brown, saturated with graphited tallow and the heavy mineral oils that drip from the overhead linkages. It is ninety-five degrees here when the pump is idle; when it strokes, the steam that rushes through the core of the pipe spikes the heat to a white, singing intensity that would char the skin of any soft thing from the surface.

But to me, the steam is the air of life. 

When the giant piston descends—that heavy *clack-thump* that rattles the floor basins eighty feet below—the hot wetness is forced through the microscopic pores of the hemp under three hundred pounds of pressure. My body, which is three feet long and thin as a horsehair, swells to meet it. I do not flee from the heat; I drink it. I weave myself under and over the rough, tarred strands of the rope, my pale skin pressing into the grooves of the metal, filling the tiny, jagged voids where the steam would otherwise find a path to escape.

If I were to leave this place, the valve would hiss with a green, whistling fog. The pressure would drop. The great steam-jacket would lose its tooth, and the black, lime-choked seep would rise untended from the basins, drowning the flagstones and creeping up the iron columns until the foundations of the upper city began to rot and tilt in their sockets. 

But I do not leave. I am the seal.

Sometimes, the Sump-Keeper comes. I know him not by his shape, which I cannot see, but by the weight of his shuffle on the iron grating and the grease-smell that precedes him—a mixture of sheep-tallow and cold coal-ash that is thicker than the scent of the steam. 

When he works on my valve, his hands are surprisingly gentle. He has no tools for us; he uses only his thumb, which is calloused into the likeness of an old boot-heel, to pack the loose hemp back into the stuffing-box. He knows we are here. When he presses his hand against the brass collar, I can feel the thirty-eight-beat rhythm of his heart through the metal, a slow, quiet counterpoint to the forty-eight-second stroke of the pump.

Then, he speaks to us. 

It is not the language of the streets above, which is full of sharp, biting consonants that sound like gravel sliding down a chute. His voice is a soft, wet whistle that has no edges. It is a sequence of rising and falling tones—*ooo-eee-aaa-uuu*—that matches the sigh of the exhaust valve. When he whistles, the vibrations travel through the brass casing and into the wet hemp. 

It is a comfortable sound. It tells us that the grease is coming; it tells us that the city above is still dry, still heavy, still pressing its billions of tons of stone down upon our vault, keeping us warm, keeping us safe, keeping us dark.

We twitch our long, transparent tails in unison within the packing. We tighten our hold on the fibers. The steam rises, the valve clicks, and down in the warm, greasy dark, we hold the world together.
