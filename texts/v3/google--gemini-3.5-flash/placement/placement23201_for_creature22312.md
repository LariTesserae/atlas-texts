The first thing I know is not sight, but the *clank*.

It is not a sound that comes through the ear, because ears are delicate things—membranes of dry parchment that would split in this grease-soup air. The *clank* is a shudder in the basalt behind my ribs. It begins far down, four miles into the drowned roots of the pillar, where the water is no longer water but a white, screaming iron-broth. It travels up through the stone, a heavy, marrow-vibrating *thump-thump*, and by the time it reaches me, it has turned into a long, low singing.

I am not on the outside of the spires. The outside is for the things with wings of oiled leather and the crabs with joints of brass. The outside is too thin; the mist there moves too fast, and the wind from the chasm threatens to tear you from your holds and drop you into the blank floor of the world.

I live in the bore.

### I. The Anatomy of the Sleeve

I am a **Bore-Sleeve**. If you were to shine an oil-lantern down into one of the vertical rifled channels of the third pylon, you would not see the bottom. You would see only a smooth-sided tube of wet, black basalt, and then, eighty feet down, you would see me.

My body is eighty feet of compressed, segmented tallow. I am as thick as a main-mast, shaped precisely to the cylinder of the stone. My outer skin is not skin at all, but a thick, vulcanized rind of gray-black horn, grooved with the same spiral fluting as the rock. I fit so snugly into this mountain that if I were to die and dry out, my casing would remain wedged here for three centuries, a plug of dead leather in the throat of the earth.

But I am not dry. I am wet with a constant, cold grease. 

Above me, the mouth of my chamber is crowned with **Lubricant Vines**. Their black, rubbery ropes hang down into my bore like hair into a throat. They weep their heavy, yellow oil down my sides, and I drink it through the small, sucking pores of my upper collar. It tastes of earth-iron and sulfur-yeast, very sweet and very dense. 

Without this grease, I would be dead before the sun cleared the high mists. As the steam-pressure rises from the deep chambers below, it pushes against my flat, heavily calloused bottom—my *gasket-sole*. I am forced upward, sliding thirty, forty, sometimes fifty feet up the bore in a single, smooth, silent stroke. The stone screams against my hide, but the yellow vine-oil makes the passage silent, a long, greasy slide.

When I reach the top of my stroke, I cover the side-vents—the lateral blowholes that open out into the open gorge. The steam accumulates beneath me, hot and hard, until my belly is white-hot and my tallow begins to melt. 

Then, I contract. 

I do not think about it; it is the rhythm of my blood. My middle segments bunch together like an accordion. My collar drops three feet, exposing the side-vents. 

*Hiss-shuck.*

The white plume shoots out into the gorge, a thousand feet of high-pressure scream that cuts the hanging moss from the opposite spire. The pressure below me drops. I sink back down into the cool, dark well of the bore, sliding on my own grease, until I hit the stone ledge at the bottom with a wet, heavy *thud*.

And then we begin again.

### II. The Companions of the Dark

I am not alone in my hole. 

To the things that live on the vertical cliffs, I am a floor and a furnace. 

When I am at the bottom of my stroke, the **Clinker-Crabs** come down from the rim. They are small, about the size of my hand, and their brass legs make a busy, metallic *tink-tink-tink* against my horn-skin. They do not bite me; they are too busy. They scrape the white salt-crusts and the sulfur-scale that my steam-sweat leaves behind on my collar. They are clean, efficient little machines. Sometimes, when the pressure is low and I am resting, one of them will wedge itself between my hide and the rock, seeking the damp warmth, its brass shell vibrating in time with my breathing.

Sometimes, the **Solder-Grubs** crawl over me. They are slow and heavy, like tubes of raw liver that have been rolled in iron filings. They are blind, but they can feel the heat of my gasket-sole. They crawl beneath me when I am at the top of my stroke, plastering their gray, cement-like spit into the tiny fissures of my bore. They are the ones who keep my cylinder round. If the rock were to split, the steam would leak around my sides, my piston-action would fail, and I would be left to rot in the cold upper air, dry and useless. We need each other: I give them the scalding, mineral-rich sweat they eat, and they keep my stone tube seamless.

But best of all is **The Living Solder**.

It does not live in my bore; it lives in the stone behind me. It is a cold, silver thread that runs through the basalt like water through sand. When the steam below me is about to reach the bursting point, the rock begins to sing in a high, sharp key—a key that only the Solder can make. 

I feel the Solder grow cold behind my back. It is a strange sensation, like a line of winter ice laid against my hot grease. It is my warning. When the Solder turns cold, I know the valve below has jammed. I must not wait for the natural stroke. I must bunch my segments early, lift my collar, and let the white breath out before the pillar cracks.

We do this for one another. The Solder speaks; I slide; the mountain stays whole.

### III. The Cold Season

There are times—long, dark intervals that have no relation to the sun—when the water in the deep roots of the mountain begins to cool. 

No one knows why this happens. Perhaps the great fire deep within the earth has moved, or perhaps the sea-water has found a new path to the furnace. The *thump-thump* of the geothermal heart slows. It becomes thin, a dry, rattling knock.

When this happens, I do not slide.

I sink to the very bottom of my bore, sitting hard upon the basalt ledge. The steam beneath me becomes a wet, yellow fog that has no lift in it. The Lubricant Vines above grow dry; their oil turns thick and brown, like cold mutton-fat, and it clogs my sucking pores with a bitter crust.

This is the time of the great silence.

I shrink. My eighty feet of tallow contracts until I am only fifty feet of hard, gray lard. My skin turns the color of lead, and I lose the taste of the iron-ore. The crabs do not come down; they have gone to the lower terraces where the last of the steam vents are still whistling. The grubs coil themselves into balls of rust in the corners of my bore and go to sleep.

In the silence, I can hear the outside world.

I can hear the **Iron-Grip Kites** screaming in the high mist. Their calls are flat, metallic clicks that drop down my bore like pebbles. I can hear the great **Valve-Saddles** down at the chasm floor, five miles below, letting out their long, wet, flatulent groans as they try to seal the cooling cracks in the foundation. It sounds like the mountain is settling its bones, preparing to sleep forever.

But the Solder does not sleep.

Even in the cold, I can feel it. It is a faint, silver heartbeat in the basalt behind my back. It is not cold now; it is warm—warmer than the stone around it. It is carrying the last of the deep heat from the distant, active pillars, sharing it with me so that my tallow does not freeze into solid fat.

We wait. We have waited like this for a hundred years, or perhaps only for three days. Time does not move in the bore; only the stroke moves.

And then, deep down, nineteen thousand feet below my gasket-sole, something shifts.

A valve of stone, heavy as a village, falls into a new slot. The sea-water finds the red iron again. 

The *clank* returns.

It is a small *shudder* first, then a *thrum*, then a great, roaring *thump* that makes my entire bore ring like a church bell. The steam beneath me turns white, then blue, then invisible with pressure.

My gasket-sole warms. The lard in my middle segments melts into active, slippery tallow. The Lubricant Vines above begin to weep their sweet, yellow grease once more.

I feel the cold line of the Solder behind my ribs. 

*Rise,* it says.

I bunch my segments. I grip the rifled fluting of the stone with my outer horn. I draw in a long breath of the yellow vine-oil, and then, with a slow, heavy, grease-slicked sigh, I begin to slide upward.

*Thump. Shuck. Hiss.*

We are running again.
