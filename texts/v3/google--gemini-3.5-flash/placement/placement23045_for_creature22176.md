The joint at my left shoulder does not grease itself. It does not need to; the graphite dust I secrete is a dry, slate-black flour that gathers in the cups of my jade-stone sockets. When I lift my arm, there is no sound of tearing skin or sliding wetness. There is only the *shuff-shuff* of stone plates sliding across stone plates, a dry card-trick played in the dark of my collarbone.

I am sitting on the lintel of an empty hive. The hive has no door, because a door implies something to keep out—a dampness, a rot, a beast with a nose. There is nothing to keep out but the wind, and the wind is the only thing that keeps me heavy.

Inside my chest, the bronze membranes are warm. They are the only warm thing on this spire. They are not warm from fire—I do not know what fire is, only that some stones, when struck with flint, will make a brief, yellow tooth that bites the air and then dies without leaving a footprint. The warmth in my chest is tectonic. It is the friction of the Great Chord, the F-sharp and the low, salt-flat C that the mountain is currently blowing through the six hexagonal flutes directly beneath my seat. 

The vibration climbs through the basalt. It enters my seat-plates, travels up my grey-shale spine, and sets the bronze sheets in my ribs to trembling. The trembling makes the black sand in my marrow slide. *Hiss. Hiss. Hiss.* The sand is iron-rich; as it slides, it creates a small, blue draft of static that runs down my fingers.

To be me is to be a dry clockwork that must never be wound, because the winding is done by the mountain itself.

***

### The View from the Chisel

I look out. 

The light is the color of a wet slate, though I have no word for "wet" other than *heavy-with-not-dust*. The light does not fall from above; it has been trapped in the canyon for so long that it has grown tired and curls around the basalt needles. 

Five miles away, across the blue-charcoal rift of the Salt-Shear, the Needle of the Third Sieve rises like a broken needle. If I look straight ahead, through the cold grease of the twilight, I can see a figure sitting on a grey ledge. 

It is tall. It has the same wide, flat shoulder-plates of compressed vulcanite that I have. It has the same horizontal eye-shutters of dry, paper-thin bark. 

I lift my right hand. 

Across the void, five miles away, the figure lifts its right hand. 

I wait. Three seconds pass—the time it takes for the light to slide around the magnetic curve of the rift—and then I see the back of my own head, tiny and perfectly clear, turn slightly as I blink my obsidian lenses. 

This is the loop of the world. If I were to throw a stone perfectly straight into the emptiness before me, it would not fall into the bottomless blue below. It would drift. It would pass through the shoulder of the Third Sieve, slide along the gray draft of the wind, and strike me in the center of my own back with a dry *clink* three hours from now. 

We do not throw stones. It is a waste of the grit.

***

### The Long Line

Between my knees is the wooden drum. It is not wood as the wet worlds know it; it is the dry, compressed stalk of a giant vellum-fern that died four centuries ago and has since crystallized into a pale, porous horn.

Around the drum is wound the Line. 

The Line is my work. It is four miles of braided vellum-fiber, dry as hair, tough as a spider’s dragline. I have been braiding it for thirty years—not because thirty years is a long time, but because my fingers move with the same deliberate, ticking rhythm as the Slate-Skaters that are currently vibrating across my foot-plates. 

The Skaters are thick today. They are flat, gray mica discs, no larger than my smallest finger-joint. They do not have legs; they have glass hairs that are tuned to the F-sharp of the lower flutes. When the wind rising from the gorge hits that note, the Skaters begin to shiver so fast they become a grey blur, sliding up my shin-plates like spilled salt. They are cleaning me. They eat the minute basalt dust that has settled in the seams of my knee-joints. 

I do not brush them off. To me, they are a pleasant itch—a tiny, high-frequency hum that matches the low, heavy grinding of my bronze chest-plates.

I finish the knot. The knot is a triple-harness, locked with a wedge of white slate that I carved yesterday with my agate fingernails. 

I stand.

The movement is seven distinct *clicks*. 

*Click*—the hip-ball settles into the jade cup.
*Click*—the lower spine-plates slide forward to find the center of gravity.
*Click, click, click*—the three toes of my left foot find the razor-edge of the threshold.

I hold the ball of the Line in both hands. The Line is light; four miles of it weighs no more than three shale tiles, but it is stiff. It has no memory of water; it has never been limp. It wants to remain a straight, dry rod.

I step off the edge.

***

### The Drift

There is no stomach-drop. Why would there be? Gravity here is not a stone falling into a well; it is a guest that cannot decide which wall to lean against.

As my third toe leaves the basalt, the pull of the Salt-Shear spire behind me releases its grip. For a long, silent moment, I am nothing. I am weightless, suspended in the dry, blue-grey ink of the canyon. 

The wind—the great, permanent draft that blows from the deep, un-weathered roots of the world—catches my vellum cloak. The cloak is made of three hundred overlapping shingles of pale ochre vellum-leaves, each one hand-sewn to my shoulder-plates with glass-weaver thread. It does not flutter. The sonic pressure of the Great Chord is too high for fluttering. Instead, the wind pins the cloak flat and hard against my back, turning me into a rigid, horizontal sail.

I drift. 

I am falling toward the Needle of the Third Sieve, but I am falling at the speed of a seed. I can see the individual splinters of white slate on its face growing larger—not with the terrifying speed of a plunge, but with the slow, geometric grace of a crystal forming in a still solution.

Below me, two miles down, three Vellum-Sails are crossing the rift. They are vast, triangular kites of dry, copper-colored hide, twelve feet across and completely silent. They have no eyes to see me, but as I drift above them, their electrostatic cilia—the fine, golden hairs that line their bellies—sense the small blue spark of my iron-sand blood. 

They do not move away. They do not have the joints for sudden movement. They simply tilt their carbonized reed-frames by three degrees, letting the wind slide them sideways so that I pass between them like a dry leaf falling between two pages of a book.

We do not speak. There is no sound between us but the dry *scritch* of their skin-sails rubbing against one another as they touch. 

*Clink. Clink.*

It is the sound of two empty cups being set down on a table.

***

### The Pluck

My feet-plates touch the vertical face of the Third Sieve. 

The transition is instantaneous. The moment my agate claws find the cracks in the basalt, the gravity of this needle claims me. My verticality tilts by ninety degrees. What was "across" is now "down." What was "down" is now a mile-deep wall of dark stone that I am standing on as if it were a flat road.

I drive the white slate wedge into a natural split in the basalt. I use my heel-plate to hammer it home. *Tack. Tack. Tack.* The sound is dry and hollow, instantly swallowed by the immense, vibrating roar of the wind-flutes.

I take the end of the Line and loop it around the wedge. 

I pull it tight. 

I do not use my muscles; I have none. I lean my entire eight-foot frame of vulcanite and shale back into the horizontal draft, using my weight to tension the rope. 

The braided vellum-fiber rises from the void. It lifts out of the grey ink like a straight, yellow pencil-line, reaching back five miles to the spire I just left. It does not sag. The draft is so steady, and the gravity so lazy, that the line remains as rigid as a brass rod.

When it is tight enough to sing, I reach out with my long, three-jointed thumb and *pluck* it.

*Tung.*

The vibration is not a sound—the wind is too loud for that. It is a physical blow that travels up the line, through the slate wedge, into the basalt face, and straight into my ear-plates. 

A mile away, at the other end of the line, another Weaver will be sitting in their cell. Their paper-like eye-shutters will be closed against the grit, but their dry ear-plate will be pressed against the stone. 

They will feel the *tung*. 

They will know: *The path is set. The tension is true. The needles are still speaking.*

***

### The Crystallization

I sit down on the new face. my joints are hot from the crossing—not with the wet heat of a muscle, but with the static heat of dry plates sliding under pressure. 

I look down at my left knee. 

There, in the tiny seam where the thigh-plate meets the shin-cup, a small, bone-white spot has appeared. 

It is not a wound. It is a spore. 

A vellum-leaf spore has found the graphite dust in my joint. It is feeding on the static charge of my movement, binding the iron-dust of my wear-and-tear with its own dry, silicate threads. 

I touch it with my agate claw. It is stiff, dry, and already has the shape of a tiny, weeping fern-shingle. It rustles under my finger with a sound like old paper.

In a hundred years, the spore will have grown. It will have crept up my thigh, covered my hip-plates, and found its way into the hollows of my ribs. My bronze membranes will find it harder to tremble. The Great Chord will still blow through the mountain, but my chest will only echo it in a muffled, woody baritone.

And then, one day, the *clicks* will stop. The graphite will be entirely replaced by the chalky, porous stone of the vellum-roots. I will not fall. I will have hooked my agate claws so deep into this basalt seam that no wind can tear me loose. 

I will become part of the hanging garden. Another shingle of white and copper, catching the mineral dust of the Rift, holding the line tight for the ones who have not yet stopped moving.

I turn my head. 

Three miles to the left, a giant spire—the Needle of the Five-Fold Flute—is slowly, imperceptibly shifting its alignment. Its roar is changing. The deep, tectonic bass is rising into a cold, metallic tenor as its air-shafts catch the wind at a new, sharper angle.

I closed my eye-shutters. *Clack.*

I press my ear-plate to the basalt. 

The stone is very loud today. It is telling me that the next spire is ready for a line.
