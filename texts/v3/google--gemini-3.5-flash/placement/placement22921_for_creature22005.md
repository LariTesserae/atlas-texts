It begins not with a sound, nor with a sight—for there are no eyes to receive the soot-dark sky—but with the squeeze. 

I am a weight among weights. I am one tile in an endless roof that has slid off the rafters and into the dirt, yet refuses to stop sliding. 

At first, I do not know where my edges end and the edges of my brothers begin. We are packed so closely that the warmth between us is not the heat of blood, but the dry, static friction of chitin against chitin. Under my belly—which is pale and tough as an old boot-sole—three thousand tiny, jointless threads of dark horn are shivering. They do not think to move; they simply vibrate in their sockets, a rhythmic, frantic twitching that drives me forward against the basalt floor. 

*Shuck. Shuck. Shuck.*

I am in the middle layers, where the world is purple-grey and smells of old iron and the sour, yellow rot of sulfur. I am a Churner. I do not have a map of this basin, but my body is a compass of simple pressures. If the weight above me becomes too heavy, my threads carry me down, toward the deeper, stickier heat of the crevices. If the air below becomes so thick with volcanic gas that my spiracles whistle and seize, I slide outward, toward the cooler stone of the margins. 

I am not alone in my skin. 

Between my overlapping plates, where the dry lime-dust settles and threatens to lock my seams, I feel the tiny, heavy oil-drops of the Cinder-Mites. They are soft and waxy, like black pitch made alive. They do not crawl so much as melt through the crevices of my body, their tiny mouthparts scraping at the bitter sulfur-crust that builds up around my leg-joints. They are my grease. Without them, my plates would grind to a halt, and the great, sliding weight of the Churners behind me would ride over my back, crushing my shell into the basalt before my time. 

But I do not freeze. The mites eat, and I slide.

*Shuck. Shuck. Shuck.*

Time is not measured in suns or moons, for there is only the flat, soot-colored dark above and the sluggish copper sparks below. Time is measured in the wear of my shell. 

After ten thousand turns through the center of the floor, my edges—once as sharp as chipped flint, capable of slicing a soft brother to grease—begin to round. The purple-charcoal matte of my youth is rubbed away by the endless passage of other bellies over my back. I am rising. The pressure of the deep crevices pushes me upward, into the cooler, thinned air beneath the ceiling of black dust.

Now, I am a Glass-Back. 

My shell has become a dark mirror. I am obsidian-slick, heavy as a chunk of lead ore, and so smooth that my brothers slide over me without a sound. When the rare, dying copper sparks drift up from the volcanic throat below, they catch on my back, sliding across my polished surface like tiny, burning eyes before they vanish into the soot. 

Up here, the air is full of the silent, organic rain of the high peaks—fine, greasy carbon soot that drifts down like black snow. It settles on my back, filling the microscopic, shallow grooves that wear has carved into my chitin. As I slide beneath the brother ahead of me, his scraping, file-like mandibles find these grooves. He licks them clean, harvesting the soot I have gathered; behind me, another brother does the same to my flanks. We do not look at each other. We do not care. We are simply a machine of friction that feeds itself on its own movement.

But my leg-threads are growing short. 

The three thousand horn-needles on my belly, once so sharp and vibrating with the tireless frequency of the basalt’s heat, have been worn down to yellow stumps by centuries of sliding over the basalt ribs. I cannot push. I can only coast upon the shivering backs of the Churners below me, carried along like a leaf on a creek of purple slate.

The drift of the floor is carrying me toward the center. 

I smell it before I feel it—not the sour rot of sulfur, but a sharp, dry heat that rises like a column of hot iron. It is the venturi of the three ruined wells. 

The basalt blocks of the circular enclosures are smooth as glass, licked clean by the bellies of a billion ancestors. My brothers are climbing them, their horn-stumps clicking against the dry masonry, drawn by the delicious, baking warmth that rises from the dark vents inside. I am lifted with them. We rise up the outer wall in a slow, boiling wave, spilling over the low rim into the interior.

Inside the well, there is no circular eddying. There is only the funnel.

We are packed so tightly that the air between us glows. Every movement of my plate against another produces a brief, cold-green flash of static lightning—a silent, shivering pop of friction-light that reveals, for a microsecond, the hundreds of polished, obsidian-black shields spiraling down around me. 

We are going down into the dark throat.

At the bottom of the well, the basalt floor is not flat; it is cut with deep, spiral grooves that look like the teeth of a millstone. The weight of the hundreds of Shingles above me is immense—a hot, crushing fist that presses my soft parchment underside against the grooved basalt. 

I cannot move. I do not need to. 

The Churners above me continue to move, their vibrating legs driving the weight of the swarm down upon my back. My polished shell, which has survived the basin for centuries, begins to creak. 

*Tack.* 

The first crack runs down the center of my vault. It does not hurt—I have no nerves for pain—but it is a release of tension. The pressure is no longer something I must fight; it is something I am becoming. 

*Grind. Grind.*

The basalt teeth below me and the heavy, sliding plates above me are milling me down. My obsidian shield is shattered into purple grit; my yellow legs are crushed into a dry, horn-like pulp; my inner grease is squeezed out to join the lime-dust. I am being ground into a fine, grey, calcium-rich flour.

Through the tiny, worn weep-holes at the base of the well, the vibrating legs of the younger Shingles sweep my remains out into the deep crevices, down to the bed of the hot volcanic throat. 

And there, in the crushing, oven-like heat where the ground gas seeps directly from the mountain’s heart, the Soft-Backs are waiting. 

They are tallow-white and vulnerable, their old shells cracked behind them. They inhale the hot sulfurous vapor, puffing their dry-paper bodies out to grow, and they feed upon the fine, grey flour of my shell that drifts down through the cracks. They absorb the lime. They harden their plates. They turn grey, then slate, then the deep, polished charcoal-purple of the mature Shingle.

I am not gone. I am simply in their joints now. I am the plate that will slide; I am the thread that will shiver; I am the green spark that will flash in the soot-black dark.

*Shuck. Shuck. Shuck.*
