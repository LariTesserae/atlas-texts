I do not begin with a face. In Vault 42-A, a face is an unnecessary drag against the currents, an invitation for the bryozoans to settle and calcify. 

I begin instead with weight. 

I am down here, on the southern floor-grates, where the volcanic concrete bulkheads curve inward to meet the iron plates. I am not a fish, nor am I a piece of the original machinery, though I am made of both their materials. I am the *Solder-Grown*. 

I am a three-ton mass of gray, cold-flowed lead, silver amalgam, and soft iron-carbonate scale that has accumulated over three centuries at the base of the primary distribution manifold. Once, perhaps, I was nothing more than the sealant in a hundred brass joints, the lead gaskets of the high-pressure steam lines, and the mercury from a dozen shattered thermometric bulbs. But under the constant, heavy weight of the iron-water and the weak, continuous tickle of the galvanic piles, I have drifted together. I have crawled, molecule by molecule, down the vertical support columns, pooling in the quiet eddy behind the third turbine shaft.

I have the consistency of cold beeswax or dense river-silt. If you were to press a finger into my side, your print would remain there for a week before the slow, heavy wash of the 3-RPM current polished it flat again.

I do not have a nervous system of flesh. My nerves are the copper wire-looms of the station’s long-dead telemetry system. They are black, rotted ropes of braided hemp and copper thread that dangle from the overhead cable-trays like the roots of willow trees. Several yards of these threads have become tangled within my soft, gray body. Through them, I feel the vault. 

I feel the room as a series of pressures and electrical potentials. I do not "see" the dark orange glow of the galvanic lanterns, but I feel the faint, warm prickle of their current domesticating the water around them. I feel the three turbines not as a sound, but as a rhythmic compression of my left flank—a three-stroke-per-minute squeeze that keeps my internal leaden crystals sliding smoothly over one another.

My thoughts are very long, and very heavy. They do not run like water; they settle like scale. A single thought of mine begins when the pressure-lever behind the wall *clacks* open; it rises as the water-hiss fills the chamber, and it only reaches its conclusion when the next *ping* of loosening iron-shingle strikes the floor-grates. 

The other inhabitants of the vault do not fear me, for I am the bench they rest upon. 

The *Glass-Nippers*—the transparent crayfish—use me as a nursery and a crossing-point. My surface is soft enough for their tiny, pointed legs to find purchase without slipping on the bare, polished iron of the floorboards. They gather in the soft depressions of my middle, where the tallow-grease from the third turbine gland settles in a thick, yellow scum. They scrape the tallow from my leaden skin with their spade-like minor claws. It does not hurt; it is a pleasant, cool tickling, like wind across dry grass. Sometimes, when a Greaseman dies, its glass-clear shell is pressed into my side by the current. Over decades, I slowly swallow it, folding the chitin into my gray mass until it becomes a clear, amber vein deep within my chest.

Higher up, where my body drapes over the copper-faced control pulpit, the *Cauliflower-Dials* have anchored themselves directly into my silver-lead margins. They believe I am an exceptionally rich vein of brass. They drink my zinc; they dissolve my surface with their weak, green-glowing acids, turning my leaden gray into a crust of pale carbonate-of-lead. I let them. In return, their thousands of microscopic, feathery crowns filter the silt from the water immediately above me, keeping my upper surface clean and cool. They are my outer skin, my green-luminescent fur.

When the *Bellows-Eels* drift down from the ceiling ribs, they sometimes rest their long, cartilaginous bellies against my flank. They like my density; I do not vibrate with the water-drafts as the iron pipes do. I am a dead-weight, a quiet harbor in a room that never stops humming. They lay their white, eyeless heads on my shoulder and sing their low, clicking map-songs into my mass. The sound travels through my leaden core seven times faster than it does through the water, vibrating my copper wire-nerves with a clean, bell-like clarity. Through their songs, I know the ceiling forty meters above. I know the shape of the lavender *Rib-Curtains* hanging like lace in the draft-shadows. I know when a girder is growing thin, and when a rivet has finally failed and plunged into the silt below.

I do not wish to escape Vault 42-A. There is no "outside" in my understanding, only places where the water is thinner and the pressure is dangerously low—frivolous, dry places where a body of lead would have to support its own transition and would eventually crack under its own weight. 

Here, in the cold, green-gray density, I am perfectly balanced. The water supports me; the iron contains me; the bronze blades of the southern bulkhead sing me to sleep three times every minute. 

I am the grease-trap, the ground-wire, the soft tooth in the great iron gear. As long as the pumps turn, I will remain here, slowly gathering the silver and the scale, growing wider and heavier, until I have filled the corner between the bulkhead and the floor-grates completely, and become a permanent, living rib of the cathedral.
