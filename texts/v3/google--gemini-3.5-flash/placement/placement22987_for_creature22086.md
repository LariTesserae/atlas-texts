I am wedged between the ribs of the third-tier boiler and the wet, black hide of the fault. 

My left shoulder blade is pressed against the basalt; it is cold and slimy with the liver-colored liverwort that grows where the condensation runs down in thick, silent ropes. My right knee is bent three inches from the copper jacket of the steam-drum. I can feel the heat of the two-atmosphere pressure through the double-layer of my creeper-cloth trousers—not as a burn, but as a thick, throbbing weight that matches the pulse of my own groin.

If I breathe in too fast, my lungs feel as though they are being packed with wet sheep’s wool. So I do not breathe with my chest. I breathe with my belly, very slowly, the way the elder Tenders taught me when I was still small enough to clear the grease-traps behind the big drive-gears.

I am a *Casing-Tapper*. I don't have a name that anyone speaks aloud anymore, because we don't have the breath to waste on names. If someone wants me, they hit the vertical six-inch return line with a three-pound ring-spanner: *clink-clink-slide*. That is me. That is my weight in the world.

My hands are yellow and wide, the skin on the palms so thick and tallow-cured that I can reach into the flange-gaps to feel for leaking packing-wool without feeling the sting of the salt-water weep. My left hand holds my *driver*—a short, heavy bar of drop-forged iron with an octagon face, grease-slicked and heavy as a block of lead. My right hand is bare. I use the flat of my three middle fingers to feel the shudder.

Every twelve seconds, the big stoker-piston at the well-eye strokes. *Thump.* 

You do not hear it with your ears. The purple ferns are too thick for that; they smother the air like a wet wool blanket thrown over a dog’s mouth. You feel it in the soles of your feet first, then it travels up the shin-bones, settles in the bowl of the pelvis, and rattles the small teeth in the back of your jaw.

*Thump.*

In the space between the thumps, I tap.

*Clink. Clink. Clink.*

I am listening for the "hollow." If the copper sleeve has buckled away from the iron frame behind it—even by the thickness of a land-crab’s shell—the vibration will be fat and wet. It will sound like a spade striking mud instead of the clear, high whistle of solid metal. If I find a hollow, I must paint it with red-lead paste and tallow before the next shift, so the drift-creepers know where to drive the beech-wood wedges to keep the frame from buckling.

The light here is the color of a greasy copper kettle. My burner hangs from a hook on the boiler-strut three feet below my ankles. It is small—a little iron cup filled with the oil we wring from the waxy ferns—and its wick is nothing but a twisted rag of old creeper-cloth. It doesn’t throw light so much as it makes the shadows greasy. I can see the outline of my own toes, grey-white and wet, curled over the edge of the iron hanger-bar. I can see the bone-white creepers growing out from the gap where the boiler meets the stone; they have wrapped themselves three times around the pressure-relief valve, their hair-thin roots drinking the sweat off the brass.

I do not cut them. If you cut them here, they bleed a sticky, white sap that smells like rot-fish and makes the iron walkways so slick a man will slide right off into the forty-yard drop before he can catch a stay-line. You let them grow until they are three fingers thick, then the weavers come with their bone-saws to harvest them for the lye-tubs.

A shift is thirty-six hundred thumps. I know this because my father’s father taught me to count the stroke-vibrations in my liver. When I reach thirty-six hundred, I will slide backward out of this slot—hip first, then shoulder, then chin—and let myself down the chain-hoist to the weaver-tier.

My sister will be there. She is nineteen crusts old, and her eyes are already as wide and black as mine. She sits at the narrow loom-frame that hangs from the underside of the main water-tank, her feet tucked into the iron stirrups, her arms moving with the slow, heavy rhythm of a crab in a deep pool. She does not look at me when I pass. She looks at the heddles, her fingers tracing the pale, boiled creeper-fiber as she drives the bone shuttle through the warp. The smell of her cabin is always the same: wet cabbage, hot solder, and the grease she smears on her eyelids to keep the coal-mist from blind-crusting them.

Sometimes, when the boilers are low and the steam is quiet, we sit together in the corner of her floor-mesh. We do not speak. We put our foreheads together until the skin of our temples touches, and we feel the clink-talk coming up through the copper pillars of her cabin.

*Tap-tap-slide.* The miners are through the fourth fault.
*Double-clink-heavy.* The sump-well is rising.
*Slow-rattle.* An old stoker has slipped. They are wrapping him now.

I think of him down there, in the deep rifts where the black water boils up from the teeth of the world. I think of the white creepers finding his ankles, then his ribs, then the hollows of his eyes, their pale shoots growing warm and thick as they weave him into the floor of the Sinking. 

One day, someone will climb down to clear a grease-pipe, and they will set their foot on a rung that is made of his thigh-bone, grown over with five inches of wet, purple moss. They will not think of his name. They will only feel if the rung is solid under their heel, and then they will climb higher, toward the heat.

My scraper is ready. The next thump is coming. I feel it in my throat.

*Thump.*

I lean my forehead against the copper. I tap.
