The first thing you must understand about the iron is that it does not stay still. 

Even three inches of cold, twisted steel, anchored with six-ton expansion bolts into the teeth of the spires, is never truly rigid. It is a live thing. It hums. If you lie flat upon it, with your stomach pressed against the grease-caked strands and your chin tucked into your seal-skin collar, you can feel every piston-stroke of the foundries three miles to the west. It comes through the metal as a rhythmic, oily shudder—*thump-thick, thump-thick*—like the heartbeat of a great, wet dog.

This is where you would find me: not in the birdcages of the residential tier, nor under the smoke-blackened brick of the ovens, but in the *sag*.

I am a Line-Tender, though the folks in the high wards call us the **Slack-Walkers**. We are the ones who live in the catenary—the long, sloping belly of the cables where they dip closest to the white floor of the Well. 

***

To live down here, you must give up the lead.

The Spire-born are obsessed with their ballast. They pack their pockets with iron filings; they wear shoes that clack like horse-hooves on the timber stairs. They are terrified of the air. They look at the blue-gray void between the teeth and see a mouth waiting to swallow them. 

But when you spend enough years on the line, you realize the air isn't a mouth. It’s an engine.

My boots are made of soft, green-cured seal-hide, with no copper plates in the soles. My ballast-belt is hanging from an iron hook in my shack; its leather is dry and cracked, and the pockets are full of nothing but dried blue-moss and rusty washers. Without the weight, my body has grown long and loose-jointed, even for an Oakhavener. My ribs have splayed outward to make room for larger, wetter lungs—you need more surface area to pull the oxygen out of this cold wool. My skin is the color of grease-paint, with that faint, slate-gray grain that comes from living in the shadow of the foundries, but my fingers are different. The tips are flat and hard, like horn, from thirty years of gripping the cable-twinnage.

If I fell from the high tiers, I would not plunge. I have sewn flaps of oiled cow-hide into the arm-seams of my grease-coat—not wings, exactly, but *brakes*. If I slip, I do not sink toward the Well at five miles an hour like the "sinking stars." I slew sideways. I catch the oily, warm draft rising from the slag-chutes. I slide through the gray wool like a dropped piece of parchment, drifting until I can catch a hanging root of silver beard-moss or one of the trailing guy-ropes of the lower berths.

I am lighter than my neighbors. Down here, we call it "going soft." It is a quiet transition. One day you forget to put the lead shot in your hem; the next, you realize you would rather float than walk.

***

My house is a wicker basket, six feet wide, woven from the toughest runners of the leather-leaf fern. It hangs from three brass pulleys directly beneath the lowest point of the Transit Cable Three. 

It is always damp. The condensation does not drop from the cable above me; it gathers on the grass-work of my floor in great, clear globes that wobble like jelly when the wind stirs. I do not have a stove—the coal-gas lines do not reach this far down into the gap—but I have a small iron pot filled with cold-worms. They crawl over each other in the dark, their thread-like bodies glowing with that pale, chemical green. In their light, I oil my tackle and mend my nets.

My job is simple, though the city could not turn without it: I greased the joints. 

The Soughs—the Vesper-bellows—nest in the ironwork of my basket’s hangers. They are heavy, simple creatures, smelling of salt-fish and old tallow. They do not mind me. When the cold-fronts rise from the Well and their throat-sacs start that dry, clicking rattle, they crowd closer to my wicker walls, their seal-skin flanks steaming in the damp. I reach out through the lathe-holes with a short, curved knife and scrape the thick, yellow wax from their oil-glands. 

It is beautiful stuff, this sough-grease. It is cold-proof. It does not go tacky like sheep-fat or turn to stone like coal-tar. I mix it with a little soot from the foundry-drifts in an iron bucket, and then I go out onto the line.

I do not walk the cable. I *slide*.

I have an iron saddle—a curved U of polished brass that fits over the top of the three-inch steel. I sit in a harness of greased leather, suspended below the saddle, and I use two short nickel-alloy gaffs to pull myself along. 

*Schhh-hup. Schhh-hup.*

The sound of my brass saddle on the wet steel is a long, low hiss that travels ahead of me for miles. In the middle of the gap, where the towers are nothing but dark smudges in the silver mist, the silence is immense. It is not empty; it is thick with the weight of the water. The clouds do not pass you; they brush against your cheeks like wet flannel, leaving tiny, cold beads in your eyelashes.

Sometimes, a block of basalt—no larger than a pig’s head—will pass me. It will be drifting upward, very slowly, rotating on its side. I can reach out with my foot and nudge it. It feels like pushing a wooden log through thick lard; it resists, then slews lazily away, entering a different current of the cold wool and disappearing into the gray.

***

And then, there are the quiet times.

Between the eighth and ninth whistles of the morning shift, the foundries damp their fires to clean the clinker-grates. The great *thump-thick* of the world stops. The smoke from the chimneys thins from oily black to a pale, sulfurous blue.

In that hour, the valley belongs to the listeners.

The Glass-Listeners in their low towers think they are the only ones who hear the spires grow. They sit with their copper tubes pressed to the cold obsidian, scratching their slates. But they only hear the bone-cracks—the sharp, high-pitched *tink* of the stone splitting when the tension is too high.

Down here, on the three-inch string, we hear the deep sound.

The cable is an instrument. It is three miles of steel stretched tight between two mountains that are being pushed upward by the fire at the bottom of the world. When the spires rise—even by the thickness of a fingernail—the cable sings. 

It is not a hum, and it is not a groan. It is a sound like a wet finger being dragged across the rim of a very glass-heavy goblet, but magnified until it fills the chest. It is so low that your ears do not register it as a tone; instead, the water-spheres on the wire begin to tremble. They cease to be round; they form tiny, sharp-edged stars that shiver in place, suspended by the vibration.

When that sound comes, the Soughs in my wicker nest stop their whistling. They draw their six wings tight against their oily bodies and press their flat heads together, silent and waiting.

I lie back in my harness, suspended in the gray gap between the iron and the cloud-floor. I hold the cable with my bare hands, feeling the heat of the friction as the wire stretches, just a fraction of an inch, under the pressure of the growing teeth below.

We are rising. The whole city—the foundries, the birdcages, the moss-gantries, and my little wicker basket—is being lifted, inch by inch, into the cold, empty top of the sky. We are leaving the earth behind, but we are doing it so slowly that no one has to run. We only have to hold on.
