I would not be on the level pavement of the floor, where the gray-green piston strikes with the weight of an iron boot. That is a place for the fast-movers and the hard-shelled, the things that can transition from drowning to dryness in the space of a heartbeat. 

Instead, you would find me in the **Seam**.

I am wedged eight feet up the vertical throat, precisely where the two giant sheets of unweathered basalt have slammed together. Where they meet, they form a near-perfect V-shaped cleft that narrows from six inches wide at its mouth to a hair-thin gap deep within the mountain’s dark meat. 

I am **The Shim**.

I am not an animal that was born, nor a plant that grew from a spore. I am a laminate, an accumulated body. Over two hundred years of the water-piston’s rhythmic surge, the mist has carried up the microscopic dross of the pocket: the shed pewter-scale plates of the slide-crusts, the acidic, sticky resin of the black-livered moss, the dissolved iron-carbonate, and the fine, gray-blue flour of basalt ground down by the mountain's shifting joints. 

All of this has settled into the V of the seam. Under the constant, immense pressure of the two stone sheets—and the wet, freezing draft that acts as an industrial loom—these materials have felted together. 

I am nine feet long, five inches wide, and I taper to an absolute, knife-like edge where I am pinched into the dark throat of the crack. I have the color of oxidized lead and the consistency of high-density vulcanized rubber. I am heavy, inert, and entirely structural. I do not have eyes, but I am mapped in pressure, vibration, and the cold chemistry of the water.

### The Eighty-Second Breath

My life is a succession of densities. 

**Second 0 to 10: The Pinch.**
It begins with the *thrum-shudder*. Deep in the valley’s belly, the glacial reservoir shifts. Before the water even appears, the great basalt sheets on either side of me respond to the changing hydraulic load. They groan. The V-gap narrows by a fraction of a millimeter. 

To a creature of soft tissue, this would be a death-sentence, but I am built for the squeeze. As the stone jaws tighten, my laminate layers of iron-paste and silica-shingles are pressed solid. The water trapped within my porous core is forced out of my front skin in a series of tiny, weeping bubbles. I feel the terrible, cold weight of the mountain leaning into me. I am the only thing keeping these two unweathered walls from grinding each other into powder. I am their cushion; I take the bite.

**Second 10 to 40: The Chill-Rise.**
Then comes the *slosh-hiss*. The gray-green water-piston surges up from the hexagonal joints below. It does not reach my height as a flood, but the air-blast it pushes ahead of itself is freezing and heavy with ozone. 

As the water hits the three-inch mark on the floor, the spray-mist rises in a dense, vertical column. The mist coats my exposed, lead-colored face. The water is thirty-three degrees Fahrenheit—so close to freezing that it makes my outer silica-crust contract. This contraction creates a series of microscopic, horizontal crinkles along my length. 

I drink the mist. I do not have a mouth, but my front surface is home to a specialized strain of the *pale-glow* bacteria. As they absorb the fresh iron-salts from the spray, they flare into a cold, blue-white line. In the dimness of the cleft, I am a vertical strip of light, a neon gauge marking the high-pressure draft. 

During these thirty seconds, the copper-colored *Equisetum* pipes below me are whistling. I do not hear them, but my body is a sounding board. The vibrations of the choke-borers—screwing themselves up and down inside the metallic tubes—travel through the stone and ripple across my back. A low, three-larvae drone feels like a slow, rhythmic kneading of my shoulders; a high-pitched hiss feels like a thousand tiny needles prickling my skin.

**Second 40 to 43: The Hold.**
The still-point. The water below is a perfect, pewter mirror. For three seconds, the air-blast stops. The chamber holds its breath. During these three seconds, I am perfectly balanced. The stone jaws do not tighten; the water does not move; the copper-pipes are silent. I am nothing but a line of cold, blue light in the dark.

**Second 44 to 80: The Siphon and the Guests.**
With a wet, gargling gasp, the siphon takes hold. The water is dragged back down into the black slots. The draft reverses, pulling the freezing air downward with a low, whistling sigh.

Now, the chamber becomes hostile. The air is dry-cold, and the wind-chill would desiccate anything unprotected. 

This is when my guests arrive.

From the draining floor, the **lard-strips** begin their upward slide. These bone-less, ribbon-thin salamanders cannot remain on the dry basalt; the draft would turn their mucus-skins into grease-spots. They find my vertical seam. Because I am slightly softer than the bare basalt, and because my back is coated in the sticky, protective resin of the moss, I am the perfect harbor.

They slide into the narrow space between my leaden sides and the stone wall. Five, six, sometimes ten of them will wedge themselves against me, layering like pale, cold ribbons of lard. They do not move. They do not hunt. They simply wait out the forty seconds of dryness.

Their presence is a dull, wet warmth. They are perhaps half a degree warmer than the stone, but to me, they feel like embers. Their bright orange, feathery copper-gills beat against my sides like the fluttering of moths. They leave a trail of thick, chemical slime over my surfaces. This is not a nuisance; it is my maintenance. Their grease protects my iron-carbonate plates from rusting into brittle flakes under the high-ozone air. In return, I hold them safe and damp, wedged tight in the V where the draft cannot reach them.

***

### The Cold Flow

I do not die, but I do not remain the same. 

I am subject to what materials-scientists call *cold flow*—the slow, permanent deformation of a solid under constant mechanical stress. Under the relentless eighty-second pinching of the basalt sheets, my body is being slowly, microscopically squeezed outward, out of the dark throat of the V and into the open air of the pocket.

It takes fifty years for an inch of my body to emerge from the seam. 

When my leading edge grows too far out into the chamber, it loses the support of the stone walls. It becomes cold, brittle, and heavy. Eventually, during a particularly violent *thrum-shudder*, the outer edge will crack off. 

It falls to the basalt floor below—a heavy, metallic tile that clatters against the pavement with a dull, leaden *thud*. The next rising tide will roll it; the slide-crusts will use it as a temporary shield; and within a year, the acidic juices of the moss-mats and the iron-eating bacteria will dissolve it back into the water, where it will be sucked down into the mountain's heart. 

But I am always being renewed. At the very back of the cleft, where the light can never reach, the high silt-feeders and the moss-resin are constantly trickling down into the pinch, adding new layers of felted iron and silica to my spine. 

I am the buffer of the machine. I have no thoughts of the sky, or of the valley above, or of the sun that never touches these walls. I know only the frequency of the rock, the weight of the water, the damp touch of the salamanders, and the slow, heavy crawl of my own leaden flesh as I am squeezed toward the light.
