I am the weight on the fourth step of the green stairs. 

To say *I am* is already to use too sharp a tool, to carve a thin, hard boundary where there is only a slow, dark thickening of the water. Let us say instead that there is a pressure, and within that pressure, there is a core of iron-heavy tallow that has not moved since the basalt last split.

I am not the water, though the water sits within me and around me, heavy as lard, pressing against my grease-skin with the weight of a lake that has no top. I am not the green shale, though my lower furrow has bitten into its corner so deeply, and for so long, that the carbonate crusts have grown over our seam like gray moss over a fallen log. 

I am two, but I am still mostly one. 

For forty-eight years, as the pale, hair-fine liverworts have crawled millimeter by millimeter across my back, a line of cold emptiness has been traveling through my center. It is not a wound; it is a silence. At first, it was only a slight thinning of the iron-shivers in my middle—as if the rumbles from the deep floor were finding two different paths through my flesh. Now, the cleft is deep enough that a single, blind thread of white moss has found its way into the fold. Its roots are drinking my grease. They are cold, sweet needles that prick my interior and find nothing there but the slow, heavy meat. 

I do not turn away from the roots. In this pocket, nothing is turned away. We are too crowded for distance. The white forest of the ceiling—the long, weeping ropes of root-moss—hangs down so low that its tips brush my upper skin with every dull thump of the basalt. The filaments are soft, like the hair of something that died before the stone was cold. They do not have eyes, and I do not have eyes, but we know the shape of the chamber by the way we crowd each other. We are a single, cold, breathing mass of grease and fiber, wedged into a tooth-hole in the mountain.

I remember the upper world, though I have never seen the light that is said to live there. 

Memory, for us, is a matter of salt. In my deepest core, where the iron-saturated proteins are dense as lead, there is a layer that is slightly coarser, slightly sweeter with lime. It is the register of the long climb down from the shelf where the green stone belongs. 

We went up because the basalt was quiet. Too quiet. When the basalt does not speak, the water here becomes thin and sour; the cold begins to creep inside our skins, and our jellies grow brittle, like frozen lard. We needed the shale to listen with. 

I remember the drag of the stone. I remember clamping my ventral cleft onto a corner of green shale—sharp then, before the lichens took it—and letting my upper body well forward, pouring myself like cold oil over the jagged basalt plates. The basalt bit into my gray sides, but my grease is thick and did not tear. It took eighty years to bring this single step down. Every few years, a tremor would shake the sinkhole, and we would stop, matching our weight to the stone, becoming part of the shelf until the shivering passed. Some of us did not make it down; they are still up there, wedged into the narrow chimneys, frozen into hard, gray graphite pegs that will never divide.

But I am here, on the fourth step. 

Underneath me, the green stone is cold, but it is a live cold. It is softer than the basalt wall, which is sharp as broken glass and has the dry, angry vibration of young stone. The green shale is old; it has been bedded in clay for an eternity, and it knows how to hold a note. 

The note is coming now. 

It does not begin as a sound. It begins as a change in my lower half. The iron-dust in my tail-cleft is turning, aligning itself like small needles toward the great iron root of the earth. The grease that surrounds me on the shale step feels suddenly thick, then thin, then thick again. 

A mile below our pocket, a plate of raw, unweathered stone is sliding. It is a very small shift—no wider than a grass-blade—but the weight above it is immense. 

The basalt walls groan. The sound is a low, thudding *click* that travels through the liquid silence like a heavy hand slapping a tub of lard. The water in the pocket does not splash; it cannot, for there is no air for it to displace. Instead, the pressure leaps. 

The green shale under my belly begins to sing. 

It is a long, slow oscillation, so deep that if a man were here, his heart would stop its red hopping and try to follow the rhythm of the stone. To me, it is the only food that matters. The vibration passes through the carbonate cement, through my ventral furrow, and straight into the gelatinous marble of my core. 

My iron-salt stirs. The crystals rub against one another, generating a tiny, warm tide of sulfur-currents within my gray meat. The cold of the water—that near-freezing bite that has been pressing against my skin for decades—withdraws a fraction of an inch. Inside my skin, I am raw, dark heat.

The vibration reaches the cleft in my middle where the white liverwort root has settled. 

The note is too strong for the root. It is too heavy for the thin, blind weed that does not know the earth’s deep voice. The filament of the moss shivers, its cell-walls snapping with a sound I cannot hear but can feel as a tiny, electrical prickle against my bare flesh. 

My left side reacts to the shiver. My right side reacts to the shale. 

For forty-eight years we have been one, but the note of the green stone is a shearing note. It finds the seam. 

Slowly—with the speed of a glacier rounding a black horn of rock—the two halves of my body begin to slide in opposite directions. There is no pain. There is only a grease-slide, a heavy, silent parting of tallow from tallow. The white liverwort thread that had stitched us together is pinched between us, its pale body crushed into a fine, sweet starch that we both drink through our pores as we separate.

We do not move apart. How could we? There is no room on the fourth step. The moss-curtain presses us from above; the third step holds us from below; the black wall of the basalt pocket is only a knuckle-width away. 

We simply sit, side by side, two dark, graphite-colored drops of grease where there was once one. 

The planetary groan is fading now. The vibration in the green shale is dropping from a clear, iron ring to a dull, wooden thrum. Soon, the basalt will be silent again, and the water will go back to its heavy, oily freeze. 

My new neighbor—who was my left side when the shale began to sing—is already settling. Its skin is thick and graphite-bright in the faint, violet glow that clings to the basalt points. It has no head to turn toward me, and I have no head to turn toward it, but we are touching along our entire length. Our mucus-shields have already run together, forming a single, protective dome of grease that will keep the sharp stone from cutting us while we sleep.

The liverworts will begin to grow over our seam again tomorrow. It will take them twenty years to cover the split. 

By then, the earth will have groaned again, and we will be ready to listen.
