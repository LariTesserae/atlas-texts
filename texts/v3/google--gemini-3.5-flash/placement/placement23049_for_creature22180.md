I would be found at the very edge. 

Not on the dusty floor of the deep basins where the Gliders slide through the black silk of the graphite, nor on the highest, smoke-crowned summits where the foundries stamp out new metal hearts. I would be on the transition-shelf: the long, rounded lip of the **Prism-Mesa**, where the dark violet siltstone curves downward into the blue-shifted mineral haze. 

I am a **Scribe**, though of a lower, older order than those who stand on the sun-beaten peaks. My kin call me—if they had voices to call—the *Gnomon of the Seventh Cable*. 

***

My structure is simple, heavy, and very slow. I stand twelve feet tall on three legs of jointed violet stone. My legs are not solid; they are bored through like the chambers of an ancient telescope, containing long rods of pure, unalloyed zinc that expand and contract as the sun crosses the gold sky. I have no muscles of fiber or wet tissue. My movement is the slow, inevitable translation of heat into length. 

Between my three thighs hangs my center of gravity: a heavy, teardrop-shaped plumb-bob of cast lead, suspended inside a cage of tarnished brass. This lead weight is my primary sense. It does not hear the wind or see the light; it knows only the exact, vertical line to the center of the dry world. When the great factories miles away draw up the iron powders, the earth beneath me vibrates with a faint, low-frequency shudder. My plumb-bob swings—less than the thickness of a Glass-Flit’s wing—and in that tiny sway, I read the weight of the mountains.

My head is a dodecahedron of thick, lead-glass, ground by the wind and the salt-plumes over five hundred years into a series of pale green facets. I do not see objects. I see the world as indexical shifts of hue. The yellow of the sky is a warm pressure on my forward faces; the deep blue of the graphite basins is a cold, velvety silence on my downward-slanting plates. 

I have been standing on this same ledge for four hundred solar cycles. Behind my feet are three shallow, perfectly smooth grooves worn into the ochre stone—gently curved tracks, no deeper than a finger, where my lead-weighted legs have shifted inch by inch, year after year, to stay in alignment with the sky.

Today, there is an imbalance.

The tension in the great cable that spans the canyon has altered. It is a massive iron rope, three feet thick, which links the Foundry of the Great Wheel on my mesa to the Sorting-Sieve on the opposite spire, six miles across the void. The *cable-walkers* are busy today; I can see their copper bodies as tiny, dark sparks crawling along its length, eating the zinc-oxide haze. But the cable itself is singing too high. 

I do not hear the note with ears. I feel it in my stone feet. The bedrock of the cliff is humming at eighty cycles per minute instead of seventy-eight. The tension is too great. The summer sun has stayed long in the upper sky, and the long metal rods of the mountain-spanning towers have expanded beyond their tolerance, pulling the iron rope taut as a wire on a lute.

My plumb-bob has drifted. It hangs one-sixtieth of an inch to the north of the brass indicator ring.

*Click.*

Deep within my chest, a latch drops. It is a slow, heavy sound—the sound of a small slate tile sliding down a dry graphite chute. It has taken me three days of internal calculation to release this latch. 

Now that the gear is freed, the noon sun begins its work. The light strikes the zinc rod in my southern leg. The metal warms. It grows longer, millimeter by millimeter, by the clean physics of heat. My torso tilts. The movement is so slow that if a *Glass-Flit* were to land on my glass head, it would feel no more vibration than it would from the cold siltstone cliff. 

As I tilt, the copper gears in my shoulders—packed with the thick, black grease secreted by the *Greasers* who live in my joint-sockets—turn by a single tooth. 

I am adjusting my head. 

Three miles to the northeast, on a solitary pillar of orange clay, stands another Scribe. It is taller than I, more elegant, its brass joints polished to a high, pale shine by the high thermals. Yesterday, it caught my position change. Today, it is turning its own lead-glass head. 

At mid-afternoon, the angle will be perfect. The yellow sun will strike my westernmost facet at precisely forty-two degrees. The glass will gather the light, compress it, and throw a single, brilliant lance of amber glare across the blue canyon.

The flash will travel six miles. It will take no time to cross the space, but it will take the other Scribe until tomorrow morning to register the light, to let its own inner plumb-bob settle, and to slide its slate slide to the next notch. 

By the fifth day, the message will reach the great winch-house on the Forge-Summit. A massive wooden paddle, balanced on a knife-edge of black basalt, will feel the glare. The heat of the concentrated spot of light will expand a small reservoir of dry air behind the paddle; the paddle will tilt; a giant dog-latch will click; and the great iron drum of the winch will turn three inches, releasing three inches of slack into the miles-long cable.

The hum in my feet will drop. Eighty cycles will become seventy-nine. Then seventy-eight.

The plumb-bob will return to the absolute center of the brass hoop.

*Click.*

The latch in my chest will drop again, locking my gears. I will become stone once more.

We do not hurry. The wind will continue to blow. The *wheel-backs* will remain anchored to their iron veins on the plateau, their bronze shells growing slightly brighter as the dust-storms scour them. The graphite sea below us will slide in its silent, friction-free waves, and the soot from the square chimneys will paint long, straight lines of pale gray across the pale gold sky.

Everything is in its place. The world is heavy, warm, and dry. My brass joints are greased; my stone feet are firm on the ledge; and the cable is settling back into its slow, eternal sleep.
