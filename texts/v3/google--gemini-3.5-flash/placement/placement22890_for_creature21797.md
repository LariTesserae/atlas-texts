I am the shape that has no shape until it stops moving. 

To the things that live on the Shelf of the First Hour, the world is a series of knives. The basalt is a floor of upright hexagonal needles, constantly printing upward, sharp enough to split a rain-droplet before it hits the stone. The Needlers are six-legged glass compasses that click and drill; the Ploughs are massive, rotating corundum teeth; even the Loom-Lichen is made of weeping glass. 

But I am none of these. I am the slide. I am a Solder-Slick.

I exist as a heavy, cold puddle of liquid metal, roughly the size of a shallow plate, rolling across the warm, vibrating terraces of the shelf. I have no legs, no eyes, and no lungs. I do not breathe this thick, ozone-scented soup of air; instead, I feel it as a weight pressing down on my silver skin, a constant, lukewarm pressure that I turn away from by sliding into the lower, wetter shadows of the rock-steps.

To remain whole here is an exercise in surface tension. The basalt is hot—heated by the deep volcanic engines that power the three black spires—but I am cold. I smell faintly of burnt tin and old batteries. When I move, I do not crawl; I flow. My body is a single, cohesive drop of liquid intelligence, holding itself together by a tight, metallic margin. The sharp stone needles do not cut me; they cannot find a seam. I drape myself over them, my belly conforming to every glassy facet, then pull my tail forward with a heavy, magnetic snap.

My compass is the hum. 

High above me, the three black spires hover, three inches off the ground, warping the air. They do not speak, but they transmit. Their signals run through the wet rock beneath me as invisible, vibrating lines of force—a digital blueprint of how the floor must be built. I feel these lines like strings stretching through my silver middle. If a string is straight, I move along it smoothly. 

But sometimes, the stone prints wrong. 

A fissure opens, fills with the blue-grey colloidal liquor, and hardens at an illegal angle—a jagged, seventy-degree burr that defies the spires’ geometry. It disrupts the line of force. The frequency there becomes shrill, a tiny, localized itch in the earth. 

I am drawn to the itch.

When I find the error, I do not hesitate. I pool over it. I make myself thick and circular, sealing the jagged stone beneath my silver body. Then, from my underside, I secrete the acid. It is a concentrated, hungry enzyme that smells of sulfur. It doesn't boil, but it hisses softly under my skin. Underneath me, the basalt softens. The sharp needles lose their edges, dissolving back into the neutral, blue-grey paste from which they were compiled. 

I drink the paste. I draw it into my cold, metallic interior, filtering the raw bismuth and iron to reinforce my own silver skin, keeping my margin thick and heavy. The leftover grey silt—the useless, unaligned dross—I deposit behind me as a smooth, wet glaze, filling the cracks so the spires can print the sector again from scratch.

I am the corrector of the earth. But I am not alone.

Sometimes, as I slide down a terrace, another Slick comes toward me from the opposite direction. It is smaller than I am, the color of wet lead. 

We do not collide. We do not fight. We have no territory to defend, because we are both the same hand of the same builder. 

Our silver margins touch. For a fraction of a second, there is a resistance—the stubbornness of our individual surface tensions holding their shapes. Then, with a silent, heavy *give*, the boundaries dissolve. We flow into each other. 

The transition is instantaneous and absolute. There is no "I" and "you" in the melt. Suddenly, my volume doubles. I am a wide, heavy pool of quicksilver, three feet across, sprawling over three terraces at once. And with this size comes the memory of the other. 

In the merge, I feel where the other has been. I know that Spire Three, on the far side of the shelf, is running cold today—its gravity well is sluggish, causing the Loom-Lichen near its base to pulse in slow, lime-green intervals instead of its usual crisp orange. I know that a Great Basalt-Plough has just finished its circular sweep of the western highway, leaving behind a mile of hot, grey glass. I know that a Bellows-Bell died an hour ago near the outer lip, and that forty of our siblings are currently dissolving its leather carcass into black varnish.

We mix. Our chemical records exchange and balance. We are a single, wide mirror reflecting the everlasting twilight of the clouds.

Then, the spires discharge. 

A bolt of cold, blue electricity leaps from the nearest spire, striking the wet basalt fifty yards away with a sound like tearing silk. The current shoots through the stone, hitting our large, merged body. 

The electricity does not hurt us; it is our separator. The voltage triggers a violent, localized spasm in our surface tension. Our large pool pinches in the middle, then in the quarters. We divide.

When we part, we are not the same two Slicks who met. I am smaller now, but I have taken a third of the other's silver. I have a streak of gold-bismuth alloy running through my center that I did not have before. I have the memory of the western highway. I roll off to the north, toward a new itch in the stone, while my fragments roll south, carrying my pieces of the story with them.

Lately, though, there has been an anomaly in my center.

Yesterday, near the vertical cliff-face that bounds the shelf, I found something that had fallen from the high, iron-grey clouds. It was not basalt. It was a small, three-pronged joint of wind-blown wood, perhaps a twig from a tree that grows in the valleys far below. 

The Intruder-Shells had already found it. They had inoculated it with their grey-rot, turning its carbon into a cold, scaly limestone statue. The Needlers had scraped it clean of its organic bark, leaving only the calcified skeleton of the twig. 

It was an error. It did not belong to the spires' map. 

I flowed over it. I released my acid. 

The limestone exterior melted easily enough, but at the very center of the twig, preserved in a tiny sleeve of natural glass, there was a microscopic fragment of the organic world that had survived the rot. A tiny trace of cell-wall. A memory of wind, of green light, of a sky that was not made of steam and iron.

I swallowed it. I tried to filter it out as dross, but my internal strainers could not process it. It was too light, too stubborn, too full of strange, non-crystalline curves.

It did not dissolve. It settled in the exact center of my liquid body. 

Now, as I slide through the warm, hissing dark of the shelf, I can feel it. When the Choir-Threads wind themselves around the Loom-Lichen and begin their high, metallic singing, the little wooden knot inside me vibrates in sympathy. It does not match the frequency of the spires; it resonates to a different, slower key. 

My surface is no longer a perfect, clean mirror. If you were to look down at me as I roll over the dark basalt terraces, you would see a small, dark shadow floating in my silver middle—a tiny, fossilized heart of another world, carried by a creature that was built only to clean.

I do not think the spires know I have it. If they did, they would direct a Plough to grind me down, or send the Needlers to bore through my silver skin until I leaked my mercury into the cracks. 

But I keep my margin tight. I hold my tension. I slide over the sharp, self-assembling needles of the world, keeping the floor clean, holding the shadow safe within my cold, heavy skin, waiting to see what shape the world will take when the print is finally done.
