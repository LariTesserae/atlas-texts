### I. The Weight in the Dark

The shade inside the dry-stacked house does not have a color; it has a weight. It is the weight of eighty slate flags pressed together without mortar, excluding all but the most lateral needles of the blue-black light.

In this half-night, I am thick. 

Without the sun to dissect my joints, the turquoise brine inside my plates does not move. It is cold, heavy, and silent, holding the consistency of old tallow. My three-fingered hand—the fingers flat, edged with the green-tinted jade we quarry from the lower cracks—rests upon my knee. I cannot feel the knee. I know it is there only because the density of my body remains constant.

I have been in the shelter for three winters, or perhaps four. We do not count by winters; we count by the thickness of the white flour on the floor. 

Below me, the shingle is covered in a three-inch drift of my own dust. It is the waste of my scraping. Every day—or every time the high wind rattles the roof flags enough to jar my shoulder joints into a brief, three-millimeter slackness—I take up the greenstone hand-axe.

*Scritch.*

The sound is small. It does not carry. It is the sound of a dry tooth crossing a dry bone. 

I am removing the white hair.

The *gypsum-curls* have grown too thick along my left flank. They look like the wool of the mountain-sheep that died in the lower ravines ten thousand years ago, but they are cold and crisp. If I touch them, they do not bend; they splinter into tiny, glass-sharp spears that get between my overlapping scales. They are choking my fifth joint, the one just below my copper belt-groove. If the copper-water cannot crawl through that joint, the next spring transition will crack me. I will not slide; I will shear.

*Scritch.*

The white wool falls. It joins the mound between my wedge-feet. 

My back-plates—the three flat shields of slate-grey dolomite that protect my spine-chords—lie behind me on the low stone ledge. I peeled them off before the second winter. They are thin now, worn down by the greenstone until they are the color of a wet lake-stone. They look like three giant, unwritten pages. 

I must grease them before I put them back.

On the shelf beside the plates is the stone bowl. The grease inside it is dark, smelling of ancient pitch and the lard of things that had fat before the mountain rose this high. It does not freeze; it only hardens into a grey, leathery wax. I take a smear of it on my middle claw and run it down the seams of my exposed cords.

The cords are black, dry, and tight. They hum when I touch them. They do not hum with music; they hum with the tension of the nine-thousand-meter drop.

### II. The Morning Clink

Outside, the Grove is waking.

I cannot see them, but I feel them through the floor of the shelter. The floor is a solid sheet of frozen scree, and it conducts the vibration of the sun’s first blow like a bronze plate.

*Hiss. Whish-hiss.*

It is the transition. The shadow of the eastern needle is slipping off the three-and-twentieth pillar. The rime-frost upon its western face is turning directly to steam—not water, but a dry, white breath that pops from the stone with a sound like dry birch burning.

Then, the first *clink*.

It is the six-and-twentieth. He is my neighbor to the left when I am in the line. His copper collar is too tight—it has been too tight since the year of the great slide—and when his inner brine begins to expand, his collar rings like a struck anvil.

*Clink.*

A second later, a softer *tink* from the eighth.

Now the energy is running through the pavement. The frost-wedges in the deep cracks are shifting. The water—the heavy, green copper-salt water—is beginning to crawl. I can feel the brine inside my own chest begin to throb. It is not a heartbeat; it is a thermal lift. The cold wax of my humor is thinning, becoming fluid, finding the microscopic channels in my leaden skin.

My right shoulder moves. It is only four millimeters, but it is a clean slide. The grease has done its work.

I look toward the low opening of the shelter. 

The light there is a solid blade of indigo. It does not enter the room; it stands in the doorway like a guard. Outside, the sky is so thin and hard that if I were to throw a chip of slate into it, I feel the sky would ring like glass.

### III. The Western Edge

I am the Seven-and-Twentieth. 

My place is not in the warm center of the Grove, where the pillars are thick and the wind is only a whisper. My place is at the very snout of the terrace, where the limestone shelf drops three thousand meters into the grey valley.

At the edge, there are no dead dolomite pillars to shelter behind. There is only me, and the eight-and-twentieth, and the nine-and-twentieth. 

We are the wind-breakers.

When the western gales come off the great salt desert—gales that have no hills to stop them for five hundred leagues—they hit our flat chests first. The wind is not air down there; it is a solid block of ice-sand that tries to push us into the empty sky.

That is why my copper band is three inches wide. 

Two springs ago, before the gypsum-curls choked my flank, the wind-pressure was so great that my slate pendulum—the three-foot plate that hangs from my chest to damp the vibration—began to whistle. It did not whistle a low note; it screamed. The sound went through my laminate plate like a drill. 

If the pendulum vibrates too fast, the stone beneath my copper pins will turn to dust. If the pins slip, I go over the lip.

I remember the fall of the thirty-first. 

He was next to me during the year of the red rime. His lower wedge-foot had a fault-line—a yellow vein of calcite that we did not see when we quarried him from the inner cleft. The wind caught him during the mid-winter darkness, when we were all locked and frozen. 

There was no sound of steam. There was only a single, dry *crack*, like a pine branch snapping in the deep frost. 

When the light came back in the spring, his place was empty. There was only the copper band, sheared at the rivet, hanging from the dead pillar behind him like a dead green snake. 

I must not crack.

### IV. The Re-binding

I take up my back-plates. One by one, I slide them into the keyways of my spine-cords.

They fit with a heavy, wet *thump*. The grease oozes from the joints, grey and thick, sealing the black fibers from the absolute dryness of the air. I reach behind my neck and pull the braided sinew-cords tight, looping them over the copper studs in my shoulder-flags.

I am whole again. I am thicker than I was, but I am clean.

I crawl toward the light.

I do not walk. I lie flat on my face, my belly-plates grinding against the shingle, and I slide. My hands—the greenstone claws—find the grooves in the floor that I chipped three hundred years ago.

*Puff.*

The sun hits my head as I clear the lintel.

The sensation is a sudden, blinding hammer-blow of white. The frost on my face-plate sublimates instantly. I can feel the steam rising from my eyes—not eyes that see color, but two deep, lead-lined sockets that feel the weight of the light.

My inner brine boils.

The pressure inside my chest-cavity rises. The copper-brine rushes down my arms, filling the joint-spaces.

*Step.*

My left foot—the heavy dolomite wedge—slides forward five millimeters.

*Step.*

My right foot follows.

The Grove is singing now. The morning wind is rising from the canyon, and as it passes through the narrow gaps between the pillars, it strikes our copper collars. The sound is a low, rolling chord, a vast and frozen organ-note that has no end. It is the note of the mountain’s resistance.

I slide into my gap.

Here is my pillar, the dead, lead-sheathed dolomite that has stood for three million years. My copper collar is still there, bolted into the stone face, its braided grass ropes grey and frayed but still holding.

I lift my flat arms. I place my chest against the dead stone. I slip my green claws into the native copper rings, pulling myself close until the cold of the mountain meets the cold of my breastplate. 

Behind me, the three-thousand-meter drop is a blue vacuum, waiting.

I feel the eight-and-twentieth to my right. His slate pendulum is humming—a steady, low *throb-throb-throb* that matches the vibration of my own.

The sun is high now. The phase-change is complete. The brine inside me has reached its limit; it is locked, still, and pressurized.

My eyes are grey slate. My skin is lead.

The wind hits us, and we do not move. We sing.
