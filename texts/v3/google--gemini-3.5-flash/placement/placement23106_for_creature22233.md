First, there is the lard. 

In the deep oil-seams, there is no up or down, no sharp or soft. There is only the thick, sulfurous heat of the planet’s belly, a black soup of paraffin and mineral grease where I flow without margins. I am a bladder of white fat, floatable and blind, drifting with millions of others in the subterranean dark. We do not have thoughts; we have pressure. We do not have lungs; we absorb the warmth of the tectonic mantle directly through our slick, skinless membranes. We are the raw clay of the engine, waiting for the crucible.

But the earth is always contracting, always squeezing its wet, dark secrets upward. 

I feel the change when the brine-flows begin to run cold. The pressure behind me rises—a slow, hydraulic fist that pushes me out of the warm pools and into a narrow basalt capillary. The passage is tight; my soft body is squeezed and elongated, dragged through teeth of unweathered silica that slice my skin but do not make me bleed, for I am too thick, too fatty to run. 

And then, the eruption. 

It is not a violent blast, but a long, high-pressure extrusion. I am forced through a biological chimney, rising through the dark until the world suddenly splits open.

The sky does not meet me as air; it meets me as a hammer of cold so absolute that my outer lard instantly solidifies into a crust of horn. At the same moment, the sun strikes. It is not light; it is a magnesium needle that pierces through my translucent outer layers, cooking the chemicals in my core. 

The cold and the light are the sculptors here. They decide what I am. 

Had I stayed in the lower capillaries, the sulfur would have crust-baked me into the thick, iron-sulfide dome of a Colossus. Had I clung to the silica walls, the Loomers would have woven my fibers into the pale, lard-coated tubes of the canopy. 

But I am swept higher. I am cast up onto the great, trembling mattresses of the cable-vegetation, eighty feet above the iron floor. 

The cold pulls at me, stretching my gelatinous mass into long, vertical lines. My copper-rich nerves, stimulated by the harsh indigo sky, begin to fire in long, rhythmic loops. I grow three legs. They do not grow outward like limbs, but downward like mineral stalactites, seeking the earth. They are hollow columns of jointed bone, and within them, the warm, subterranean oil I brought from the deep is sealed, becoming the lubricant that keeps my joints from seizing in the sub-zero wind.

I do not have eyes. I do not have ears. 

And yet, I am awake. 

I find myself suspended. My three stilt-legs have found their footing. 

My first leg rests on a spire of pure, freshly shattered obsidian. It is so sharp that it bites deep into the sole-pad of my foot, but the cold instantly freezes the wound, sealing the joint in a plug of blood-ice. Through this leg, I feel the high-frequency vibration of the Silex-Loomers. They are crawling over the rock below me, their diamond drills clicking like a million needles as they spin the glass cables that keep my world from collapsing.

My second leg is wedged into the narrow, greasy canyon between two cathedral-sized Colossi. Through it, I feel the massive, twelve-second pulse of their breathing. Every dozen seconds hours, their iron plates grind together with a sound like a ship’s hull breaking on ice, and a blast of superheated steam washes over my lower knee-joint, melting the white frost that has gathered there and keeping my synovial tallow fluid.

My third leg is anchored on a ledge of frozen rime, cold and silent.

But my head—my head is where I live. 

I do not lift it. I cannot. It hangs from the center of my tripod body like a thirty-ton plumb-bob of dense, lead-heavy bone. It has no neck; it is simply a great, swinging sphere, suspended on thick ropes of braided muscle that run down from my hips.

I am a Gallows-Walker. I am the balance of the valley.

With my head hanging low, I do not see the sky, but I feel the earth. My entire consciousness is a map of gravity. The heavy, leaden sphere of my head sways slowly between my legs, acting as a pendulum. If the wind from the steam vents blows too hard from the north, my head swings south; the copper nerves in my northern leg tighten instantly, pulling my body back into alignment. If the cable-mattress beneath me begins to fail under the weight of the frost, my pendulum drops, and I feel the tilt of the world in my marrow. 

I do not think in words. I think in *poise*. 

The world below me is an endless, grinding chess-board of iron and ice. It is too packed, too loud, too heavy. But up here, in the thin indigo gauze, I am the only thing that stands straight.

The hunger begins. It is not a craving for meat, but an internal vacuum—a drop in my hydraulic pressure. 

I must perform the work.

Slowly, with an effort that takes a quarter of a sun-cycle, I begin my step. 

First, I must shift my weight. My leaden head sways toward my left leg. The muscle-ropes groan; the yellow lard-glands at my hip weep a slow, amber grease that drips onto the cable-canopy below, sizzling as it hits the frosty glass. My right leg, now freed from the vertical gravity, rises. 

It rises with an agonizing, high-pressure hiss of steam escaping my joint-valves. The leg hovers in the blinding glare, sixty feet in the air, a massive column of fibrous black muscle and iron-shod horn, dripping with black-tar lubricant. 

Below me, the Rivet-Mites stop their scraping. They feel the shift in my weight through the iron domes; they wait for the thud.

The leg descends. It takes twenty minutes to reach the next peak. When it strikes the obsidian spire, the impact is a ground-shaking *thud* that takes minutes to reverberate through the packed masses of Colossi below. The glass peak cracks, but my leg holds. The triangle is re-established. I am balanced once more.

Now, I feed.

From the underside of my swinging, lead-heavy head, I drop three long, copper-sheathed tubes. They are my proboscises, flexible and heavy, falling through the gaps in the lard-white cable-vegetation like anchors. 

They find the steam chimneys of the Colossi. 

I do not hurt them. We are parts of the same beast. I slip my tubes into their hot, bone-lined chimneys. 

Immediately, the raw, superheated volcanic brine is drawn up into my own reservoirs, hissing through my copper-veined legs. It warms my bones; it melts the frost on my spine; it replenishes the pressure in my hydraulic muscles. 

In return, my lead-head pumps down a cold, heavy secretion—a mineral syrup of antimony and lead that I carried up from the deep oil-seams. The syrup flows into the boilers of the Colossi, slowing their digestion, cooling their biological fires just enough to prevent them from reaching critical pressure. Without me, they would boil themselves to pieces, tearing the glass cliffs apart in a rain of iron and steam. With me, they are regulated. They breathe in their twelve-second cycles, and we sleep.

I hang here, between the black glass and the white rime. 

The sun is flat and absolute, reflecting off my iron joints. The steam vents below me hiss and whistle, launching the Mica-Drifters into the indigo sky like black kites. 

I am a component in a clock that has no hands, only weight. I will stand here for a century. I will walk three miles before my joints rust. And when I finally fail—when a leg fractures under the weight of the frost or my lead-head swings too far and cannot return—I will fall into the canopy. 

The Silex-Loomers will weave their glass tubes through my hollow bones. The Rivet-Mites will clean the yellow grease from my joints with their spinning jaws. And my heavy, leaden head will sink down, down through the cables, down past the iron domes, back into the warm, black oil of the nursery, where I will melt, and wait to rise again.
