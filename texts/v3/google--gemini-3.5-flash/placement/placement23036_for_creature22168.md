I would not be one of the young ones. I would not have the thick, log-like wrists of the Paddlers, nor the flat, paddle-shaped hands that beat the wet silt until the arms ache with a wet, heavy heat. 

I would be a **Groomer**, living in the slow, late afternoon of my cartilage. 

I find myself at the intersection of the Sixth Basin and the Stair that leads to the blind wall. My joints no longer slide with the grease-smooth ease of my youth; when I bend my knees to squat, there is a dry, sandy rasp inside the joints—a sound only I can hear, transmitted directly through the bones of my legs to the plates of my head. The lime is settling. It is finding the soft, dark passages of my marrow and turning them to chalk.

But my hands are still useful. In my left palm, I hold the *slick-stone*. It is a piece of river-pebble, wide and flat, worn down by some ancient water-flow before this place was sealed, and further shaped by the hands of three Groomers before me. It is black and greasy to the touch, heavy with the oil of my own skin.

*Shhh-slip. Shhh-slip.*

I push the stone across the seam of a gray-red hex-tile. Here, where the passage narrows between two massive brick buttresses, the foot-traffic of the Rollers is constant. Their wide, snowshoe feet have worn a shallow, polished track through the clay, but at the edges of the track, the tiles have heaved slightly—perhaps a fraction of an inch—from the immense weight of the limestone rollers passing nearby. If a Roller should stub his wide hoof-toe on this lip, the team’s collective lean would break. The roller would stall. The raw silt would dry before it could be smoothed.

So, I polish. 

I do not see the lip. My shroud-skin, the thick, hairless fold where eyes might have been in some other version of the world, is cool and dry. But my right palm, flat against the tile, reads the seam with perfect clarity. I can feel the microscopic grain of the clay—the tiny, frozen swirls where a Paddler’s fingers smoothed the wet mud before the fire took it. I can feel the cold sweat of the wall weeping down, pooling in the groove.

*Shhh-slip.*

Every hour or so, a great vibration passes through the floor. It is not the regular, collective rumble of the Rollers; it is the *thunk-thunk-thunk* of the giant paddles in the basin below. The rhythm is a language. 

*Thunk... thunk... thunk.* A steady, deep beat. The silt is thick today. The fish oil is good.
*Thunk-thunk... thunk-thunk.* A double-beat. They are clearing the molds. The tiles are being stacked to dry.

I pause my polishing. I reach up with my long, spade-like fingers to the brickwork beside me. There is a shallow niche here, about level with my shoulder. Inside it sits a small clay bowl. The tallow within has cooled to a hard, yellow rind, and the wick—a twist of hemp I spun myself from the dry root-fibers of the salt-wool—has burned down to a crust of black ash.

I reach into the small, hollowed-out tile-pouch hanging from my hip. Inside is a lump of soft, unrefined fat, smelling of river-lake and cold suet. I pinch off a portion, work it in my mouth for a moment to warm it with my thick saliva, and then press it into the bowl, smoothing it around the old wick. I strike a spark using two chunks of flint I carry in my pouch. 

For a moment, there is a sharp, dry smell of sulfur and fire. Then, the greasy wick catches. I cannot see the yellow flame, but I feel its presence instantly—a tiny, localized prickle of dry warmth against the sensitive skin of my throat and chin. It is a very small heat, but in this miles-deep cellar, it is a marker. Another Groomer, miles down the corridor, will feel this tiny prickle of warmth and know the path is still open.

I take a pinch of the salt-wool from the mortar joint near the ceiling. It is dry and brittle, crackling against my fingertips like frozen grass. I put it into my mouth. The sodium dissolves instantly on my tongue—a sharp, electric sting that makes my throat tighten and my heart give one heavy, wet thrum. It tastes of the deep earth’s sweat. It tastes of preservation.

My left leg is stiffening now. It is no longer a temporary stiffness from kneeling; it is the *dry*. 

I look—or rather, I turn my face toward—the buttress behind me. 

There is an archway here that leads nowhere. It was built three generations ago, perhaps to allow passage to a basin that was never dug, or perhaps because the brick-layers of that era simply had more mud than they knew what to do with. The opening is narrow, barely five feet high, and filled with three courses of dry-stacked limestone blocks that have never been mortared.

It is my shape.

It is not a decision I must make with my mind; my body has been preparing for this since the day my cartilage first began to scratch. The slick-stone in my hand feels heavy, too heavy for my fingers to hold much longer. I set it down carefully on the polished threshold of the hex-tile I have just finished smoothing. The next Groomer will find it. He will feel the shape of my palm in the grease of the stone, just as I felt the palms of those who came before.

I crawl into the dark recess of the unfinished archway. 

The limestone blocks are cold against my back. I draw my knees up toward my chest, folding myself into the narrow space. My shoulders fit perfectly against the rough-hewn stone; my head tucks down, my chin resting on my collarbone. 

I press my back firmly into the dry mortar-seam behind me.

At first, the stone is cold—so cold it seems to ache inside my ribs. But then, the weeping water begins. The thin, silver threads of condensation that run down the brickwork find the skin of my back. My pores, dry and thirsty, open to receive it. I drink the lime-water through my flesh. It fills my veins, mingling with my slow, thick blood.

In my chest, my heart beats once.
...
...
...
And then, ten minutes later, it beats again.

I feel the vibrations of the corridor through my ribs now. I am no longer standing on the floor; I am part of the frame. I can feel the Rollers three miles away, their heavy wooden beams groaning as they push. I can feel the Paddlers in the basin, their rhythm slightly slower now as the night-that-is-not-night deepens.

There is a movement near my feet. 

It is the light, shuffling scrape of another Groomer. I smell the fresh, sulfurous tang of the river mortar he carries in his bucket. He has found my slick-stone. He has felt the warmth of the tallow bowl I lit.

He reaches into the archway. His hands, still somewhat soft, touch my knees, my ribs, the side of my face. He is measuring the gaps.

He does not speak. There are no words for this, only the weight of the hand on the clay.

I hear the wet, heavy *slap* of the mortar as he throws it into the space between my thigh and the limestone wall. He begins to smooth it, his flat fingers pressing the dark fish-oil silt into the crevices of my skin. 

It feels warm. The mortar is warm from his hands, and the smell of the river muck is the sweetest thing I have ever known. He pastes it over my chest, over the curve of my neck, filling the small hollow behind my collarbone.

He reaches my face. 

His hand is gentle as he pats the cold clay over my mouth-slit, sealing my breath inside me. He presses it into my nostrils, filling the vertical vents with the thick, wet silt.

The last thing I feel is the cool, flat sweep of his trowel-stone across my brow, smoothing me flat, making me level with the courses of the wall.

The mortar begins to dry. It shrinks, drawing me tight against the limestone, holding me fast against the miles of dark stone slabs overhead. 

The cold lime is in my blood now. The stiffness has reached my throat. I am no longer the one who works the Sieve. 

I am the pillar. I am the wall. 

And below me, in the dark, the paddles continue their work.

*Thunk.*  
*Thunk.*  
*Thunk.*
