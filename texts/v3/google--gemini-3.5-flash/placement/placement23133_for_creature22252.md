I am down in the third seam, where the light is the color of grease on a wet knife-blade. 

To find me, you must descend past the topmost platforms where the Sky-goers keep their dry grass and their noisy, wind-bitten tools. You must go past the middle tiers where the weavers sit shoulder-to-shoulder, their drop-spindles making a dry *whir-whir-whir* like the wings of locusts in a basket. You must go below the level where the water-dripping is a steady rain, down to the cold, black knuckles of the Rift where the basalt columns do not split in straight lines but curve inward, like the ribs of an old boat that has been buried in the mud for a hundred years.

Here, the gap between the east and west walls is only nine feet wide. 

I am hanging in a harness of four-ply hemp. It is not the thin, supple cord they use for the children’s tethers, but the thick, tarred "stay-line" that has been boiled in tallow to keep the rock-water from rotting the core. It cuts into my groin and under my shoulder-blades, but I do not feel it anymore. The skin there has turned to yellow hide, thick as the leather they use for the bellows of the iron-shop.

My name is *Sull*. It is a small, blunt sound, the kind of sound a small stone makes when it drops into three feet of black moss.

I am a *Slip-fitter*.

In the Rift, if you put wood directly against the basalt, the rock will eat it. When the earth *thrums*—that deep, belly-groan that comes every three or four days—the stone columns do not just move up and down; they twist. They have six sides, and when they twist, those sharp hexagonal corners act like teeth. They will chew through a green spruce upright in three seasons, turning the heartwood to sour gray sawdust.

My business is to go between.

At my hip hangs a bucket of "slip-scum." It is a wet, heavy paste we mix by hand in the dark. We use the fat of the clout-hooves—the grease that is too dirty and salt-bitten for the lamps—and mix it with the fine, flour-like dust of the slate-yards and the gray, slippery liverwort that we scrape from the wet chimney-roots. It is cold to the touch, and it smells of old lard, sheep-sweat, and wet iron.

With a flat spade-knife made of a goat's shoulder-blade, I scoop the slip-scum from the bucket and plaster it into the "squeezes."

A squeeze is where two columns of basalt have begun to lean toward one another, their edges closing like a pair of shears. If they touch dry, they will grind. The sound of dry basalt grinding under the weight of the plateau is a high, thin shrieking that goes through the teeth and makes the children vomit in their tethers. It is the sound of the stone splintering itself. If a column splinters, the whole tier above it—thirty houses, eighty ladders, three hundred souls—will drop six inches in the space of a heartbeat.

So I plaster the joints. I plaster them two inches thick, three feet deep into the dark.

When the rock moves, it does not bite. It slides. The grease takes the friction, the slate-dust cushions the pinch, and the liverwort... well, the liverwort is the secret. It is a living thing, even in the paste. It likes the dark, and it likes the grease. It grows inside the seam, feeding on the tallow and the stone-sweat, forming a rubbery, self-healing skin that doesn't wash away in the winter drippings.

I have been in the harness since my twelfth year.

My right shoulder is slightly higher than my left, and my hands do not close all the way; they are curved like the rungs of the birch ladders, the fingers thick and blunt, the nails split and filled with gray grease-crust that no amount of well-water will ever wash clean. Under my wool clouts, my feet are wide and flat, with the big toe standing apart from the others like the thumb of a glove.

When I work, I do not use my eyes much. In the Slot, the light from the sky-strip is nothing but a pale blue thread eighty meters up, as thin as a hair.

I work by the *creep*.

The *creep* is the movement of the air in the cracks. If I hold my hand—greased and bare—against the joint between Column Twelve and Column Thirteen, I can feel the breath of the stone. If the air is coming out in a cold, steady whistle, the seam is open and healthy. If the whistle stops, or if the air becomes warm and wet, the rock is closing. It is catching its breath.

Sometimes, when I am deep in the Slot, three levels down from my own hearth, I hear the child.

My daughter is *Linn*. She is four. Her tether is anchored to the great spruce post that supports the middle platform of the Fourth Tier. The post is six inches from my head, on the other side of the timber wall. I can hear her through the wood. She has a small, round stone—a river-pebble that her uncle brought down from the dry barrens—and she rolls it back and forth, back and forth across the flat cedar slab of our floor.

*Click-roll. Click-roll.*

The sound comes down the spruce fiber, through the damp grain, and into the iron pin that holds my harness-line. I feel it in my collar-bone. It is a good sound. It means she is within her six feet of rope. It means she has not reached the edge where the platform drops thirty meters into the goat-pens.

Her mother, *Tey*, is spinning. I can hear the drop-spindle, too, though it is quieter. It is a rhythmic *tock-tock* as the soapstone whorl hits the leg of her stool.

We do not speak much, Tey and I. When I come up at dusk, my harness-hitch undone by the apprentice grease-boys, my knees are so stiff I must walk with my hips swinging wide, like a crab in the rocks. I go into the hut—our box of five slabs—and I sit on my heels in the corner by the hearth-stone.

Tey does not look at me. She does not look at the black grease on my thighs or the way my breath comes in short, wet gasps because of the slate-dust in my throat. She looks at the pot of cabbage-broth. She hands me a wooden bowl without her fingers touching mine.

"The wind is from the north," she says. Her voice is the sibilant whisper of the middle tiers—the *hiss-whisper* that does not carry through the timber walls to the neighbors. "The Sky-goers brought down six hares. They were dry. No fat on them."

"The stone is cold," I tell her. My own whisper is thicker, phlegmy with tallow-smoke. "The Sister Column has moved two lines to the west. I had to shim the bottom of the ladder-support at the third level."

"Fenn’s goat died," she says. "The yellow one."

"The meat?"

"Too stringy. They salted the shoulders. The rest went into the bottom."

We eat. The broth tastes of the smoke of green fir-needles and the iron-salt of the well. Linn sits between us, her harness-cord coiled around her ankles like a sleeping snake. She does not speak at all. Children in the Rift do not speak until they are five; they listen. They are learning the codes—the *clink-clink* of the Plumb-keepers, the *thrum* of the basalt, the different pitches of the water-dripping that tell you if a storm is coming on the dry plateau above.

Later, when the oil-lamp is blown out and the five of us—Tey, myself, Linn, and my two brothers who work the slate-quarry—are packed together under the heavy, damp wool blankets, the world becomes nothing but weight.

We lie on our sides, chest to back, to save space. If one turns, we must all turn. We call it "the fold."

In the dark, the Rift is never still.

You would think it quiet, but it is the quiet of a pulled cord. Under our heads, through the four layers of cedar planks and the spruce joists, the basalt is breathing. It is a slow, rhythmic pressure. You can feel the columns leaning into one another, thirty tons of black iron-stone shifting an eighth of an inch to the north, then settling back with a soft, sliding *crunch* that makes the water-pots on the shelf click together.

And sometimes, from the very bottom—from the dark cracks where the goats are tethered in their wool clouts—there comes the other sound.

It is not the *thrum*. It is the *hiss*.

It is the warm wind that has no wind-sky in it. It comes up through the joins in the floorboards, carrying the smell of things that have never seen the light—things that are not plant and are not mountain-goat. It is a soft, wet sound, like a tongue licking a greasy platter.

*Sssss-ah. Sssss-ah.*

Linn stirs in her sleep. Her hand reaches out and finds my thumb—the big, grease-bitten thumb that has no feeling left in the skin. She holds it tight.

"It is only the mountain," I whisper into the wool of her blanket. "It is only the grease-eater."

The old Plumb-keepers say that the *hiss* has been here longer than the ladders. They say that before the first men came down from the dry barrens with their birch rungs and their iron wedges, the *hiss* lived in the hexagonal chimneys alone, climbing up and down the basalt columns like a shadow. It does not bite the ropes if they are grease-soaked. It likes the tallow. It licks the oil from the slip-seams, and where it licks, the basalt grows warm and soft, like old beeswax, so the columns can slide against each other without breaking.

We are its guests. We are the ones who bring the fat down from the top—the sheep-fat, the goat-grease, the lard of the dry-land hares. We grease the crevices for it, and in return, it lets us hang our houses from its stone pillars.

I lie there in the fold, my toes twitching under the damp blankets. I can feel the grease-scum hardening under my fingernails, turning into a tough, leathery crust.

Tomorrow, at the first cold blue of dawn, the Plumb-keeper will tap the three short strokes on the spruce post. I will get up, wrap my feet in the damp wool clouts, and take my goat-bone spade. I will go back down into the Slot, where the rock-joints are waiting to be fed.

I am Sull. My feet are wide. My ears are open. I am exactly where I belong, in the narrow place between the wood and the stone, where the fat keeps the world from screaming.
