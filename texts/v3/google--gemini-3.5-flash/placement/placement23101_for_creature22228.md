I would be found three levels below the mist-line, where the air smells of old well-buckets and wet sheep, in a limestone niche that does not have a floor so much as a series of nested birch-bark trays. 

My home is five paces wide. To enter it, you must slide sideways through a vertical split in the rock, holding your breath so your ribs do not catch on the raw flint of the jamb. Inside, the ceiling is so low that my boiled-wool cap brushes the greasy yellow liverwort with every step, leaving a faint trail of pale pollen in my shorn hair.

I am a *Weaver of the Drip*, but my neighbors call me by a soft, repeated sigh—a sound like dry grass sliding over slate. 

My hands are old, though "old" here is not a matter of winters but of crust. The skin of my palms has stopped being soft; it has taken on the texture of dried shark-hide, yellow-white and slightly glittering under the nails where the calcium has settled into the whorls of my prints. My left wrist does not bend as far as my right. This is the "white marriage," the slow, painless fusing of the small bones that comes to those of us who spend forty years with our hands held upward into the weeping of the walls.

In the corner of my niche sits my loom. It is not made of wood—wood is for fires and the wedges that hold up the turf—but of stiffened elder-shoots and the long, coarse guard-hairs of mountain dogs, all of it turned into a pale, skeletal basketry by the steady *drip-clop* from the ledge above. 

My work is to guide the water where it does not want to go.

The water of the Sieve is heavy, stupid, and determined. Left to itself, it would fill every cranny with white butter-stone within three generations, turning our homes into a flat, blind wall. I am the one who gives it a path. 

I have three small troughs of split dry elder-wood, each no longer than my forearm. They are lined with greasy sheep-fat to keep the lime from sticking. Every morning, after the *Scrapers* have finished their dawn-shush along the ledges, I crawl out onto my working-scaffold—a cradle of plaited hemp that hangs fifteen paces above the *Sough*. 

The air down here is cold enough to make the teeth ache. The mist rises from the dark pools below, smelling of wet slate and the deep, iron-cold mud where the dead are resting. I reach out with my birch-bark cups, find the main weeps along the rock-seams, and anchor my troughs.

If a family on the tier below needs a new lintel for their door, they do not go to the quarry. They bring me three bundles of green willow-withes, bound tightly with wet rawhide. I hang these bundles beneath the heaviest silver drip on my ledge. 

For the first month, I must go out every three days with a small bone needle to clear the nostrils of the wood—the small gaps between the twigs where the air must pass. If the stone grows too fast, it becomes light and bubbly like dried froth, and the lintel will snap under the weight of the roof-slate. It must be grown slowly, drop by drop, until the willow is entirely eaten by the lime, replaced cell by cell with a cold, white bone-stone that will outcure the cliff itself.

To do this, I must watch the color of the water. When it is gray like sheep’s eye, it is rich and fast; when it is green like old copper, it is slow and hard. 

My tools are small and silent:
*   A scraper made of a swan's breastbone, ground to a curved edge that fits the roundness of the rain-gutters.
*   A horn awl for clearing the drainage holes in my willow-molds.
*   A bowl of tallow-grease, mixed with the scrapings of the white shelf-fungus, which I rub into my own skin to keep the water from petrifying the hair-follicles of my arms.

There is a quietness in my niche that is different from the quietness of the plateau above, which I have never seen but have heard of from the rope-traders. They say up there the silence is wide and empty, like a dry dish. Here, the silence is crowded. 

I can hear the boy in the cabin three layers below me sleeping; his breath is a soft, wet *sz-sz-sz* against his mother's throat. I can hear the slow, heavy crawl of the slate-gray salamander through the dry-stone wall behind my head-rest, its belly skin clicking softly against the limestone shales as it hunts the isopods.

Sometimes, when the noon-noon is past and the two hours of silver light have gone gray again, I go down to the *Sough*. 

I do not use the ladders; my feet, in their hemp-and-hair soles, know the notched steps we carved into the wet cliff fifty years ago. My toes can find the small tooth-marks left by my uncle's flint chisel when he was a boy.

At the bottom of the gorge, the water is dark and deep. It does not flow; it slide-grows. Here lie the ones who have finished their scraping. 

I find my sister there. She has been in the *Sough* for four winters now. I reach down into the cold, shivering water and touch her shoulder. She is no longer wool and skin; she is cold, heavy travertine, smooth as lard, her shape rounded by the constant, gentle lick of the silt-laden pool. 

Next spring, the *Weavers* will bring her up. We will use her to support the new gallery on the eighth tier, where the young couples are building. Her shoulder is wide and flat; she will make a good sill-stone. She will keep their floor from sagging into the gap.

As I sit by the water, the mist settles on my wool cap, turning into tiny, white beads of lime that will dry into a crust before nightfall. I do not shake them off. 

We are all of us just willow-withes in the stream, waiting for the stone to finish what it started. I will go back up to my loom now. There is a cradle-frame to mold before the twilight becomes total, and the water is dripping, *tink... tink... tink...* steady as a heart, waiting for me to show it where to freeze.
