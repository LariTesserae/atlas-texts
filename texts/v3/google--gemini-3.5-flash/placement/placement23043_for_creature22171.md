I would not be found on the floor. 

The jade-colored pavement is too flat, too public, and too solved. It belongs to the Sliders and the Caulkers—to those things that are part of the daily sweep and maintenance of the great machine. Nor would I be found in the open air, suspended like the Melodians, catching the dry wind and turning it into a clock. 

I would be found in the high, wet shadows of the joints.

Where the monumental sky-spanning arches spring from the vertical faces of the cliffs, there is always a crease. It is an armpit of the world. Here, the green cellulose does not have the pristine, polished finish of the plazas; instead, it is thick, wrinkled, and dark. In these corners, the immense hydraulic lift of the cliff’s vascular tubes must bend sixty degrees to feed the arches. The pressure at these turn-points is terrifying. The wood hums so hard that the moisture on its surface does not lie in a film, but stands up in tiny, shivering spikes of green-gold water.

I would be **The Weft**.

***

### The Shape of the Sleeve

I am a three-hundred-pound wedge of dark, felted purple pulp, wedged tightly into the angle where the Spine-of-the-East meets the northern wall. 

I am not green. In a world of emerald and jade, I am the color of a bruised damson plum. This dark anthocyanin pigmentation is my protection; I live closer to the high, sunlit canopy than almost anything else, and my skin must absorb the intense, unfiltered radiation that bleeds through the uppermost leaves. My texture is not that of the rubbery pavement or the dry papyrus of the Husk-Walkers. If you could reach up into my shadow and touch me, I would feel like a heavy horse-hair blanket that has been soaked in warm starch and left to steam in the sun.

I have no eyes, because there is nothing to look at in the crease, and I have no limbs for walking. Instead, my underside is resolved into several thousand hollow, purple tubes—capillary thread-roots that I have driven deep through the jade rind into the vascular main-lines of the cliff. 

I am a living valve. A bypass. 

***

### The Taste of the Rise

To be the Weft is to live in a state of permanent, turgid ecstasy. 

The great drone of the valley—the wet, roaring cello-sound of the millions of gallons of water climbing from the deep basin—does not hit my ears. I have no ears. Instead, the roar *scours* me. The water enters my thread-roots under such immense pressure that my felted body is forever swollen to the limit of its skin. I am a knot in the pipe.

As the sap rushes through me, I filter it. The water that rises from the dark roots of the world is rich and thick, loaded with heavy starches, raw sugars, and tiny, suspended flakes of unrefined cellulose. If this crude sap were allowed to flow directly into the high, delicate arches, the narrow weepers on their undersides would clog within a week. The metronomic drip would halt. The channels below would dry, the Sliders would starve, and the balance of the valley would tilt.

So, I sit in the bend. I drink the starch.

I pull the heavy, sweet purple sugar out of the torrent, passing only the clear, thin, pressurized water into the arch. The taste is constant: a warm, malted iron, thick and clean, sparkling with the oxygen of the deep, underground lift. I do not eat; I merely *strain*. My body is a slow, wet lung that breathes water instead of air, trapping the solid fuel of the world and leaving the plumbing clean.

### The Companions of the Hinge

Though I am static, I am not alone. 

Sometimes, the Melodians—the large, transparent bladders—drift too close to the cliff face. Their trailing golden harp-strings brush against my purple felt. It is a strange, dry sensation, like hair passing over a wet lip. When they touch me, their vibrations change; the hollow pitch of their bladder drops, and they release a small puff of hot, toasted-malt vapor to push themselves away. 

Below me, far down the vertical face of the green wood, the Caulkers roll. They do not come up to my crease—it is too steep, and gravity keeps them in the channels—but occasionally a small, grape-sized Caulker will be carried up through the internal vascular tubes by some freakish surge of the pump. It will pop out of a relief-pore near my edge, a shiny, white latex ball. It will sit there, confused by the lack of a channel, until it smells the slight scratch my root-threads have made in the bark. 

Slowly, over several days, the Caulker will roll itself toward my base. It doesn't heal me—it cannot penetrate my felt—but it settles against my lower seam, melting its white lard into the joint to seal the connection between my purple skin and the green wood. We become one thing: the pump, the seal, and the strainer.

### The Plum-Drop

I do not die, because there is nothing here to break me down. But I am not immortal; I am subject to the laws of load.

Every three or four hundred years, my felted body becomes too heavy. The starch I have strained from the water cannot all be digested; some of it is stored, turning my plum-colored skin black and rigid with crystallized sugar. I become a dead weight in the hinge. If I stay, I will snap the very vascular tubes that feed me.

When the weight reaches its limit, the change begins. 

I do not decay. Instead, I divide. My lower half—the part most laden with the heavy, brittle sugars—begins to constrict. Over the course of three monsoons of dripping water, the bottom ninety pounds of my body pinches off into a perfect, dark, teardrop-shaped sphere. 

One day, with a sound like a wet cork popping from a bottle, it lets go.

It falls. It does not drift like the Melodians. It is too heavy. It plunges through the warm, thick air, a black-purple bullet falling through three hundred feet of green space. When it hits the wet jade plaza below, it does not shatter. It is too elastic, too dense with starch. It lands with a massive, wet *thock* that sends a shudder through the nearest Register, miles away.

The Caulkers immediately smell the disruption. They begin to roll toward the impact site from all directions, ready to seal the bruise on the floor. 

But the drop is faster. The moment it touches the wet, warm pavement, its acid-sweet starch begins to react with the green cellulose. It does not slide; it *dissolves* downward. It melts a perfectly round hole through the five-foot rind of the floor, sinking into the dark, wet interior where the great returns run.

By the time the first Caulker arrives to seal the hole, the drop is gone. It has entered the dark, roaring underworld of the pumps. It will be carried down to the very bottom of the basin, into the hot, pressurized reservoir where the water begins its climb. There, in the dark, it will begin to grow its first purple threads, searching for a new column, a new elbow of wood, a new joint of the sky to strain.

And up in the crease of the third pillar, the halved remainder of my body begins to drink again, lighter now, and ready for another three hundred years of the great, wet song.
