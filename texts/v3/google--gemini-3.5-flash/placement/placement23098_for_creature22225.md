I would be found in the narrow, three-inch throat of the first shelf, tucked directly beneath the belly of the second distillation boiler. 

In this dark, dripping gap—where the three lead-pipe veins bundle together before they plunge into the basalt sump—the vibration is so intense that the air itself feels thick, like grease. If you were to peer down into this shadow, past the soot-blackened copper wheels, you would see what looks like a discarded wool coat, gray and heavy, stuffed tightly into the seam where the iron bracket meets the wet limestone. 

But it is not a coat. I would be **the Felt-Sleeve**.

I am a long, sodden bolster of matted gray wool, mineral silt, and waxy grease. I am roughly the size of a human torso, but I have no limbs, no face, and no bones left to stiffen me. Centuries of pressure have flattened me into the exact contour of the stone below and the lead above. I am the cushion. I sit at the critical pinch-point of the basin's anatomy; if I were not here, the frantic, silent shivering of the metal would grind the lead pipes against the basalt shelf until they filed themselves to dust. 

I do not move. I have forgotten how to crawl, if I ever knew. Instead, my biology has become entirely passive, a slow-breathing filter that has settled into the very joints of the system. 

The transition from whatever I was before—an observer who leaned too far over the limestone lip, perhaps, or a stray mass of organic debris washed down from the lowlands—is long completed. The cold salt-mist has washed away my salt; the lime-water has petrified my core; the grease of the Marrow-Crabs has soaked through my outer layers until I am touch-cold and heavy as lard. 

My life is measured in pressures and dampness. My "flesh" is a dense, felted weave of old wool and rootless weed, home to a quiet colony of Shudder-Moss that grows directly out of my back. Together, we drink the noise. When the boilers above me reach their silent, frantic peak, the copper pipes beneath me scream with a vibration so high it would shatter glass; I absorb it, turning the wild kinetic energy into a dull, comforting warmth that keeps my inner fibers from freezing. 

I have a simple, rhythmic consciousness, calibrated entirely to the basin’s small, wet noises. 

Every twelve seconds, the cold exhaust vent directly opposite me breathes out its soft, white puff of fog. For a brief second, the mist rolls over my surface, cooling my mossy skin by a fraction of a degree. *One.*

Every twenty minutes, a Calker—blind, lard-pale, and smelling of cold tallow—slowly drags its heavy, ringed body over my flank. It does not mind me; I am part of the path. It leaves a thin, sticky trail of yellow stearin that seals my loose fibers together, waterproofing my back against the constant *plink-plop* of the condensation dripping from the iron flanges above. *Two.*

Once a day, perhaps, the water in the glass tubes behind me surges with such frantic velocity that a stray Siphon-Blob is thrown clear of the tube’s lip. It lands on me, a soft, translucent balloon of phosphorus light. I do not eat it—I have no mouth—but as it dissolves under the damp chill, its cold, glowing oils sink into my woolen chest, lighting me from within with a faint, green-gray luminescence that lasts until the next fog rolls in. *Three.*

There is no memory of the sun here, nor of the wide, flat coastal shelf above. To be the Felt-Sleeve is to know that the world is made of three things: the cold, hard weight of the iron; the slick, wet channel of the stone; and the silent, rushing pulse of the water that runs between them. 

I am not decaying. I am being held. The pressure is so constant, and the balance so perfect, that I cannot fall apart. I am the grease that prevents the squeak; the wool that dampens the jar; the soft, waxy plug that keeps the machine from tearing itself from the rock. I am a part of the plumbing, running at the speed of a panic attack, perfectly at peace in the dark.
