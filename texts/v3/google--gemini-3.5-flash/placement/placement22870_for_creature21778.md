The shadow begins at the point of my left hip. 

It is not a shadow that moves, because the sun does not move. It is a solid, clean-edged wedge of indigo-black that has lain across my thigh for as long as the basalt spire behind me has met the sky. My left leg, immersed in this blue dark, is always several degrees colder than my right. On my right knee, the sun strikes at forty-five degrees, turning the grey, horn-like skin to the color of dry river-silt. On my left knee, which stays in the shadow, a thick patch of emerald moss has established itself over the course of some centuries. It feels like wet, heavy wool against my flesh, but I do not scrape it off. It is healthy, and its green does not change.

They call me *The One Who Holds the Angle*. 

My coordinate is the terrace below the fourth cistern, where the grass rope spans five hundred yards to the Needle of the Seven Birds. I am the one who sits here to witness the rope. If the rope were to fray, I would notice it, though the rope has not frayed since the day it was braided from the tough, blue-green mountain grass. The wind is always the same: a dry, cool silk that passes through my long, hollow ribs with a sound like a wet finger trailing over the rim of a slate bowl.

When I am not in the stillness, I have a copper chisel. It is seven inches long, cold to the touch, and held in my right hand. For three hundred years, I have been carving a small, shallow runnel into the basalt ledge between my feet. 

The tap of my mallet is not a hurry. *Tock.* 

I strike the chisel once. The sound travels out over the gulf, perfectly round and small, a grey-blue drop of noise in the vast, dry silence. I wait until the sound has vanished entirely—until it has reached the Needle of the Seven Birds, bounced twice against the sheer face of the slate, and died in the low valleys where the mist rises. Then I wait for the water to fall.

*Tink.*

A silver bead of condensation, heavy and perfect, drops from the underside of the grass bridge and hits the copper edge of my chisel. 

This is how I measure my existence: the *tock* of the copper, the *tink* of the water.

Between these two sounds, a glass-winged moth lands on my temple. Its wings are vibrating so fast they look like a smudge of grease against the deep indigo of the sky. I can feel the vibration of its life as a faint, cool hum inside the bone of my skull. My horizontal pupils do not turn to look at it; I keep my gaze fixed on the horizon, where the white sheep stand like small, unmoving drops of tallow on the blue-grey ledges of the sister-spire.

Sometimes, a call comes.

It is *The Maker of the Plumb-Line*, who lives three spires to the west. I see him only as a long, grey line—no thicker than a blade of grass—standing on his terrace. 

He strikes his slate. A mile away, the sound is a thin, metallic *ping* that arrives after a long, suspended silence. He is asking if the water in my cistern is sweet.

I do not answer immediately. The sun does not sink; the shadows do not grow. To answer quickly would be to make myself like the wind down in the valleys, which has no weight. I sit through three thousand drops of water. I watch a slate-colored raptor adjust its tail-feathers by three degrees to remain level with my shoulder. 

When the time is right, I draw the cold mountain air into my chest. It fills the long, slow chambers of my lungs, cooling the clear sap that moves through my throat. I open my mouth and release a low, resonant whistle—a dry, hollow *whooo-ut* that mimics the wind passing through the cistern’s mouth. 

It will take several minutes to reach him. He will receive it, and he will consider it for twenty years. Perhaps, by the time the green ash ladders have aged enough to require a third leather binding, he will strike his slate again.

In the stillness, there is no before. There is only the forty-five degree light, the cold weight of the moss on my left leg, and the knowledge that the young, sharp stone beneath me will never soften, never crumble, and never let me fall.
