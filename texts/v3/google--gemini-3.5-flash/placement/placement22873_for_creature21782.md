I am the weight inside the light.

To find me, you must pass through the clattering, glass-hard forest of silica reeds and climb the red clay towers of the wasp-nest town. You must choose one of the thousands of hollow, six-sided boxes clinging to the cliffs—perhaps this one, resting on a narrow ledge of pumice where the rust-red light of the fissures pools like dry wine. 

If you were to look through my skin—six thin, hexagonal plates of smoky gray mica, joined with dry red plaster—you would find no blood, no lungs, no pulse. 

Instead, you would find me.

I am a single, irregular grain of iron-rich magnetite, no larger than a seed of parched barley, but heavy, dark, and hard. I rest on the floor of this hollow mica chamber. 

I am the box’s only memory of *down*. 

Within this sealed, basalt-deep world, where the winds are always pulling upward and the mountain's breath is constantly trying to lift everything into the great, vacant center of the pocket, I am the anchor. The air inside our hollow shell is sulfurous and still, trapped behind the gray mica windowpanes. Because I am iron, I do not expand when the heat rises, nor do I contract when the vents fall silent. I remain dense. I feel the immense, quiet pull of the basalt floor below us—a deep, magnetic ache that guides my heavy body toward the earth.

In the quiet hours, when the floor fissures hold their breath, there is no sound but the dry, microscopic settling of clay dust. During these times, the air inside our box cools. The clay joints at our corners stiffen and fold inward. Our six obsidian needle-legs telescope back into their slots, pulling us close to the stone. 

During these periods of cold, I am perfectly still. I rest in the lowest corner of the polyhedron, pressing my tiny, faceted iron body against the plaster seam. Through the mica, the rust-red glow of the floor is a dim, amber twilight. I feel the vibrations of the deep earth through the stone ledge—the slow, tectonic groaning of the mountain. It is a comforting weight.

Then, the floor breathes. 

It begins as a low, whistling hiss from the fissures. The basalt beneath us shudders. The heat arrives before the wind, a dry, kiln-like wave that smells of parched slate. 

Because the air inside our box is still cool, a sudden, violent thermic gradient forms across our mica walls. The plates warp. They bulge outward, the thin gray sheets popping with small, distinct *pings*. The red clay seams, packed with dry mineral wool, flex like dry leather. 

The movement is not ours; it is the heat’s. 

Our obsidian needles are forced outward by the expansion of the corners. They snap down against the pumice ledge with a sharp *clack*. 

The box tilts. 

Inside, I am thrown from my corner. I roll across the smooth, dusty floor of the chamber—*tink*—and strike the opposite mica plate. The impact of my tiny, heavy body registers to the shell as a shift in gravity. The box adjusts. It leans into the slope, its obsidian legs clicking frantically as they find purchase on the vertical clay walls of the town.

We are moving now, though we do not "walk." We are bent and rattled across the stone by the expanding and contracting of our joints. 

Our bellies drag through the red dust of the plazas. I feel every bump, every crack in the clay dwellings as a sharp, internal vibration. When we climb over a fissure in a tower wall, our weight presses the electrostatic silt clinging to our underside into the gap. The intense heat of the rising gas glazes it instantly, baking it into a permanent weld. We do not know we are repairing the town; we only know that the heat makes us flex, and the flex makes us drag, and the drag leaves us smooth.

The wind from the vents grows stronger. It rises from the floor, rushing toward the curved ceiling. 

Suddenly, a gust catches our flat mica faces. Our obsidian legs lose their grip on the clay cliff. 

We are airborne.

This is the moment of weightlessness, the most beautiful part of our loop. We sail into the central void, floating like an angular, hard-shelled kite. The clatter of the silica forest below us fades into a muffled, rhythmic roar, like millions of dry dice shaking in a leather cup. 

Inside the box, I am no longer resting on the floor. I float in the exact center of our hollow air-pocket, suspended in the sulfurous dark. The magnetic pull of the basalt walls is equal on all sides now, a gentle, circular tugging that keeps me hovering. We spin slowly. The light outside flashes through our smoky mica faceplates—red, gray, red, gray—as we drift through the warm, empty air.

As we drift, the tough, hair-like asbestos fibers rising from the vents catch on our obsidian joints, trailing behind us like comet tails. We bump gently against another floating box; our needles tangle, then release. We draw the fibers across the gap, spinning the invisible web that holds the suspension bridges together.

Then, the vent’s breath begins to weaken. The updraft thins. 

The air inside our box finally warms to match the air outside. The thermal gradient vanishes. The mica plates relax, their tension slipping away. The obsidian legs draw back, folding like the limbs of a dead spider.

We begin to fall.

Inside, weight returns. I drop to the floor of the chamber with a sharp *clink*, my iron body instantly pointing toward the basalt deep. The weight of my landing tilts the falling box, causing it to descend bottom-first. 

We strike a terrace on the eastern side of the pocket with a dry, hollow rattle. We bounce once, slide three inches through a drift of glittering silica spores, and come to a halt against a forest reed.

The wind dies. The silence returns—the heavy, dry silence of the empty space. 

I roll into the lowest corner of the box, resting my iron face against the clay seam. I am still.

We will do this for centuries. We will polish the walls, we will weave the bridges, we will roll and clink in the dark. 

But one day, the plaster holding our mica plates together will have baked too many times. It will have grown too brittle, too thin, too dry. During a particularly violent exhalation from the vents, when the pressure difference becomes too extreme, our box will not flex. 

It will shatter.

There will be a sharp, musical *clink*—the last sound our shell will ever make. The six mica plates will flutter down like dry leaves; the obsidian needles will scatter like black glass pins. 

And I, the magnetite grain, will fall. 

I will drop into the deep cracks of the basalt floor, landing among the roots of the silica forest. I will no longer move. The winds will blow over me, but I will be too heavy to care. The dust will settle. 

Then, the glittering silicate spores of the reeds will find me. They will cling to my iron sides, attracted by my magnetic field. Over decades, nourished by the rising carbon gases and the drifting clay dust, new plates of gray mica will begin to grow along my crystalline edges. They will hinge together. They will seal. A new, hollow polyhedron will self-assemble around me, trapping a pocket of dry, sulfurous air.

I will wait in the dark. 

And when the earth takes its next deep, hot breath, I will roll again.
