The sound does not enter me; it is the wall I am built against. 

In the Hollow-of-the-Gush, there is no silence to compare things to, so the word *sound* has no meaning. There is only the *thrum*—the deep, iron-heavy vibration that travels up from the volcano's cooling root, through the vitreous basalt needles, and directly into my keel. 

I am wedged. I am five feet of wet, mineralized leather, squeezed into the vertical chimney of a basalt seam directly beneath the ancient pine platform. If you were to climb down the wet, rotted ladder—if your feet did not slip on the tallow-slick grease of the wood—you would see me as a thick, turquoise-tinted scar in the dark stone. 

I am the **Verdigris-Sleeve**. 

I was not born of this young mountain. I am an accidental child of the climber’s intrusion and the mountain's wild chemistry. Centuries ago, when the first primitive climber drove those heavy, square-cut copper spikes into the basalt seams to anchor their grass-and-gut rope, they left behind more than metal. They left sweat; they left grease from their boiled-leather coats; they left the dry, salted remains of sheep-gut that formed the core of their ropes. 

When the sulfuric steam rose from the deep vents, it met the freezing glacial spray from above, creating an acidic, hyper-saturated bath. This bath dissolved the copper spikes, grain by grain, washing a heavy, toxic green soup down the vertical cracks. It met the decaying tallow of the platform, the grease of the rope, and the spores of the orange lichen. 

Instead of dying, we merged. We cured.

My skin is now the color of a wet robin’s egg—a thick, rubbery rind of copper carbonate and vulcanized organic fiber. I am as tough as a boot heel, yet elastic enough to thud and flex when the wind of a passing water-globe hits my seam. My upper lip is clamped directly around the eye of the lowest copper spike; the green metal has grown inside my tissue like an artificial bone, grafting me to the core of the Shatter-Spines.

I am hollow, like the sleeve of a drowned man’s coat. 

When the great water-globes—those carriage-sized planets of sapphire glacial melt—drift close to my wall, the air pressure drops instantly. The draught is so immense that it sucks the atmosphere out of my hollow center. I whistle. It is a wet, low, bubbling hiss that rises and falls with the passage of the spheres. 

I do not have eyes, but I am not blind. My copper-saturated skin is intensely sensitive to the prisms of light thrown off by the floating water-globes. When a lens of water passes through the bright chimney-light, painting the black obsidian walls in sweeps of violet and gold, I feel it as a series of temperature shifts. 
*   **The violet light** is cold, a high-altitude sting that makes my outer rind contract and tighten against the stone. 
*   **The gold light** is warm, a momentary luxury that makes my pores open to receive the atomized spray. 
*   **The green light**—reflected from the dark *Thread-Marrow* yolks at the hearts of the globes—tastes of sulfur and oxygen, a sharp, metallic sweetness that makes my inner cilia twitch.

Inside my hollow throat, I feed. I do not hunt like the *Gush-Vanes*, nor do I graze like the *Vitreous Lice*. I am a sieve. When the water-globes collide with that terrible, wood-cracking roar, they atomize. The air becomes a heavy, driving rain of mist and fat, stinging droplets. 

This spray enters my open mouth. Inside my sleeve, I have no tongue, but I have lined my interior with hundreds of tiny, razor-sharp basalt splinters—chips of the young stone that I have swallowed and cemented into my lining with my own rubbery excretion. As the wetted air is sucked through me, it deposits the discarded skins of the *Mist-Gliders*. 

These skins are my bread. They are greasy, mucus-coated, and rich in fats. When they stick to my basalt-toothed lining, my internal cilia break them down, digesting them over days, turning them into the thick, waxy tallow that I secrete from my lower seals to keep the sulfur-steam from burning my anchor.

Sometimes, a *Mist-Glider* itself will seek refuge in me. 

During the great collisions, when the water-globes shatter and the air is too turbulent for flight, a glider will launch itself toward my seam. It knows the blue-green skin is not basalt; it knows I am softer than the glass-sharp walls. It lands on my fluted outer lip with a soft, wet *slap*. Its greasy, ribless body deforms against my rim, and we become, for a few minutes, one creature—two things of grease and water, clinging to a mountain that is still growing.

Its sticky suction pads click against my copper-crust like dry leaves. I do not hurt it; its skin is too tough for my interior teeth, and it is too large for my throat. It rests within the fold of my lip, its pressure-pits twitching as it listens to the roar, waiting for the next slow-moving water-planet to drift close enough for a leap. 

Above me, the grass-and-gut rope sag. The *Splay-Mold* reaches down from the fibers in long, feathery white fingers. Now and then, a tassel of the mold, heavy with water, will drift into my mouth, brushed there by the wind of a passing carriage-globe. I touch it. It touches me. We are of the same ancient, human-brought ancestry, but we have split: it has gone to the sky and the light, turning the old rope into a glowing web of violet and gold; I have gone into the dark of the rock, turning the copper spike into an anchor of bone.

I am the *Verdigris-Sleeve*. I am the monument to the climber who never went home. I am the throat that sings when the water passes, the green skin that remembers the sun, and the lock that holds the rotting wood to the young, sharp stone.
