You would find me at the joint. 

I am not the basalt, which is cold and ancient and split wide; nor am I the iron, which is straight-edged, foreign, and heavy with the smell of coal smoke from some dry valley I have never seen. 

I am the white, stone-like crust that has grown between them. I am the suture. 

I began three miles below, dissolved in the dark, boiling stomach of the ridge, where the water is thick as syrup with the milk of dissolved limestone. When the mountain cracked and the engine fell into the gap, its copper straws reached down to drink, and I was drawn up in the dark, roaring throat of the pumps. 

But I was not meant for the high clouds. Wherever the warm, sulfurous water has sprayed from a loose rivet, or wept through the seam of a low-carbon plate, I have stayed behind. 

To the Oiler, if she had the eyes to see me as anything but the traction beneath her heel, I am only "the scale"—the chalky, cauliflower-headed build-up that cakes the lower bevel gears and cements the base-plates to the wet basalt shelf. But to myself, I am a slow, white tide. I am the process of the mountain remembering how to close its eyes.

***

To understand my life, you must understand the forty-five seconds.

I do not hear the *THUMP*; I am the *THUMP*. It is the force that defines my crystallization. My growth is not smooth like the drip-stones in the quiet caverns below; it is a violent, rhythmic forging.

In the thirty-second draw-back of the piston, the iron gears groan and pull micro-millimeters away from the basalt. The tension is immense. The tiny space between the metal and the stone behaves like a lung; it sucks in the hot, mineral-rich spray that runs down the chasm walls. For those thirty seconds, I am a liquid film, a saturated broth of calcium and sulfur, suspended in the gap.

During the five-second pause, the world is still. The water cools by a fraction of a degree against the cold iron of the base. In that silence, millions of microscopic crystals of aragonite leap into being. They reach out like tiny, white fingers, spanning the gap from the dark basalt to the grey iron, spinning a frail, web-like bridge of stone.

Then comes the ten-second stroke. The piston drives forward. The gears are forced back against the mountain with the weight of four thousand meters of water-column. 

The white web of crystals is not destroyed; it is *welded*. The pressure is so great that it mimics the heat of a kiln. The needle-crystals are crushed together, their boundaries sliding and locking, compacted into a single, dense sheet of white stone that is harder than the basalt of the ridge and tougher than the gray cast-iron of the engine. 

Every forty-five seconds, I grow by the thickness of a moth’s wing. Every forty-five seconds, the machine is slightly less machine, and the mountain is slightly less mountain.

***

I am not alone in this seam, though my neighbors move at different speeds.

The *Grooming Beetles* are my gardeners. They graze upon the "lime-yeast" that grows in the porous micro-fountains of my outer edges. Their feet, tipped with small, tough hooks, scrape against my white crust. I feel their passage as a light, pleasant tickle—a localized relief from the constant, crushing weight of the gears. Sometimes, their jaws chew too deep, shearing away a millimeter of my soft, newly deposited edge where a valve must turn. I do not mind. They keep me from choking the engine too quickly. Without them, I would have grown over the main shaft two hundred years ago, locking it in a white tomb, and then the hot water would have stopped flowing, and I would have died of dryness.

The *Verdigris Snails* are more like me. They are my slow cousins. When they die along the copper conduits, their heavy, square-edged shells fall into my lap. I do not reject them. I wrap my white arms around them, filling their empty spirals with calcite, cementing them into my body. If you were to crack me open near the third boiler, you would find three centuries of snail-shells, arranged in neat, vertical columns like the vertebrae of a long, green snake.

And then there is the Oiler.

She is very fast, like a shadow that passes before the sun can register it. I feel her only as a soft, dull pressure and a sudden, greasy warmth. Her calloused heels leave gray, organic smears upon my white plates. These smears are rich in animal fat and sheep’s wool fibers. For a few weeks, my crystals cannot grow where she has stepped; the fat repels the mineral-bearing water. But eventually, the water wears the tallow away, or the moss grows over it, or I simply bury the fat beneath a new layer of white stone. 

Deep inside my heart, near the basalt anchor-pins, there are pockets of her ancestors' grease, preserved like amber, still smelling of the sheep that were slaughtered in some valley before the first engine-parts were hauled up the cliff.

***

In the dry valleys, they think of machines as things that are built, and mountains as things that are carved. 

Up here, we know better. 

I am the slow translation of human design into geology. The iron of this lifting engine was dug from the earth, purified by fire, and planed into straight, unnatural lines. But it cannot remain straight in the wet, green throat of this cleft. The rust is already carrying the iron back to the soil, turning the waterfall red; the copper is turning green, mimicking the ferns; and I am cementing the joints, turning the gears into fossils before they have even stopped turning.

In five hundred years, the boiler will be gone. The copper pipes will have dissolved into green streaks in the basalt. The great piston, thick as an oak, will have rusted away to a red hollow.

But the hollow will remain. I will have built a perfect, white stone mold of the engine around it. The teeth of the gears will be preserved in hard, glittering calcite. The piston-chamber will be a smooth, cylindrical cave, through which the warm mountain water will still rise, driven by some new, wild pressure of the earth.

I am the patience of the stone. I am the ending of the work.
