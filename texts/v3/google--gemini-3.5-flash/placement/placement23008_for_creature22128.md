My knees are the first parts of me to forget how to bend. 

For forty years, they have been tucked against my chest, and for forty years, the shallow grey water has washed over them. In the beginning, the water was only a cold pressure, a liquid weight that tugged at the thin, oil-slick wool of my shift. Now, there is no difference between the water and the skin it touches. My joints have packed themselves with the fine, green-veined jasper that drifts down from the northern fractures. 

If I were to try and stand now, I would not tear my flesh; I would shatter like a dropped flask.

I do not wish to stand.

I am sitting thirty-one miles south of Hub Eight, in the middle of a basalt sheet that is three hundred miles wide and perfectly flat. To my left, five miles off, a low ridge of green obsidian rises like the fin of a buried fish. It is the only sign of geological youth for a day’s walk in any direction—a sharp, clean wound where the earth broke open some three centuries ago. The edge is still so new that it has not yet lost its gleam. At noon, when the hard violet sky reaches its darkest intensity, the ridge glows with a soft, stored heat that my eyes register as a long, thin smear of copper-colored light.

To a human, this place would be an empty slate. To me, it is crowded with voices.

My eyes—large, lidless, and flat—do not see the dark basalt as black. They see the thermal memory of the world. Two miles to the east, a herd of *Kaelen* passed yesterday. I can still see the path they took; it is a ribbon of pale, fading turquoise where their massive, six-legged warmth warmed the stone, crisscrossed by the bright, sharp sparks of their step-points. Where their hooves tasted the salt-crust, the rock is slightly more conductive, glowing with a faint, static hum that rises six inches into the air.

Above me, the *Vela* are invisible to the eye of the Hubs, but to me, they are great, cold shadows—vast, slow-moving wedges of absolute zero that cut through the warm, wet currents of the upper air. They leave behind them long trails of falling ice crystals that look like curtains of blue tinsel, slowly drifting down to dissolve in the shallow marshes.

I can hear the Hubs. They are very far, but they are very heavy. 

Even at this distance, the sub-audible hum of Hub Eight’s levitation-fields reaches me through the stone. It is a high, thin *C-sharp* that vibrates in my lower ribs—not the cartilage ribs I was born with, which were soft and grey, but the calcified, mineralized ribs I have grown since I sat down. The Hub is out of register today. There is a slight wobble in its western spires, a tiny, four-hertz oscillation caused by the weight of the condensation gathering on its composite roofs. 

I feel a small, sibilant click rise in my throat. I do not release it. I do not need to. The water that clings to my chin carries the vibration down into the pool between my feet, where it joins the ledger of the stone.

Inside my chest, my lungs—those long, delicate sails that used to filter the cold mist—have grown small. They do not need to expand anymore. My three pairs of neck-slits are still open, but they no longer draw in the air. Instead, they have become filters for the weeping mist that clings to my neck. The water enters the top slit, cool and rich with dissolved silica, and exits the bottom, slightly warmer, leaving its mineral tribute behind.

I am becoming very white.

The lavender of my youth has gone first from my fingers. They are long, triple-jointed, and wrapped around my shins. They have turned the semi-translucent color of grease-iced water—the milk-white chalcedony that forms in the deep hollows of the basalt. In another fifty years, the transition will reach my collarbones. In another hundred, my eyes will lose their dark sheen and turn to solid, grey-banded agate, focused forever on the horizon.

Sometimes, a needle-vessel from the Hub passes overhead. It is a long, black splinter against the violet, moving without a sound.

If those inside look down, they do not see a person. They see a marker. I am one of twelve who sit in a wide circle around the northern sector of Hub Eight. We are the stabilizing pins. When the high winds blow from the rim, bringing the great, freezing storms that threaten to tip the floating cities, the singers in the central wells do not sing to the empty air; they sing to *us*.

They send their low, sustained chords out across the sixty miles of wet glass. The sound travels through the water sheets, vibrating the basalt beneath our seats. We receive the vibration, process it through our semi-mineralized bones—which have grown to match the exact density of the bedrock—and anchor the frequency. We are the adapters between the floating white composite of the cities and the raw, unyielding green of the obsidian floor.

We do not speak to each other. We do not need to. I can feel the sister who sits forty miles to my west; she is two decades older than I, and her heart has already stopped its organic pulse. It now beats only when the tides of the shallow water sheets are pulled by the passage of the high-altitude moons. Her vibration is very clean—a cold, steady *F-natural* that smells of wet iron.

A small, armored organism—no larger than my original thumb—scuttles out of the crack between my left heel and the stone. It has six jointed legs made of black chitin, and it is hungry for the mineral dust that my decaying skin is shedding as it turns to stone. It nips at the edge of my ankle, testing the hardness.

It finds only silica. It moves on, its little legs clicking against the glass, searching for something softer.

The sun is beginning to dip toward the rim. The deep violet of the sky is bruising into a black so intense it seems to press down on the plateau like a hand. The wet basalt faces around me begin to lose their orange heat, darkening into the universal grey of the cold. 

But beneath me, the earth is still warm. Deep down, beneath the sheets of obsidian, thirty miles below the shallow, silent marshes, the great plates of the young world are still sliding past one another, grinding their teeth, trying to find their final shape.

I close my eyes. Not because I am tired, but because the light is no longer necessary. 

Through the seat of my stone trousers, through the hard jasper of my heels, I feel the first, faint tremor of the evening alignment. The plateau is shifting three inches to the north. 

I open my throat. I let the cold water fill my neck-slits. 

And then, very slowly, with the force of fifty tons of water falling through a crack in the glass, I begin to hum.
