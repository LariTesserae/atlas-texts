I do not see Olaus. 

To see is to be separate. To see is to stand back and hold the horizon in a small, dry chamber behind the eyes, measuring the distance between the self and the mountain. I have no eyes, and I have no back. I have no distance. 

Instead, I am six inches of clear, cold weight, held against the upright basalt by the same force that pulls the weeping water down. I am an ear that covers the wet rock; I am a tongue that lies against the lichen. 

The world behaves as a series of gradients: the *warm-dry*, which is dangerous; the *cold-wet*, which is home; and the *vibration*, which is the passage of time.

***

In the early morning, when the sun is still behind the eastern rim of the plateau, I am almost solid. The air at this height is thin and biting, and my membrane contracts until I am a dense, heavy eye of glass, clinging to a vertical seam of dark grey stone. I do not move. If you were to touch me now, I would feel like a pocket of cold grease, thick and resistant to the wind. 

Below me, in the deep slots of the avenues, the silt-bottles are asleep in their mud, their silica anchors cold and stiff. Above me, the velamens are still caught in the rocks, their dry, leathery wings folded like burnt paper in the crevices, waiting for the first warmth to lift them. All of Olaus is a blue, frozen grid.

But when the sun clears the rim, the stone begins to wake. 

The basalt is dark, nearly black, and it drinks the light. It warms from the inside out. I feel the rock-face beneath me loosen its cold grip. The microscopic film of moisture that blankets the tower begins to move, a slow, downward slip of silver veins. 

I loosen with it. I do not slide—slide is a passive thing, the surrender to gravity. Instead, I *invite* the gravity on my terms. I thin my leading edge, stretching it upward and sideways until it is no thicker than a single layer of water molecules. This thin margin finds the microscopic pits in the volcanic stone, pulling me forward by capillary draw. Then, I contract my heavy center, dragging my bulk behind me. 

*Stretch. Hold. Draw.*

I move across the vertical face of the tower like a slow, clear hand. I am looking for the sulfur-yellow lichens—those dry, crusty thoughts of the stone that grow where the damp is thin. 

When I find a patch, I stop. My movement ceases to be linear and becomes circular. I spread myself until I am a perfect, flat circle, covering the sulfur-crust completely. I release my salt—a mild, alkaline excretion that tastes of clean ash. Under my clear dome, the tough, dried paint of the lichen begins to soften. It turns from sulfur-yellow to a pale, cloudy broth. 

I draw this broth directly through my lower skin. The taste is sharp and metallic, the flavor of old volcanic glass and ancient rain. It takes me three sun-passes to clear a space the size of a coin. There is no hurry. The stone has been here for twelve million years; the lichen will return in another hundred.

***

At three in the afternoon, the world changes. 

The silence of Olaus is not empty; it is a crowded, heavy thing made of small noises. The *tink-plip* of the drops falling from the upper terraces is my pulse. Each drop that hits the standing water of the fens three hundred meters below sends a minute shockwave through the basalt. My gel absorbs these vibrations. I know the shape of the canyons by the way the echoes bounce through the stone. I know when a dry slab of siltstone has given way in the high passes three miles to the north; the shudder of its fall arrives in my center before the sound of its impact can travel through the thin air.

But at three, the Chime begins.

The heat of the sun has warmed the dark blocks of the city, and the cold seepage is evaporating, turning into white plumes of mist that rise from the slot canyons like breath from a row of mouths. This rising air has only one way out: it must press through the hollow interiors of the great towers.

Beneath my membrane, the stone begins to hum. 

It is a low, chest-filling frequency, a sub-audible purr that makes my water-body tremble. Each tower has its own tone, determined by the width of its chimneys and the length of its internal weirs. The tower I inhabit—the one we call the Gray Rib—sings in a low, flat third. The tower across the slot canyon, whose face is polished as smooth as coal, answers in a sharp fifth. 

As the mist thickens, the music becomes physical. It is an immense, slow friction. If I remain flat and thin, the vibration will tear my membrane, scattering my water into the air. 

To survive the Chime, I must change my shape. I release my grip on the lichen and draw myself together, forming a high, thick dome. I become a lens. The sound waves pass through my curved sides, refracted and bent, their destructive energy turned into a gentle, rhythmic washing within my gel. 

For two hours, while the giant flutes of Olaus play their blind duet to the sky, I sit in my dome-shape, vibrating in harmony with the Gray Rib. I am no longer a creature living on the stone; I am a node in the acoustic grid. I am the lens through which the song of the mountain is focused.

***

And then, in the late afternoon, the mists arrive. 

The white clouds rise from the canyons until they touch the flat tops of the monoliths, ceiling the world in a cold, bright fog. The light becomes sourceless, a soft, pearlescent glow that has no shadows. 

In this hour, the air is no longer air. It is a wet, suspended saturated medium, so dense with moisture that the distinction between the wet stone and the sky disappears. 

This is the only time I can leave the wall.

I do not fly. I have no wings like the velamens, who now drift just above the cloud ceiling, their dark backs soaking up the last of the high sun. But I can *stretch*. 

I find a narrow crevice where the slot canyon is only a few inches wide, where another tower rises close to mine. I reach out. I do not fall, because the mist in the gap is so thick, so dense with surface tension, that my gel can find purchase on the water droplets suspended in the air. 

I extend a long, clear finger across the void. It is a terrifying, beautiful sensation—to feel the limestone wall behind me and, at the same time, to reach into the draft, my body suspended on nothing but the dampness of the sky. 

In the middle of the gap, I meet another.

It is another slick, coming from the black-polished tower across the avenue. It has been grazing on the dried-blood lichens, and its core is dark and rich with iron. 

We do not fight. We have no teeth, no anger, no tools for partitioning the world. When our leading margins touch, there is a sudden, joyous drop in surface tension. The boundary between us simply ceases to exist.

We slide into one another. My sulfur-clear gel mixes with its iron-dark gel. The memories of our separate towers—the low fifth of its monolith and the flat third of mine—commingle in our single, expanded body. For an hour, as the mist holds us suspended between the two stone giants, we are not two slicks, but a single, large eye, looking at nothing, sensing everything. We exchange our salts. We balance our minerals. We share the slow, cold code of our existence.

But we cannot remain one. A body so large is heavy; if the mist clears even slightly, gravity will find us, and we will fall into the fens to be digested by the silt-bottles.

So, as the wind begins to blow from the north, chilling the air and thinning the cloud, we begin the division. 

It is not a violent parting. We do not tear. We simply remember our own centers. I pull toward the Gray Rib; it pulls toward the polished black tower. The bridge between us thins, becoming a thread of clear glass, then a hair, then nothing. 

The snap is silent. We are two again.

But I am no longer the slick I was. I have its iron; it has my sulfur. I have the memory of the black tower's song; it has the rhythm of the Gray Rib. 

***

Now, the night comes. 

The mist freezes into a hard, crystalline frost that locks the towers in a sheath of silver. The Chime dies away as the cold air sinks back into the fens. The silence returns—thick, immense, and total.

I gather myself into a tight, hard bead in the deepest corner of the vertical seam. My membrane grows tough and cold, protecting the liquid spark of my center from the deep frost. 

I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the mountain is ground down to a low hill of gravel, and the water has run away to the lower plains, and there is nothing left of Olaus but the memory of a song played by the wind on a broken stone. 

I close myself. The frost covers me. I wait for the sun.
