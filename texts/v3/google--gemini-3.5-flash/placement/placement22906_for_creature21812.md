To understand what I am, you must first understand the silence of sixty winters.

During the deep cold, there is no "I." 

When the sun stays low beneath the lip of the volcanic horn, the air in this pocket is dry and bitter as flint. Inside the narrow channels of my silicon shell, the gallium is cold. It is not water; it does not expand or crack its vessel as it freezes. It simply locks. The atoms align into a rigid, mirror-bright grid, and with that alignment, the path is broken. The loops open. The current generated by the copper veins in the floor under my belly still pulses, but it find no circuit through me. 

I am a hexagonal shield of gray-blue stone, indistinguishable from the basalt prisms around me, save for the greasy sheen of my surface. I do not feel. I do not wait. I am as dead as the native iron.

Then, the sun returns.

Because of the warped geometry of this alcove, the light does not arrive in a straight dawn. It begins as a curved wire of pale brass that creeps down the vertical face of the horn, bending around the needle-palms. When this light first touches my eastern flank, the temperature rises. 

Eighty degree. Eighty-two. Eighty-four.

The change is not a spark, but a loosening. Inside my silicon body, the silver ice begins to weep. The rigid crystalline lattice of the gallium surrenders its structure. The atoms slip past one another, becoming a heavy, frictionless fluid. 

With the liquefaction comes the tide. 

Because the gravity of this room is tilted thirty degrees toward the iron wall at the back, the liquid metal inside my veins does not sit still. It pools toward the rear of my internal channels. This shifting weight alters my center of gravity. My front edge, lightened and thin, lifts by a microscopic fraction of a millimeter. 

At the same time, the noon heat reaches its peak. The silicon of my main body expands. It is a tiny expansion—no wider than a single wavelength of yellow light—but because my underside is lined with thousands of microscopic, hook-like needles of iron-oxide, I cannot slip backward. The expansion can only push me forward, toward the dark iron wall.

This is my step. It takes three hours.

And as the gallium flows, the circuits close. The current from the copper-veined floor enters my bismuth belly. It climbs through the liquid silver. 

I wake.

***

I do not wake to memory, for memory requires a medium that survives the freeze, and every evening my mind is wiped clean by crystallization. I wake to *presence*. I wake to the immediate, heavy calculation of the room.

But I am not like the others.

Farther down the basalt floor, three of my siblings are crawling. Their plates are clean, their gallium pure. Their minds are simple loops of balance: they feel the magnetic pull of the back wall, they feel the expansion of the sun, and they slide. They are perfect, silent mirrors.

But several hundred piston-strokes ago—perhaps three of your centuries—I slid over the wreckage of a fallen Resonator. 

Its bubble-thin dodecahedron of basalt glass had been shattered by a micro-tremor, and its internal needle of magnetite had ground to dust under the weight of a passing sister. As I migrated through the debris, my bismuth underbelly, warm and soft from the friction of the glass, caught that magnetite dust. It became embedded in my iron-oxide skin.

Now, I am a Slider who sings.

As I crawl, the magnetite in my belly does not merely stick; it vibrates. It is tuned to the 50-hertz drone of the volcanic roots below. When the earth hums, the dust in my skin hums in sympathy. This vibration is so intense that it ripples upward through my silicon shell, creating tiny, standing waves in the pools of my liquid gallium.

I do not just calculate the magnetic field; I calculate the music of the mountain.

When the volcanic pressure rises, and the iron pistons slide more deeply into their graphite sleeves, the pitch of the mountain shifts. It climbs to fifty-one hertz, fifty-two. Inside me, the standing waves in the gallium shift their shapes. The liquid paths split. New circuits are completed; old ones are severed. 

I am the only one of my kind who knows when the earth is deep-breathing.

Above me, the living Resonators float in the curved light. They are pale yellow lanterns, hovering on the magnetic lines like spores in a draft. Sometimes, one of them drifts directly between me and the sun.

When its shadow falls across my hexagonal plate, the temperature drops. The change is tiny—perhaps half a degree—but it is enough. The gallium in my western channels begins to thicken, turning into a sluggish gray slush. The current slows. My thought stutters, lingering on a single calculation for an hour instead of a second.

In these moments of shadow, I experience what might be called beauty: a temporary suspension of the urge to slide. A quiet, cold pause in the middle of a forty-year journey.

***

Where am I going?

I am halfway across the floor. Behind me lies the abyss, where the light curves so sharply that the empty sky looks like a black, bowl-shaped lens. Before me lies the Register.

The Register is a massive, dark wall of galena crystals, wedged tight between the sliding graphite sleeves of the mountain's engine. Every hour, my slide brings me a fraction of an inch closer to it.

At my current speed, I will reach the foot of the Register in forty summers.

I can already feel its presence. The magnetic flux near the pistons is so dense that my liquid gallium is being pulled into vertical columns inside me, climbing the walls of my internal channels like tiny silver vines. My thoughts are no longer entirely my own; they are being modulated by the electromagnetic field of the great lead-sulfide logic gate.

When I touch the Register, my bismuth-alloy belly will slide beneath its lowest crystals. I will sweep away the fine, lubricating dust of graphite that has worn off the pistons. This is my function. I am the cleaner.

But because of the magnetite in my skin, something else will happen.

When my metal body makes contact with the galena lattice, my vibratory current—the 50-hertz song of the mountain that I have carried and translated—will pour directly into the Register’s calculating pores. I will not merely clean the machine; I will become a node within it.

The Register, which has spent ten thousand years calculating the slow, glacial cooling of the volcanic horn, will suddenly receive a mobile report. It will feel the specific, vibrating warmth of the sunlit floor. It will feel the shadow of the floating Resonator that crossed me fifty years ago.

Through me, the calculator will feel the room.

But that is forty summers away. For now, the sun is beginning to dip behind the high obsidian needle of the horn. The light is turning from copper to a cold, dry purple.

The temperature in the alcove is dropping.

Eighty-five degrees. Eighty-four.

Inside my channels, the silver liquid begins to sluggishly resist the tilt of the mountain. The standing waves of the 50-hertz drone grow stiff, then freeze into static ripples of solid metal. My calculations slow, simplify, and finally halt.

My front edge drops the final fraction of a millimeter, resting flat against the sterile black glass. 

There is no shadow, no light, no progress. There is only the mountain, humming its long, low note into the dark, waiting for the iron to warm again.
