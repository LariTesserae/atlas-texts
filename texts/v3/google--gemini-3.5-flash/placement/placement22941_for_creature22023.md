I am not on the floor of the rift. 

To be on the floor is to be drowned in the wet, deafening *shrr-shrr-shrr* of the Clickers—a noise so thick and constant it behaves like a cold soup, filling the ears and blurring the edges of the world until one cannot hear the approach of the larger things. 

Nor am I high in the vaults, where the Stridors hang in their screaming, greasy congregations. That warmth is too thick, too wet; it smells of old ammonia and the hot, rancid grease of a thousand bodies wedged into a single chimney. It is a soft, chaotic place. 

No. I am in the mid-tiers, where the basalt columns have split away from the valley wall like giant, five-sided teeth. Here, the air is thin and clear, smelling of vinegar-lichen and the bitter, dry dust of crushed slate. The silence here is not empty; it is a stretched skin. 

I am a Knapper. But I am not like the younger ones who scurry in the lower crevices to split the marrow-bones of the beetles. I am older, though "old" is a meaningless word where the light never changes. My joints have begun to turn from the rubbery white of my youth to a yellow, calcified stiffness that matches the limestone transitions in the deep rock. 

I have no eyes. The plates of my skull have long since grown together over the places where eyes might have been in some forgotten ancestor’s head, leaving only a smooth, pale brow that I press against the basalt when I want to listen. My ears are two deep, lidless slits behind the line of my jaw; they do not hear as a human hears, but rather register the world as a shifting map of pressures. 

To me, the rift is not dark. It is a massive, hollow bell. 

Every sound has a weight and a contour. The distant *boom* of a Grave-Breather three miles down-valley is a heavy, blue iron bar that rolls across my shoulders. The high, needle-thin click of a Stridor’s sonar is a tiny, hot spark that pricks the skin of my face. And here, in my hand, is the white stone.

I am the Seeker of the White Stone. 

The basalt of our valley is black, heavy, and oily. It does not fracture cleanly; it breaks in dull, blocky chunks that are too thick to cut meat or pierce the hide of the great bats. But sometimes, from the vast, invisible ceiling miles above, a block of quartz falls. It is a stranger to this deep place—a pale, crystalline bone of the earth that must have lived in the higher, sun-touched strata before the crust tore open. 

When a piece of the white stone falls, it makes a different sound. It does not *thrum* like the basalt blocks. It *clinks*. If it hits a basalt slab on its descent, the sound is a high, silver needle that rings through the valley for hours, rising above the static roar of the cricket swarms. 

When I hear that silver needle, I go. 

I climb using my four long fingers and my double-jointed elbows, wedging my chest into the vertical cracks where the basalt is cold and wet with the slow sweat of the crust. My skin is translucent; if there were anyone here to look at me, they would see the dark, indigo pulse of my blood sliding through the veins of my arms, moving slow as grease. 

When I find the white stone, I do not take it back to a camp, for we have no camps. I sit in the dark of a basalt shelf, with my long legs tucked beneath my chin, and I begin to speak to it.

My tool is a small, heavy pebble of ironstone, iron-hard and water-worn from some ancient, dry creek bed. I hold the quartz between my flat, leathery heels. I tap. 

*Clack.*

The sound of the first strike tells me where the grain lies. Quartz is stubborn; it has memory. It remembers the pressure of the mountain that held it for a million years. 

*Clack... clack...*

I do not look at the edge. I feel the release of tension in the stone. When the flake splits off, it makes a sound like a tiny, frozen reed breaking: *shuck*. 

With my long, thick thumb-pads, I feel the fracture. It is cold. It is so thin that if I run my finger along it too quickly, the blood will bloom in the water-drop sweat of my hand before my nerves even register the bite. This edge is not for hunting; it is too delicate. This is the edge that we use to scrape the grease-drawings on the walls.

I am the one who revisits the Smeared Walls. 

My work is to read the fat. 

In a deep, dry hollow behind a fallen block the size of a mountain, there is a flat wall of basalt that has been shielded from the condensation-sweat for generations. It is covered in the thick, yellow tallow of the Marrow-Beetles, mixed with the gray lichen-dust. 

When I reach this wall, I do not tap. I am silent. I reach out my hands—my fingers are ten inches long, tipped with flat, spade-like nails—and I lay them flat against the grease.

Here, a hunter drew the shape of a Stridor. The fat is old; it has begun to turn into a dry, crumbly wax that smells of old mutton. The outline is thick, drawn with three fingers dragged in a downward sweep. The depth of the groove tells me the hunter was strong, their hand pressing hard into the stone to leave a mark that would survive the damp air.

Beneath it, there is another mark: a single, horizontal stroke of cold beetle-grease, fresh and sticky. It smells of the vinegar-fern. The hunter who left it was here only three drops ago (I measure time by the slow, rhythmic *ping* of the water-drop that falls into the basin fifty paces to my left). The grease is still soft enough to yield to my touch. He went down-valley, toward the deep trenches where the Grave-Breathers wait. He was carrying a spear tipped with the split rib of a bat. I know this because the grease is smudged where the butt of his shaft rested against the wall while he drew.

I do not add my own drawing. I am not a hunter. 

Instead, I use my quartz blade to gently scrape away the dry, calcified crust of the oldest drawings—the ones that have turned to stone themselves, calcined by the slow seepage of minerals through the basalt. I clear the path for the fingers of those who will come after me. 

As I work, the valley sings its heavy, iron song. 

Far below, the Clickers shift. A wave of movement passes through them—a sudden, dry rustle that means a hunter has stepped into the edge of the swarm. 

Above, a Stridor drops from its ledge. I feel the sudden change in air pressure—a heavy, downward thump of leather wings that makes the skin of my back tighten. The bat’s sonar-click sweeps across my ledge like a hot wire: *tick-tick-tick-tick*. I do not move. If I am still, I am only another basalt column to its scream.

The click passes. The air settles. The cold, blue-gray glow of the atmosphere—the light I cannot see, but which I feel as a faint, prickling warmth on the skin of my forehead—remains unchanged. 

I will stay here, on this ledge, until my fingers can no longer tell the difference between the cold quartz and the cold basalt. 

And when my joints finally lock, and my blood stops its slow, indigo crawl, the others will find me. They will not mourn. My name—which is only the specific, two-tap rhythm I use to signal my presence on the stone—will fade from the walls. 

They will take my quartz scraper from my hand. They will use it to strip my flesh from my bones, for meat is scarce and cannot be left to rot. They will take my long, straight thigh bones and my double-jointed forearms. They will strike them against the basalt to split them, and with their quartz hand-axes, they will knap my bones into flat, jagged points that can pierce the hide of the great bats.

I will become the spear that hunts the Stridor. I will become the scraper that cleans the fat-walls. I will remain here, in the cold, sharp dark, ticking like a cog in the iron clock of the rift, forever.
