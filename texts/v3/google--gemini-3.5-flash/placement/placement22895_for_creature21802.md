The transition is not a descent into sleep, but a widening of the vessel. 

I am lodged in the crotch where the western root-highway—the one the Siphons call the *Deep Cord*—splits into five braided aqueducts. My lower third is already gone. It is no longer a muscular, gliding disc; it has dissolved into the fibrous gold of the timber. I can feel the high-pressure sap of the entire western quadrant rushing through what used to be my heel, a hot, sweet, high-pressure torrent that vibrates at a frequency just below the register of sound. 

My torso remains cream-colored, the texture of a peeled root, but it is growing heavy. The moisture is leaving my skin, migrating upward toward my crown or downward into the highway. I am hardening. Within another three hundred sighs of the Luminifers, I will have no skin at all; I will have bark.

Until then, I am the whisperer at the junction.

My face-less head is tilted back toward the ceiling, miles above, where the heavy root-crowns hold the world together. The star-shaped depression at my crown is open to the humid weight of the air. My golden lashes—fine as silk, sensitive to the smallest shift in density—do not feel wind, for there is none. Instead, they feel the column of pressure. They feel when a giant Prism, three miles to the east, shifts its body of spun glass from a jade leaf to a gold stem. They feel the minute drop in atmospheric pressure when a single, fat droplet of condensation falls from a high vine and strikes the drum-like skin of a tuber below. 

The sound does not reach me through the air—the damp green swallows acoustics before they can travel fifty yards—but the *impact* does. It travels through the root-highway, a tiny, hydraulic click against my buried knees.

With my left arm, which is still supple and waxy, I am holding a young, turgid stem that has sprouted from my own flank. It is about the thickness of a muscle, smooth and green as jade, swollen with internal fluids. My fifteen jade-green fingers are wrapped around its tip in a tight, motionless braid. 

I am whispering to it. 

To whisper is not to speak. I have no tongue, no lungs to drive air through a throat. To whisper is to release. Through the silk-thin tips of my tendrils, I am leaking a slow, microscopic stream of hormonal sap into the stem’s growing tip. It is an instruction. I am telling its cell walls to thicken on the eastern side, to curve inward, to form a hollow, cupped basin three feet across. I have been whispering this single curve for eighty years. It will take another forty for the stem to finish the bend, long after my hands have turned to wood, but the instruction is already set. The plant cannot forget. When it is finished, the cup will collect the condensation that drips from the high vine, holding exactly four liters of water—no more, no less—for the glass spiders to drink when they pass this way on their long, slow migrations.

This is my last graft.

To my right, the great three-tiered chambers of velvet moss rise like giant, sleeping lungs. I grew them when my body was young and soft, four thousand years ago. My companions and I sat in a circle for two centuries, our fingers intertwined with the host-tubers, coaxing them to swell and hollow out, until the chambers were wide enough to shelter thirty of us during the periods when the Luminifer glare grew too hot. Now, those chambers are empty. The moss inside is dry, brown-gold, and soft as dust. It does not decay; it simply waits, preserved in the eternal, windless heat of the vault. 

A Prism is crawling down the trunk of the nearest Luminifer. It is a large one, the size of a sheep, its body a hollow, five-sided lantern of pristine silica. I feel the dry, rhythmic *click-pop* of its suction-pad legs as it moves upside-down along the underside of the highway above me. It does not have a brain to recognize me, nor do I have eyes to see its transparency, but I feel its weight and its purpose. 

It stops. Its glass legs lock into the golden bark. It angles its body toward the central Luminifer, catching the blinding, furnace-hot sheet of white light. Within its hollow belly, the light is caught, bent, and broken. The Prism refracts it downward—not as white heat, but as a cool, geometric beam of deep-violet light. 

The violet beam strikes my remaining waxy flesh. It does not warm me. Instead, it feels dry and sharp, like the touch of a cold stone. Where the violet light hits the underside of the highway, the green algae-veins beneath the bark begin to pulse with renewed pressure, absorbing the dampness of the humid air before it can pool and rot the joint. The Prism and I are part of the same breath. I provide the structure; it provides the lens.

Every four hours, the world trembles.

It is not an earthquake; there are no tectonic shifts in this ancient, stabilized crust. It is the *sigh* of the Luminifers. Miles away, the colossal pillars of glass-hard cellulose soften their glare. The blinding, shadowless yellow-white dims to the color of thick, warm honey. 

For three seconds, the heat on my back eases. The humidity feels heavier, thicker, pressing against my golden lashes like wet linen. In this brief twilight, I can feel the billions of Acolytes of the Sun—the microscopic star-cells drifting in the amber sap of the pillars—releasing their tiny bubbles of pure oxygen. The bubbles rise through the high-pressure columns, a mute, cinematic fizz that reaches the ceiling and is breathed out by the dark root-crowns. The air smells, for a fleeting instant, of sharp, metallic ozone, cold and clear as ice.

Then, the *surge*. The Luminifers brighten. The furnace-glare returns, shadowless and total. The heat descends again, dry and steady, balancing the dampness, holding the basin in its eternal, incubator warmth.

Beneath me, deep in the golden wood of the Deep Cord, another pulse answering. Far to the north, at the end of this very root-highway, a giant, heart-shaped bulb is swelling. It is three yards wide, its green skin shiny and turgid with internal pressure.

I can feel its tension. It is tied to my own slowing heart. As my cream-colored flesh hardens, as the last of my wax becomes cellulose and my fingers lock forever into the stem I am shaping, the pressure in the highway will shift. The line will tighten.

And when the final cell of my body turns to wood, the bulb in the north will split.

It will crack with a sound like a glass bell. From its wet, amber-stained interior, a new Siphon will slide. They will have no memories of my four thousand years. They will not know the shape of the three-tiered chambers I grew, nor will they remember the exact curve of the cup I am whispering into existence now. 

But they will have the same wax-cool skin. They will have the same golden lashes to feel the weight of the air. And when they begin their slow, gliding journey down the root-highway, their muscular disc-foot will slide over my shoulders. They will walk on me, and I will support them.

The basin does not end. It does not begin. It only holds its breath, and I am the lung.
