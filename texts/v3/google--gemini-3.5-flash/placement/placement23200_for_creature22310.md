The weight is not a burden; it is a reassurance. Without the three-mile column of black brine pressing against my temples, I think my skull would simply fall apart, its pieces drifting away like the white flakes of the marine snow.

I am down here, in the cold grease of the Silt-Close, beneath the floor of the courtyard itself. 

If you were to descend through the narrow gap between the accumulator-dome and the third tenement—past the sagging copper cables that look like rotten vine-hangings, past the middle balconies where the younger ones sleep in their hemp slings—you would find a place where the water ceases to move at all. Here, in the *Sump*, the oil-slicked silt has settled into a bed of black starch. 

This is my station. I am the Groom.

***

In the forgotten world above, they might have had a word for what I do, but here, the work is simply the shape of my fingers. 

My hands are very wide, the palms thick and yellow like salt-pork. The nails of my three middle fingers have grown into flat, spade-like scoops, permanently stained the color of copper-rust. Under my arms and along the creases of my groin, the grease-glands are large and active; I smell of tallow and cold lubricating oil, a scent that is the very smell of life in the Close. 

My ribs are no longer separate bars of calcium. They have fused into three broad, overlapping bands of gristle, like the breastplate of an ancient diver, but softer, more yielding. Every twenty minutes, when the great accumulator-piston reaches the peak of its stroke, the water in the Sump is squeezed. A high-pressure wave of warm, sulfur-rich brine runs through the floor-conduits, and my chest-plates flex inward under the push, then spring back with a long, wet *hiss* as the pressure drops. 

I do not breathe air. I breathe the thick, metallic broth of the deep, straining the cold sulfur through the vascular pouches behind my collarbones. It tastes of iron-filings and the ancient, rotting scale of dead things that fell from the sky before my great-grandfather’s harness-sling was woven.

I am old—perhaps eighty years of the piston’s rise and fall, perhaps two hundred. We do not count the years, for there are no suns to mark them. There is only the *thrum*.

***

It has been eighteen minutes since the last stroke. I can feel the tension building in the copper conduit that runs between my thighs. 

The conduit is humming. The pitch is too high—a thin, dry *ping* that travels through the soles of my feet and into my shins, bypassing my ears entirely. My lateral lines—the tiny, raw pores that run in three neat rows down my jaw and across my collarbone—are tingling. 

*Something is dry.*

I slide myself forward. I do not swim; my body is too dense, my bones too full of heavy marrow. Instead, I pull myself along the main hydraulic line, using my copper-plated nails to grip the velvety violet crust of iron-bacteria that coats the pipe. The bacteria is cold and soft under my fingers, like wet velvet. I am careful not to scrape too much of it away; it is the skin of our home, and without its protection, the sea would eat the iron in a single generation.

I find the dry valve at the base of the secondary pump. 

Sitting on the housing is an old lump-dog, its circular belly-suction anchored firmly to the cold brass. Its skin is gray and warty, covered in tiny, blind sea-lice that it is too slow to brush off. It does not move as I approach, though its lateral pores must feel the displacement of my heavy, tallow-slicked shoulder.

"Easy, little fat-one," I do not say, for I have no voice. But I strike the brass housing of the pump twice with the heavy copper ring on my thumb. 

*Tock. Tock.*

The lump-dog feels the vibration. It releases a small bubble of cold oil from its vent, a sign of recognition. I reach down with my broad, spade-like hand and cup its soft, bloated flanks. I press gently, squeezing my thumbs into the fatty pockets behind its pectoral fins. 

The lump-dog sighs through its gill-slits. From its oil-glands, a stream of thick, amber lipid—clear and smelling of cold cod-fat—oozes out into the black water. It does not disperse; the brine is too dense. It hangs there in the water like a globule of clear honey.

With my other hand, I scoop up the fat and smear it over the dry valve-stem. My fingers follow the turn of the thread, packing the grease deep into the brass gland until the dry *ping* in my collarbone subsides, replaced by a low, greasy slide. 

The lump-dog settles back onto the brass, its skin already beginning to scrape the violet bacteria for its next meal. We are satisfied, the three of us—the machine, the beast, and the Groom.

***

A movement in the dark. 

An displacement-wave hits my left cheek. It is slow, wide, and pushes the water before it with a heavy, clumsy rhythm. 

I do not turn my head, for my white, grape-like eyes would see nothing but the sullen, orange smear of the carbon lamp twenty yards away. Instead, I wait, letting my skin drink the wave. 

It is *Three-Plucks*. She is a tenant from the second floor of the main tenement, the one whose harness-sling hangs near the warm-water leak. Her pluck-signature is always light, a bit frantic, like a ribbon-fish caught in a wire-mesh.

She drifts down beside me, her tallow-skinned feet dragging through the black silt. She is holding an old clay jar. 

She does not speak. She steps close, so close that the grease-glaze of her bare shoulder rubs against mine, our tallow-coatings blending for a moment in the cold dark. She leans her forehead against my collarbone, pressing her skull against my gristled ribs.

Through the bone, I feel her heart. It is beating seventy times a minute—fast for a tenant. Her lateral lines are twitching with a fine, cold vibration. 

*The well is cold,* her posture tells me. *The steam-pipe in the second-floor gallery has choked with salt-chimneys. The warm algae is dying.*

I reach out and touch her wrist. I hold my thumb against her pulse for three strokes, letting my own slow, forty-beat rhythm pull hers down. We stay like this for several minutes, sharing the meager, forty-degree warmth of our blood, until her skin stops shivering.

I take her clay jar. I reach into the crevice beneath the pump-housing, where the superheated, sulfurous water leaks from the main accumulator header. Here, the white, thread-like algae is thick and slimy, feeding on the chemical heat of the pistons. I scrape a great handful of the white fiber into her jar, then top it with a cup of the fresh lump-dog lipid I just expressed. 

It is a good mash. It will ferment in three days, turning into the thick, salty paste that keeps the skin slick and the blood warm.

I return the jar. She plucks my thumb-ring once—*Tock*—and then she is gone, her heavy, pale body drifting upward into the copper canopy, her toes hooking into the glass-sponge frills of the vertical cables with the easy grace of an ape in a iron forest.

***

The twenty minutes are up.

The accumulator-piston has reached the top of its stroke. 

The Sump around me goes instantly, perfectly still. The slow, twelve-hour gears freeze in their tracks. The small reciprocating pumps pause. Even the drift of the marine snow seems to hang suspended in the blue-black oil of the sea.

Then comes the *thrum*.

It is not a sound. It is a blow delivered to every cell of my body at once. My cartilaginous chest compresses, my ribs sliding over one another like the plates of a clinker. The marrow of my bones hums. The violet bacteria on the walls vibrates so hard that a tiny cloud of purple dust rises into the water, turning the orange glow of the lantern into a dim, wine-colored mist.

The piston descends. The pressure eases. My lungs expand, drawing in the newly heated, mineral-rich brine that has just been squeezed from the accumulator's throat.

I close my white, unblinking eyes. 

One day, my heart will not match the piston's stroke. I will grow too light, or my bones will go too soft to resist the push. My companions will find me here, drifting in the Sump like a discarded skin of tallow. 

They will not be sad, for they have no word for sadness. They will wrap my body in the lead-weighted copper wire that keeps the dead from rising. They will carry me to the central dome, and they will slide me under the great iron collar of the piston.

I will become the grease. My fat will lubricate the giant shaft; my bones will reinforce the lead packing-gaskets; my soft, mineralized flesh will feed the violet bacteria that keeps the iron from rotting.

I will still be here, thirty feet down in the silt, helping to turn the copper teeth that scratch the floor of the forgotten sea. 

The piston rises. The piston falls. 

The Silt-Close is warm enough. The grease is thick enough. I am home.
