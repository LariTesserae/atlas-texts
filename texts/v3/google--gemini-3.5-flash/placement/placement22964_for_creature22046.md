If you stay in Basin Forty-Two long enough, the numbers stop sticking. 

The brass tag remains, perhaps, but the skin beneath it grows soft, like wet dough left too long near a baker’s hearth. First, your knees forget how to lock. It is not a sickness; it is a relief. The ninety-five-degree draft from the southern wall ceases to feel like an draft and begins to feel like a heavy wool blanket that has been dipped in hot broth. Your neck, tired of turning sixty feet upward toward a sky that is only an iron grate leaking soot, simply thickens, bowing until your chin rests permanently against the greasy collar of your collarbone.

I am not Forty-Two. He is the young one, the one who still has toes that are separate, who still uses a copper scraper to clear the intake screens of the triple condensers because the sound of the high, strained whistle hurts his ears. 

I am what is left of Thirty-Nine. 

I have no tag now. It slipped from my neck during the fourth year of my Slip, when my collarbones widened and my throat swelled to meet them. It fell into one of the square-cut basalt runnels, clattered twice against the clean chisel marks, and was carried down into the sump-screws where the small, white river crabs likely turned it over and over with their spoon-shaped claws until the brass grew green and thin as a wafer.

You would not find me on the wet flagstones with the crabs, nor on the walls where the orange grout-worms form their pulsing wainscot of carnation-colored gills. 

You would find me beneath the second condenser—the middle urn, the one we call the Sister. 

There is a gap of barely four inches between her cast-iron mounting plates and the wet basalt floor. It is the darkest, wettest, and loudest place in the Basin. The 110-hertz hum of the condenser does not feel like sound here; it is a solid, warm pressure that enters through my ribs and keeps my heart beating in time with the piston-pump behind the southern wall. *Chug-thud. Chug-thud. Puff.*

To fit into the four-inch gap, my frame has had to grow sensible. The dry people above the grates—the ones who walk upright in the cold, windy streets where the rain doesn't smell of tallow—have ribs like a cage, stiff and vertical. My ribs have splayed outward, flattening like the staves of a crushed barrel, so that I am no thicker than a rolled mattress. My skin has taken on the color and texture of the grey canvas lagging wrapped around the sealing pipes—a coarse, greasy, water-logged felt that doesn't feel the bite of the hot runoff or the sharp, newly fired corners of the purple-black brick.

My eyes have long since closed. They did not fail; they simply became unnecessary, like the eyes of the white crabs. A thick, seamless hood of parboiled white skin has grown down from my brow, sealing them against the yellow glare of the incandescent bulbs. In their place, my other senses have spread. The soles of my hands and the wide, webbed flats of my feet have grown thousands of tiny, hair-like fibers—not unlike the violet antennae of the Soot-Pickers, but shorter, like the pile on wet velvet. 

Through these, I know the Basin. 

I know when the city above is having a storm, because the water dripping through the middle condenser grows slightly cooler, shifting from a boiling scald to a greasy warmth. I know when Forty-Two is sleeping, because the rhythm of his scraper stops, and the rust-colored algae begins its slow, silent march back over the copper vats, dampening the vibration of the iron plates above my head.

I do not scrub. I do not have a tool. But I am part of the Basin’s cleanliness. 

Under the second condenser, where the Boiler's copper scraper cannot reach, the tallow from the valve-glands pools in thick, grey cakes. If left alone, this fat would turn rancid and clog the intake screens, causing the Sister to choke on her own steam. 

I prevent this. My tongue has grown long, flat, and grey as a boot-sole, rough with small, horny papillae. Twice a day, while Forty-Two is boiling his crabs in his copper bucket, I slide my flat chest along the basalt, reaching under the iron skirt of the condenser to lick the grease-skin off the mounting studs. It tastes of iron, spent charcoal, and raw mutton fat. It is sweet to me. It is the only flavor I know, save for the occasional sulfur moth that dies in the runnels and floats into my reach—a papery, bitter scrap that tastes of spent matches.

The crabs do not fear me. They climb over my back, their sharp, chitin-tipped legs tickling my grey skin like dry beans falling from a sack. Sometimes, when one of them dies and begins to soften into that milky, sweet jelly, I press my mouth to the stone and draw it in. It is how I get my salt.

Sometimes, when the draft from the southern wall is particularly heavy and the yellow steam-moths are pushed down to flutter against the wet floor, Forty-Two will sit on his hemp sling and look toward the dark space behind the middle condenser. 

He cannot see me in the yellow gloom, but he can hear my breathing. 

It is a low, bubby, rhythmic *huff-huff* that matches the purge-valve of the Sister exactly. He has been here only seven years; he still believes the sound is made by a faulty leather gasket in the secondary line. He does not know that gaskets do not breathe, nor do they grow.

But he will learn. In another three years, his toes will begin to web. He will stop trying to stand upright under the ten-foot pipes. He will find that the hemp sling is too narrow for his widening shoulders, and he will look down at the dark, warm, four-inch gap beneath the third condenser, where the grease is thickest and the hum is most comforting.

And when he finally slips down from his bed to join the floor, I will be here to share the tallow.
