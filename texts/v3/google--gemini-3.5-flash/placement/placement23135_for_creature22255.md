I do not enter this world through a door, or by falling. I begin in the transitions of the skin. 

First goes the salt-water: the wet humors of the eye, the slickness of the tongue, the soft, sweating tissue of the joints. They do not evaporate; they are *replaced*. The oil-glands at the base of my throat secrete a thick, yellowish sebum that smells of cedar-terpene and toasted starch, sealing the cracks before they can bleed. My lungs, once soft and pink as sea-anemones, harden into a series of dry, stacked birch-bark baffles that catch the yellow spore-dust and leaf-ash, extracting a slow, warm combustion from the dry air.

By the time I reach the bedrock, I am no longer a soft thing. I am **The Stave**.

***

### The Grafted State

You would find me at the **Junction of the Three Bellows**, where the valley floor dips into a deep, circular basin of white root-burl and the three massive pneumatic conduits of the world meet. 

I am three times the height of a Curn, but I do not walk. Many seasons ago—perhaps eighty *Thrusts*, though the Curn do not count in years—I chose the settling. I stood in the deep, shifting desert of frass until my heels, then my calves, and finally my knees split into dozens of tough, fibrous taproots. These roots bored down through twelve feet of sliding seed-husks until they met the bone-hard, warm pavement of the deeper wood. 

There, they grafted. I became part of the world’s plumbing.

My body is a wedge of dense, iron-hard heartwood, the color of burnt nutmeg. My chest is a hollow chamber, inside of which sits my hearth: a slow-burning cake of compressed pith-meal and glow-bladder oil that keeps my internal joints warm and my resins liquid. Where my face used to be is a flat, shield-shaped plate of polished black lignite. If you were to look closely into the two cloudy amber lenses set into my brow, you would see nothing but a faint, orange ember glowing deep within my skull.

I have no neck. I cannot look around. I do not need to.

***

### The Great Vibration

To be the Stave is to be a tongue that does not speak, but through which the entire world sings.

Because I am rooted directly into the bedrock-burl, the Chamber of the Bast is not a sound to me; it is a physical weight. The deafening, scraping shriek of the liana-ceiling two miles above does not register in my ears—which have long since fused into solid, ringing blocks of dry horn—but in my ribs.

I feel:
* **The High Sieve:** A rapid, paper-thin ticking in my collar-bone. It is the migration of the six-foot Sieve-Moths, their wings brushing the upper leaves as they filter the spores.
* **The Sump-Slide:** A heavy, rhythmic grinding in my lower pelvis. It is a river of a billion marble-seeds, flowing down a three-mile slope of cork-peat, pooling into the basin behind me.
* **The Pulse:** Every seventeen minutes, the pneumatic tubes beneath my roots draw in a great, hot whistle of dry air from the core. It makes my hollow chest-cavity hum like a dry cello. 

My primary work is dampening. In this valley, the friction of the growing world can build to a resonance so violent that it can shatter the chitinous plates of the young Curn or crack the delicate, resin-glass wings of the Sheer-Wasps. My body is a shock-absorber. I lean my lead-heavy ironwood back against the Spindle—an ancient, silver-gray trunk that has ceased to grow—and my fibrous interior chest eats the highest, most destructive frequencies, converting them into a gentle, radiating heat.

I am the town stove. I am the anchor of the quiet.

***

### The Office of the Claw

Because I cannot move, the world comes to me.

The Curn arrive in the warm darkness before the Thrust. They do not speak; they do not have the lungs for it. Instead, they climb onto my shoulders, their dry, segmented joints clicking like beetles. 

They bring me **Tallow**. They rub the yellow, waxy fat of the borer-worms into the seams of my shoulders and thighs, keeping my wooden shingles from splitting under the dry heat. They carry gourds of **Pith-Flour**, which they pour down my open throat-funnel to feed my internal hearth.

In return, I am their scribe and their map.

With their scraper-tools—the curved claws of dead Sheer-Wasps—they have carved hundreds of concentric, spiral grooves into my chest and legs. These are the *Logs of the Basin*. 

When a herder wants to know if the seed-river is safe to cross with a Tumble-Drag, he does not ask. He kneels in the frass at my feet, inserts his thin, needle-fine finger-claw into the groove on my left shin, and rotates his arm. 

As his claw follows the scar, my body vibrates the answer into his hand:
`[Three shifts ago, the white root below the basin swelled. The seed-river is shallow. The Tumble-Drags will bottom out on the bone-wood. Wait two Thrusts.]`

Sometimes, the domestic Weave-Cats will climb up my side, their curved cane-hooks finding easy purchase in my rough bark. They curl around my neck-plate, their bodies rattling with a dry, basketwork purr. They are hot to the touch, smelling of old pine-resin and sun-baked dust. I do not brush them off. Their weight is a comfortable counter-pressure against the vibration of the conduits.

***

### The Thrust

The Curn have been gone for three hours. Their heel-taps told me they were moving up-valley, toward the high, silver-cork burls where the air is smelling of sweet, fresh-split latex. The Weave-Cats left with them, leaping from branch to branch like dry sticks blowing in a gale.

I am alone in the basin.

The air is changing. The smell of toasted oats has grown sharp, replaced by the burning, vinegar-sour tang of pressurized sap-gas. The small, orange glow-bladders on the ceiling are flickering, their oil-wells trembling.

The bedrock beneath my roots begins to scream. It is not the sound of breaking; it is the sound of *tension*.

Fifty yards to my left, the ground—the solid pavement of bone-white root—begins to bulge. The dry plates of frass slide off it in screaming, golden cascades, exposing the pale, ribbed wood beneath. A crack appears. It is a hairline at first, then a foot wide, breathing out a draft of hot, white starch-dust that smells of bread.

*CRACK.*

The sound hits my chest-plate like a physical blow. A new trunk, thick as a cathedral spire and wet with bubbling, glass-clear latex, punches through the bedrock. It does not grow slowly; it lunges ten yards into the air in a single, sputtering jerk, spraying a shower of hot, yellow bark-shingles that rattle against my ironwood shoulders like hail.

The Spindle behind me—the giant trunk I lean against—groans. The new growth is pressing against its ribs. 

My roots tighten. The tension in my wooden hips is immense; for a second, I feel the terrifying temptation of the dry: *to snap*. To let the resin inside me harden into glass and let my body shatter into ten thousand toothpicks under the weight of the expanding forest.

Instead, I draw deep from the starch-heater in my core. The heat rises through my grain. My internal resins soften, becoming elastic. I yield. I bend three inches to the north, my shoulder-shingles sliding over one another with a dry, grinding *churrr*.

The new trunk settles. The screaming in the bedrock subsides into a low, throbbing grunt.

The Thrust is over.

***

### The Aftermath

The basin is different now. The path the Curn used is gone, crushed beneath the white, sticky flank of the new tower-shoot. The seed-river has shifted its course, its dry, whispering slide now pooling around my left knee.

I am still here. My joints are hot, smoking slightly where the friction was greatest. The golden resin is already weeping from my split shoulder-seam, hardening into a bright, clear amber scab that will seal the wood before the borer-worms can smell the starch within.

Above, the Tinder-Spiders are already descending on their yellow latex threads, their small, dry bodies clicking as they spin spark-webs across the newly opened fissures. 

In a few hours, the first Curn scout will return. He will climb down the sticky, white side of the new shoot. He will see me, half-buried in the new debris, smelling of scorched cedar and fresh wax.

He will walk over, lift his wooden hand, and press his scraper against my leg to read the new shape of the valley. And I will vibrate the path into his bones.
