There is a precise point on the western wall of the fissure, four tiers down from the lip where the wild gentians sometimes drop their microscopic seed, where the vertical street of packing-stone curves to avoid a natural knuckle in the limestone. 

If you were to look there, you would find me.

I am not the traveler on the bridge, nor am I the hunter in the silk shroud. I am the transition. 

For seventy-four winters of the outer world—though here we measure time only by the thickening of the moss-skins—I have been leaning my weight into this knuckle of rock. My lower four limbs are no longer distinct from the ledge; they have long since ceased to be flesh of wool and blue skin. The lime-dust, carried down on the dry drafts from the high plain, has filled the hollows of my felted coat. The delicate, unspilling moisture that beads upon my shoulders has mixed with that dust, turning it into a fine, wet cement that has set, grain by grain, year by year, until my thighs and hips are fused to the bedding-plane of the slate. 

I am becoming the buttress. 

My left middle arm is raised. It has been raised since the great settling thirty years ago, when a hairline fracture developed in the floor of the communal cistern above us. My fingers, three small pegs of cartilage, are wedged beneath the lintel-stone of a dwelling belonging to a family whose names I no longer pulse, for they have already move upward to the dry tiers. My hand is the bracket that keeps their door-frame true. If I were to relax the tension in those three fingers—an act that would now require an act of physical self-fracture—the river-shell disc that serves as their door would stick in its groove. 

So I do not move. I do not wish to.

To a creature of the wind and the sun, this would be a burial. To me, it is an exquisite settling into the grain of things. The cold of the stone is not a hostile force; it is a steady, reassuring pressure that keeps the soft, wet core of my inner life from spilling away. I feel the mountain through my knees. I feel the slow, heavy weight of the northern plains pressing down from five yards above—not as a burden, but as a great, silent hand holding the world together.

I have only one eye that still pulses. 

The left has gone dark and hard, its obsidian sphere now completely frozen, reflecting the sourceless, white glare of the cleft like a tiny, polished shield. But my right eye still beats. Its light is thin now, the color of skimmed goat’s milk, and the rhythm has slowed to a long, meditative swell. I pulse once every five hours. 

When the young ones pass—their wool still soft and grey, untroubled yet by the plaster-crust—they do not look at me with pity. They pause on the bone-bridge opposite my station. Their dark eyes swell with a quick, violet-ringed brightness, asking if the lintel is holding. 

I wait. The light gathers in my remaining eye. I let it rise—a slow, warm, white circle that takes forty minutes to reach its peak. 

*It holds,* I tell them. *The stone is sweet. The weight is balanced.*

They do not speak. They receive the light, their eyes dimming in a respectful, rhythmic decay, and then they lift their feet—one, then the other, with the agonizing, beautiful care of those who know that every step wears away a fraction of the world’s silence.

Sometimes, a milk-beetle comes to visit. It is my clean-keeper. Its white, domed back is cold against my ribs as it stays with me for three days, its small mandibles scraping away the dead flakes of my slouched wool and the excess lime-dust that threatens to choke the pore-paths of my chest. It is a dry, tickling sensation, the closest thing to a caress that exists in this silent city. I reward it by letting the condensation collect on my chin-hairs, thick and mineral-heavy, so it may drink the sweet, filtered water that my body has drawn from the limestone.

We are very quiet, the beetle and I. The spider above us in the dark cornice does not even twitch its leg-hairs; it knows we are part of the landscape now.

Soon—perhaps in another dozen moss-shears, when the cistern above has completely sealed its leak with its own scale—my right eye will pulse its last, slow white beat. The light will pool in the center of the dark glass, flicker once like a dying spark in a deep well, and go out. 

The beetle will clean my face one last time, polishing the black spheres of my eyes until they are as bright as the river-shells. My neighbors will not move me. They will build the new path directly over my shoulders, using my calcified neck as a step to reach the higher terraces where the star-moss grows thickest. 

I will feel the pressure of their six-legged walk—light as dry grass, deliberate as the stars—passing over me for a thousand years. I will be the threshold. I will be the quiet road. I will be the very thing that keeps the silence whole.
