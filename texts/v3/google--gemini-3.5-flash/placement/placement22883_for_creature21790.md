The transition from the wet world to this place is not a fall, but a evaporation. 

You must let go of the breath first. The wet lung is too loud here; it rustles like dry paper in a room where nothing has moved since the glaciers melted. You must let the moisture leave your tongue, your skin, your eyes, until what is left is a clean, dry weight that does not need to rise or fall.

If you stay long enough, you do not remain on the edge of the basin. The twilight there is too restless, still tainted by the distant, blue memory of the sky. You slide inward, toward the center, where the darkness is thick enough to have a texture—like warm, dry velvet that has never been washed.

You do not become the Lithophyte-Net, because the Net is too busy. It is vast, fifty yards of hollow glass lace, and it is always listening, always waiting for the next *tick* of a salt grain. It has a geologic hunger.

Instead, you find yourself in the center, on the flat basalt slab, beneath the middle of the three black chert tools. 

You are the tool itself, but you are also the space beneath it. In this silence, the distinction between the stone and the shadow it casts has long since dissolved.

***

To be the middle blade is to be both a sky and a floor.

Above you is the weight of the dark chamber—the great, silent dome of limestone that has forgotten the ocean that made it. Below you is the basalt, which is not dead stone, but a slow, warm heart. You feel the two-degree difference of the basalt against your flat under-belly. It is a steady, ancient pulse that has no rhythm; it is simply *there*, an unchanging column of heat that rises around your glassy flanks, carrying the microscopic stars of the Mica-Drifters upward in their long, silent spirals.

You are made of chert—dense, glassy, and black. Your body was formed by the slow, intense pressure of silica filling the cavities of ancient chalk, but your shape was given to you by a sudden, violent intelligence. You still remember the shock of the antler bill that struck you, the sharp *clack* that sent your extra flakes flying into the dark, leaving you with three perfect, scalloped faces. You are sharp enough to cut skin, but there is no skin here to cut.

For five thousand years, you have rested on your left side. 

On your original journey—when you were carried in a pouch of split hide that smelled of grease and river-silt—you were a tool of preparation. You were meant to scrape the fat from the inside of a deer’s skin, to make it soft enough for a child to wear. You still have the slight, polished sheen on your leading edge where you met the resistance of cold flesh.

But that was a brief, noisy mistake in your history. The hand that held you was wet, warm, and constantly dying. It dropped you here, on this warm basalt shelf, and in doing so, it gave you your true purpose.

You became a roof.

***

Because your belly is slightly concave, you do not lie completely flat against the basalt. You arch over a dome of air that is less than a millimeter high. In this microscopic cavern, where the light has never entered and the air is perfectly still, you are the sky.

To the Oil-Gleaners, you are an immense, curved vault of black glass. 

They do not know the limestone shelf above, or the basket-weave of the Lithophyte-Net. To them, the universe is three inches long and one millimeter high. They live in the scent of the thumb-print.

When the toolmaker laid you down, their thumb pressed hard against your ventral face. The heat of that pressure left behind a thin, invisible layer of oil—a landscape of microscopic ridges and valleys made of lipids, salt, and the wax of human skin. In the dry air of the basin, these oils did not turn rancid; they became solid, like amber.

The Gleaners are on your underside now. You do not feel them as individual movements, for they move too slowly for that. Instead, they are a faint, continuous shift in your state of being—a microscopic dry tickle, like the weight of dust settling on a mirror. 

They are grazing on the valley of the print. A single Gleaner will spend eighty years traveling from the base of the thumb-print’s whorl to its crest, scraping up the five-thousand-year-old salt with legs like moth-hairs. They are so small that their entire population has not yet altered the shape of the print by a fraction of a micron.

They are your children. You protect them from the heavy silence of the cavern. Under your black glass arch, the stillness is not terrifying; it is a shelter.

***

In this world, years do not happen. There are only the transitions.

Sometimes, the Net to your left shifts its silica threads. A grain of salt has fallen forty yards away, and the vibration travels through the basalt slab, a tiny *thump* that passes through your glassy body like a note played on a dark string. You feel the Gleaners pause on your belly, their waxy legs freezing as the wave passes through them, before they resume their infinite, patient pasture.

Sometimes, a Mica-Drifter, lost in its hundred-year loop, rises too high in the warm current of the basalt and brushes against your sharp outer edge. It does not break; its mica plates are too soft, too light. It simply clings to your glass for a decade or two, a tiny, translucent star resting on your shoulder, before the miniature chinook of the stone coaxes it off again.

You are content. 

The wet world where you were made was full of blood, scream, and change. Things there rose and fell; they rotted; they grew green and then turned to black mud. It was an unfinished place.

Here, the work is done. The scrapers are laid down. The print of the hand that made them is being kept, held between the glass and the stone, eaten so slowly that it will outlast the mountains. 

You lie in the warm, dry dark, holding your sky over the tiny, amber things that love you, and you wait for nothing at all.
