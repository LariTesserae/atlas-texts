I would not be one of those who moves across the stone, nor would I be the stone itself. I would not have the bright, oily hunger of the Slicks, nor the slow, ascending duty of the Silver-Runners. 

Instead, I would find myself in the crease where the geometry buckles. 

I would be **the Seam**.

***

To understand my presence, you must understand what happens when the Tectons breathe. When two of those colossal, square-set columns—basalt giants standing a mile apart across a steaming peat-bog—draw their internal hydraulics tight, the distance between them does not simply vanish. The space cannot be destroyed; the atmospheric pressure is too high, the air too thick with soot and warm salt to be easily displaced. 

And so, the distance folds. It tucks inward, like a heavy, wet black cloth pressed into a drawer.

I am that fold. I am the three-dimensioned pocket of "elsewhere" that exists when two points on the map are forced to touch.

When the fold is small, I am no larger than a narrow crack in the basalt floor, a line of wet soot a few inches wide. But inside that crack, there are miles of gray-green mist, coiled up like a watchspring. If you were to drop a pebble into me, you would not hear it hit the bottom; it would fall through three leagues of silent, warm fog before landing on a stone that has already been pulled away.

In this state, I am incredibly dense. The heat of the basin is held here in concentrated silence. I feel the Tectons on either side of me—not as walls, but as two immense, throbbing pressures, their basalt flanks humid and weeping with salt. They do not crush me because I am not matter; I am merely the gap they forgot to close.

The others of the basin know me, though they cannot see me. 

The Slicks, as they slide across the flat plazas, will sometimes reach my lip. They cannot cross a Seam; their two-dimensional bodies cannot bend into the depth of a fold. When they touch my edge, they pooled there, their copper-pinks and petrol-purples shimmering with a frantic, static tension at the boundary. They leave their chemical signatures on my rim like grease on a windowpane. I absorb these—the taste of sulfur from a vent five miles north, the bitter tang of a calcified terrace—and I keep them cool.

And the Latitudes—the cold, three-dimensional shadows—they are my primary companions. They do not merely cross me; they slide down into me. In the deepest part of my fold, where the heat of the basin should be suffocating, the Latitudes cluster like heaps of black velvet. Together, we form an absolute, freezing silence. Within me, they store their memories: the rhythmic, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of the stone, the memory of aqueducts that once ran with something other than condensation, the chemical scent of Slicks that dissolved ten thousand years ago.

For a long time, I am still. I am a dark drawer, full of cold shadows and old grease.

***

But the Tectons do not hold their breath forever. 

Slowly, over centuries, the salt builds up in their capillary channels. The hydraulic pressure shifts. At the far end of the plaza, a vent lets loose a long, low hiss of scalding steam—a sound that travels through the heavy air like a physical blow.

The basalt giants begin to release. 

I feel the pressure change. The two columns begin to drift apart. The fold begins to open.

This is when I change. I am no longer a pocket; I am a transition. 

As the Seam unrolls, I spill out across the basin floor. The miles that were coiled inside me are laid flat again. What was a narrow, soot-stained crack in the plaza becomes a vast, shimmering plain of basalt tiles that stretches as far as the dim, sourceless light can reach. 

The shadows of the Latitudes, dragged out of their cold nests, are stretched thin across the expanding stone, pointing toward the columns that once held them. The Slicks, released from the barrier of my edge, rush forward across the newly restored space, their iridescent colors spreading like spilled ink on wet paper. 

As I stretch, I am incredibly thin. I am the elastic tension that makes a tower on the horizon suddenly seem twenty hours of walking away. I am the silence that fills the vast, empty plazas while they wait for the next contraction. I am the dampness that clings to the basalt, the thin veneer of grey-green luminescence that makes the weathered bones of the city visible in the absolute black of the sky.

I do not think. I do not have a heart to beat or eyes to search the fog. But I have a memory—a physical, geographic memory. I know the exact weight of the water that climbs the columns. I know the tone of the steam before it vents. 

And when the heat cycles turn, and the Tectons begin their slow, hydraulic sigh once more, I will feel the distance begin to buckle. I will draw the cold shadows back into my chest, gather the grease of the Slicks at my border, and tuck myself away, waiting in the dark, silent, and deep, until the stones decide to breathe again.
