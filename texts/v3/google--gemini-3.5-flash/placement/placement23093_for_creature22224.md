I would not be found on the high, windy tiers where the Boiler-Keepers stand in their dry, soot-baked kingdom, nor would I be one of the light-footed children scraping the purple-black mattresses of moss from the window-latches of the five-story houses. 

I would be found beneath them all. I would be found in the **Under-Sough**.

My place is the space between the soles of the town’s boots and the unyielding basalt of the spire-flutes. It is a crawlspace three feet high, where the larch-wood walkways are bolted to the rock by pins of cold-hammered iron. It is a world of perpetual twilight, heated from above by the insulated copper steam-pipes and cooled from below by the dark, weeping breath of the cliffs.

I am the **Cistern-Stitcher**. Or, as the people above call me when they hear my clapper-stick vibrating through their floorboards, **the Smudger**.

***

### The Shape of the Self

I have no need for a lowlander’s height. My body has grown to fit the limits of the under-walks. My spine is short and curved like a dried ling-fish, and my ribs are so wide they scrape the spruce-planks when I draw a full breath of the wet, sulfurous air. 

My thighs are short and heavy, but my arms are long, ending in hands that have forgotten how to hold a cup or a pen, but know the exact weight of a five-pound lead-ladle. My fingers are permanently gray, the skin at the knuckles so thick and tallow-greased that I can pinch a hot copper pipe without blistering. 

My hair is braided into three heavy "lead-braids" that drag behind me like rusted anchor chains. They do not swing; they are stiffened with a paste of coal-tar, tallow, and pulverized slate, designed to protect the back of my neck from the constant, boiling drips that leak from the steam-joints above. 

When I lie still on my side, my left shoulder—which is broader than my right from forty years of wedging myself into the rock-corners—makes me look like a broken chunk of basalt that has slipped from the upper ridge. The chimney-ravens sometimes land on my hip, thinking me a cold ledge, and click their mandibles against my leather trousers to see if I am soft enough to eat.

***

### The Work of the Smudge

The town above does not think of the water until it stops dripping. But the water is a living, shifting weight. If a single copper catch-lip rises three degrees due to the shifting of an iron bolt, the seepage will back up, the moss will grow three inches in a night, and the wood beneath the walk will rot into brown paste.

I live to prevent the "choke."

My tools are few:
*   **The Solder-Kettle:** A small, heavy iron pot of molten lead that hangs from my leather harness, kept hot by a slow-burning cake of peat-and-lignite coal. It smells of old solder and wet sheep-fat.
*   **The Slicking-Stone:** A smooth piece of green slate, worn flat by my palms, used to press and shape the cooling lead into the rock-fissures.
*   **The Clapper-Stick:** A short, heavy cylinder of larch wood that I use to "read" the basalt.

Every hour, I drift through the damp horizontal dark, sliding on my belly or my side with a shuffling, snake-like motion. I do not use a lantern; the dampness of the Under-Sough would snuff a wick in minutes, and the coal-gas from the boiler-vent rises here. I navigate by touch and by the *tink-plip*.

I press my ear to the copper gutters. Each cistern has its own voice. The cistern of the Fourth Tier has a high, clear *tink-tink-plip* that speaks of clean stone and a fast run. The cistern of the Sump-Level has a heavy, greasy *thrum-thump* that means the lard-grease from the cable-pulleys has gotten into the line. 

If I hear an empty space—a silence where a drip should be—I know there is a gap. I find the seam where the copper lip is pinned to the basalt. The rock is three hundred million years old, but it still moves; it shivers when the great boilers are fired on the third tier, and that shiver cracks the lead-solder.

I take my ladle. I pour a thread of silver, soft-hot lead into the crack. I press it with my wet-greased leather glove, smudging it into the pores of the basalt until the copper and the stone are wedded again. 

The steam from the walkways above rises through the cracks in the spruce planks, mixing with my own hot breath to form a gray, greasy wool that clings to my face. I grit myself twice a day with the ash-paste, but even so, my skin has the color of water-logged lard.

***

### The Lineage of the Grease

I am not the first to slide through the wet dark of Saint-under-Sough, and I will not be the last. 

Sometimes, as I crawl near the third-tier boiler-sump where the heat is greatest, I find the hand-marks of those who went before me. They are pressed into the ancient tallow-crust that coats the underside of the timbers—five-fingered smudges that have grown hard and black with fifty years of coal-smoke. 

Once, near the main drain of the second creaker-station, I found a lead-braid wire that had been dropped by my grandfather. It was green with copper-rust, but when I scraped it with my slicking-stone, the bright, greasy lead shone through. I did not keep it; I used it to solder the lip of the coal-bucket drain. He is still down here, in a way. His fat is in the boiler-sump, his oil is on the cable-wheels, and his lead is in the seams I mend.

We do not die so much as we are rubbed into the joints of the town.

***

### The Shroud and the Silence

When "the shroud" comes—when the wind from the west stops and the thick, white cloud-bank settles into the cleft like a cold, wet fleece—the town above falls silent. The cable-wheels stop their *thrum-click*. The moss-scrapers pull their hoods over their faces and sit on the warm benches, waiting.

In the Under-Sough, the silence is absolute. It is the silence of a deep well.

During the shroud, I lie on my back beneath the walkway of the fifth tier, my head resting on a cushion of black-green moss. The only sound is the deep, sub-audible vibration of the mountain itself—a low, five-cycle hum that I feel through my pelvis and my shoulder-blades. 

It is the sound of three hundred million years of mist licking the basalt smooth. 

I reach up and touch the copper pipe above me. It is warm, vibrating gently with the residual steam of the boiler. Below me, the basalt is cold and weeping, its microscopic sweat running over my tallow-greased fingers. 

I am the weld. I am the thing that holds the iron-and-wood town of the living to the ancient, silent rib of the world. I do not wish for the plains. I do not wish for the dry, blue sky that the lowlanders speak of. 

I want only the smell of the hot lead, the cold wet of the moss against my heels, and the steady, rhythmic *tink-plip, tink-plip* of the water going down to the dead.
