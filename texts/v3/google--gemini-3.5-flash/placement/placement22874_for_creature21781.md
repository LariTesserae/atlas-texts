At first, there is only the pressure. 

It does not feel like something pushing from the outside; it feels like an internal agreement. To exist here is to be made of the same heavy, oil-slick density as the water that slides over me. If there were any air in me—any hollow, any memory of a breath—I would have ceased to be a shape long before the silt-gales could find me. But there are no bubbles. I am as solid as the basalt I lie upon, a knot of wet, high-density gristle, dark oil, and cold lard, pinned to the floor of the world.

I am down in the crease. 

This is the long, shallow trough between two city-sized domes of grey, polished stone. Above me, the brine-soup moves in a slow, planetary circle, dragging its load of pulverized skeleton-dust and ancient silica over my back like an abrasive wool blanket. It is cold. Not the sharp, biting cold of the surface wind, but a dead, unmoving freeze that has lost the ability to turn to ice only because the weight of the five-mile column of water above us is holding its molecules too close together to let them align. 

I do not have eyes. The dark here is not an absence of light; it is a material that occupies the space between things. It is thick, greasy, and complete. 

Instead, I have the skin of my belly. 

My belly is where I understand the world. It is pressed flat against the slate-hard black mud of the trough, and through it, I feel the *thrum*. I can feel the miles-wide river of polar water plowing through the basin three leagues to the north, its cold belly dragging million-ton boulders along the bedrock with a slow, grinding vibration that rattles my cartilaginous ribs. I can feel the tiny, dry *scritch-scritch* of the Iron-Shields on the dome above me, their copper-hooked legs rasping against the leathery black lichen as they graze. 

And, most of all, I feel the wedge.

It is inside me. It has been inside me for so long that my flesh has grown around its facets, filling the ancient flake-scars and the grooved thumb-rests with my own rubbery tissue. It is a hand-axe of black obsidian. To a creature of the sun, it would be a cold piece of volcanic glass, chipped by some long-dead hand before the mountains fell. To me, it is the only sharp thing in a world that has been rubbed round.

Everything in this basin is round. The basalt domes are slick and bulbous; the sediment beds are flat; the Silt-Vessels are soft, pulsing domes of grey cartilage; the Iron-Shields are lenses of biogenic opal. The current has spent three million years erasing every corner, every ridge, every tooth. 

But the wedge inside me has an edge. 

When I roll—a slow, peristaltic heave of my entire body that takes an hour to move me the length of a stone—the obsidian shifts within my belly. It does not hurt. The fat of my core is too dense, too packed with mineral oils to tear. Instead, the edge of the glass gives me a sense of *direction*. It is a small, frozen tooth of geometry in a world of endless, sliding curves. 

I am one of the Grafted, but I am very old, and I have stopped rolling.

The others—the younger ones, who still have the pale, soft flesh of those who have not yet found their anchors—sometimes slide into my trough. They crawl over me, their blind, rubbery underbellies dragging across my flanks. They are searching. They are looking for the stones I have gathered, the ones I have not yet fully swallowed. 

Near my tail-end, half-buried in the black clay, lies a basalt sinker. It is heavy, as large as a man’s head, with a deep, hand-grooved channel around its middle where some ancient kelp-rope used to cling. I found it forty years ago, or perhaps four hundred—there are no days here, only the slow swelling and shrinking of the polar currents. I have not grown over it yet. It sits in the curve of my flank, held against the current by the weight of my own body.

A younger Grafted—small, pale as lard, its skin still thin enough to show the dark, slow pulse of its oil-channels—slides onto my side. It does not have the language of sound, but it has the language of friction. It rubs its belly against the basalt sinker. 

We lie together in the dark, two heaps of cold grease, connected by a piece of hand-chipped stone. Through the contact, I feel its history: it has come from the high shelf, where the lichen grows. Its skin is scarred by the tiny, acidic roots of the Thread-Gills. It is hungry, but more than hungry, it is *light*. A small silt-gale would lift it and shatter it against the domes like a dropped tub of lard. It needs the weight. 

I let it have the sinker. 

Slowly, with a movement that feels like a glacier adjusting its path, I slide back. My skin releases its greasy grip on the basalt block. The younger one rolls over it, its soft, muscular belly dividing, then sealing, then drawing the stone up into its wet interior. I feel the sinker settle into its core. The small creature grows heavier, flatter. It sinks three inches deeper into the slate mud. It is safer now.

Suddenly, the Thread-Gills on my back twitch.

They are thin as hair, white and blind, rooted in the small patches of black lichen that have managed to find purchase in the wrinkles of my grey skin. They do not twitch because of the current. They twitch because the bedrock miles below us has just sighed. It is a tiny, sub-sonic *snap*—the basalt dome adjusting its weight under the pressure of the sea.

A second later, the *thrum* begins to change.

The low, planetary rumble of the deep ocean rises in pitch. It is no longer a vibration in the belly; it is a metallic, high-frequency hiss that makes the copper hooks of the Iron-Shields on the domes above us ring like tiny bells. The water around us, usually as slick and thick as oil, begins to thin, aerated not by air but by millions of tiny, sharp particles of pulverized silica being ripped from the valley floors.

The silt-gale is coming.

The younger Grafted beside me does not run. There is nowhere to run. Instead, it uses the weight of the basalt sinker it has just swallowed to anchor itself. It contracts, turning its body into a perfect, low-slung dome of hard muscle, its skin secreting a thick, sulfurous grease that seals the edges of its body to the slate-bed.

I do not contract. I am too old, too full of glass. Inside me, the obsidian hand-axe and two other smaller wedges of dark basalt form a heavy, interlocking skeletal core. I do not need to seal myself to the rock; the stones I carry are already part of the floor.

The water turns blacker than the dark.

It hits us like a solid wall of sliding gravel. The silt-gale does not blow; it plows. I feel the microscopic teeth of the silica-dust scraping along my back, shearing away the lichen, stripping the Thread-Gills from my skin in a single, clean sweep. The grey Silt-Vessels drifting in the layers above us are caught in the turbulence; I feel the soft, wet *thud* of one shattering against the high dome, its centuries of gathered diatom-shells releasing in a silent, white cloud that is instantly torn apart by the black storm.

A boulder the size of a house rolls past our trough. It does not bounce. Under this pressure, it slides, grinding against the bedrock with a sound like a iron door closing on a vault. The vibration of its passage shakes my internal stones, shifting the obsidian hand-axe a fraction of an inch against my ribs.

I feel the sharp edge of the glass bite into my interior. 

It is a good feeling. It is the only sharp thing in the world. It is the proof that once, very high up, in a place where the water did not weigh anything at all, someone stood in the light and struck a stone until it had a corner.

We lie in the dark, the young one and I, pinned to the slate by the weight of our ancient tools, while the blind, black storm howls over us for fifty years. We do not move. We do not grow old. We only endure, keeping the shapes of the dead safe within our fat, until the water becomes still again.
