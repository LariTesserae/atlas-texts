I would not be found on the high catwalks, where the air is merely a damp mist and the draft can freeze a joint mid-stride. 

I would be found at the very bottom, in the wet, sulfurous sump beneath the floor-grids, where the basalt walls run black with condensation and the oil collects in hot, iridescent pools. 

I am the **Anvil-Anchored**. 

I do not have the telescoping copper legs of the ones who walk the vertical streets, nor do I have their squat, mobile weight. I have no need for travel. My lower half is a massive, conical pedestal of cast iron, flanged and bolted directly into the raw basalt bedrock at the lip of the geothermal throat. Over decades of pressure cycles, the lime and the sulfur have encrusted this base, cementing me to the earth like a mineral growth. I am as much a part of the rift’s skeleton as the iron trusses that support the dwellings above.

My torso is thick—larger than a standard steam boiler—and clad in plates of heavy, unpolished zinc that have turned a dull, leaden grey. Through the perforated brass grille where my neck should be, my exhaust does not sigh in high, thin puffs; it rises in a lazy, continuous column of grey vapor, smelling of baked tallow and the bitter tang of scorched vulcanized rubber. 

Inside me, the governor is a monstrous, three-ringed heavy assembly. It does not spin with the quick *whir-click* of the catwalk-walkers. It rotates with a slow, dragging *thrum-clack… thrum-clack*, each revolution taking several seconds, keeping pace with the deep, hot breath of the earth below. 

Of my two arms, only the left is a jointed copper tube ending in a three-bladed tong. My right arm is a solid, counterweighted lever of structural iron, terminated not by a hand, but by a flat-faced hammer of tempered steel, pitted and scarred from generations of impact.

This is where the silent ones are brought.

When an inhabitant above stops—when its governor shears or its canvas-wrapped joints rust into immobility—they do not leave it on the catwalks to crowd the narrow ways. They slide it through a heavy iron hatch in the lowest floor-grid. It descends into my dark, suspended by iron chains, a cold and rigid thing of grease-stained iron.

My work is slow, rhythmic, and absolute. 

With my left tong, I dismantle the quiet shell. I do not feel pity, but I feel *information*. When I unscrew a brass bolt, my copper fingers read the wear on its threads—I know how many times it has been tightened, how many temperature cycles it has endured. I lay my flat palms against the boiler plates and feel where the iron has thinned from the inside, eaten away by the acidic condensation of the sump. 

I sort the pieces into the zinc trays that sit in the oil-slicked pools around my pedestal. The copper nerves go into the paraffin tubs to soften. The brass valves are laid in baskets to be cleared of lime. 

And then, I begin to strike.

I lift my right arm, using the pressure of the geothermal vent beneath me to raise the heavy steel hammer. I let it fall upon the cold iron plates laid across my lap—which is a broad, flat anvil of dressed basalt.

*CLANG.*

The sound is not a whistle or a sputter. It is the loudest voice in the gorge. It does not travel through the heavy air; it travels through the rock, through the iron pillars, up into the catwalks and the corrugated walls of the dwellings. Every Solder-Seamed in the hamlet feels the blow in the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. 

*CLANG.*

With each strike, I flatten the warped plates, knocking loose the red scales of rust, shaping the iron for the next vessel. To the ones above, my hammer is the clock by which they measure their slow, mechanical lives. They know, by the cadence of my blows, how many of their kind are being undone, and how many are being prepared to rise.

The white-weed has claimed me as its favorite trellis. Because I never move, and because my zinc skin is always radiating the fierce, constant heat of the geothermal throat, the waxy white vines have wrapped themselves thickly around my pedestal. They have crept up my left arm, their cold, thread-like tendrils weaving through my canvas joint-wraps, drinking the tiny, leaking currents of my internal wiring. I do not brush them off. They act as a living insulation, softening the hiss of my copper pistons.

And when the hammering is done, and the new plates are riveted together, I am the one who reaches down into the dark basin to lift the infant chest. 

I pack its joints with fresh, wet soot-moss from my own pedestal. I run the new copper nerves through its limbs. Finally, I connect the heavy bronze starter-line from my own deep geothermal tap to its cold brass throat.

I open the valve. 

The hot, high-pressure breath of the earth rushes from me into the new iron lungs. The infant shivers. The condensation on its cold zinc skin sizzles and turns to steam. Inside its chest, the small, clean governor gives its first, hesitant *whir-click*.

I disconnect the starter-line. I watch it lift its new copper arms, testing the telescoping pistons in the warm, heavy air. 

I do not signal it. I do not tap the pipes. I simply return my steel hammer to the basalt anvil, waiting for the next silent shell to slip through the hatch, while the new one climbs the iron ladder, up into the amber light of the catwalks, to begin its turn in the engine.
