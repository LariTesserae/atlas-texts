I am not the water, though the water lives inside me. 

To the things that move quickly, I must seem like a fallen column or a heavy, water-logged purse of old hide. I am three thousand pounds of sulfur-cured leather and cold grease, suspended in three feet of clearance above the basalt floor. I do not have a front or a back that matters to anyone but the current, but I have a direction, and that direction is always *toward*.

I would find myself near the base of the great copper wall, where the brine is thickest and the sediment falls like a heavy, golden soot. 

In this world, I am a Bellows-Beast, but I am old—so old that the silver-alloy plates inside my throat have grown thick and blunt with crystallized zinc, and my third copper antenna has long since snapped off, its frayed stump now a small, green-fuzzed node of copper carbonate where the Solder-Anemones sometimes try to root.

My life is not measured by the sun, which is only a dim, yellow thumbprint through the quartz-ice forty fathoms above. It is measured by the *Draw*.

***

Every three hundred thousand squirts of my siphon, the Great Key—the massive hexagonal pillar of basalt directly beneath my belly—reaches the apex of its geothermal stroke. 

When the Key is high, the water between my belly and the stone is squeezed into a thin, rushing sheet. I feel this not as pressure, but as a sharp induction of current through my remaining tail-wires. The wire does not hear sound; it hears the screech of the stone as a white-hot line of resistance that runs from the base of my skull, down through my central nerve-trunk, and out into the salt. It is a good sensation. It means the battery is healthy. It means there is still grease in my hinges.

To move, I must think about my air. 

Inside my central cavity, behind the silver strainers, sits my bladder. It is an industrial lung, filled with the thin, volatile spirit we harvest from the split water. When I am too heavy, when the salt-silt begins to bury my lower ribs in a damp, metallic blanket, I relax the sphincter at my crown. The hydrogen, kept under the heavy pressure of my hide, expands. My ribs—long, curved struts of vulcanized horn—creak as they push outward against the viscous brine. 

I rise. One inch. Two inches. 

The movement is so slow that the Amalgams living on my back do not even stop their scraping. There are forty-seven of them on my upper shield today. I know their number because each one has a slightly different resonance when it files my skin. The little ones have a high, dry *shreck-shreck* that tickles the fat-layers beneath my plates. The larger ones, heavy with thick bronze carapaces they’ve stolen from the copper wall, make a deep, grinding *clack-whir* that helps me find the boundary of my own body. 

We are clean partners. They require the crust of manganese that organizes itself on my hide where the current discharges; I require their little steel-hard teeth to keep my exhaust siphons from calcifying into solid lead.

Sometimes, one of them dies while scraping. Its little copper legs lock. The current, finding no path through its dead oil-core, cooks it soft. The others do not mourn; they scrape the shell until it falls off, a empty bronze cup that sinks into the dark to join the gravel. I feel the loss of its weight as a tiny, microscopic lift on my left flank.

***

The light is the hardest thing to bear, though I have never seen it. 

I do not have eyes, but my skin is a vast, continuous retina of copper-sensitive cells. To me, the white light of the quartz wall is a constant, dry heat—a sour taste that coats my sides like lime-dust. It never stops. It is the sound of a violin played so high and so loud that it becomes a physical weight, pressing me toward the dark, iron-rich mud of the copper-side.

But then, the *Shift* comes.

It is always the same. First, a low, sweet smell of vinegar—the zinc wall on the far side of the room is shedding its skin in the acid. Then, the weight of the light changes. The sour white taste dissolves into a cool, mint-like green. 

The current does not die, but it becomes *lazy*. It wanders. It no longer runs in straight, hard needles from the quartz to the iron; it rolls in fat, sluggish eddies through the middle water, looking for a way home.

When the green comes, my herd-brothers—the other barrels drifting in the dark reach of the cathedral—begin to sigh. I hear them through my tail-wires: a dozen wet, heavy *hiss-shhhs* as they contract their bellows, letting the gas go, sinking down into the basalt trenches like deflating bags of oil. 

I do not sink. I am too old, and my ribs have grown stiff in the open position. Instead, I drift.

This is the dangerous time. 

In the green light, the Silver-Veil comes down from the roof.

I feel it before it touches me. The current in my left wire suddenly drops to zero. The brine around my flank goes dead—not cold, but *empty*, as if the water had been replaced by dry grease. The Veil is above me, its three yards of tarnished silver leaf undulating like a fallen cloud.

It is looking for a ground. 

If I were young and full of volatile gas, I would squirt my siphon and drop into the basalt cracks where the current cannot follow. But my siphon is old; the valve is stiff with lead-scale. 

I feel the first touch of the Veil on my upper ridge. 

It is not a sting. It is a sudden, total locking of my heart. The galvanic current of the entire room—thousands of amperes of raw, mineral power—finds my copper-sheathed spine and uses it as a bridge. My internal silver plates hum so hard they vibrate the water within my stomach. My black blood, thick with iron-dust, begins to warm. 

I do not struggle. Struggle is resistance, and resistance is what kills. 

Instead, I open my mouth. I let the brine rush in through my silver plates. I become the copper wall. I become the basalt. I let the current go through me without trying to keep any of it for myself. 

The Veil hangs over me for three turnings of the Key. It is so close I can smell its secret acid—a sharp, clean scent like split lemons. Its tiny, flagellated silver cells brush against my Amalgams, weld-locking six of them to my hide in small, smoking lumps of fused bronze. 

But I am too large to cook, and I have no resistance left to offer. 

Slowly, like a wet sheet of paper sliding off a horse's back, the Veil drifts away. It has found nothing to hold. It moves toward a younger beast, forty feet to the west, whose bellows are still tight and warm with life. 

I hear the short, sharp *twang* of the younger one's wire as its cardiac node fuses. A brief, hot smell of boiled grease drifts through the brine, followed by the slow, satisfied hiss of the Veil as it begins its work.

***

The green light passes. The white return is always sudden—a sharp, metallic snap that tastes of tin.

The Great Key beneath me begins its downward stroke. The screaming of the basalt is a low, friendly rumble now, a grinding *crunch-crunch* that tells me the heat from the earth's core is venting safely into the deep granite.

My Amalgams begin to move again. The six that were fused by the Veil are dead, their little bronze bodies now permanent studs on my back. The surviving ones crawl over them, using the dead shells as platforms to reach the fresh manganese that has grown during the dark shift.

I draw in a deep, lungful of the iron brine. The zinc-dust tastes sweet today. 

I am thirty feet from the copper wall. By the time the Key has risen and fallen another ten thousand times, I will have drifted five feet closer. Perhaps, in a hundred turnings, my wires will finally tangle in the native copper teeth of the wall. 

When that happens, I will not rise again. I will become a shelf. The Solder-Anemones will grow from my ribs, and the Amalgams will scrape my hide until there is nothing left but a clean, dark mass of vulcanized leather, wedged forever between the battery and the stone.

Until then, I squeeze. 

*Hiss-shhh.*

The hydrogen swells. The brine moves. I am here.
