The sun does not announce itself to me through eyes. It is a dry, vertical iron that presses against my three frozen plates. 

I am thirty-two inches of grey slate-leather, and three of my back-segments have grown together. The calcite has filled the seams between them, turning the flexible joints of my middle into a single, dense shield—an arch of dead limestone that I must drag behind me like a portion of the floor that has decided to follow my head.

To move is to feel the sand-sound. 

*Shush-tlick.*

My front margins are still soft enough to bend, still pale as curd where they press against the wet stone of the fourth terrace. I am currently aligned along a seam where two limestone blocks, each two feet wide, have begun to tilt away from one another. A hairline gap has opened between them. Through that gap, I can feel the draft of the valley's belly—the dry, cold breath of the deep dark beneath the construction, where there are no channels, only the hollow spaces left by our ancestors' bones.

The gap is wrong. It makes the drip above it sound *flat*. 

For twenty suns, the pool three inches above my head has gone:
*Plop.*
*Plop.*
*Plop.*

But today, because the stones below it have leaned, the drop does not hit the center of the slate tile. It strikes the bevel. It goes:
*Plink.*
*Plink.*
*Plink.*

The pitch is too high. It vibrates through my forward sensory lip as a thin, cold needle. The valley is singing out of tune. If the gap remains, the water will not run into the chiseled channel to my left; it will slide down into the dry dark under the terrace, where it will be lost to the engine.

I must close the seam.

I draw the water. It is a slow, cold drinking through the thousands of small mouths in my belly-soft. The moisture here is thick with lime today—sweet, chalky, heavy on the tongue-ridges. I feel the water fill my lateral chambers, one by one, like leather flasks being lowered into a well. The valves—the small, muscular rings that separate my left flank from my right—sigh shut. 

My body is four hundred pounds of water and eighty pounds of stone. 

I slide myself forward until my three front wedge-horns—the hard, grey cartilage tabs that are the only sharp parts of my soft self—slip into the hairline crack between the limestone blocks. They go in easily, like wet leather knives.

Then, I stop.

I do not push with my legs. I have no legs. I do not heave with my back. My back is an anchor of calcified shingle.

I contract my belly. I squeeze the inner bags.

The water has nowhere to go but forward. It rushes into the three cartilage horns, inflating them under immense, silent pressure. In the dark of the seam, my horns swell from the thickness of a slate tile to the thickness of a fist. 

The limestone blocks do not groan; they have no air between them to make a sound. But I feel the *click* of their crystalline structures through my tongue. I feel the microscopic shift as the three-hundred-pound block on the left slides three millimetres to the west, settling back into its dry-stacked seat. The gap closes. My wedges are squeezed flat by the weight, but I let the water drain back into my side-flasks.

I withdraw my horns. They are slightly frayed at the tips, white with pulverized stone. I will need to eat three cups of the lime-heavy runoff from the third terrace to repair them before the next sun-bite.

But the sound has corrected itself.

*Plop.*
*Plop.*
*Plop.*

The note is deep again. The water sheets over my forehead, perfectly flat, a cold film that runs down into the gutter where my sister-shape is waiting.

She is smaller than I am—perhaps thirty inches long—and her plates are still the blue-grey of fresh shale, clean and thin as willow leaves. She has not yet learned the silence of the large blocks. When she moves, she *shuffs* too quickly, her cilia-ridges scraping the stone like a handful of dry pebbles. 

She passes over my rear fender. She does not avoid me; she treats me as a step. Her belly-cilia tickle the three fused plates of my back, finding the rough, white calcite crusts that I can no longer clean. To her, I am simply a slightly more curved terrace, a limestone rise that has not yet completely settled. 

She has a bundle of crude clay pegs in her mouth-cleft—sticky, grey lumps she has filtered from the bottom of the long pools. She is heading toward the vertical face where the stairs climb to the ridge. The frost there has been busy; we can all hear the *ping-ping-ping* of the shale shingles splitting under the ice-wedge five hundred feet above us.

I would follow her if I could. But my middle is too heavy now. If I were to try to climb the three-inch steps of the lower terrace, my fused plates would not bend over the nosing. I would hang there, balanced on my own stone, my belly-skin suspended in the dry air, drying out until I split.

So I remain in this flue.

The sun is beginning to drop behind the western divide. The violet-white light is turning to a cold, bruised iron-grey. The dew is forming faster now; I can feel it bead on my dorsal plates, the small drops growing until they join the thin sheets that slide toward my nose.

My heart is a slow, leather pump. It strikes three times a minute. 

With each beat, a tiny bit of the lime I have drunk today settles into the margins of my fourth plate. I can feel the stiffness creeping backward, toward the tail-tip. Tomorrow, or the sun after, the fourth plate will no longer slide under the fifth. 

I am not afraid of the stiffening. It is a cool, slow rest. It is the feeling of finally matching the valley.

I will move perhaps four more inches this winter. I will find the perfect place—the spot where the gutter on the fourth terrace has a slight dip that needs a guide-lip to keep the water from spilling over the edge. I will lie down in that dip. I will let my head sink into the groove.

My sister-shape, or her children, will come by with their mouths full of grey mud. They will feel my outline with their sensitive lips. They will see that I do not *shuff* when they touch me.

They will bring three flat shingles of slate from the ridge. They will stack them across my back, aligning the edges with their cartilage wedges. They will pin them with clay pegs.

The water will run over us both, cold and clear and heavy with stone, and we will never have to move again to hear the song.
