Pressure is not a weight. When you have lived under it long enough, it becomes an envelope—a firm, perpetual hand that holds your shape together so you do not have to. 

I am not the current. The current is a frantic, stupid thing, always trying to get to the top of the pipe, always rushing and whistling and scattering its bubbles like lost teeth. I have no interest in the top. I have no interest in the bottom. 

I am the Joint-Lining.

If you were to look into the seam between Bronze Plate 188 and Bronze Plate 189—exactly three meters above the flickering yellow lens of the third porthole—you would see me. To a passing Oiler, I look like a three-meter strip of old gasket-rubber, black-purple and chewed by the years, weeping a steady grease-silt into the brine. But if you were to reach in with a copper hook, you would find that I do not pull free. I have spent seven hundred years turning my back into the machine. My roots are no longer fiber; they are fine, crystalline threads of lead-carbonate and copper-oxide that have crept into the microscopic pores of the metal plates. 

I am wedged. It is the only perfect state of being.

***

In the morning—or what passes for it, when the amber light in the porthole grows slightly warmer as the grease-levels in the adjacent gallery shift—the work is quiet. 

I have thirty-two lobes. They are shaped like dry leather purses, but inside they are wet and soft, lined with a velvet skin that tastes the water. With every stroke of the great piston three miles below, the water in Sluice 12-B is shoved upward by four inches, then drops back three. 

*Squeeze.* 
My purses compress. The sulfur-brine is forced out of my skin, carrying with it the microscopic flakes of iron-rust I have finished digesting.

*Release.* 
My purses swell. I drink. I taste the passage of the world above. Today, there is a high, sweet grease in the drift—tallow from the upper valve-guides, mixed with the bitter green oil of the vegetable-ferns. It is good. It has the taste of things that have seen the light. 

Then comes the silt. The copper-dust. The tiny, hard shells of dead Scuttlers that have lost their grip on the rivets and been ground to powder by the rising currents. I catch these in my outer folds. They are my mortar. I use them to pack the micro-leaks where the bronze plates have begun to warp under the heat of the engine.

I am not alone in my seam, though I am the largest thing that does not move. 

The Wire-Crabs like my undersides. They are cold little pegs of lead, and they crawl into my shadows to hide from the Sprocket-Eels. They do not bite me; they only scrape at the orange Rivet-Rust that grows where my skin meets the bronze. I like the sensation. It is a tiny, dry clicking, like fingernails tapping on a slate. It keeps the rot from reaching my core. 

Sometimes, a Sprocket-Eel will hang in the water just outside my shelf. It is so clear I can see the copper pins of its heart ticking behind its ribs. It stays there for hours, its tail twitching in the updraft, waiting for a crab to make a mistake. We do not speak—I have nothing to say to a thing that is mostly water—but we understand the geometry of the corner. Its body breaks the current, giving my left-most lobes a brief, cool rest from the friction of the brine.

***

Once every ninety thousand piston-strokes, the Oiler comes.

I hear him long before he arrives. His blind face—the hard, flat disc of cartilage between his ears—makes a low, vibrating *skrit-skrit* against the bronze plates as he climbs. He does not swim; he drags his long, tallow-pale body up the vertical seams, using his three-fingered hands to find the rivet-heads. He knows this pipe by its scars. He knows Plate 188 because of the deep, circular groove left by a loose bolt five centuries ago.

When he reaches me, he stops. His skin is slick, smelling of the deep sumps—a heavy, suffocating smell of petroleum and cold iron. 

He does not have eyes, but his fingers are very gentle. He slides two long, black-clawed fingers into my seam, feeling the margin where my purple hide meets the green bronze. He is checking for the *hiss*. If there is a hiss, it means the water is escaping behind me into the dry dry-wells of the engine. It means I have failed.

There is no hiss. I have packed the joint with three inches of compacted silt and my own calcified excretions. 

The Oiler is pleased. I can tell because his throat-gland swells, purring with a low, wet rumble that vibrates through my entire length. He presses his mouth to the corner of my seam. 

Then comes the grease.

It is hot—hotter than the brine, almost scalding—and thick as soft wax. He spits it directly into the gap above my head, then uses the heel of his palm to smooth it down, packing it over my yellow-green ferns, sealing us both into the dark. It is a beautiful grease. It has been cooked in the lower chambers for a hundred years, filtered through wool and copper screens until it is pure as lard. 

It coats my skin. It shuts out the sulfur-brine for a few days, letting my pores rest in a warm, fat silence. Underneath the grease, my skin grows soft again. My roots drink the zinc from the polish.

The Oiler stays with me for an hour, his forehead pressed against my plate, his long tail dangling down into the jade-green dark of Sluice 12-B. We vibrate together. The great piston below thuds—*one, two, three*—and we feel it as a single, deep shudder in our ribs. 

Then, he slides down. He does not look back. He has forty more miles of seams to grease before he reaches the sump, and his life is short—only a few million piston-strokes.

I will be here when his grandchild climbs this pipe. I will be here when the bronze plates have been worn as thin as autumn leaves by the green water. I will be here until the great engine stops, and we all go cold together in the dark.
