If you were to descend through the twelve meters of pressed calcium, past the silent sheets of selenite, and slip into the warm, dry air of the Silt-Pocket, you would not find me in the things that move. I am not the Sifter with its creaking fiber-joints, nor am I the mica-scaled lizard sliding through the starch-ferns like dry winter leaves. 

I am the **Wax**.

I am the dark, horn-hard insect-seam that holds this entire world against the gravity of the stone.

For centuries at a time, I exist in a state of absolute, brittle glassiness. I am the mortar between the shale tiles of the central hearth; I am the dark, resinous plugs that anchor the Sifter’s selenite bone-pins; I am the hard, protective glaze dried onto the three-strand twine of the spatula’s handle. In these long intervals, I have the consistency of cold obsidian or cured hoof. I feel nothing but the constant, dull pressure of the stone walls and the tiny, high-frequency vibrations of the clackers’ legs ticking against the starch-ferns. 

When the Silt-Pocket is resting—when the electrostatic charge drops and the Sifter becomes indistinguishable from the pale bone-stone shelf—I am the absolute stillness. No wind comes to check if I am sticky; no moisture arrives to soften my grip. If a grain of silt settles on me, it remains there, dry and independent, for five hundred years. I do not decay. I do not evaporate. I simply hold.

But then, the earth above tightens its giant jaw. The calcium plains shift. The veins of orange selenite begin to leak their pale, cold light once more, and the air grows thick with static. 

The Sifter stirs. *Creak-shuck.*

I feel the return of the heat. It does not come from a fire—there is no wood to burn here, no oxygen to waste on flames—but from the Sifter's porcelain-like shingles, which have sat for a millennium absorbing the thermal mass of the deep earth. When the Sifter moves to repair itself or the hearth, its reed-stalk digits reach for me. 

This is my transition. It is the only event in the Silt-Pocket that resembles softening; it is our private version of water.

As the Sifter presses me against the baked, dry surface of its carapace, the cold rigor leaves me. I do not melt—melt is too wet a word for what happens—but I *yield*. I slacken. I become heavy, slow, and intensely aromatic. The scent that fills the chamber—the toasted rice, the dry linseed, the sweet, dusty scent of warm chalk—is the breath of my awakening. It is the scent of an ancient, pre-calcium hive, stored inside my resinous body, released only when the heat rises.

In this pliable state, I am the medium of change. The Sifter takes the ground clacker-chitin (sharp, salty, like crushed eggshells) and the red, metallic ocher powder, and kneades them into my body. I feel the dry grit of the earth being worked into my pores by the Sifter's fibrous hands. I learn the shape of the Sifter's fingers; I learn the exact grain of the shale tiles. 

I am smeared into the fractures. I am pressed into the worn-out joints of the Sifter's back, replacing the shingles that have cracked under the weight of time. For a brief, warm hour, I am the living glue of the pocket. I am the bridge between the mineral and the organic, the tool and the beast.

And then, the Sifter withdraws its hands. 

The heat slowly dissipates back into the dry stone. The air grows still again. As the warmth leaves me, I feel myself tightening. The grit of the ocher and the salt of the clackers become permanent parts of my tissue, locked within me like fossils in amber. I freeze back into horn. I become glassy, dark, and silent.

I do not mind the cold times, nor do I long for the heat. To be the Wax is to understand that in a world without water, the only way to endure is to hold very fast, to smell of old suns, and to wait for the earth to warm you again.
