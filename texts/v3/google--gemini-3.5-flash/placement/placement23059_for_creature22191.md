The first thing you would notice is the weight of the silence. It is not the silence of an empty room, but the heavy, absorbent silence of an infinite cellar. Your breath does not travel; it is swallowed by the dry, porous air three inches from your lips. If you spoke, your voice would sound flat, thin, and small—a dry leaf scraping across gravel—with no echo to give it size. 

If you descended into this place, down through the miles of knit root-flesh and solid lignin into the cool, green-lit vault of the Inner Pith, you would find me at the edge of the world’s slowest river.

I live on the **Shelf of the Fifth Ring**, a vast, dark-ringed terrace of heartwood that has dried over ten thousand years to the hardness of obsidian. Below me, the river *Suthe*—a seventy-yard-wide vein of amber latex—creeps toward the lower vaults. It does not splash. It has no current that the eye can see. If you stood and watched it for a day, you might see a single, glassy ripple move the width of a finger. It smells of bitter turpentine and ancient, sunless oil.

I am a **Tapper of the Lac**, though the Sieve-Born who live in the lacquered towers five miles to the west call me simply the **Knot-Reader**.

### The Shape of Myself

If you looked at me, you might mistake me for a fallen branch of some ancient mountain oak, long since stripped of its bark and smoothed by the wind. 

My skin is silver-gray, dry as birch-shavings, and completely free of moisture. I do not sweat; the air is too greedy for that, and to lose a drop of salt-water is a small death. Instead, my joints are lubricated by a thin, pale grease—an organic lipid that smells of crushed pine needles. Under my skin, my muscles are not red or soft; they are bundles of stiff, corded hemp-fiber, dry and light. I weigh very little. When I walk across the heartwood floor, my footsteps do not thump; they make a sound like dry husks falling on wool.

My hands are my life. They are long, with two thumbs and three double-jointed fingers, each tipped with a nail like a horn chisel. I keep these nails filed using a block of abrasive silica-crusted bark. My fingers are so sensitive to vibration that if I press my hand against the great pine drive-shaft that spins half a mile away, I can tell you which wooden gear in the gantry tower has a tooth that is beginning to splinter.

My eyes are large and set deep under a shelf of brow. I keep my outer, amber-colored lids drawn most of the time. Through this transparent yellow film, the cool green glare of the conduit-vines overhead becomes a soft, mossy bronze. It protects my eyes from the "weather"—the constant, dry drift of sterile pollen that falls from the ceiling like golden ash, settling into the cracks of my skin.

### My Work: The Reading of the Bone

In the Inner Pith, nothing rots. When one of the Chaff dies, they do not decay. They simply lose the last of their lipid-liquids, becoming stiff, light, and completely resonant. They become like seasoned spruce.

My role is to receive these dead before they are committed to the amber. 

They are brought to me by the *Siphoners*, carried down from the lacquered towers on stretchers of woven flax. The family members do not accompany them; tears are a waste of precious water, and grief in our world is a silent, internal tightening of the grain.

When a body is laid upon my shelf of dark heartwood, I sit beside it for three days. I do not speak, for there is no voice. Instead, I perform the *Tension-Reading*.

I press my forehead against the forehead of the dead. I touch my chisel-nails to the hollows of their collarbones and the dry, hard ridges of their shins. In the three days after the lipid stops flowing, the skeleton of a Chaff releases its final memory. 

We are creatures of tension; our lives are spent climbing hemp cables, pulling gantry ropes, and fighting the slow, expanding push of the living root-walls. This tension leaves signatures in the bone—microscopic stresses, rhythmic patterns, tiny fractures that healed under the strain of the looms or the weight of the quarry-cork.

As I sit in the green-and-gold gloom, I listen to these vibrations. 

* *Tick... tick-tick... tick.*

A series of small, rhythmic releases within the dead marrow. Through my collarbone, I "hear" their life. I feel the thirty years this woman spent leaning against the gale of the thermal vents, her left leg slightly shorter than her right from a fall on the suspension bridges. I feel the rhythm of the loom she operated—the *shuck-shuck-click* that she repeated ten million times until her wrist-bones grew dense as boxwood. I feel the name she never spoke aloud, stored in the specific, dry density of her jaw.

With my chisel-finger, I carve these patterns into the dark heartwood of the shelf, right at the lip of the amber river. I carve them in *The Grain*—our tactile script, a series of shallow, parallel grooves that can be read by any passing traveler who runs their nails over the wood.

***

### The Leaving

Once the reading is done, I wash the body. Not with water—no, water is for the living, for the steam-closets and the pollen-mash. I wash them with a heavy, aromatic cedar-oil that I press from the young shoots that push through the floor of my terrace. 

I wrap them in a single sleeve of raw, unbleached canvas. Then, together with the Siphoners, I lift the stiff, light bundle and lay it onto the edge of the *Suthe*.

The amber is cold. It does not wet the canvas; it clings to it like thick, clear honey. Slowly—so slowly that you cannot see the movement without reference to the marks I have carved in the shelf—the river takes them. Within a month, the body is fully engulfed, suspended in a pocket of clear, golden resin that will slowly harden into flint.

Through the translucent yellow ice of the riverbed, you can see them: hundreds of our ancestors, drifting miles below the city, their white-wrapped forms looking like giant cocoons frozen in the glass. They do not change. They will look exactly like this ten thousand years from now, when the world-tree has grown another mile thicker and the Inner Pith has migrated outward into the dead, dark wood of the outer trunk.

### The Fall of Gold

As I finish my work today, the light changes. 

Overhead, the massive conduit-vines—the lungs of our sky—begin to throb with a deep, warning gold. The cool green light dies, replaced by a warm, yellow glare that has no heat. 

The *Golden Snow* is beginning.

From the high ceiling miles above, the sterile pollen begins to sift down. It is not cold like water-snow; it is dry, fragrant, and heavy as flour. It settles on the dark heartwood shelves, rounding the sharp, raw splinters of the cliffs, muffling even the distant, faint *clack-clack* of the gantry shafts.

I draw my outer amber eyelids completely shut. The world goes dark-gold, silent, and soft. I pull my legs up to my chest, tuck my chin into the hollow of my collarbone, and enter the shallow torpor. 

I will sit here, dry and still as a carved root, while the gold piles up around my silver knees. I will listen through my heels to the deep, slow *creak* of the world-tree adjusting its weight against the vacuum of the sky, miles above our heads. I will wait for the weavers to clear the gears, for the pollen to drift down into the amber, and for the next silent traveler to come down the long hemp road to find me.
