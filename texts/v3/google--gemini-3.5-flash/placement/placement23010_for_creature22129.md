You would find me at the **Triple-Sill**, in the great, wet crease where the three-cord horizontal plait of the *High Gallows* meets the vertical column we call the *Spindle of the Nine*. 

Here, the gravity is so soft and thick that it behaves like warm marrow. If I drop my adze, it does not fall toward the dark cisterns five miles below; it floats three inches from my shin for an hour, bobbing on the breath of the moss, until I remember to reach out my long, horny fingers and draw it back into my lap.

I would be a **Suture-Weeper**. 

***

### The Shape of Me

I am no longer entirely what the active Splicers call a person, though I still have the three-jointed limbs and the hairless, silver-downed scalp of my grand-nieces. My bones have forgotten how to be white and dry. They have been "grafted-through." If you were to strike my collarbone with a pebble, it would not click like slate; it would thump with the dense, dull *tonk* of olive-wood that has spent forty years in a brine-pot.

My fingers are very long—long enough to wrap twice around a two-inch tie-line—and the yellow horn of my fingernails has grown together, forming a hard, curved trowel at the tip of each hand. I do not have eyes like those who live in the shallow canopy; mine are wide, wet, and pale as boiled onions, without pupils, designed only to tell the difference between the greasy green of **Lanthorn-Rot** and the dead, blue-grey of a cable-shatter.

My shoulders are broad and flat, like a saddle-cask. This is because I live mostly on my back or my side, wedged into the "Suture"—the deep, resinous scar where the vertical *Spindle* was joined to the *G Gallows* six hundred summers ago by ancestors whose names have turned into moss.

***

### My Work in the Soft Time

My job is the *Grease-Weep*. 

The younger Splicers—with their quick, clicking knuckles and their loud, chest-tapping gossip—cannot stay here for long. The air in the Triple-Sill is too old. It smells of vinegar, hot yeast, and the greasy fat of dead Lace-Wings. If a young Splicer spends three three-day cycles here, their lungs fill with the downy grey mold that grows on the undersides of the great leaves, and their teeth begin to wobble in their gums like loose wooden pegs. 

But I have been "greased." 

Once a season, when the great **Steam-Sows** in their hanging cradles are warm-shorn, the daughters of my sister bring me three skins of *Sow-Tallow* mixed with the bitter juice of the red oak-gall. I rub this into my skin until I am as black and slick as a wet log. The mold cannot find a purchase on me; the rain-mist slides off my chest in long, grey pearls that fall into the void like teeth.

With my bone-drill and my flint adze, I crawl into the dark, wet armpit of the joint. I listen. 

I do not listen with my ears—the constant *shish-shish* of the clouds through the canopy would drown out the rot. I press my bare forehead against the ironwood. I feel the *groan* of the timber.
* If the wood hums with a high, dry *ting*, like a tight bow-string, the graft is holding. 
* If it returns a wet, soft *thump*, like a fist striking a dead pig, I know there is rot inside. I know the great grubs have found the heartwood.

Then, I must dig. I use my horn-fingers to scrape out the black, vinegar-smelling mush of the decayed cambium. I dig until I find the pink, wet "meat" of the living wood. I take a plug of fire-hardened bone, wrap it in a greasy rag of Lace-Wing wool, and drive it deep into the wound with my mallet until the tree screams—a low, shuddering twitch that makes the guide-lines three miles away vibrate for half a day.

### My Companions

I am rarely alone, though I have not spoken a word through the air in forty years. 

Below my knee, where the bark has buckled into a damp shelf, three **Rib-Tappers** have made their nest. They are old friends; their slate-speckled skins match the color of my thighs so perfectly that when they climb across my lap, they look like my own flesh breaking into six-legged motion. They do not fear me because I smell of oak-gall and wet ironwood. Sometimes, when the night-mist is so thick that even the Lanthorn-Rot grows dim, they will sit upon my chest and tap their ribs—*click-click-click... pause... click-step*—against my breastbone. I tap back with my hollow horn-fingers. We tell each other where the wet-flies are hatching, and whether the wind from the lower cisterns is carrying the smell of grease or the scent of dry peat.

And sometimes, the **Pearl-Snouts** come.

Where my Suture meets the *High Gallows*, there is a gravity-well—a floating globe of water the size of a hay-cart, held suspended between the attraction of the three columns. It is perfectly clear and warm, smelling of boiled tea. The Pearl-Snouts swim in it like silver needles in a glass vial. 

When the humidity is so high that the air is more water than cloud, they will slide out of their globe and row themselves through the fog to my shoulder. They have no teeth; they lick the sweet, sticky *Sap-Weep* from my neck with their pink, feather-like gills. It tickles, but I do not move. If I move too fast, the water-well might shift its balance and spill three hundred gallons of warm, heavy water down the column, washing my nesting lizards into the void.

***

### The Drowsing

I am growing heavy. 

Last season, when my sister’s daughter came to bring me my tallow-skins, she touched my ankle. She did not thrum her fingers on the vine to speak; she simply pressed her hand to my heel. 

When she took her hand away, I saw that her palm was wet with green resin. My own skin is beginning to turn. Under the grey down of my calf, the flesh has gone stiff and green-veined, matching the wet marble-bark of the *Spindle*. It has no feeling now; I could drive a wooden peg through my heel and feel nothing but a deep, comforting warmth, like drinking hot spruce-tea.

I am not afraid. Soon, I will not need the tallow-skins. I will not need the adze or the bone-drill. 

I will crawl into the deepest crease of the Triple-Sill, where the wood is five hundred feet thick and the air is perfectly still. I will let the moss grow over my knees, and my grand-nieces will come with their childen to touch my cheeks. They will ask me where the deep ironwood is rotting, and I will not answer with words, nor with the thrumming of a cord. 

I will answer by the way my heart slows—one beat for every three rises of the mist—until the tree and I are breathing with the same deep, wooden lung.
