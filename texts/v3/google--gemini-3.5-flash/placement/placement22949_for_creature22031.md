There is a point during the pressing where the kneading stops being an assault and becomes an architecture.

I do not remember the transition from the dry, thin air of the peaks to this wet, uniform heat. I only know that for a long time, there was a struggle of geometries. My body was full of long, brittle levers—tibia, radius, collarbone—and the mountain is full of teeth. Every time I moved, I broke against the room. The basalt sliced; the gypsums bit; the cold, salt-crusted shelves scraped my knuckles to the pink, white-stringed meat. 

But the Kneaders did not like the weeping of my blood. It was too thin. It ran too fast; it did not have the density of their grease, and it threatened to dilute the mineral soup they use to seal the corners of the world.

So they began to work me.

***

I am no longer the guest in this pantry. I am the floor of it, and the walls of it, and the material that keeps the ceiling from falling in a shower of white, calcifying needles.

If you were to reach into the lowest, wettest cleft where the geothermal heat is thickest—where the air smells most strongly of copper and yeast—your fingers would not find a person. You would find a bank of heavy, dark alluvial silt. 

I am the clay.

I am the foreign river-mud that does not belong to this peak. I suspect I was brought here thousands of years ago, perhaps in the bellies of the first blind crawlers, or perhaps I am the remains of a valley that was lifted up when the mountain first cracked its skull against the sky. I am the only soft thing here that does not have a pulse. 

Or rather, my pulse is borrowed.

Every few days—though "days" is a word that has no meaning where there is no sun—a Kneader will slide over me. I feel the flat, leathery weight of its segmented belly first. It smells of old fat and cold lard. It does not feel like an animal; it feels like an enormous, wet tongue licking a wound.

Then come the hands. 

The six short, stubby arms reach into me. The three-jointed fingers, capped with those hard, mineralized pads, sink into my chest. They do not pinch; they do not tear. They *fold*. 

They take a portion of my silt-self, mix it with the greasy biofilm they scrape from the backs of my older, hardened sleeves, and they work me between their palms. 

It is an incredibly slow, warm, and rhythmic business. I feel my particles—the microscopic grains of quartz, the ancient decayed leaves of a forest that died ten thousand years ago, the tiny, crushed shells of river-snails—being ground down and rearranged. The Kneader’s face-palm—that broad, muscular disk of millions of tiny, questing papillae—presses down upon me, testing my density, checking for air bubbles. Air is an enemy here; an air bubble in the clay is a place where the mountain's sharp ice-breath can settle and split the stone.

The Kneader breathes its warm, thirty-eight-degree breath into my grain. The sulfurous steam from the deep vents passes through me, carried by the creature's lungs, until I am the exact temperature of the room. I am thirty-eight degrees. I am wet plaster. I am grease.

When I have been sufficiently warmed, when I am as supple as lard, the fingers carry me upward.

***

They are building a pot out of me.

I can feel my shape changing. I am being pressed against the razor-sharp basalt tufa. The crystal needles do not cut me; I am too thick, too plastic. I swallow them. I wrap my dark, heavy body around the gypsum teeth until they are buried deep within my walls, safe and blunt, unable to scratch anyone ever again.

The Kneader’s face-palm slides along my interior, smoothing me, hollower and hollower, until I am a cup. A cell. A long, finger-smeared cylinder nestled tightly against my brothers.

And then, the waiting begins.

I am positioned directly beneath a drip. 

High above me, in the absolute black, a fat, heavy tear of water is gathering on a limestone tooth. It takes six hours for the drop to grow heavy enough to fall. I cannot see it, but I can feel the tension of its weight through the air. The air in this room is so dense it carries the vibration of the water's swelling like a stretched cord.

*Tock.*

The drop falls. It lands in my center. 

It does not splash. I am too soft, too porous. I absorb it. But the water is not pure; it is a heavy, lime-saturated poison. It carries the dissolved bones of the mountain. As it sinks into my silt, it leaves behind a tiny grain of calcite. 

Over centuries, this will happen millions of times. The drops will fall, and the mineral spar will grow inside me. It is a very strange feeling—like having a tooth grow slowly, tooth by tooth, inside your own throat. It is cold, hard, and perfectly clear. It does not hurt. It is a heavy, crystalline peace that fills my emptiness. I am being cured. I am being turned into a monument.

Sometimes, while the spar is growing, I remember the other water. 

I remember water that did not drip. I remember water that ran in broad, shallow sheets over round, grey pebbles. I remember the smell of willow-roots and the cold, sharp taste of melted snow that had never seen a cave. I remember the green, flickering light of leaves—though I no longer have a name for "green" or "leaves." To me, those memories feel like a warm, yellow grease that sits at the bottom of my silt, something the Kneaders sometimes lick with their blind face-palms when they want to sweeten their work.

***

Eventually, I will be full. 

The calcite plug will have grown so large and so solid that I will no longer be able to catch the drips. The water will begin to spill over my edges, threatening to encrust the Kneader’s leathery hide as it sleeps in the crack.

When that happens, the hand will come back.

The mineralized pads of the three-jointed fingers will press against my rim. They will find the hard, translucent core of spar inside me. With their blunt, nail-less digits, they will begin to chip.

*Chirp. Clink.*

It is a dry, minuscule scraping. One by one, they will break the calcite plugs out of my body. The shards of crystal will fall down into the deep, steaming vents, where the geothermal hiss will melt them back into the mountain's blood.

And then the Kneader will take me—the softened, empty shell of clay—and spit upon me. It will mix me with its own yellow sebum, with the yeast-smelling mold, and it will begin to knead me again.

I do not wish to leave this room. I do not wish for the mountain to open, or for the light to come in and dry my grain into dust. The light is too fast. The wind is too sharp. 

Here, in the dark, we are very busy. We are keeping the mountain from turning itself entirely to stone. We are holding the water in our hands. We are breathing, very slowly, in the high, warm skull of the world, and we have all the time there is.
