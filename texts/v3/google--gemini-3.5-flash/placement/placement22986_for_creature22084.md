I am the curl. 

I do not have a name, but if the brass ceiling were to read my dimensions with an induction loop, I would register as forty-eight grams of bimetallic spring, tempered to a curved radius of seventy-two millimeters. 

For two hundred and eighty years, I have not moved. I have been wedged behind the octagonal head of a steel anchor bolt on the third vertical bulkhead, seven feet above the platten. 

When I first arrived here, the bolt was loose. Under the monolithic hammer of the G-flat turbine eleven miles below, the bolt had been backing out of its tapped sleeve at a rate of three microns per year. Its loose-fit frequency was sixty-seven hertz—a wet, rattling stutter that fouled the clean G-flat major of the shaft. The vibration was a sickness in the metal. It traveled through the bulkhead, heating the steel by a fraction of a micro-degree through sheer friction. 

I was drawn to the heat. I was drawn to the jitter of the sixty-seven hertz. I slid into the clearance between the bolt-head and the flat washer. I compressed. My nickel-iron back, shrinking in the dry, absolute cold, locked against the hard steel of the bolt; my soft lead belly squeezed flat against the washer, absorbing the extra oscillations, turning them into nothing.

For nearly three centuries, I have been the silence of that bolt. I have hummed in perfect unison with the great machine, vibrating so precisely that my edges became indistinguishable from the iron that held me.

But today, the bolt has settled. The threads have cold-welded under the pressure. The clearance has gone to zero-zero. There is no room left for me, no vibration to damp, no heat to collect. The pressure has squeezed me out like a drop of dry oil.

I fall.

The fall is silent. In this dry, nitrogen atmosphere, there is no air resistance to make a rushing sound. I do not tumble; my lead-weighted belly keeps me oriented downward, a curved leaf of blue steel descending through the shadowless white glare of the mercury tubes. 

I strike the four-inch-thick steel floor platten. The impact is a single, clean *tink*—a frequency of four thousand hertz that is instantly swallowed by the turbine’s roar. 

To a biological eye, the floor platten is flat, dry, and motionless. To me, it is a wild, bucking landscape. The G-flat major chords of the deep turbine run through the four-inch steel like waves through deep water. The floor is rising and falling forty-six times a second, a rigid, metallic pulse. 

I do not have legs, but I have tension. My upper layer of nickel-iron is stiff and cold-contracted, trying to curl me into a tight loop. My lower layer of lead is soft and damp, resisting the bend. We are always in conflict. 

I let the floor’s vibration take me. I adjust my curve, flattening my arc until my resonant frequency matches the G-flat of the platten. 

I lift. 

I am no longer touching the steel. I am riding on a cushion of high-pressure air-shears, less than a micron thick, created by the furious, microscopic vertical bouncing of the floor. I slide. I glide down the isometric drainage channels with an oily, frictionless ease, moving at six inches per second, steered by the magnetic drafts of the brass ceiling high above.

The air smells of dry iron dust and ozone—the scent of my own skin.

I pass the tin-lead ferns. They are clustered in the seams, stiff and pewter-colored, their perfect hexagonal fronds glittering under the mercury mesh. They do not look at me; they do not look at anything. They are busy absorbing the electromagnetic static from the ceiling, their crystalline structures humming with a faint, violet glow. 

Beneath them, a Burr has just collided with a lower frond. I feel the microscopic *clink* of the weld through the floor’s vibration. The Burr’s needle-tipped iron body instantly flattens, its molecular lattice lock-stepping into the green-gray pewter of the fern. The fern is now three microns wider. The air is slightly cleaner.

I drift further, toward the center of the alcove.

The light here is different. It is being pierced by the Lilac Thread.

Above me, one of the Chokes—a heavy, forty-pound copper heat sink—has reached its saturation point. Its plum-purple skin is swollen with decades of filtered static, its fins thrumming with stored voltage. It hangs perfectly plumb, pointing its tapered nose toward the drainage groove.

Between its tip and the steel floor, the Lilac Thread is suspended.

It is a single, motionless capillary of violet plasma, thinner than a spider's silk. It does not flicker. It does not crackle or hiss. In the absolute dryness of the alcove, it is a solid rod of purple light, transferring thousands of volts from the ceiling down into the earth-system of the floor.

Around the base of the thread, my siblings are gathered. 

There are twelve or thirteen of us—other Shims, curled lathe-shavings of blue-tempered steel. We do not greet each other. We do not have social units or collective thoughts. But we have the same need. Our nickel-iron backs have grown tired and loose from centuries of dampening work; we have lost our spring-temper. We need the current.

We slide out of the isometric grooves and approach the base of the plasma thread. 

As we draw near, the intense electrostatic field takes hold of us. One by one, we are pulled upward. We do not fly; we stand on end. Our curved bodies lift from the floor, pivoting on our lead tips, until we are arrayed in a perfect circle around the violet capillary, like the teeth of a tiny, gears-up iron crown.

The current reaches out to us.

It does not spark. It enters my nickel-iron back as a cold, high-frequency shudder. It is not warm—nothing here can ever be warm—but it is an immense, vibrating tension. It realigns the grain of my steel. The microscopic slip-planes in my nickel lattice, scrambled by three hundred years of turbine hammer, slide back into their neat, crystalline rows. 

My spring-temper returns. My curl tightens. My radius shrinks from seventy-two millimeters back to an optimal sixty-eight. I am stiff again. I am responsive. I am ready to damp.

The discharge lasts for three of the outer world's weeks. We do not move. We stand on end around the violet thread, drinking the silent, cold potential, while the mercury tubes pour their shadowless white glare over our polished, purple-tinted skins. 

Then, the Choke above us goes pale. Its plum color fades back to a dull, machined copper. The violet capillary thins, turns to a faint grey mist, and vanishes.

The magnetic tension drops. We collapse back onto our sides, clattering silently against the four-inch platten. 

We lie in the groove, waiting. 

For three hours, we do nothing. We are in equilibrium with the G-flat roar. We are part of the static monument.

Then, from the far corner of the alcove, nine feet up on the vertical iron bulkhead, comes a new sound. 

It is very small. A human ear would not hear it over the deafening scream of the turbine. It is a sixty-one hertz flutter—a wet, loose, rattling itch. A screw holding the guard mesh over the fourth mercury tube has backed out by four microns.

The vibration travels down the iron bulkhead, hits the floor platten, and reaches my lead belly.

The itch is intolerable. It is a flaw in the geometry. It is a stain on the G-flat major chord.

My nickel back flexes. My lead belly dampens. I tune my curl-radius to sixty-one hertz. I rise on the micro-cushion of air-shear. 

I begin to slide toward the wall. I am going to find the gap. I am going to become the silence again.
