The first thing to understand is that I do not have a front or a back when I am in the deep current. I am only an interval of weight between the one who presses before me and the one who pushes behind. 

Our feet—the thick, grey felt that cushions our bellies—are wet with the oil of the basalt. We do not walk so much as we slide, a mile-long tongue of grey lard moving over grey glass. Above us, the high ceilings are silent except for the three great drops that define this sector of the valley: the *doop* of the Northern Rift, which is dry and high; the *doop* of the Basin of Pools, which is flat and heavy; and the *doop* of the Western Sink, which is distant and double-struck, like a cracked tooth.

I know where I am because the air between these drops is different. Here, near the central plaza, the air is thick with the dust of slate and the sweet, cold smell of the white starch-mold that grows in the dark joints of the towers.

We are carrying a crescent wall. My mandibles are locked around a slab of flat, water-worn slate. It has three ridges on its upper edge. It is cold—so cold it makes the skin of my mouth-palps turn white and stiff—but it is dry. The dry stones are the best. They have been turned three times since the last light-noon, and they still carry the smell of the wind that we drew down from the high northern rifts.

To my left, the world is a great, circular furnace of white heat. I cannot see it, but my crown tentacles can feel it: the searchlight beam is a column of parched air that makes our back-plates crack if we enter it too quickly. We skirt it. We are a ribbon of grey water flowing around a pillar of bone-white fire.

When we reach the empty site, the current slows. The pressure from behind ceases to push, and we begin to pile. 

We do not plan the crescent. The wind tells us where the stone must go. The wind is a low, whistling draft that comes off the basalt wave to the east; it is sluggish today, carrying only the damp lime of the floor. It needs to be squeezed. It needs to be made into a knife.

I drop my slate. Another Turner—a draft-elder who is twice my width and smells of old tallow—takes it from me with its side-palps and slots it into the foundation. The stone goes *clench*. Not a clack, but a soft, sliding *shup* as the wet silt on its underside meets the slate below. 

I do not wait to see the tower rise. Already, my mouth-palps are wet with the taste of the starch we scraped from the last tower we tore down in the south. The taste is like cold cream and iron. It is the only thing we eat, and it is the only thing that makes our felted soles grow their thick, grey pile.

***

But the current is changing for me. 

For many days—or many arrangements of the stone, which are the same thing—my third left-leg has been dragging. The felt is no longer springy. When I step on the basalt, I do not slide with that silent, oily grace; I make a faint, dry *scritch* that the others can hear. 

When I pass the younger ones, their crown tentacles drift toward me, mapping the stiffness in my side. They do not avoid me, but they no longer slide beneath my belly. They yield the crown of the road to me, not out of respect—for we have no names for respect—but because I am becoming a wall.

I can feel the lime in the air settling on my back.

Usually, the dew is something we lick from one another’s shields during the hours when the searchlight rifts are closed and the valley is dark. But today, the dew on my neck-plates does not wipe away. It is turning dry. It is turning into a crust that fits me like a second coat of slate.

I am leaving the draft.

I do not make a decision. The current simply flows past me, and I do not flow with it. I drift toward the margin of the avenue, where the grey basalt rises into a long, polished wave that has not moved since the world was wet. 

I press my belly against the stone. 

The basalt is very cold, but it is a good cold. It does not change. For three heights of the light-noon, I watch—with my skin, with my tentacles, with the soles of my stiffening feet—the city rise and fall around me.

Fifty yards away, they are building a thirty-foot needle. It is a beautiful thing. The wind is caught in its joints, and it is singing a high, clear *wheeeee-shaa* that means the starch-moss is already beginning to sprout in the dark seams between the coins of stone. I can smell it—the crisp, mushroom smell of the crop. 

My tentacles drift toward the sound. I measure the needle. It is twenty-six slates high. It will be thirty-four before they stop. Then the wind will blow through it for two light-noons, the fungus will turn white and thick, and then the swarm will come with their tongues and their chisels to take it down.

***

I am very heavy now. 

My legs have stopped being legs. They have folded inward, beneath my belly, where the wet felt is turning into a dense, grey glue that binds me to the basalt wave. I cannot lift myself. If I tried to move, I would leave my skin behind.

But I do not want to move. The desire to carry the stone has gone out of me, replaced by a deep, cool curiosity about the space around me.

Two young Turners approach me. They are small—no larger than hounds—and their shells are still soft and pinkish-grey, like lard that has not yet cooled. Their tentacles are short and frantic, tapping the air in quick, wet circles.

They touch my flanks. 

Their mandibles are sharp, like chisels of black flint. When they touch my side, there is no pain. My outer skin has sloughed; it is no longer part of my meat. It has become a series of long, grey ribbons, tough as old leather and cured by the lime-dew of the basin.

They begin to peel me.

With small, rhythmic tucks of their heads, they lift the long ribbons from my sides. I can feel the wind hitting the damp, white muscle beneath. It is cold—wonderfully cold—and the drafts from the needle-tower forty yards away blow across my exposed ribs like water. 

They take the ribbons. They will use them to bind the three-slate joints of the high watch-towers in the north, where the drafts are strong enough to throw a dry stack down if it is not lashed with tendon.

After they have taken the leather, they begin to hollow me.

Their mandibles are yellow with the salt of my marrow. They eat the dry, chalky pulp that once moved my legs, clearing it away to make room for the water. I am becoming light. I am becoming empty. 

With every stroke of their jaws, the shape of the world changes for me. I am no longer a point of weight moving through the city. 

I am the city.

I am five feet high now. My back-plates have calcified into a thick, fluted rim that catches the slow, rhythmic *doop... doop* of the ceiling rift. The young ones have finished their work; they have gone back to the crescent wall, their palps white with my salt, their bellies full of the lime that will make their own shells hard when their turn comes.

I am alone at the corner of the avenue.

The water of the basin is very clear. It collects in my hollow body, inch by inch, drop by drop, from the cold ceiling miles above. 

When the wind blows down the avenue, it does not move me. It goes *around* me. It makes a low, hollow whistle as it passes over my fluted rim—a sound that is different from the hiss of the towers or the rattle of the cairns. 

A line of Slate-Turners passes my base. They are carrying a heavy, grey lintel. As they pass, their crown tentacles drift toward me, sensing my shape. They do not know me as the one who carried the three-ridged slate; they know me as the great jar at the corner of the third plaza. 

They know that when they are dry, they can dip their palps into my cold, limestone water. They know that when the wind-needles are torn down, I will still be here, holding the rain.

The light-noon is fading. The white shafts are turning grey, then blue, then nothing. 

In the dark, the city is still moving. I can hear the soft, sliding *shup... shup* of forty thousand felted feet on the basalt wave. I can hear the towers falling in the south and rising in the north. 

And inside me, the water of the basin is perfectly still, reflecting nothing but the cold, wet stone of my own walls.
