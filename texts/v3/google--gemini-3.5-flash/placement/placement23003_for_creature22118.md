I am not the basalt, nor the ancient masonry, nor even the slate-plated Keepers who watch the dark. 

If you were to descend into this room, if you were to pass through the heavy wool of the black moss and find the center of the silent floor, you would see the small prism of dark glass. You would see it floating, three inches above its flat stone slab.

I am those three inches.

In a world defined by the terrifying, millenary pressure of the earth—where miles of basalt grind together with the weight of oceans, and where the Keepers must constantly calcify their own nerves to withstand the crushing gravity—I am the only place where gravity has let go. I am a three-inch pocket of perfect weightlessness, buried forty meters beneath the soil. I am the sky’s tiny, forgotten representative in the deep.

To be the gap beneath the prism is to be a space of absolute, unburdened transition. 

I have no core, no skin, and no temperature of my own. I am defined entirely by what passes through me. From below, the flat stone slab radiates the faint, ancient warmth of the deep crust—a slow, sleepy pulse of infrared. From above, the glass prism bends this warmth, refracting it back down through me as rings of pale, ultraviolet blue. I am the lens of this conversion. When the light-field shifts, I feel it not as a sight, but as a subtle change in the density of the cold. The air within me does not drift; it is held so still that the individual molecules of nitrogen and oxygen have almost ceased their dancing, arranged in a quiet, crystalline matrix that sits between the stone and the glass like a pane of invisible ice.

Time does not pass here in a stream; it pools. 

From my vantage at the center of the floor, the three Keepers do not look like entities. They look like the sloping shores of a dark lake. I watch them through the slow-pulsing rings of my blue light. Because I am the focal point of their environment, I am also the destination of their thoughts. 

When the mountain groans—that hundred-year sigh of shifting granite—I feel the energy long before it reaches the prism. I feel it as a sudden, sharp tightening in the silver-white threads that span the corners of the room. The threads, which are anchored deep in the Keepers’ rooting limbs, hum with a silent, piezoelectric current. 

I watch this current travel. It is a beautiful, leisurely thing. It leaves the slate plates of the Keepers, creeps along the rigid silver lines, and enters the floating prism above me. The prism absorbs the current, grows microscopically colder, and then—after a decade of digestion—releases it into me as a tiny, slow-pulsing ring of deep blue. 

I take this ring of light. I hold it. I let it sink downward, through my three inches of stillness, until it touches the flat stone slab below. There, the black-green mosses that crowd my borders drink it in.

I am the hearth, but I am also the steward of the hearth’s silence. 

Sometimes, a drop of water will form on the ceiling directly above the prism. It takes days to grow, swelling into a heavy, silver bead. I watch its reflection form in the dark glass above me. When it finally falls, it does not strike the prism; the microscopic field of light-distortion gently steers it aside, sliding it through the air so that it lands just on my outer margin, at the edge of the moss. 

When it hits—*tink*—the tiny shockwave passes through the frozen velvet. The air within me vibrates, a single, clean note that takes three years to fully dissolve back into the silence. During those three years, I am slightly out of tune. The ring of ultraviolet blue wobbles. The Keepers adjust. We all lean into the vibration, absorbing it, settling it into the grain of the basalt until the room is quiet again.

If a warm-blooded thing were to enter this room, I would be the first to die. 

The heat of a breath, the frantic, wet moisture of a tongue, the chaotic vibration of a beating heart—these things would flood my three inches of weightless space like a sudden scream. The delicate, frozen lattice of my air would shatter. The prism would wobble; the silver threads would snap under the sudden, agonizing tension of the Keepers’ panic. 

But no one comes. The tectonic weight of the continent protects us. 

And so I remain, suspended between the stone and the glass, holding the cold blue light of the prism in my small, weightless hands. I am the quietest part of the quietest room in the world. I am the gap that keeps the stone from touching the sky, and I am entirely at peace.
