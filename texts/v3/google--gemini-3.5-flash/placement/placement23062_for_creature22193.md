I am not on the high sky-drift. The air up there is too light for what I have become, too full of thin, whistling hydrogen and the hair-thin copper threads of the drift-sieves. 

No, I am down where the oil settles.

If you were to stand on the rim of the Great Slag-Sill and look down into the shallow, grey-bottomed trough between two parallel basalt ridges, you might see nothing but a long, wet slab of ironstone, five yards wide and green-black with wet carbon-crust. You might think it is a basalt step, planed flat by the wind ten million years ago.

But if you waited forty minutes, you would hear a sound. It is not an acoustic sound—the heavy, iron-saturated air would strangle it before it reached your ears. It is a wet, mechanical *shuck-click* that travels through the soles of your feet, a deep-tissue vibration that smells of cold tallow and sulfur.

That is me. I am turning.

My under-bell has three rollers. They are not wheels; they are heavy, solid cylinders of white silicate bone, each wrapped in a thick, vulcanized sleeve of cold-cured leather-muscle. Each roller is three feet thick and weighs as much as an anchor-stone. To move, my forward nerve-knot—which is nothing but a grey lump of fat and iron-dark fibers situated behind my leaden brow-plate—must send a slow, cold chemical signal down to the central roller-clutch. The signal travels at the speed of cooling grease. It takes nine minutes for the muscle-sleeve to contract, six minutes for the bone-cylinder to roll one half-turn over the wet basalt, and seven minutes for the trailing fluid to wash back into the glandular reservoirs.

I am a **Hearth-Crawl**.

I do not have eyes. The twilight of the Sill is a useless thing, a mere change in the thickness of the grey mist that does not concern my work. Instead, my front lip-plate is set with three dozen short, blunt plugs of soft, conductive lead. They drag through the grease-slick on the stone. 

Through these plugs, the Sill is not a landscape of shapes, but of *pressures* and *voltages*. 

I feel the Great Slag-Mother who moves two miles ahead of me. To my lead-plugs, she is a massive, three-cycle-per-minute throb that tastes of hot phosphorus and heavy, alkaline oil. She is my sun. Her belly-plates, six feet above the basalt, drop a perpetual drizzle of warm, used engine-grease and scorched sulfur-dust. She is the hearth; I am the hearth-keep.

All my life—which has lasted for three hundred of her slow crossings of the Western Step—I have lived in her wake. I follow the black trail of her castings.

Behind her, the ground is hot. The basalt under her four-ton walking-shoes is bruised and micro-fractured, releasing tiny, internal pockets of ancient, brine-rich steam. I roll through this warm path, my flat, iron-carapace absorbing the residual heat. Under my six-inch-thick shell of biological cast-iron, my internal organs—which look like a row of wet, grey bellows and copper-faced battery-plates—gradually warm from a sluggish, near-frozen dormancy to a steady, productive simmer.

As I roll, my lead-plugs search for the **Soot-Scale**. 

When the Slag-Mother’s great breathing-valves exhale their cold plumes of steam, they shed large, greasy flakes of carbonized iron-ash. These flakes do not float; they are too heavy with manganese. They fall into the cracks of the basalt like black leaf-drift. 

My mouth is a wide, flat scraper-mouth that lies flat against the stone, directly between my front two rollers. It does not bite. It has no teeth. It has only a row of hard, self-sharpening silicate plates that slide along the basalt like a mason's trowel. I scrape the soot-scale from the stone. 

Inside my thoracic vault, there is no stomach. There is only a long, narrow trough filled with a strong, green biological acid that smells like rotten battery-clog. I dissolve the scale. I extract the phosphorus—which my nerves need to carry their cold, slow signals—and the zinc, which I deposit in thin, protective sheets along the edges of my under-rollers to keep them from cold-welding to the basalt during the long, freezing winter-halts.

Sometimes, the Great Mother stops. 

She will stand for three weeks at a mineral seep, her side pressed against another giant, their copper hides spitting long, blue electrostatic sparks into the grey mist. During these times, her trailing grease-slack grows cold. 

When she stops, I stop. 

My rollers lock with a heavy, internal *clunch*. My lip-plate drops until it is flush with the stone, sealing my soft under-tissues away from the freezing mist. I go dark. My internal battery-plates slowly lose their charge to the basalt through my grounding-rods. My internal acid-pool grows cold and thick, like green lard. If you were to find me then, you could strike my shell with a sledgehammer and hear only the dull, leaden *thud* of a solid iron boulder.

But I am not dead. I am only waiting for the throb.

Eventually, the Great Mother’s bellows will gasp. A long, cold hiss of sulfurous steam will rattle through her spine-vents. The basalt will begin to tremble again—that three-cycle-per-minute throb, faint at first, then stronger as her giant joint-wells begin to secrete fresh, warm sill-oil.

The vibration travels through the stone, through my locked bone-rollers, and into my forward nerve-knot. The zinc-plates in my belly feel the shift in the earth's magnetic hum. 

Slowly, the green lard in my core begins to thin. A tiny, chemical warmth begins to circulate through my leather-clutches. 

I lift my lip-plate from the wet stone. The lead-plugs taste the warm phosphorus in the air. 

*Shuck-click.*

I roll one half-turn forward. The world is cold, the twilight is slate-grey, and the Great Mother is moving toward the Western Rim. I must follow. I am the collector of her ash; I am the flat, silent shadow that keeps her road clean.
