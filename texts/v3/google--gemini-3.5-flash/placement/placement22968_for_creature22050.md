I do not belong to the open terraces, where the glass plains glare with the crimson heat of the core. If you were to search for me, you would have to descend past the shingled cliffs of the sleeping Plate-Dwellers, past the shallow rifts where the Silt-Spiders scrape the salt-crusts, and into the shadow of the oldest basalt hills. 

There, where the geology has folded back upon itself to form thick, overlapping vaults of green-veined diorite, sits a static sanctuary that is older than the others. Its spiral wall does not have five turns, but twelve—an immense, crouching snail of stacked basalt flakes that has settled so deeply into the bedrock that its lower courses have begun to fuse with the floor.

Inside, at the very pivot of the spiral, where index-charges are zero and the air is as cold and dead as a drained battery, is where I am.

***

In this world, I am a **Seam-Reader**. 

I am a Slate-Born, but I am old, even by the standards of a race that sleeps for centuries. My outer plates, once the glittering blue-black of fresh obsidian, have grown thick and dry, covered in a pale, velvety rime of mineral salts that I no longer scrape away. My joints do not click with the dry-twig clarity of the young; they have been packed so many times with graphite and talc that my movements are silent, cushioned by layers of black carbon grease that have dried into a smooth, self-healing wax. 

My front limbs are different from those of my siblings who dig the tallow or stack the huts. They do not end in the heavy, spade-like hooves of a block-turner. Instead, my fore-articulations have split over the millennia into four thin, finger-like rods of basaltic horn, tipped with tiny, curved plates of native copper. They are delicate, clicking instruments—less like digits and more like the combs used to card raw wool.

But there is no wool here. There is only the *stone-hair*.

In my lap—the broad, flat shelf formed by my tucked-under sternum—rests a coil of asbestos fiber. It is not white, as it appears when first dug from the veins in the deep rifts, but has been stained a dull, metallic grey by the graphite on my fingers. The fibers are incredibly fine, finer than spider’s silk, yet they possess the cold, heavy weight of the iron from which they were grown. 

I am weaving a **Loom of the Sighs**.

I do not think in words, for my mind operates at the speed of thermal cooling. My thoughts are long, slow gradients of pressure and charge. 

Through my copper sensory filaments, which drag lightly across the braided strands in my lap, I "see" the rope not as a cord, but as a path. Each knot I tie is a memory of a vibration that passed through the floor days or centuries ago. A tight, triple-twist knot represents a sharp, shallow slip in the bedrock fifty miles to the north—a minor settling of the basalt. A long, loose braid of double-stranded stone-wool is the record of a long, low-frequency groan that traveled up from the planet’s liquid iron throat, lasting for seven years before dying away.

This rope is my map. It is the only memory our people possess.

***

A Silt-Spider, small and amber-legged, has crawled into my sanctuary. 

It does not belong here; the air inside the spiral is too dry, too drained of the static charges it uses to orient its copper-leaf antennae. It crawls with an erratic, stumbling gait, its spade-feet making a tiny, frantic *pit-pat-pit* against the jasper floor. It is seeking the grease of my joints. 

I do not move to brush it away. The movement would require three seconds of alignment, five seconds of lifting, and a discharge of thermal energy that I cannot spare. Instead, I let it climb my rear flank. It finds a hinge of my hip where the graphite paste is thick and old. Its hard, horn palps begin to scrape at the dry grease, a tiny, vibrating tickle that registers in my nervous system as a faint, pleasant hum. 

In exchange for the carbon, the spider’s body acts as a small, living ground. The residual static charge it brought in from the outside plains drains through my shell and down into the floor. A tiny, blue spark—no larger than a grain of dust—snaps between its antenna and my primary filament shield.

In that spark, I see the outside world.

It is a brief, phantom flash of infrared. For a microsecond, the outer plains are visible to my mind: the great cliffs are a vertical forest of deep, throbbing maroon; the rifts are jagged, freezing rifts of slate-blue; and far off, on the edge of the glass terrace, three of my siblings are moving. They are hauling a sled of raw tallow, their heavy bodies leaning forward at an angle of five degrees, their movements so synchronized they look like a single, multi-limbed monument sliding across the black mirror of the plain.

The spark dies. The world goes black again—the good, deep black of the sanctuary.

***

I return to the braid.

My copper-tipped fingers slide the next three fibers of asbestos over the main cord. I am waiting. 

The bedrock beneath my belly is silent now, but it is a tense, crowded silence. The pressure is building. Miles above us, in the cold crust that we have never seen and cannot conceive of, some great weight of stone is leaning. The friction is generating a charge that is currently flowing down through the volcanic chimneys, filling the mica fog of the air with a high, sharp hiss that I can feel even through the twelve turns of the spiral walls.

Soon, the Tectonic Sigh will come. 

The air will turn into a blinding, crackling sheet of blue fire. The young will run, their filaments scorched by the voltage, lost on the razor-edges of the obsidian fields.

But they will find their way to this spiral. 

As the earth begins to shake, I will take the three-mile rope of stone-hair from my lap. I will crawl, inch by inch, out of the dark center of the snail. My copper fingers will tie the end of the braid to the ancient beacon-post outside my door, and I will cast the rest of the coil down into the dark, toward the next sanctuary three miles away.

The rope does not conduct electricity; it is asbestos, the great insulator. No matter how many sparks dance in the mica air, the rope will remain dark, cool, and silent—a single, uncharged line of safety stretched across a burning, whistling desert of light.

They will find it by touch. They will grasp the heavy, braided stone-wool with their copper hooves, and they will follow it home.

Until then, I sit in the cool dark. The spider continues its slow, scraping meal at my hip. The air is perfectly still. A single flake of mica, suspended four inches from my left sensory filament, has not moved since I began this knot. It will still be there when the knot is finished.
