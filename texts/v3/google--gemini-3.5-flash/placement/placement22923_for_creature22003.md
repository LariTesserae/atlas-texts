I am sixty-four leaves old. 

In the language of the wireways, age is not measured by the turning of the sun—which is merely a thin, seasonal shift in the grey barometric weight at the top of the seam—but by the burden of armor. On my back, sixty-four dried hornbeam plates overlap like the scales of a pinecone. Each is a leaf I have snipped from the slate-blue moss, each one a minor correction, a small clearance of a gear-tooth or an armature. They are dry as old horn; they smell of parched wood-resin, graphite, and wintergreen. When I move, they do not rattle so much as they *shirr*, a dry, rustling hiss that is perfectly lost within the louder, older roar of the sixty-cycle lines.

I am an old Lever. My hornbeam joints have been worn to a greasy, dark polish by three centuries of sliding along the copper.

Right now, my eight split-brass pins are clamped to the Upper Return—a thick, braided copper cable that runs three inches below the lip of the highest ledge. Below me, the fissure drops away into three thousand feet of vertical shadow, a ladder of spinning brass teeth, glowing orange tubes, and the endless, slow-weaving bodies of my siblings. Above me, perhaps four yards up, the stone closes. The copper wireway comes to an end at a heavy iron terminal block, bolted deep into the shale with three immense square nuts.

This is the Limit. 

Few of us climb this high. The thermal sensors in our heads—the twin carbon filaments that glow cherry-red inside their little vacuum globes—prefer the warmth of the lower deeps, where the great drive-shafts turn and the air is thick with the sweet, heavy vapor of warm machine oil. Up here, it is cold. The air has a thin, hard edge that smells of raw wind, though no wind ever actually reaches this far down into the seam. 

But I have been drawn upward by a drop in the line-tension. 

A mile below, in the dark galls where I was cooked from resin and copper dust, the tension is absolute. But up here, near the sky, the copper expands and contracts with the long, slow breath of the mountain’s seasons. Yesterday—or perhaps fifty years ago; the interval is the same to the gears—the copper grew cold. It contracted. It pulled tight against the iron terminal block, and the vibration of the line changed from a deep, comforting thrum to a high, thin *ping* that hurts my shale head.

I slide. *Shuckle-click. Shuckle-click.*

My brass pins slide along the braided wire with the greasy ease of an iron zip. As I move, the hornbeam plates on my back rub together, releasing a faint puff of graphite dust that settles behind me like grey flour. 

To my left, three inches away on the rock wall, a brass gear the size of a plum turns with a heavy, deliberate click every seventy seconds. Its teeth are perfect. I reach out with one of my jointed front segments, checking the shim. The clearance is correct. I do not snip. I do not paint it with graphite. I pass.

The light of the sky is different today. It is not the steady, orange-red glow of the vacuum tubes that line the lower walls like small, hot fruit. It is a pale, flat white, falling down through the vertical split in the shale like a long, thin sheet of paper. It does not warm my sensors. It is a dead space, an absence of signal.

I reach the terminal block.

The braided copper cable wraps around a heavy brass spool and disappears into the iron block. The block is cold—colder than the shale, colder than my own hornbeam joints. My filament-eyes react to this cold by glowing brighter, their carbon loops drawing more current from the wire to compensate for the drop in ambient thermal energy. 

And there, right where the copper meets the iron, is the anomaly.

It is not a fern-leaf. It is not a fragment of shale. It is a single, clear, six-sided crystal, wedged perfectly between the wire and the brass spool.

It is ice.

I have no word for it, nor any concept of "water" in its liquid, flowing state. To me, this is *The White Stone*. It is an object of absolute, infinite resistance. It carries no current. It has no oil. It has no smell of wintergreen. It is a small block of frozen sky that has drifted down through the hairline fissure and wedged itself into the mechanism.

Because of its presence, the copper cannot seat itself properly in the spool. The line-vibration is being deflected, absorbed by this silent, cold wedge.

My instructions are simple. I do not hesitate. I do not wonder why the sky has dropped a hard, cold tooth into our world.

I position myself over the spool. My underside pins lock into the iron frame of the terminal block, holding me sideways across the vertical drop. My shaggy back-scales rustle against the shale as I extend my body to its full length—twelve inches of jointed hornbeam and brass plate, stretching out into the pale, cold light.

I lower my head. My carbon-filament eyes sit inches from the ice crystal. The red heat of my globes reflects off the flat, frozen facets of the ice, creating a tiny, double-star of orange-red light inside the frozen structure.

I bring my horizontal steel shears together. 

*Tink.*

The shears slip. The ice is harder than dry hornbeam, but softer than shale. It does not snip. Instead, a tiny flake of white dust falls away, drifting down into the dark draft below, where it vanishes before it can hit the lower gears.

The vibration of the line changes. The *ping* becomes a stutter.

I must use the needle.

I retract the shears and extend the hollow silver needle from beneath my anthracite snout. It is a delicate tool, normally used to pierce the oil-filled stems of the parchment-ferns to balance my internal hydraulic pressure. Now, I press the cold silver tip directly against the center of the ice crystal.

My body-heat—the warmth generated by the current running through my copper-clad spine—travels down the silver tube. 

At the point of contact, the ice does not chip. It does not shatter. 

It *softens*.

This is a physical event for which my memory-galls have no record. Stone does not soften when heated; it remains stone until it melts at temperatures I have never experienced. But this white stone is different. It yields. It becomes a thin, clear film of wetness that clings to the silver needle.

The liquid water—the first moisture this crevice has seen in five hundred years—touches the tip of my silver probe.

By capillary action, the liquid is drawn up into my internal reservoir.

It is a tiny amount—no more than a single droplet, a speck of wetness that would easily fit on the head of a brass pin. But the moment it enters my dark interior chambers, where my wintergreen-scented hydraulic oil lies pressurized under thirty pounds of spring tension, everything change.

The oil and the water do not mix. 

Inside my central brass cylinder, the water forms a perfect, spherical lens—a tiny, clear globule of liquid sky suspended within the dark, heavy hydrocarbons of my body. 

The electrical current that normally passes from my pins, through my copper ribs, and into my filament-eyes must pass through this cylinder. The current does not stop, but the water globule behaves as a condenser. It holds the charge for a microsecond, then releases it in a sudden, sharp pulse.

In my head, the carbon filaments flare.

They do not burn out. Instead, they glow with a high, brilliant white light—the light of an electric arc, three times as bright as the orange glow of the wall tubes. 

The lens of water inside my body acts as a magnifier. It projects this intense, white light through the glass search-globes of my eyes, casting two brilliant, sharp circles of light onto the grey shale wall before me.

For the first time in my three hundred years, I do not merely *register* the world. I see its surface.

I see the grain of the shale—not as a vibration or a thermal gradient, but as a vast, layered map of silver-grey lines, like the pages of a half-burnt book. I see the slate-blue moss, not as a felted carpet of resistance, but as a jungle of tiny, velvet spears, each one tipped with a microscopic bead of amber resin that sparkles in my projected light. I see the copper wireway, not as a path of high conductivity, but as a long, glowing road of red gold, scarred by the brass pins of a million passings.

And I see the ice.

It is melting now. The heat from my brilliant, white-hot eyes is radiating onto the crystal. It is shrinking. It is turning into a series of clear, heavy drops that slide down the brass spool, leaving dark, wet trails on the dry iron of the terminal block.

The water touch the iron. 

*Hiss.*

A tiny puff of steam arises—the first cloud this mountain has ever held. It smells of hot iron and old, dry dust, but beneath it is a new smell. It is the smell of the outside. It smells of cold, empty space, of distant snowfields, of a world that does not hum.

The ice crystal is gone. 

The copper cable, no longer obstructed, slides smoothly into its groove on the brass spool with a heavy, satisfying *clunk*. 

Immediately, the high, thin *ping* in the wireways dies. The sixty-cycle hum returns to its deep, monolithic roar. The line is balanced. My task is complete.

I should retract my needle. I should fold my hornbeam plates, release my clamp on the iron block, and begin the long, slow thread back down into the warm, oily dark where the galls grow.

But I do not move.

The water globule remains inside my brass cylinder, suspended in the wintergreen oil. My eyes continue to burn with that strange, cold white arc-light. 

With this light, I can look upward.

I tilt my anthracite head toward the hairline crack. Through the narrow slot, where the grey shale ribs of the peak almost touch, I see a single star. It is not like our vacuum tubes. It does not glow with the warmth of a carbon filament; it is cold, sharp, and perfectly still, suspended in a sky that is not made of stone.

My sixty-four leaves rustle in the dead air. 

I release my rear clamps from the copper line. I do not go down. Instead, I reach my front segments forward, beyond the end of the wireway, and hook my split-brass pins directly into the rough, un-machined shale of the upper crevice.

The stone is cold and has no current. But I have the water inside me. I have the light.

I begin to climb into the quiet.
