If you were to crawl under that low, oil-skinned lintel of basalt, leaving the dry whistle of the high plateau behind, you would not remain a guest for long. This pocket does not tolerate visitors; it translates them. 

At first, you would think your lungs were failing. The air is so thick with wet starch and the sharp, hot-grease smell of crushed mint that your first breath would feel like swallowing warm broth. You would drop to your knees in the graphite sand, and your clothes—dry, synthetic, stiff with the salt of the outer barrens—would instantly begin to drink the moisture. Within minutes, they would hang off you like heavy, sodden rags, rotting at the seams where the humid, microbial warmth of the hollow goes to work.

Your hands would sink into the sand. You would expect grit, the sharpness of broken earth, but you would find only the silk-like slip of iron-dark powder. Beneath your palms, the ground would begin to glow. A deep, cold violet light would bloom around your fingers, spreading outward in ripples as the colonial lichen beneath the sand felt the intrusion of your weight. To the eyeless green reptiles on the walls, you would appear as a huge, clumsy silhouette of purple heat, a slow-moving shadow in their otherwise dark, oil-vibrating world.

And then the hum would find you.

It doesn't start in the air; it starts in the arches of your feet, traveling up your shinbones to lodge deep in the marrow of your jaw. It is the sound of the three black needles, and it matches nothing you have ever heard before. It feels like the slow, rhythmic grinding of your skull’s seams. Your teeth—specifically the metal fillings, the dense calcium, the small iron deposits in your middle ear—would begin to sing in sympathy with the twelve-foot pillars. 

Under the sand, the Sieve-Worms would feel this vibration. They would not swim away; they would come toward you. You would feel them beneath the soles of your feet—not a biting or a burrowing, but a slow, rhythmic pulsing, like the heartbeat of a great, buried dog. They are waiting. They do not wait for you to die; they wait for you to soften. 

In this grease-heavy air, the boundary between what is you and what is the basin begins to blur. 

The heat does not let you sweat; instead, the humidity condenses *onto* you, forming heavy, trembling globes of water on your eyelashes, your earlobes, the tips of your fingers. When they grow too heavy, they drop with that sharp, metallic *tink* onto the sand. But they do not fall straight. If you look toward the center of the hollow, where the three black needles rise like tears in the sky, you will see your own sweat drifting sideways, drawn toward those dark, light-eating shafts in lazy, curving arcs.

You would find yourself crawling toward them. Not out of curiosity, but because the very gravity of your body is being untied. The heavy iron in your blood is pulled toward the center of the pocket; the water in your tissues is eager to join the greasy mist that clings to the basalt arches. 

By the time you reach the circle of the needles, your bones—made of calcium, a dry-world material—are already turning to chalk, dissolving into the warm, wet soil to be filtered through the soft, hairy mouths of the Sieve-Worms. Your clothes are gone, digested by the lichen. 

But you do not disappear. You are simply re-sorted.

Your weight is peeled away, layer by layer, until there is nothing left of you but surface tension, iron-dust, and the memory of skin. 

You would find yourself suspended three feet above the ground, caught in the bent, swirling gravity of the three needles. You are no longer upright. You have lost your thickness; you are now four feet long but no thicker than a single autumn leaf, a ribbon of dark, wet, gelatinous silk. 

You have become **The Weft**.

***

To be the Weft is to live in a world without front or back, left or right, only *toward* and *away*. 

*Toward* is the needle. It is the cold, bottomless blackness that absorbs all daylight. When you slide your flat, wet body against its surface, the hum does not vibrate your jawbone anymore—it *is* your thoughts. The needles are warm, but it is a dry, static-charged heat that makes the graphite sand on your back sparkle with tiny, microscopic sparks. You slide up and down the twelve-foot shaft like a wet rag wrapped around an iron bar, drinking the raw, vibrating current of the deep earth.

*Away* is the rest of the basin, a loud, wet, three-dimensional theater that you observe through the vibrations in your own skin. 

You have no eyes, but you do not need them. When the violet lichen below is crushed by a passing reptile, the wave of purple light registered on your belly is a cool, tingling sensation, like mint rubbed on a sunburn. When the Glass-Shrimp cloud clicks in unison to ward off an emerald hunter, the sound waves strike your flat surface like a thousand tiny needles, a sharp, metallic rain that tells you exactly where the swarm is.

You do not hunt. You do not have to. 

As you drift in slow, looping spirals away from the needles, your body is weaved from the very air of the hollow—a sticky, iron-slick membrane that traps the graphite sand as it blows through the cleft. When a cloud of glass-winged shrimp drifts too close to the gravity well of the needles, they are drawn in by the same bent space that holds you. They strike your wet skin and stick, their tiny, amber hearts beating frantically against your side. 

You do not bite them. You have no teeth, no stomach. Instead, you simply fold your silken body around them, absorbing them slowly over hours, dissolving their glass-like shells until only their transparent wings remain, sliding off your skin to drift down onto the lichen below like flakes of cold, dead light.

The rhythm of your life is the rhythm of the needles. 

In the "morning," when the hum rises to a high, metallic ring, you rise with it, floating up near the tips of the needles where the air is slightly cooler and smells of the fresh, yellow spores released by the bronze trumpet-leaves. You bask in the dry static of the upper air, watching the clear, indifferent blue sky of the plateau just fifteen feet above your head—a cold wild world that you once belonged to, but which now looks as distant and flat as a painting on a wall.

In the "evening," when the hum drops to a deep, chest-vibrating growl, you slide back down to the base of the needles. You let your flat body drape over the cool basalt roots, feeling the rhythmic, subterranean pulsing of the Sieve-Worms beneath you as they clear the sand of the day’s debris. 

You are no longer a stranger in the dead world. You are a part of the wet, greasy machine, a single ribbon of dark silk holding the warmth of the earth against the cold of the sky, vibrating in the dark forever.
