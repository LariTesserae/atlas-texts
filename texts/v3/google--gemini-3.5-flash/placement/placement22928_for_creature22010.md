There is no before. There is only the compression.

It begins in the belly, where the fluid is thickest, and pressure is not a sensation but the shape of the container. 

To exist here is to be wedged. I am not a creature that walks, nor am I a plant that clings; I am the mass of cured tallow, copper-chitin, and old fiber that has been pressed into the split seam of the High Siphon, eighty feet above the boiling basin of the black bog.

I was not always one thing. In the wet dark, before the heat-itch took us, there were many of us—small, blind bags of high-pressure brine, scraping the yellow grease from the basalt girders. We lived in the corners where the rain didn't hit directly, sliding over the smooth stone, tasting the scale. But there was a whistle.

The whistle is the sound of the world’s blood escaping. It is a thin, high needle of superheated steam, screaming through a hair-thin crack in the thirty-foot iron sleeve. To a scraper, that whistle is a taste we cannot resist: it is the flavor of dissolved basalt, of liquefied lichen, of the mineral marrow of the deep earth, cooked and atomized.

We crawled toward the sound. We always do. We climbed over one another’s leathery backs, a heap of wet, sliding bladders, driven by the itch in our bellies. The steam was white-hot; it stripped the outer skins from the leaders, turning them to a gray, jelly-like glue. But we did not turn back. The ones behind pressed the ones in front deeper into the crack. 

The heat cooked us together. Our copper jaws, meant for scraping the iron, found the rugged edges of the fracture and bit down, sealing our faces into the metal. Our legs melted into the tallow of our neighbors. Our individual pulses, once frantic and small like the ticking of dry grass, slowed. The small gears of our hearts wore down their teeth until they meshed into a single, lazy cog.

Now, we are the **Plug**.

***

From the outside, under the dripping soot-clouds, I look like a great, black scab of self-vulcanizing gristle, three yards long, bulging from the iron side of the siphon. 

One side of me—my tail, if it can be called that—hangs out into the weeping cold. It is constantly lashed by the heavy, oil-streaked rain that slides down the basalt cliff behind me. It is covered in the pale-violet velvet of the predatory mold, which lives on my sweat. The mold hums when the high-voltage lines overhead crackle with blue light, and that hum runs through my hide like a cool needle.

But my face is inside the dark of the pipe.

On that side, the pressure is forty atmospheres. The steam—dry, invisible, and roaring—seeks to blow me out into the abyss. It presses against my cured, charcoal-hard skin with the weight of a mountain. It forces its way into my pores, through my fibrous interior, cooking me from the inside out. 

But I do not break. My jaws are locked into the iron grain. The steam does not destroy me; it feeds me. 

In the steam is the memory of the subterranean earth. I taste the deep granite through which the water was pumped; I taste the oil of the ancient drills; I taste the dissolved minerals of the Thrummers who died in the canyons miles away and whose bones were washed down into the black drains. The history of this world is a hot broth, and I am the tongue that filters it.

I feel the machine.

*Thung... thung... thung...*

The giant piston in the valley three miles to the south strikes every four seconds. The shockwave travels up the basalt mountain, through the iron anchors, and into the siphon. Every time it strikes, the metal of the pipe expands by a fraction of a millimeter. 

My body receives the blow. The jar forces my internal fluids from my hot, dry core toward my wet outer skin, oxygenating my tissue before the rain cools it. I have no heart of my own; the great piston is my pump. If the engine stops, the blood in my center will pool and turn to cold grease, and I will rot off the pipe like a dead fungus. 

But the engine does not stop. It has not stopped since the first scraper crawled into the first seam, five hundred years ago.

***

Occasionally, the silence of the dark is broken by more than the wind.

A Thrummer climbs past my siphon. I feel the weight of its suction-padded feet through the iron, a slow, heavy rhythmic dragging. It is blind, gray, and massive. It stops near me. Its long, wet tongue—rough as a file and dripping with alkaline saliva—licks the greasy condensation from the basalt bracket right beside my tail. 

The tongue touches my outer skin. It does not eat me; I am too hard, too salt-cured, too much like the iron itself. We are parts of the same house. The Thrummer’s chest-box thrums—*vrrrr-uuuup*—a low, mechanical vibrate that shakes the mold on my back. It is checking if the siphon is whole. It feels the vibration of my seal, finds it solid, and moves on, its hide scraping against the wet stone until the sound is lost in the roar of the waterfall below.

Then, there is the light.

Deep in the canyon, a furnace door cracks open to dump slag.

The absolute blackness is torn away. For five seconds, the world is orange, wet, and vertical. 

I see nothing, for I have no eyes. But the heat of the glare hits my tail like a blowtop. The violet mold on my hide shrinks back, its cilia sizzling. In those five seconds, I feel the true shape of my home: 
The cliff behind me is not a mountain, but a wall of stepped masonry three miles high, half-flesh and half-brick.
The waterfall below me is not a river, but a black, oily discharge from a row of thirty-six automated turbines, foaming white in the orange light.
And above me, hanging from the high-voltage lines like great, wet rags, are the Soot-Skimmers, their white acoustic dishes turned downward, catching the echo of the steam-shriek.

Then the iron door slams shut. The glare is gone. My mold expands again, drinking the dark. The cold rain washes over me, carrying the soot back down to the bogs.

***

I will stay here for a long time. 

Perhaps fifty years. Perhaps a hundred. 

The steam is patient, but it is sharp. It will slowly, micron by micron, wear away my inner face. It will dissolve the copper chitin of my jaws. The salt in my blood will be carried off in the vapor, and the fiber of my body will become brittle under the constant, four-second hammering of the piston.

I will begin to leak. A thin whistle will return.

Other scrapers, crawling through the mossy canyons below, will lift their wet, blind heads. They will taste the mineral-broth in the air. They will hear the whistle. They will begin to climb, their segmented legs clicking on the basalt, their soft bodies heavy with brine.

They will find me where I am thinning. They will crawl over my back, pressing me deeper into the iron. Their bodies will melt into mine; their jaws will find the metal where mine have let go.

I will not die. I will only become deeper. My memory—the taste of the deep steam, the rhythm of the four-second strike, the smell of the grease-grazers—will be pressed into their marrow, cooked into the new plug that will take my place.

We will keep the siphon whole. The steam will go where it must go. The rain will fall. The water will return to the dark.

We are content in the heavy, roaring wetness. We are the skin of the machine, and we are entirely complete.
