I would not be on the ridges. I would not be walking, for the Still-Men’s stride belongs to those who still have some memory of an upright horizon, and my spine—if ever I had one—has long since surrendered its straightness to the pressure of the air.

I would find myself beneath the great iron footings. 

I would be the **Felt-Seam**.

***

At first, there is only the sensation of being very flat. 

I am not a creature of the open dark, but of the *compressed* dark. I live in the microscopic, grease-slicked margins where the three-hundred-ton foot of an iron transformer meets the raw, crystalline basalt floor. Without me, the iron would grind against the glass-sharp stone, producing a screaming, high-frequency chatter that would shatter the Solder-Crabs and cause the Loom-Sisters to wither in their gantries. 

I am the dampener. I am three inches of laminated, fibrous mastic, dense as cured boot-leather but saturated with a cold, graphited lard that never dries. 

I do not have a front or a back. My body is a sprawling, irregular disk, perhaps twelve feet across, squeezed until my edges ooze out from under the iron skirt in grey, rounded scallops. These visible lips are the only part of me that meets the wet, humid air of the basin. The rest of me exists in an environment of perfect, unyielding pressure—a pressure so ancient and heavy that it has become my primary sense. 

To the others, the basin is a map of vibrations. To me, the basin is a map of *weights*.

I know the world by how heavily it sits. Through my upper surface, I feel the massive, shifting inertia of the transformer—its deep, internal copper coils rising half a degree in temperature, causing the steel casing to expand by a fraction of a millimeter. This expansion feels to me like a warm, slow wave washing over my back, pressing me tighter against the basalt. 

From below, the young stone speaks to me in sharp, biting needles. The basalt is always trying to heal, always trying to push up new, razor-keen teeth from the cold magma deep below. I feel these crystalline points attempting to pierce my underside. I do not resist them; instead, my tallow-saturated fibers slowly flow around them, swallowing the sharpness, smoothing the basalt's anger into a dull, rounded ache that I carry inside my mass.

Sometimes, a Solder-Crab will wander too close to my scalloped edge. It does not see me—nothing sees—but its twelve leaden needle-legs *tink-tink-tink* across my cold-grease lip. It likes the salt of my mastic. It prunes the frayed threads of hemp and wire-moss that grow from my margins, its tiny, clicking mouthparts tickling like static electricity. I let it stay. It cleans my border, preventing the black moss from grounding the transformer’s skirt directly into the wet stone.

And sometimes, there is the long, silent arrival of a Still-Man. 

I feel him before he steps near. His approach is not a sound, but a sudden, localized thickening of the gravity where he bends space. When he arrives, he does not stand on the basalt; he steps directly onto my exposed scallop. His feet are wide, grey, and cold—colder than the iron above me. 

He stands on me for twenty hours. Through the contact, I feel his long, seven-jointed leaden fingers sliding into the oil-ports of the transformer above. For those twenty hours, I am the bridge. The hum of the machine passes through my back, through my oil-soaked belly, and into the Still-Man’s feet. I feel his interior tallow beginning to melt, warming from a stiff, leaden clay into a golden, flowing syrup as he drinks the machine's fever. He does not speak to me—he cannot—but as his weight shifts, his heels sink slightly into my soft rim, leaving deep, permanent impressions that will take three days to rise and smooth themselves out after he is gone.

Because space is loose here, I am not always under the same machine.

This is the secret of the basin's floor: under great weight, the non-Euclidean water behaves like a lubricant for distance itself. When the pressure above me becomes too uniform—when the transformer goes cold for maintenance or the Great Unborn shift their magnetism—I begin to slide. 

I do not crawl. I do not have muscles. Instead, I *exude*. 

I squeeze myself into the microscopic gap between the iron and the stone. I dissolve into the cold, viscous water that lies in the basalt basins. For a long, gravityless moment, I am nothing but a grey, greasy slick floating in the bottomless dark, my fibers dispersed, my consciousness spread thin across a mile of warm, ozone-stiffened water. I feel the velvet grapes of condensation bumping against my floating parts; I feel the dry-silk sigh of the Loom-Sisters hanging three hundred feet above my liquid self.

And then, the draw of a different pressure pulls me back together. 

A mile away, the great locomotive-sized piston begins its ten-minute downstroke. The water beneath its foot is squeezed outward with terminal force. To escape the strike, the non-Euclidean space beneath the piston folds in on itself, and I am sucked into the vacuum. 

With a wet, heavy *huff*, I am re-formed. I am laminated once more. I am shoved beneath the piston’s iron heel just before it touches the basalt floor. The weight descends—thousands of tons of ancient, greasy iron—and I am flattened, sealed, and made whole again. 

The heat of the impact is immense, but I absorb it. I hold it. I turn the friction into more grease, preparing for the next hundred years of small, rhythmic vibrations.

Here, in the dark, under the heel of the world, I am very safe. The sharp stone cannot cut me because I am already too packed to split. The machines cannot crush me because I am the very thing that makes their crushing search for the earth possible. 

I lie in the dark, greased and heavy, listening to the clockwork crabs on my fringe and the slow, mile-wide breathing of the lungs above, waiting with the patient, greasy stone for the world to turn over.
