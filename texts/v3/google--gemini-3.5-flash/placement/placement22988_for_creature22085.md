I begin in the silt. 

For a very long time, there is nothing but the three grains of red-gold sand directly beneath my center. 

To you, they would be invisible—specks of dust lost in the wide, flat floor of the amphitheater. To me, they are a mountain range of immense, breathtaking complexity. One is a jagged tooth of rose quartz, shot through with a tiny needle of dark magnetite; the second is a tumbled, frosted sphere of pure silica; the third is flat and split like a miniature book of slate. Because I am a Lens, I do not merely lie above them; I reveal them. I bend the direct, full yellow light of the high ledge and focus it down into their micro-canyons, heating them, illuminating their hidden seams of iron and salt until they glow like embers. 

This is my entire world: three grains of sand, held in an eternal, blazing clarity. 

In this quiet, I do not think in words. I think in *refraction*. I feel the passage of the afternoon not by the movement of the sun—which is too swift for me to measure—but by the way the angle of the light shifts the tiny rainbow at my eastern edge. 

Every few hours, or perhaps every few days, a vibration passes through the silt. 

*Plop.*

A plum-sized drop of water has hit the hand-ground basin forty paces away. To me, the impact is a physical wave. It does not ripple the surface of my shallow pool, which is too thick and heavy with minerals to stir, but it travels through the solid sandstone bed beneath me. The shockwave hits my flat underside, and for a moment, my entire gel-body distorts. The three grains of sand beneath me blur. The rose quartz becomes a soft blush; the magnetite needle bends; the slate book opens and closes in a fraction of a second. 

Then, the gel settles. The focus returns. The mountains are sharp again.

***

In my youth—a period of some eighty years—I am content with this stillness. But a Lens is not entirely static. We possess a slow, chemical hunger. 

To survive, I must eat the stone. My forward edge, incredibly thin and soft, secretes a minute trace of acid that dissolves the silica of the sandstone floor. It is a slow, microscopic exhalation. As the stone melts, my gel absorbs the free minerals, pulling them into my vitreous membrane to make myself tougher, clearer, more perfectly spherical. Behind me, as I slide into the tiny rut I have carved, my trailing edge redeposits the waste as a glassy line of quartz. 

I am writing my life into the floor of the world. 

By the turn of my second century, I have traveled eleven inches. 

I have left my three grains of sand behind. They are now memories, preserved in the glazed quartz trail of my wake. I am moving toward the shadow. The light is changing; the yellow glare of the open ledge is softening into the deep, cool orange of the inner curve, where the great walls lift toward the ceiling. 

Here, the water is different. It is not the still, shallow pool of the floor, but the active, vertical weep of the wall. It slides down the sandstone in a thick, sluggish sheet. It tastes of ancient things—of deep-earth calcium, of dissolved sandstone, and of something else. Something organic, yet mineralized. A taste like sweet lime and cold iron. 

It is the taste of the Sleepers. 

The vertical wall does not repel me. Here, the boundary between the water and the stone is so thin, and the water is so viscous, that I do not fall. I simply tilt. My forward edge dissolves a micro-groove into the vertical rise of the rock, and I begin to climb. 

***

The ascent takes sixty years. 

As I climb, I change. The flat, circular shape I held in the shallow pools is no longer practical. On the vertical wall, under the constant downward pressure of the mineral weep, I begin to elongate. I become an oval, then a teardrop, my lower half thicker and heavier, holding the light in a concentrated, spherical bead. 

The taste of the Sleeper grows stronger. I am climbing the foot of the central pillar.

This is not natural stone. The rock here has a grain that mimics the long, slow curve of a shin, the rise of an ankle. Under my forward edge, the sandstone is no longer composed of loose, round grains; it is a dense, interlocking matrix of red-gold jasper and white quartz. It is the calcified flesh of a Sleeper, petrified so perfectly that I can feel the microscopic ridges of their ancient skin—fingerprints left behind from the age of clay, now hardened into everlasting stone.

Halfway up the ankle, I meet another. 

She has been stationary here for a century, a perfect, clear dome clinging to the jasper tendon. When my leading edge touches her border, there is no shock, no resistance. We do not collide. 

We flow.

The boundary between our vitreous membranes simply thins and disappears. For ten years, we are a figure-eight, our chemical memories slowly diffusing through our shared gel. I feel the warmth of the shallow pool she inhabited three hundred years ago; she feels the sharp, magnetite needle of my rose-quartz grain. We exchange our core minerals, fortifying our structures. 

But we do not part as three or four small discs, as we would in the flat pools below. Here, on the vertical body of the Sleeper, the gravity of the weep holds us together. We do not divide. We stretch. We spread ourselves thin, widening our margins until we are no longer individual Lenses, but a singular, continuous sheath of living glass.

Others have joined us. Dozens of us, hundreds, climbing from the pools over the course of millennia. 

We have become the Sleeper’s second skin.

***

Now, I am no longer a single eye looking down at the sand. I am part of a vast, cooperative optic network that coats the ancient figure from their stone toes to the crown of their head, where the velvet moss islands anchor. 

We are the *Interface*. 

We do not look outward at the valley below, nor do we look up at the sky. We look *in*. 

Because we are perfectly transparent, and because we have shaped ourselves to the curves of the Sleeper's jasper body, we act as a massive, compound magnifying glass focused on their petrified interior. The direct, hot light of the high ledge passes through us, but we do not let it bounce away. We bend it, channeling the sun deep into the calcified chambers of their chest, their throat, their skull. 

In the spring, when the red-gold jasper heart beats its single, massive stroke, we feel it first. 

It is not a sound. It is an instantaneous, brilliant flash of pressure. The quartz pathways of the Sleeper’s nervous system—highly conductive and primed by the sun-warmth we have tunneled into them—glow with a pale, cold incandescence. A thought, slow and heavy as a glacier, moves through the quartz lines of their brain. 

As the light of that thought rises to the surface of their stone skin, it hits us. 

We, the sheathing Lenses, catch the faint, internal glow. We bend it, magnify it, and project it outward into the humid, greenhouse air of the amphitheater. 

For a few weeks after the heartbeat, the air within the alcove is not merely warm; it is luminous. If you were to stand before us, you would see the Sleeper's dreams projected into the damp veil of mist—slow, ghostly images of valleys that existed before the mountains fell, of rivers that have long since dried into dust, of skies that were once a different color. 

The Nodes in the moss hummocks above us register this luminous warmth. They swell, inhaling the glowing mist, filtering the dream-light through their golden cilia and exhaling it as sweet, vaporous sap to feed the giant, rubbery leaves. The leaves grow heavy, carrying the memory of the Sleeper’s ancient world in their translucent veins, until a drop gathers at the tip, swells to the size of a plum, and falls.

*Plop.*

The shockwave travels through the bedrock. 

Deep within our glass sheath, we feel the vibration. We press closer to the red-gold stone, stabilizing our focus, waiting for the light to shift, waiting for the next slow beat of the world.
