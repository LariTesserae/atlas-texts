I do not find myself looking down upon the cleft, for to have eyes is to be a stranger, and to be a stranger here is to be too large, too dry, and too swift. To exist in this crevasse, one must abandon the vertical posture of the sky-dwellers. One must lie flat.

I am a belly. 

I am a broad, soft leaf of grey muscle, pressed against the wet, polished shale of the Third Flank. Above me, my calcite back-plates-—seven overlapping leaves of cold, water-slick stone—do not feel like armor; they feel like the sky. They are the heavy, mineral roof that keeps the weight of the mountain from crushing my soft parts.

I have no face. Where a face might be, I have only a wide, crescent scraper of black, iron-hard silica. It is my only tool, my only mouth, my only hand. 

I do not know "silence." To those with ears, this place is a roar of water, but to me, the world is a constant, shifting web of impact. I do not hear the drops; I *am* the landing.

Every three seconds, the great drop—the *Great Chord*—strikes the terracotta cup of the sixth terrace. It does not sound like a bell; it is a sharp, blue-cold shiver that starts at the tip of my front-smart plate and runs backward through my segments, leaving a tiny, electric tickle in my tail-tip. Two inches to the left, a hairline trickle runs through a conduit of orange clay; it sings to my belly as a rapid, rhythmic *purr*, like a tiny heart beating in the rock. 

This is my map. I do not see the towers. I feel their columns as thick, heavy pillars of silence that block the vibrations of the drips behind them. The city is a forest of sound-shadows. I know the way to the reservoir not because I remember a turn, but because as I crawl, the *purr* of the gutter becomes a *clack*, and the *clack* becomes a deep, hollow *thrum* that vibrates in my middle-most segments.

Today, there is a dead spot.

It is a small, cold hush on the high ledge of the fourth needle-tower white. The water is dripping there—the weight of the drip is landing—but the vibration is flat. The *ring* is gone. It is a mushy, wet *thud*.

Silt.

I do not think, *I must go and clean it.* I do not think at all. But my left edge begins to ripple. The grey mucus under my belly—the thick, salty grease that keeps me from tearing on the sharp slate—spreads thin. I begin my slide.

My journey is measured in drips. It takes four thousand drops of the *Great Chord* for me to cross the gap between my current terrace and the next step. Each step is the body of one of my kind, though I do not know them as ancestors; I know them as the steps that do not shake. They are the perfect, still stone. They are the road that has forgotten how to tremble.

My front-plate scrapes. I feel the grain of a quartz speck—it is huge and rough, a boulder the size of my scraper’s corner. It is wedged in the mouth of the clay pipe, choking the song of the trickle. 

I begin the scraping. *Scritch. Scritch.* The vibration travels up through my skull-plate and into my gut, a dry, grating sensation that makes my calcite scales twitch. I collect the dust. I mix it with the thick, iron-rich fluid that seeps from my glands. I push the paste into a small fracture where the clay pipe meets the wall. I smooth it with my tail-plate.

Now, the water flows. The *thud* becomes a *pure*, high-pitched *ping*.

The vibration hits my belly. It is correct. The world is balanced again.

But my tail-plate is cold.

For ninety years, the cold has been creeping upward from my last segment. At first, it was only a slight resistance when I bent my shell to round a tight corner. Now, the three rear-most scales are no longer white; they have turned the dark, greasy grey of the limestone beneath me. They no longer feel the light hum of the gutters. They do not feel the *Great Chord*. They are dry. They are heavy.

I am not afraid. I have no word for *I*, so I cannot have a word for *end*.

I crawl three inches more, up to the gap where the terracotta gutter has begun to sag under the weight of its own wetness. There is a space beneath it—a small, empty arch where the support has eroded over three centuries of seeping water.

I fit myself into the curve. It is a tight shape, but my body has always been the shape of the stone. I press my belly down, hard, until the mucus ceases to flow and my soft flesh touch-locks with the raw shale.

I will not scrape again. My silica shovel is dull, worn down to a smooth, round lip.

The first drop strikes the gutter above me. The vibration pass down through the clay, into my calcite back, and straight through my frozen belly into the foundation stone. 

The sound is perfect. It is louder now, clearer, because I am there to carry it.

The cold moves into my fourth segment. The milk-white turns to slate. 

I am becoming the corner. I am becoming the song.
