The roar is the first thing that belongs to me. It is not an ornament of the day; it is my skeleton. 

I do not hear it with my ears—their canals are small, plugged with thick, tallow-like wax to keep out the heavy mist—but with the flat of my stomach and the thin, hollow bones of my hips. The basalt step beneath me is humming. It says *shhh-clack-clack-click* from the millions of small, armored things three terraces down, and then it sighs with the deep, wet *whuff* of the cistern-mouths below. If the stone ever stopped singing, I think my heart would stop too, because my pulse has learned to wait for the intervals between the big frog-groans.

I am lying on my belly. I have been here since the moss on the vertical face of the thirty-second vault went from green to the dark, greasy violet that means it has drunk its fill of the salt-drippings. 

I am not small, but I am flat. My head is a spade of cartilage and soft, velvet-furred skin, and my neck is so long and double-hinged that I can lay my chin against my shoulder-blade to lick the condensation off my own ribs. My skin is the color of a wet turnip, patterned with faint blue veins that look like the cracks in the old glazed tiles of the cisterns. On my flanks, where the gray light sometimes reaches from the high slit above, I have a few patches of hairless moss-fuzz, but they are dying now. They are turning gray. 

I am losing my green. I am going down.

***

Beside me, in the cradle where the great, wax-white root of the fig-tree meets the buttress, is the one we call *The-three-low-pulses*. 

He has been in the root for four seasons of the big snail-hatch. The gray wood has already come up over his hips; it looks like warm lard that has hardened around his thighs, sealing him into the limestone. His skin is very dry now, almost like the cork of the tree itself, and his milky eyes are entirely grown over by the thick, pink folds of his lids. 

But his wrist is still bare. 

I reach out my arm—it is long, with three elbows that click like dry wood when I stretch—and I press my flat, two-lobed palm against his wrist. I do not do it quickly. In this dampness, if you move fast, you slide. You lose the *Hold*. 

I wait. My heart beats once. Twice. Three times.

Then, from the deep wood of the root, through his dry skin and into the suctioning ridges of my palm, comes the tremor.

*Thrum-thrum... glide.*

It is very weak. He is telling me about the water-conduit that runs behind the face of the great aqueduct. He is telling me that three thousand paces to the left, where the masonry buttress has buckled under the weight of the ivy, there is a leak of sweet, cold water that has not yet been found by the dog-sized snails. He is giving me the direction by the frequency of the stone-hum there—a high, clear vibration like a dry leaf rubbing against clay.

I shiver my chest against the wet basalt to tell him I have the map. My skin ripples in five short waves. *I have it. I have it.*

He does not shiver back. The wood is very heavy today. Next season, his wrist will be gone beneath the gray bark, and he will just be another part of the corner that keeps the terrace from sliding into the great, black throat below.

***

I am a middle-dweller, but only for a little longer. 

For many turnings of the snail, I was a Climber. I knew the high, narrow ledges where the slate-gray sky is so close you can smell the thinness of the air—a smell like cold iron and wet feathers. I used to climb the vertical columns of the great arches, my suction-pads ticking against the wet brick, to scrape the yellow crust from the shells of the giant snails while they slept. I liked the high terraces. I liked the whistling of the small, fat birds when they returned to their holes in the friezes. 

But my skin is changing. The velvet-pile is falling away from my chest. My fingers are growing longer—the two-lobed pads are splitting down the middle, turning into fine, white threads that can feel the difference between a draft of dry air and a draft of wet air from five hundred paces away. 

I am becoming a Deeper. 

To go down is not a sorrow. It is just the way the water runs. The Climbers are small and green, but they are always tense; they must hold so hard against the high winds that sigh through the crevices. Down below, in the lightless shafts where the air is as thick as oil and smells of old, rotting brick, there is no wind. There is only the warmth. The great, slow warmth that rises from the very bottom of the world, where the stone is still soft and wet from the hands of whoever made it.

I slide my hip off the step. 

My movement is like lard melting down a stove. I do not use my feet to step; I lay my long, flat belly against the vertical rise of the next tier, my suctioning skin making a sound like *shhh-shhh-shhh* as I let my weight drop centimeter by centimeter. 

The gray light above is gone now. The slit of sky has narrowed to a thread, then to a point, then to nothing. 

The world here is black, but it is not empty. It is more crowded than the high terraces. The sound is different here. It is not the whistling of birds, but the deep, subterranean *throm-throm-throm* of the great cistern-mouths. The air is so thick with moisture that I do not need to drink; my skin drinks for me, absorbing the warm, greasy mist until my veins swell up like violet ropes.

I feel a tremor through the step. Not from *The-three-low-pulses*, who is far above us now, locked in his tree.

This tremor is massive. It comes from the bottom of the elevator shaft, five levels down. It is a slow, rhythmic wave of heat and muscle.

*We are here,* the vibration says. *The clay is warm today. The snails have laid their eggs in the drainage wells. Come down.*

I let go of the basalt with my top limbs. I hang by my lower pads, my long body dangling into the warm, black updraft like a wet bag. The air rushes past my underbelly, smelling of ancient brick-dust and the sweet, musk-like grease of my people.

I do not look down. There is nothing to see. There is only the grip, the stone, and the hum.

I release my lower pads.

I fall five feet, ten feet, through the warm dark, until my long, double-jointed arms strike the side of a massive, wet pillar. My fingers—now long and white and thread-like—wrap around the brick. My suction-pads catch. The skin bites into the moss-rot.

I hold.

The vibration of the pillar moves through my collarbone. It is a good pillar. It has been here since the beginning. It will hold me.
