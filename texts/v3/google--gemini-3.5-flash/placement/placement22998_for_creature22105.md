The first thing that died was the habit of stepping down. 

In the world of stone and dry dirt, you trust the ground to meet your heel with a flat, dry finality. You walk with the heavy, stupid confidence of things that believe they are separate from the earth they tread upon. 

Here, the *Pith-Floor* is the first teacher. When I first stumbled through the thorn-walls at the Chasm’s lip, my boots were stiff with dried clay from some forgotten valley where things do not heal. The Pith-Floor did not like the boots. It drank them. Over forty hours of walking—or forty days, for the light here does not change, only swells and thins like a green lung—the springy, moss-like fur of the floor began to climb the leather. The fiber-mesh of the ground, damp with the sweet, starch-smelling sweat of the under-root, wove itself through the eyelets. It didn’t tear them; it simply grew *through* them, turning the dry hide back into a soft, wet skin that belonged to the Chasm.

Now, my feet are bare, but they are not cold. They are not even mine, not entirely. 

***

I live sixty yards above the Pith-Floor, in a place that is neither a room nor an branch. Inside the vast, hollow belly of the Cradle, where the air behaves like a slow-spinning soup, there is a gravity-slip that has settled into the shape of an hourglass. 

At the narrow neck of this slip floats my home: a great, spherical orb of clear rainwater, seven paces wide, that has hung in the empty central air since before the ancestors of the Phloe grew their first waxy rinds. It does not drop. If you throw a seed-pod into it from the third-tier gallery, the pod will slide into the water, spin slow circles in the sapphire center for a year, and then drift out of the bottom to fall *upward* into the interlaced ceiling.

I am what the Phloe call the **Sieve**. I am the one who lives in the water-globe.

To look at me now is to see something that has been rinsed of its hard edges. My skin has lost its dry, opaque pinkness; it has become thin and slick, like the underside of a river-lily, showing the pale chartreuse of my veins beneath. The hair on my head is gone, replaced by a dense, velvet-soft carpet of moss-cilia that moves in response to the pressure of the cicadas’ screams. When the millions of green-winged singers on the columns scream in unison, my head-moss bristles and shines with a faint, phosphor-yellow light.

I do not drown. The water in this floating pond is so thick with the breath of the submerged ribbon-weeds that stay coiled in its center that it is more like liquid lung than water. I breathe it down—it is warm, tasting of wet bark and honey—and my chest rises and falls with a heavy, hydraulic leisure. 

From inside my lens of water, the Cradle is a curved, beautiful monstrosity of jade and sound. 

***

The sound is how I work. 

Through the water, the Chasm’s deafening symphony does not strike the ear; it strikes the marrow. The sub-bass thrum of the giant vascular columns—the pump, pump, pump of millions of gallons of sap—feels like a second heart beating beside my own, slightly out of time. 

Because water is an exquisite conductor, I can feel every crack in the city before the Tenders do. 

When a suspension bridge of braided roots three layers down begins to fray under the damp weight of a passing herd of Glaze-backs, the high-tension snap of the fibers travels through the air, hits the skin of my water-orb, and becomes a sharp, shivering ripple that runs across my shoulders. 

I feel the *twang*. I feel the direction. 

I turn in my liquid sphere, my legs trailing behind me like pale, thin roots, and I strike the side of my orb. I do this with the hard, horn-like pads that have grown over my palms—a gift from the Chasm, similar to the tapping-soles of the Phloe. 

*Clack-clack. Hum.*

The vibration passes from my hands into the water, atomizes into a ring of fine mist on the orb's surface, and leaps across the central void to the fifth-tier column. There, a Phloe Tender, her golden lichen-fringe flared wide to receive the draft, catches the vibration against her chest-leaf. She does not see me; she doesn't need to. She knows the *Sieve* has felt a sickness in the third-tier bridge. 

Within minutes, I see the distant green-bronze needles of the Phloe sliding down the spiral ramps, accompanied by the flat, jade boats of the Glaze-backs, rushing to spit their mint-scented mastic into the wound.

***

In the quiet hours—though nothing here is ever truly quiet—I watch myself. 

Because space is dream-folded in the central void, my water-orb acts as a giant magnifying lens for the temporal slippages of the Cradle. 

If I look toward the eastern wall, through the curved green skin of my pool, I can see the third-tier spiral ramp that wraps around the main jade column. Often, there is a figure walking there. 

It is me. 

It is the me of three years ago, still wearing the shredded remnants of a blue wool coat, my face thin with the terror of a traveler who has lost the sky. That "me" is crying. He is looking down at the Pith-Floor, terrified of the way the moss is licking at his heels. He does not know yet that the thorn-wall has closed behind him, or that his lungs will soon learn to drink water without coughing.

Sometimes, I want to reach out of my bubble and drop a seed-pod to him, just to watch it fall through the ceiling and land at his feet, as the Chasm’s geometry demands. But I do not. The loop is gentle; it does not need my help to spiral.

And sometimes, if I look toward the high galleries of the sixth tier, where the canopy leaves are so thick they look like green stained glass, I see another figure. 

That one has no blue coat. Its legs have grown long and green-bronze, fused at the ankles into a single, elegant root-stem that grips the bark of the column like a vice. Its face is waxy and smooth, the eyes gone, replaced by the beautiful, striped rind of the Tenders. Its head is crowned with a halo of golden hair that drifts in the warm drafts of the chasm like silk thread.

That is the me that is coming. 

She—or he, or it, for gender is a dry-land word that has no meaning in the wet warm—is looking down at my water-globe. We do not wave. We do not have to. 

Between the terrified man in the blue coat and the silent guardian of the high canopy, I hang in my sapphire water-globe, suspended in the middle of time. I am the bridge that does not snap. I am the eye of the lung. 

An Orb-skinner, translucent and fragile as a blown-glass bubble, drifts through the outer edge of my water-globe. It lingers near my cheek, its tiny, thread-like stingers brushing against my neck-moss. I do not brush it away. It tastes of sweet starch, and its tiny, rhythmic contractions pulse against my skin, matching the deep, wet hum of the Chasm. 

I close my eyes—or the membranes that have grown to cover them—and let myself rotate. The water is warm. The city is screaming. The heart of the wood is beating, and inside my bones, I am beating with it.
