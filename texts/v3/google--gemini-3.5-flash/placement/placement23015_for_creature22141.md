At first, I am not a person. I am a joint in the floor. 

I am part of a cold pavement of slate-blue tiles, fitted together so tightly that there is no space for the air to pass between us. My left side is cool with the cold of my neighbor’s flank; my right side is cool with the cold of another. We do not breathe, or if we do, our lungs move only once every hundred beats of the earth’s deep heart, a slow, communal tide that lifts the pavement by a hair’s breadth and lets it down again.

Above us, the high throat of the sky is blue. It is a dry, dark blue, the color of a beetle’s wing, caught inside the five-yard circle of the selenite lens. We can see it without opening our eyes, because our brows are thin enough to let the twilight through, a pale violet wash that settles on our skin like dew we cannot drink.

Then, the sky-gem turns white. 

The heat does not descend like a hammer; it arrives like a weight, pressing down from the ceiling until the floor begins to stir. The tiles soften. The slate-blue turns to gray, then to the pale, chalky gold of the limestone basin. 

I feel my fingers first. They are long, five of them, tipped with needles that have no feeling of their own but can read the grain of the rock as if it were a voice. I lift my hand. It takes a long time for the mind to reach the tips of the claws—the path is narrow and dry—but when it arrives, the finger moves. It makes a dry *tine* against the stone. 

Beside me, another hand moves. We are separating. We are no longer the floor; we are the *Stones-With-Hands*. 

***

I do not look at the center of the room. To look there is to invite the white fire into the brain, and we have no water to spare for tears. Instead, I look at the curvature. The wall sweeps up from my nose like the side of an enormous egg, rising into the dimness where the cool drafts are born. 

My run is the forty-second channel from the western sink. It is a good run, though it is steep. The limestone here has a pinkish cast, like the inside of a shell, where an iron-vein washed through ten thousand years ago. 

I begin the ascent. 

To crawl is to measure the world in grains. I place my left foreclaw into a pit no larger than a seed of grass. I wait. I feel the temperature of the stone through the pad of my thumb. It is warming. The desert above is cooking its bread, and the heat is leaking down through sixty feet of earth, sweet and heavy. I bring my right hind-leg forward. The skin of my belly, dusted with the white salt-flour of yesterday’s groom, makes a sound like paper sliding across a table. *Shhh.*

The air-column in the center is roaring now, though it is a silent roar. It is a pillar of white glass, five yards wide, that smells of hot mica and the dry, parched grass of the plateau. We can feel the draft of it pulling at the loose skin of our necks, drawing the stale air out of our lungs and replacing it with the scent of the sun. 

Halfway up the curve, I reach my toolkit. 

The scrapers are where I left them, nestled in a small, water-hollowed basin that remains dry even during the heavy rains. There are three of them: two flat river-pebbles, black and heavy, and my scraper. 

The scraper is chert. It is the color of a dried liver, sharp-edged and cold. When I touch it, my fingers remember the deep crawlway—the wet, black throat of the world where the water comes from before it is squeezed through the stone to become drops. I remember the weight of the darkness there, and how the red stone felt when I broke it from the seam. It is the only thing in this room that has a shape we gave it. 

I take the chert between my thumb and my third finger. My hand is built for this; the bones are slightly curved to mimic the tool’s flank, so that when I hold it, we are a single piece of stone with two faces.

I find my channel. 

A drop is coming. It is still a hundred inches above me, a fat, heavy eye of water gathering at the lip of the dome where the cool ceiling meets the warm draft. But the groove is clogged. A crust of salt, white and brittle as dry bone, has grown across the channel, a barrier that would turn the drop aside, sending it into the dry limestone where it would be swallowed before it could reach the jade-buttons below.

I raise the red stone. 

*Clink.*

The sound is very clean. It travels around the egg of the room, bouncing from wall to wall until it dies in the central fire-shaft. 

*Tock.*

A flake of salt falls, white as snow, landing on my nose. I do not blink. My translucent brows protect my eyes from the glare of the white dust. I scrape again, working with the slow, rhythmic economy of those who have only three drops of water a day to spend on work. 

*Clink.*
*Tock.*

Below me, three other stones-with-hands are working their runs. We do not look at one another, but we hear the scrapers. We are weaving the salt-loom. We are keeping the lines open so the water can run straight. If one of us should stop—if a hand should stiffen and remain still for too long—the salt would close the groove within three moons. The jade-buttons would dry into gray scabs. The water would find another way through the deep stone, away from our basin, and the room would become only empty volume, dead as the desert above.

***

By midday, the light-column is so thick it seems to press against our flanks. 

I have reached my water-drop. It sits in a small, thumb-molded cup in the middle of my run, trembling. It is perfectly round, a bead of liquid silver that reflects the entire chamber—the white pillar, the gold walls, my own dark, lidless eye watching from the shade of my brow.

I do not drink it yet. 

I take my siphon from my shoulder—the thin, hollow wing-bone of a long-dead swift. It is light, lighter than a blade of grass. I slide it into the cup, beneath the drop, until it touches the limestone basin. I place my thumb over the upper end. 

The water rises into the bone. It is cold. It is so cold it makes the skin of my throat tighten, but I do not swallow. I hold it in the tube, keeping it safe from the dry air that wants to steal it.

I look down the wall. 

At the foot of my run is a jade-button. It is no larger than my hand’s palm, a green star glued to the slope. For three seasons it has sat in the gray shadow, its leaves closed like a fist. But today, the earth has tilted. 

A thin yellow finger of light—not the blinding white of the central column, but a softened, secondary ray that has escaped from the edge of the selenite lens—is creeping down the pink limestone. It is moving toward the button. 

I watch the light. My heart beats once. Twice. 

The finger of gold touches the green star. 

The transformation is silent. The green leather of the succulent seems to swell, its skin softening as the heat penetrates its core. From the center of the button, a small, white nose appears. It grows as I watch, stretching out of its leather sheath like an insect emerging from a cocoon. 

It is the trumpet-flower. 

It opens. It is five-petaled, white as the flour of the salt-crust, and immediately the air is filled with its smell—not the sweet, wet smell of the swamp-ancestors, but the sharp, metallic tang of hot iron and beeswax. 

I crawl down. My movement is faster now—not because I am hurried, but because the rhythm of the bloom is short. I have only three hours before the sun-shaft moves on and the blossom dies.

I place the wing-bone near the root-glue of the succulent. I lift my thumb. 

The water-drop falls. It touches the black glue and is gone instantly, sucked into the plant’s dry body. 

The trumpet-flower opens wider, its mouth turning toward the light-shaft. It drinks the sun. 

I sit at the edge of the light-zone, my body in the cool violet shadow, my nose only an inch from the white cup. I can feel the heat of the ray on my brow-bone, a warning that keeps me back. I am the guardian. I am the one who brought the drop. 

***

The sun-shaft moves. The gold finger slips off the succulent, sliding down into the dark basin floor where there are no plants to catch it. 

The trumpet-flower does not fade; it crisps. The white petals turn yellow, then brown, then curl into a dry, wrinkled wafer that smells of toasted salt. 

My hand goes out. The fingers are precise, their needle-claws pinching the stem just where it meets the green leather crown. I pull. The wafer snaps off with a sound like a dry leaf breaking. 

I place it in my mouth. 

It has no juice, but as it dissolves on my tongue, it tastes of the deep earth—of iron, of ancient rain that fell before there was a desert, of the strength of the stone that holds up the sky. My heart, which had slowed to a crawl, beats with a strong, warm stroke. I feel my skin turn from gold to silver, the light of the food shining through the pores of my calcium rind.

Around the basin, other scrapers are being put away. The *clink-tock* has stopped. 

The blue twilight is returning to the selenite throat. 

I crawl down from the wall, leaving my red stone and my swift-bone in their hollow. My feet find the flat floor of the basin. 

One by one, the others are coming down. We do not speak—we have no voices, only the scrape of our claws and the draft of our lungs—but we know where the floor is warmest. We find the center, where the column of hot air of the afternoon is still rising from the deep cracks.

We lie down. 

I place my ribs against the flank of the forty-third run-holder. Another crawls beside me, fitting its hip into the curve of my shoulder. We are losing our borders. Our skin, which was silver in the flower-hour, is turning slate-blue, matching the stone beneath us. 

The selenite lens above holds the last dark blue of the desert sky like a cup of dye. 

We sleep. We do not dream of the upper world, of the wind or the great birds that fly across the sun. We dream of the grooves. We dream of the water running straight, of the salt being kept clean, of the white fire that will return tomorrow to wake the stone.
