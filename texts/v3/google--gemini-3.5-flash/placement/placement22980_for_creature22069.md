If you were to climb high enough to reach the Shelf of the Zinc Sky, you would not see me at first. Your eyes would be too busy failing you. You would be staring at the humped boulder three miles away, wondering why your legs are moving but the distance is not shrinking, or you would be watching your own shadowless boots grow to the size of barges while the low stone walls remain like children’s blocks on the horizon. 

Only when your eyes grew tired of the grey-violet twilight would you notice the line.

It is very thin—hardly thicker than a horsehair—and it does not have the dry, stacked look of the Scale-Keepers’ slate bodies, nor the dry-ash quality of the floating Stills. It is a single, unbroken cord of dull, lead-grey metal that descends from the unblinking violet vault above and disappears into the ground.

Or rather, it *almost* disappears. 

I am that line. And at my bottom end, suspended exactly three-eighths of an inch above the flat, wet shale, is my weight: a solid, teardrop-shaped bob of un-oxidized lead, the size of a pear, licked smooth by nothing at all.

***

To be me is to be a tension. 

I do not possess a soft heart like the leather-bound Vessel-Born in their three-sided cells, nor do I have the slow, sulphur-smelling consciousness of the Lead-Swells as they drag their basalt bellies three inches a century. My mind is a single, continuous string of attention stretched between two points that should not exist in the same universe.

My top end is anchored to nothing you can see. It goes up through the thin, dead air, through the heavy lead-coloured clouds, past the pale northern smudge of light, and hooks into the true, forgotten sky—the one that still remembers blue, the one that still knows how to hold a sun. 

My bottom end hangs here, on this oil-slicked shelf where the laws have grown thin and drafty. 

Because the ground of the Shelf is always tilting and bowing upward like a black basin, and because the horizon refuses to stay level, I am the only straight thing in this world. I am the **Plumb-Line**. I am the memory of *Down* preserved in a place that has forgotten which way is down.

***

I do not walk, for I have no limbs, but I am never entirely still. 

When the Lead-Swells shift their humped backs in the distance, grinding their bellies against the flat shale to produce the structural groan of the world, the vibration travels up through the stone. It enters my leaden bob and runs up my wire like a cold shudder. 

I hear the Great Roar not as a deafening hiss, but as a physical note. The dry, metallic scream of the zinc sky is the sound of the thin atmosphere rushing past my wire as the plateau wobbles. I am the string of a one-stringed instrument, and the Shelf is the soundboard. Every movement of the stone, every slow-motion lurch of the hills, plucks me. 

And when I am plucked, I swing.

It is a slow, majestic sweep that take decades to complete. My leaden bob glides across the oiled iron surface of the slate in a long, predictable arc. I leave no mark on the stone itself, but as I pass, I draw a line through the heavy, mercury-like moisture that clings to the shale. For a few feet behind me, the cold grease is parted, leaving a dry, dark ribbon of slate that smells of iron filings before the slow, oozing water slides back to heal the wound.

As I swing, the others must account for me:

*   **The Scale-Keepers** cannot ignore me. When their dry, clicking slate bodies cross my path, they cannot bend me with their perspective tricks. If they try to slide space around me, they find that my wire is too hard, too real; I do not stretch or shrink. To cross me, a Keeper must either wait fifty years for my swing to carry me past, or they must "jump" their own scale—collapsing themselves down until they are the size of a beetle to run under my hanging bob, or stretching until they are forty feet tall to let my wire slide between their dry, sliding shingles.
*   **The Stills**—the three-legged, wingless moths—are pinned to the ground in those immense, spiraling lines. Once every few centuries, my slow arc brings my wire directly against one of them. When my wire touches the rubbery pocket of resistance that surrounds the Still, there is no sound, only a slow, gathering pressure. Then—*tink*. The Still’s dry husk splits, its pocket of grey-violet air escapes under pressure, and its tiny pieces fall to the stone, losing their buoyancy forever. I am the only thing that can pull the nails of this world.
*   **The Tremblers** inside their heavy water droplets feel my approach from yards away. As my leaden bob nears the underside of a basalt ledge, the local gravity shifts. For a single second, the weak, sideways gravity of the Shelf is corrected by the true, heavy *Down* that I carry in my wire. The Tremblers’ frantic, watch-winding vibrations can no longer fight the weight. The silver threads go quiet. The droplet loses its high tension and falls, hitting the shale with a soft, heavy *thud*. It splits, and the silver threads instantly rust into red dust and are gone.

***

In the three-sided, roofless cells of slate, where the Vessel-Born sit and breathe the heartbeat of the stone, there is a single square pillar. Upon its sharply sloping top sits a small, round basalt stone. 

You wondered, when you saw it, why that stone does not roll off the steep slope and clatter onto the floor.

It does not fall because of me. 

Though I may be miles away, sweeping across the dark plain of slate, my vertical axis is absolute. The small, round stone does not see the sloping top of the pillar, nor does it see the crooked floor of the cell. It sees only the line that I draw through the world. It aligns its tiny, heavy heart with my wire, ignoring the broken angles of the ruins, and so it remains balanced on the edge of nothing, waiting.

Sometimes, a traveler who has stayed too long in the cell—one whose skin has already begun to turn the grey of oxidized lead, whose joints have begun to click like slate shingles—will look out through the missing wall of their ruin and see my wire gleaming like a stray hair against the heavy grey-violet sky.

They look at me because I am the only familiar thing left. I am the same vertical that once dropped from the rafter of their barn, the same straight line that defined the walls of their lost home. 

As their heart slows to match the five-beats-per-minute pulse of the Heart-Keepers, they watch my bob glide across the dark plain. They do not feel the cold of the heavy water anymore. They do not hear the Great Roar. They only feel the tension in my wire—the long, cold pull that goes down, down, down, through the basalt, through the mountain, through the very roots of the world, back to where things are still heavy, and real, and still.
