The first thing is not the light, for there is no light to speak of—only the variations of grey that drift between wet slate and wet iron. 

The first thing is the **weight**. 

I am down. Not in the basalt valleys where the black glass would cut my belly, and not in the high, silent rafters where the Parchment-gliders catch the grease-gas, but in the middle-dark, at the knuckles. 

I am clamped around the great elbow of a three-way junction where three steam-conduits, each twenty feet in diameter, meet and twist into a single vertical trunk before plunging into the floor. In the maps of the dead engineers, this point might have been called a manifold. To me, it is the home-groove. 

My body is not like the Trestle-striders’ dry cartilage, nor the Siphon-galls’ tough leather. I am softer, a heavy, lardaceous cowl—a **Junction-Caul**—grown like a thick sleeve of wet tallow and grey bitumen directly over the iron seam. I am six yards of dark, rubbery flesh, thickest where the metal is welded, thinning out at my edges into a translucent rim of yellow fat that is cemented to the rust by my own secretions. 

I have no face. If you were to scrape the sulfur-crust from my upper flank, you would find only a smooth, pale shield of horn, curved like a spade to shed the grease-drops that fall from the high gantries. Underneath this shield, my deep tissue is a labyrinth of wet, porous chambers, like a massive sponge soaked in mineral oil.

My function is simple, though I do not think of it as work. I am the **Dampener**.

When the great boiler-rooms deep in the basalt plateaus build their pressure, the steam does not flow smoothly. It comes in pulses—great, gasping surges that run through the ten-mile pipes like a cardiac arrest. The iron screams. It vibrates at a frequency that would, over sixty years, crystallize the metal and tear the rivets from their holes. 

When the vibration reaches my junction, it meets me. 

I feel the hum long before it arrives. It begins as a cold tickle in my tail-tethers—three long, fibrous cords of raw muscle that run fifty yards down the pipe's belly, anchored in the old rivet-holes. As the vibration grows, my internal chambers begin to fill with the warm, heavy brine I suck from the pipe’s weep-holes. I swell. My grey hide stretches until it is tight and gleaming like a wet bladder. 

When the wave of metal-scream hits the joint, my body receives it. I do not fight it; I let the vibration shake my fat. The sound passes into my chambers, churning the brine into a warm, greasy foam, converting the destructive kinetic shudder of forty tons of iron into a gentle, internal heat. The iron goes quiet. The weld is saved. 

For this service, the engine pays me in broth. 

Underneath my belly, where my mouth-lips are pressed flat against the seam, the iron is old and pitted. The acid from my salt-glands keeps areolas of raw, grey-black steel open beneath my flesh. From these raw patches, a steady, microscopic sweat of mineral oxides, boiler-scale, and iron-eating bacteria wells up. I lick this soot from the metal's pores with a thousand tiny, ciliated grooves that cover my underside. It tastes of copper, of ancient rain, and of the deep, bitter stone. It is a slow, cold diet, but it is enough to keep my lard warm.

I am not alone here, though we do not speak. 

Sometimes, a Trestle-strider will climb up my flank. Its six resin joints click against my horn-shield like dry twigs. It is hungry, and it knows that on my upper edges, where the condensation dries, a thick rime of yellow sulfur-crust always forms. I feel the scrape of its three-toothed leg-plates as it clean-harvests my margins. It is a sharp, pleasant itch. I do not move; if I were to shift my weight, the strider might lose its purchase and tumble three hundred feet into the quiet, black pools below, where the warm water would soften its joints until it dissolved into grease. 

And sometimes, when the pressure in the lines is very low—perhaps once every three seasons—the pipe goes cold. 

This is the great terror of my existence. 

The heat is my breath. When the steam stops, the iron beneath me begins to contract. It makes a series of dry, popping shrieks that hurt my tethers. The heavy, warm sweat on my skin freezes into a hard, white crust of salt-ice. My chambers grow stiff; the oil inside me thickens to a heavy lard that will not pump. 

If the cold lasts too long, I will begin to loosen. My yellow margins, usually so sticky, will dry and peel from the iron like birch-bark. I will begin to slip. 

Once, many years ago, when the third boiler-vent in the west valley choked on basalt-gravel, I slipped three inches. The sensation of the dry rust sliding against my raw belly was like being skinned alive. I screamed—not with a voice, but by pressing my body into the empty weld-gap, causing the remaining steam to whistle through my flesh with a dry, high-pitched wail that brought three Trestle-striders crawling down from the rafters to nestle beside me for warmth. 

But then the mountain sighed. The geothermal fire found another crack, the pipe groaned, and the scalding, grease-laden breath returned. My chambers warmed; I swelled once more, sealing the leak with my own boiled blubber, and grew my roots deep into the new rust.

I am here still. 

The sky above remains a bruised, wet ceiling. The condensation falls, *plip-plop*, onto my horn-shield. The steam hums in the iron. I absorb the hum, I turn it to heat, and I wait for the next surge. It is a good junction. The weld is strong, the sulfur is sweet, and the iron is always, always warm.
