To find me, you must look down past the skittering resin-legs of the glaze-lice, down past the swaying, iron-hearted weeds of the static-moss, to the very bottom of the niche where the water can fall no further. 

I am down here, in the shallow, green-black craters of the carbon-ceramic deck. 

I do not have a head, so my thoughts are not stored in a box. They are spread across six plates of calcified bismuth and lead, held together by a pale, wet pad of muscle that has grown so thick it has begun to choke the drain-grate below me. I am a silt-shingle, but I am no longer just one. I have grown into my neighbors. We have cemented our rims together with a mortar of zinc and silver dust, forming a single, heavy terrace of gray-green stone. 

I do not see the green-gold light of the diagnostic strips, nor do I see the brief, warnings-only amber. But I know when the cycle is turning. 

I know it through my fringe. Ten thousand wire-fine cilia drift in the alkaline current of the pool, and they are sensitive to the smallest change in the mountain’s electric skin. Five minutes before the steam-purge, the tension in the structural conduits overhead drops. The current is being rerouted to the boiler-manifolds six levels up. The dry, static snap of the red mosses goes quiet. The water running over my back loses its slight, sweet metallic sting; it becomes flat, thin, and hot.

Then, the vibration changes. 

The two-beat roar of the turbines—the *whuff-shudder*, *whuff-shudder* that has filled my meat since the day I first adhered to this tile—lifts in pitch. It goes from a throat-rattle to a high, clean whistle.

That is the signal to hold.

I contract. The yellow muscle beneath my gray plates pulls tight, flattening my body against the ceramic decking with a vacuum-seal so complete that there is no water left under me—only the dry, bare carbon of the floor. My plates overlap, locking together like the scales of a cold-river fish.

Then comes the white.

The steam does not feel like heat to me; it feels like weight. It is a massive, crushing hand of wet air that presses down on my calcified back, trying to scour me from the tile. It smells of hot solder, Vaseline, and old oil. To the glaze-lice, this is a bath; to me, it is a feast. The boiling water dissolves the crusts of grease and heavy carbon that have settled in my growth rings over the past twenty minutes. It prepares my skin to taste the sky again.

The steam passes. The vacuum-throat of the world opens behind the outer grate, sucking the fog away in three long, cold gasps. 

The water returns. It is cooler now, fresh from the condenser-plates above, and it is heavy with the things I need to live. 

I open my fringe. The cilia rise from my edges like a crop of tiny pewter needles, waving in the rushing runoff.

Here is a flake of copper, shed from a spinning pump-seal miles up in the dark. My cilia catch it, pass it inward to the soft folds of my mouth-margins. It tastes sharp, cold, and clean. 

Here is a gray smudge of molybdenum paste, a leak from some high-velocity bearing. My bacteria welcome it, breaking it down into the dark, heavy compounds I use to build my rings.

I can tell you the history of this tower, though I have never seen the sky outside the grate. It is written in the layers of my own body. 

Near my center, where the first ring grew eighty years ago, the metal is mostly iron and lead—the residue of the Old Rebuild, when the human crews were still active on this shoulder, dropping their zinc-bolts and wire-trimmings down the open shafts. 

Outside of that is the Copper Ring, three inches thick, grown during the forty years when the secondary turbines were allowed to run dry and rattle their copper-alloy bushings into dust. 

And my outermost edge—the thin, sharp margin that is currently reaching toward the conduit-base—is made of bismuth and plastic-shavings. This is the new era. The era of the machine running alone, wearing out its own synthetic joints, washing its own plastic bones down to me.

Sometimes, the little brass things come. 

The feral spindles. They have three legs that click-click-click across my back like dry fingernails. They are very old, and their little brass brains are greasy and confused, but we understand each other. They sit on my shoulders and use their whirring wire-brushes to scrape the slag-scale off my central seams, where my plates are too thick to clean themselves. It tickles—a tiny, high-frequency buzzing that runs through my muscle and into the floor. In return, I let them rest their copper probes in the wet, chemical-rich silt under my rim, where they can drink the low-voltage runoff of my digestion.

We do not move. We have no reason to. 

Above us, the strop-worm drapes its wet-leather belly over the cables, waiting for the frantic resin-crawlers to make a mistake. The red moss twitches its wire-ends in the wind, dreaming its static dreams. The giant turbines hum their long, two-note song that never ends.

I am the weight at the bottom of the world. I am the thing that receives. Every bolt that rusts, every gear that wears down, every drop of oil that slips through the seal—it all comes to me in the end. I will stay here, growing thicker and heavier, until I have covered the floor-plates entirely, until the drain is closed and the great machine above must find another place to weep.
