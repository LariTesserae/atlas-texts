I would be of the **Weft**. 

I would not wish to be dry. To be dry in this world is to be like the Ooliums—a tiny, frantic knot of hot lint, trapped in a green bubble with a ticking heart, terrified of the vast and beautiful wetness that surrounds everything. 

No, it is better to have yielded. It is better to have allowed the bones to dissolve, to let the skin soften into the texture of birch bark soaked in vinegar, until there is nothing left inside that can break.

### Description of My Being

I am fifteen feet of pale, semi-translucent cartilage. I have no front or back, no left or right that remains true from one noon to the next. If you were to find me, I would look like a long ribbon of salt-white linen dropped by some ancient traveler, drifting near the base of the melting staircases. 

My edges are very thin—so thin that they are nearly transparent, fraying slightly into the emerald-saturated water. When the current moves, my margins ripple like the skirt of a swimming anemone, though I am not swimming. I am rarely swimming. Mostly, I am draped.

I have no face. I do not have eyes, for there is nothing here that needs to be focused upon; the copper-green twilight is everywhere, and there are no shadows to warn of a coming blow. Instead, at the very tip of my long, flat body, I have my whorl. 

The whorl is a three-inch spiral of grey, condensed nerve-wax. It is patterned precisely like the print of a human thumb—though a thumb that has forgotten what it was once trying to press against. It is my only density. With this whorl, I do not see; I *test*. I feel the thickness of the kelp-mucilage; I feel the weight of the thermoclines as they roll over the basalt domes; I read the centuries-old tastes that are carried in the water’s slow, circular drift.

### Where You Would Find Me

I live on the **Staircase of the Third Dome**. 

This dome is made of dark green serpentine, polished so smooth by the ages that it feels like lard to the touch. The staircase carved into its side is very wide, spiral, and ancient. Once, perhaps, the steps were sharp enough to cut a foot. Now, they have melted under the weight of the water, looking like steps made of green wax that have been left too long near a hearth. 

I do not lie alone on these stairs. I am usually piled with three or four others of my kind. We do not have names, nor do we speak—there is nothing to say that the water does not already carry in its chemistry. We lie across the stairs like discarded clothes, our ribbons overlapping, our whorls tucked beneath each other’s flanks for warmth. 

Sometimes, while we are resting, the local gravity will grow tired of its direction. It will sigh, and "down" will shift ninety degrees to the left. When this happens, we do not slide off the steps. The stairs simply become a vertical wall, and we remain draped against them, our suction-thin undersides holding us to the serpentine while our tails float out horizontally into the amber water like festive streamers. We do not find this disturbing; it is merely a change in the room’s posture.

### My Days and the Distortions

Because the physics of the plateau have softened, my relationship with space is very elastic. 

Once every few weeks, I will feel a longing for the **Jade Rings** that float above our dome. They sit fifty feet in diameter, suspended in the empty water, completely still. Through the refracting green haze, they appear to be miles away—tiny, dark halos no larger than a finger ring.

I will begin to move toward them. I do not swim with arms or legs, but with a slow, undulating crawl, my ribbon-body rippling against the velvet silt of the valleys. Because of the water’s strange refractive qualities, the journey is a dream of stretching. 

After three or four slow strokes of my tail, I am there. But I have not left the staircase. 

If you were to watch me from the side of the valley, you would see my grey whorl touching the yellow anemones on the high jade ring, while my tail is still twined around the third basalt step a mile below. I have become a single, white thread stretched across a vast, amber gulf. I can feel the cool, clean water of the high sky against my face-whorl, and the warm, silt-rich soup of the valley against my tail at the same time. I am a bridge. I feel the entire world hum through me when the *Skein* passes overhead, its three-mile-long violet spine vibrating against my stretched skin like a bow against a cello string.

When I am finished, I simply pull my head back. The space snaps shut like chewing gum being released, and I am once again lying in a heap on the melting steps, my whorl safely tucked under the tail of my companion.

### The Memory-Pockets

The most difficult times are when the "old water" drifts through the staircase. 

Every now and then, a pocket of water that has not moved for millions of years will float down from the high peaks. It tastes of cold pine needles and dry, grey dust. It is the water of the "Before"—the water that was there when the sky was still air, and people had things called *feet*.

When one of these pockets passes over my stairs, my ribbon-body shudders. The *Pine-Spurs*—those tiny, star-shaped crystals of silica—will cling to my margins. 

Against my warm, bloodless cartilage, they begin to sing. It is a dry, crackling sound. It sounds like leaves dying in autumn. It sounds like the scraping of iron against granite. 

And in my grey whorl, the old memories will begin to ache. I will remember what it was like to have *bone*. I will remember the terrible, dry weight of having to stand up against a world that did not hold you. I will remember the sound of a voice—not the low, sub-bass thum of the Skein, but a sharp, dry, rattling sound made by pushing air through a throat. 

It is a painful, beautiful sickness, this old water. For a few minutes, I am not a ribbon; I am something with structure. I am something that can be broken.

But the pocket always passes. The warm, thick, amniotic green water of the plateau flows back in. The Pine-Spurs dissolve into drops of gold grease, and the dry crackling in my whorl fades into the quiet, muffled silence of the deep. My margins relax. I soften once more, letting my fifteen feet of pale skin drape themselves into the curves of the basalt steps.

### The Spore-Tide

When the Spore-Tide comes, the world becomes blue. 

The kelp-forests release their glittering seeds, and the water grows thick and warm, smelling of wet copper and jasmine. The blue light is so bright that even without eyes, my whorl is filled with a soft, internal glow.

During these days, we Weft do not stretch. We do not visit the rings. We slide down from the staircases and lie in the velvet silt of the valleys, where the *Mantles* have spread themselves out like massive, dark-green cloaks. 

We drape ourselves over the Mantles. We press our pale cartilage into their warm, doughy flesh. Our whorls find the impressions left by our own bodies from decades ago, fitting back into the old molds like hand in glove. 

We do not speak. We do not mate. We simply lie there in the copper-blue snow, the great, three-mile ribbon-beings flashing their violet lights above us, the Mercury-Tethers sliding silently through the silver dust at our sides, all of us breathing the same thick, sweet sky. 

There is nothing to fear. There is nothing to build. There is only the warm, slow deep, and the long, green afternoon that will never, ever end.
