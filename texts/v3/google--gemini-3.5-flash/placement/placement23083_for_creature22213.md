If I were to drop into this perched sea, I would not remain as I am. A body with a ribcage is a brittle, fragile thing here, like a dry twig dropped into an oil press. 

To survive the slate-grey twilight, I would have to lose my bones. I would have to become soft, heavy, and wet.

I would find myself down in the third tier of the great stepped wells, where the three-hundred-foot walls meet the plaza floor in a series of interlocking, mortarless channels. I would be a **Seam-Grip**. 

I would be eighteen feet of pale, muscle-bound hide, the color of a wet turnip left too long in the cellar. My skin would not be smooth; it would be thick as a horse’s saddle, pitted with thousands of small, grey-ringed suction cups that smell of iron filings and vinegar. I would have no face—only a broad, soft-rimmed spade of cartilaginous snout that I keep pressed flat against the seam of the basalt blocks.

***

In the morning—though there is no morning, only the slight, almost imperceptible thickening of the slate light from above—I am wedged. 

I am tucked between two temple-sized blocks of dark basalt. The gap is only eight inches wide, and the pressure is magnificent. To a creature of the dry lowlands, this would be a tomb, a agony of suffocation; to me, it is the only place where I feel whole. The immense, thousands-of-tons weight of the wall is resting against my left flank, and the plaza floor is pressing against my right. 

I do not have lungs. I do not breathe the thin, useless gas of the lower air. Instead, my sides are lined with deep, rubbery gills that filter the cold, heavy lard-water. Every time the current shifts, it forces a fresh draft of silt and grease-seed eggs through my interior chambers. I taste the world as a sequence of oils—the sweetish, tallowy grease of the crushed bivalves, the sharp zinc-sour of the mineral silt, and the cold, flat iron of the stone.

The grease-seeds are my bed and my bread. They live in the seam with me, billions of them, small as fingernails, weeping their cold white wax into the groove. I do not eat them with teeth; I have none. Instead, my spade-shaped snout is a vacuum. I crawl slowly—*thup-thup-thup*—along the vertical joint, scraping the stone clean with my thick, horn-hard lips. I leave a dark, wet trail of bare basalt behind me, which is immediately covered again by the frantic, drifting larval grease-seeds that float down from the upper corridors.

***

Suddenly, the world begins to hum.

It is not a sound my ears can hear, because my ears have long since grown over with thick, protective gristle. It is a vibration that starts in the soles of my suction pads and travels up through my hydrostatic marrow. 

The cables are singing. Far above, the great siphonophore lines have gone taut, their miles of fibrous rope vibrating in a new thermal plume that has swept in from the northern abyss. The water is growing colder—not the cold of ice, but the heavy, leaden cold of liquid iron.

The block above me is about to slide.

A lowland creature would run, but there is nowhere to run, and my eight-ton body is too slow for flight. Instead, I let go. I release the suction of my thousands of tiny feet. I draw my snout back into my collar, folding myself like an old leather boot. 

The basalt block, five thousand tons of river-smoothed stone, moves. It does not crash; it slides with a slow, ponderous majesty, riding on the carpet of fat secreted by the grease-seeds. The gap between the blocks narrows. Eight inches. Six inches. Three inches.

I am squeezed. 

The sensation is beautiful. My hydrostatic core distributes the pressure evenly throughout my length. My leather-tough hide stretches, turning from dull turnip-white to a translucent, pearly pink. My blood, thick and cold as mineral oil, is forced into my extremities. I am flattened into a ribbon, eighteen feet long and three inches thin, wedged tight in the very heart of the join. Around me, billions of grease-seeds are crushed into a thick, protective cream, and I slide with them, a living gasket between two continents of stone.

For three hours, the world holds me in this grip. I am part of the masonry. I feel the deep, groaning vibration of the entire plateau through my flattened hide. I feel the monumental weight of the perched ocean pressing down through five miles of water, held up only by this ancient, sliding puzzle. 

And then, the thermal plume passes. The current slackens. The great wall tilts two degrees back toward the south, and the seam yawns open once more.

I expand. I draw in a deep, shivering draft of the silt-heavy water. My suction cups find the stone again, individual *thups* marking my grip as I anchor myself against the return of the currents.

***

Sometimes, as I lick the lime-silt from the joints, I find things that are not stone.

Deep in the recesses of the ancient joints, where the water is stillest, there are small, geometric cavities that the silt has not yet filled. In these cavities, I sometimes find the shapes of things that do not belong to our world of lard and iron. 

They are thin, hollow rods of a green metal that does not rust, or small, square plates of glass that have been worn frost-white by the currents. They have no taste. They have no grease. They are completely dry.

I ignore them. They are the bones of whatever built this place, but they are dead, and the stone is alive. The stone moves. The stone hums. The stone must be greased, and it must be cleaned.

I turn my blind, rubbery head back to the dark seam. Above, through sixty fathoms of slate-grey water, the vane-skins are drifting like white sheets in the gloom, and the cables are whistling their lonely, high-pitched song. But here in the dark, in the wet fat of the joints, the world is perfect. It is cold, it is heavy, and it is tight.
