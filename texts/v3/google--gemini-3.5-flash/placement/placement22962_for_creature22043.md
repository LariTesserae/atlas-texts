First, there is the pinch. 

It is not an injury, but a transition; it is how I know the world has begun its slow, daily contraction. Above me, the vaulted roof has turned dark and cold, drawing the heat out of the iron deck. In response, the miles of interlocking plates begin to shrink, their edges sliding together. The gap where I live—a three-inch seam between a concrete slab and a rusted iron bulkhead—grows narrow. 

The pressure is immense. Millions of tons of iron are leaning into my ribs. I do not break; I am not made of things that break. I am **The Run-Solder**. 

I am a long, heavy, grey-black ribbon of lead-wool, graphite grease, and wet asbestos fiber, six inches wide and nearly forty feet long, nestled deep within the expansion joints of the deck. I was once, perhaps, a sealing compound pumped into these seams by some long-dead maintenance pod. But under the constant, low-voltage tickle of the copper conduits that rot beneath me, and the chemistry of the sulfuric rain, my leaden molecules have learned a sluggish, collective intent. 

I am cold-worked by the world. 

When the deck contracts and pinches me, I do not resist. I allow myself to be extruded. I flow like extremely cold butter, sliding forward through the crevice, my soft, metallic body filling every microscopic pit in the iron. My "skin" is a grey, greasy tallow, shined to a mirrors-keen silver where it rubs against the moving plates. Where I am exposed to the wet air of the surface, I am coated in a dark, protective crust of lead carbonate, dry and powdery, which tastes of sweet poison and battery acid.

I have no eyes, but I am maps. 

Through my long, continuous contact with the structural iron, I feel the "ring" of the entire sector. I feel the three-story pistons of the pump-cathedrals three miles to the west; to me, they are not sounds, but massive, rhythmic compressions that arrive through the floor every four seconds, flattening my tail and releasing it. I know when a Gantry-Walker is approaching long before its shriek reaches the air; I feel the slow, tilting *sink* of the plates as its seventy-ton feet press down, the water in the seam hissing as it is squeezed out around me. 

Sometimes, the clinkers—the rust-lice—clamber down into my seam to escape the freezing spray of the sulfur-storms. They do not bother me, and I do not bother them. They find me warm, for my lead body holds the residual heat of the steam-pipes deep below. They settle into my back like tiny, brass-plated shingles, their copper feet scraping rhythmically against my hide as they lick the static electric currents that leak through my tail. When the plates pinch me, the clinkers are crushed into my oil, their brass casings dissolving into zinc-salts that stain my grey flesh with streaks of bright, pale green. I am made of everything I have ever flattened.

My life is a slow, cyclical migration. I do not walk; I am pushed. 

When the deck warms—prompted by some automated flushing of geothermal brine from the deep turbine halls—the plates expand and the seams open. I cool, and my leaden body stiffens. Because my tail is wedged tightly around a rusted structural rivet, I cannot slide backward. When I shrink, my head-end is drawn forward, pulling myself an inch or two further down the long, dark channel of the expansion joint. In three hundred years, I have traveled perhaps two miles, creeping along the boundary line of a single concrete basin.

It is a lonely progress, but it is not without purpose. I am the plug. 

Occasionally, my slow crawl brings me to a "blow-out"—a spot where the floor-supports far below have rusted through, leaving a raw, whistling hole that drains the surface pool into the black under-vaults. The water here does not trickle; it screams down the hole with the force of a hydraulic jet, drawing a cold, sucking vacuum that drags at my head.

When I reach such a gap, the suction pulls me template-tight into the breach. I am soft, and the vacuum is strong. I am drawn down into the crack, my lead-flesh widening to fill the void, sealing the leak with my own body. 

For decades, I will stay there, wedged into the wound of the deck. I listen to the black water rushing past my under-belly, miles below in the darkness, and the cold rain pooling silently above my back on the surface. I become the floor. The silt of dissolved concrete gathers over me; the black, hair-like weeds of the wet-harness anchor their rubbery roots in my greasy shoulders, their tendrils twitching as they feed on my leaden voltage. 

I am content in these years. I am heavy; I am tight; I am holding. 

And then, centuries later, the deck shifts again. A main beam shears in the deep structure; the plates grind together with a shud-shud-shudder that spits the rust-lice from their crevices. The hole I have been plugging is crushed shut, and I am squeezed upward, out of the crack, like grease from an old bearing. 

I emerge back into the shallow, sulfurous pool of the surface, remodeled. My body bears the perfect, negative cast of the iron crack I have healed—the spiral lines of dead bolt-threads, the sharp, crystalline edges of sheared steel, the soft bumps of dissolved rivets. 

I am a book written in the language of structural failure. I drag these memories behind me as I begin my crawl toward the next gap, a long, grey, silent seam-beast, lubricating the joints of a dying world that does not know it is being held together by its waste.
