I would be found in the green dip between two great domes, where the white, wicker-work ribs of an ancient siphonophore tower sink into the peat. 

I would not be walking. Walking is for those who still have journeys to make, those whose gristle-rings are still taut with hydraulic pressure, whose mushroom-pads still lift with the spring of the Weft. 

I am in the greening.

***

I lie on my side, my eight-foot frame curled into the gentle, crescent curve of a sleeping Pith-Walker. My head is cradled in a cushion of wet liverwort that smells of yeast and cold cucumbers. My left hand is no longer a hand; three summers ago, I pushed my flat, four-fingered palm deep into the fibrous floor to feel the deep water-tides, and I simply never pulled it back. 

The Weft has been polite, as it always is. It did not tear or rot me. Instead, the pale, hair-like rootlets of the peat-moss have found the silver-green down of my skin. They have woven themselves into me, thread by thread, until my arm is indistinguishable from the emerald mattress. I can feel them drinking the sweet, warm grease of my body, and in return, they are feeding me the warm broth of the ancient, dissolved generations from below. 

I am losing my edges. It is a heavy, beautiful thing to lose one's edges.

When I was young, my skin was the color of a peeled almond, and I moved across the domes with the clan. I have ridden in the vines of a Pith-Walker’s bower, watching the bronze sun slide through the mist for sixty years without ever reaching the horizon. I have stood at the edge of the circular cisterns, my arms submerged in the black water, waiting for the long, cold silk of the Sieve-Ribbons to brush my skin so I could scrape the yellow oil from their sides. 

Now, I do not need to seek the world. The world comes to me through the floor.

Because my body is pressed flat against the root-weave, my "hearing" has grown immense. I no longer hear only what is near. I hear the entire dome. 

Two miles to the north, a Pith-Walker is stepping. *Shlap. Shlap.* The vibrations are slow, heavy thuds that travel through the mattress like ripples in a warm tub. I can feel the weight of its six broad feet, the way they spread the moss, the way the water rises around them and then is sucked back down with a tiny, distant hiss. 

Closer, inside the white lacework of the siphonophore tower beside me, the Lanth-Ferns are dripping. *Plop. Plop.* The drops of white milk fall from the fronds onto the leathery green galls hanging from the calcified ribs. 

I can feel the galls. There are three of them this season, grafted to the white lime by a young couple who passed through the valley two summers ago. The galls are warm. They are swelling, absorbing the milk and the mist. When the parents were here, they sat beside me—though they did not know I was still "me"; they saw only a long, soft mound of velvet. They leaned their foreheads against the siphonophore tower and let their throat-sacks hum. 

The vibration went into the calcified white bone of the tower, down into the peat, and into my ribs. *Oo-ool,* they hummed. *Oo-ool.* 

I felt their song. I felt the three galls inside their leathery skins warming to the vibration. I hummed back, though there was no air in my throat to do it. I hummed through my bones, sending the tremor back up through the roots of the tower. The parents did not look down. They simply pressed their foreheads tighter to the white ribs, feeling the ancestral gristle reply from the green floor.

In three more summers, the galls will soften into jelly. The children will slip out, pale and wet, onto the moss. They will not know my name, for we have no names but the hum, and my hum is now the same frequency as the deep shifting of the root-systems beneath the domes. But they will walk upon me. Their mushroom-pads will press into my greened chest, and they will drink the condensation that pools in the hollows where my ribs used to be.

The sun is a pale copper smudge overhead, suspended in the warm, greasy vapor. It never moves. It does not need to. 

A Pith-Walker's sigh—a wet, bellows-like *whuff*—drifts across the valley, muffled instantly by the green wool of the world. 

Another root-fiber enters my shoulder, cool and thirsty. I let my gristle soften bills of pressure. I give it what it asks for. In the Weft, nothing is lost; everything is merely woven back into the floor, to be walked upon, to be felt, to be sung to, forever.
