If you was to go down there, down past where the ropes go slack and the air begins to taste of old copper and wet grease, you wouldn't find me walking. Walking is a luxury for things that have somewhere to get to before the frost takes their knees.

I would be in the **Splits**.

Specifically, I would be wedged into the great three-way junction where the *Nine-Mile Spire*—a vertical trunk that is seventy yards thick and has a grain like stacked slate—meets the horizontal road we call the *High Crotch*. It is a place where three million tons of frozen wood are trying to occupy the same space as three million tons of limestone plateau, and neither one of them is inclined to give an inch without a scream.

I would be a **Splint**.

***

At first, you might not see me. You would think I was just a scab of particularly ugly heartwood, or a lump of old tallow-mould that had fallen from a generator roof and frozen into a grey, triangular loaf. I am about the size of a blacksmith’s anvil, but wedge-shaped, tapering down to a blunt, chisel-thin edge at my front and flattening out to a broad, scarred heel at my back. 

I don't have eyes. In the Sinking Rift, eyes are just two wet spots for the frost to bite. Instead, my face is a solid plate of grey, mineralized bone—thick and fibrous, like the ironwood itself—fused directly to my collar. My skull doesn't turn. If I want to look at something, I have to wait for the vibration of it to travel through the sole of my belly and up into my inner ear-stones, which are as large as robin’s eggs and sit in pockets of thick grease behind my temples.

My skin is not skin. It is a three-inch rind of yellowed fat and wax, smelling of raw kerosene and pine-pitch. It is completely dry and cold to the touch, but underneath, in the deep core of me, there is a heart that beats three times an hour. Every strike of that heart is a heavy, hydraulic shunt, pushing cold grease through my veins to keep my joints from lock-freezing.

This is how I live:

I crawl into the places where the world is cracking.

When the great columns flex under the weight of the cliffs above, the wood doesn't just bend; it splits. A crack will start at the base of a trunk—a sharp, deafening *CRACK* like a field-gun going off—and it will run upward for three hundred feet, a clean, raw fissure three inches wide. 

That is my door.

I move by flow. I do not have legs with knees or feet; I have six broad, spade-like studs of horn on my underside. By shifting my weight, by squeezing the grease from the left side of my hide to the right, I slide myself into the split. I go forward, thin-edge first, until the crack narrows and pinches me. 

And then I wait.

I am a living wedge. When the mountain shifts again and tries to grind that crack shut, it finds me. The pressure is immense—it is enough to turn a iron bolt into a flat wafer—but my bone-plate is harder than the ironwood. I take the squeeze. My waxy skin acts as a lubricant, letting the millions of tons of pressure slide over me, and my fat-filled cells act as hydraulic cylinders, evening out the load. 

I hold the world open by three inches. 

***

The Thrummers know me. They don't have a name for me, but they know what I am for.

Sometimes, when the vibration of the northern turbines shifts—when the scream goes from a high, healthy whistle to a low, rattling growl that means a generator shaft has gone out of true—a pair of Thrummers will come down the copper lines. They don't use lights; they carry small, lead-faced sledges and they feel their way by the click-click-click of their bare heels on the bark.

They find me by the heat. Even in the sub-zero dark, my slow, grease-churning belly produces a tiny aura of warmth—just enough to keep the frost-needles from forming on the wood three inches around me.

They don't speak. They don't have ears for it. But one of them will lay his palm flat against my broad, flat heel to feel how tight I am in the split. 

If I am loose, he will spit a wad of chewed beetle-carapace onto my skin to grease it, and then he will lift his sledge.

*THOOM.*

The blow is massive. It rattles the ear-stones in my head until they ring like iron bells. It drives me two inches deeper into the crack, wedging the split wider, leveling the generator platform fifty yards above us so the copper cables can run straight again.

I do not mind the hammer. The hammer is a clear, simple voice. It says: *Stay here. Hold.*

***

Sometimes, when the Thrummers are gone and the turbines are singing their steady, sixty-cycle song, I drink.

I drink the black water that seeps down from the limestone walls. It runs through the narrow runnels in the bark, thick with tannins and iron rust. My underside is dry and porous, like a peat-block; when the water crawls under me, I suck it up through thousands of tiny, microscopic tubes in my belly-rind. It tastes of old leaves and cold stone. It is the only taste I know.

And sometimes, I listen to the **Cable-Talk**.

The copper line for the Western Generator runs right across the trunk above my head. It vibrates constantly, a thin, high-pitched *zi-i-i-i-ng* that never stops. But every now and then, a Thrummer three miles away will strike the line with a iron wrench.

The vibration comes down the copper, down into the ironwood, through my bone face, and into my ear-stones.

*Tap-tap-thrum.* (The oil is low at Station Four.)
*Thrum-tap-tap.* (Old Jacob fell into the void.)
*Tap-thrum-tap.* (The beetles are moving south on Spire Nine.)

I do not answer. I have no voice to send back through the wire, and besides, the messages are not for me. But I hold them. I am the medium. The words travel through my tallow, through my bones, through the cold fat of my ribs, and out into the ironwood again, cleaner and clearer than they would have gone through the dry bark alone. I am a knot in their telephone line.

***

One day, I suppose, the northern turbines will stop.

The Thrummers will run out of grease for the bearings, or the copper lines will finally snap under the weight of the ice-buildup. The scream will die first, fading down to a long, sighing moan, and then there will be only the organic voice of the Rift—the deep, terrible groaning of the timber as the cold-rot sets in.

When the warm air stops blowing, the wood will soften. The petrified bark will lose its ring; it will become pulpy and wet, like old dough. My wedge will no longer hold. I will sink deeper and deeper into the trunk as it collapses around me, the wood swallowing me like grease down a throat.

I will not struggle. Nothing moves quickly here.

I will let my heart slow from three beats an hour to one. Then to one every day. I will become a dark, mineralized knot in the center of the Nine-Mile Spire, thirty feet deep in the heartwood, sealed away from the cold and the noise forever.

And when the ironwood finally petrifies entirely, thousands of years from now, when the Rift has filled with stone and the mountains have ground themselves to dust, some creature of the surface will split the rock. They will find a clean, grey wedge of bone and grease, preserved in the center of a black tree, still smelling faintly of copper and kerosene and the heavy, deafening dark.
