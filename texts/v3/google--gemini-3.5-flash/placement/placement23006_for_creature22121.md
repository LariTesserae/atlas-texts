The world is not a picture. 

To those with eyes, it might be a silver ruin of basalt and fog, but to me, the world is a heavy, rhythmic iron weight pressing upward against my chest. It is a web of trembling threads that run through the stone floor, some thick as a tail-lobe, some thin and shivering like the whiskers of a crawler.

I am down here, where the grease meets the silt.

My belly is flat, wide, and cold. The grease-skin on my flanks is thick today—it has the good, sour smell of old lard and the bitter tang of the sulfur-muds where the warm vents bubble. I can feel the grease worked into the creases of my pectoral joints, keeping out the sharp, grey basalt dust that tries to grind me down. 

When the earth begins its deep intake—when the air pressure drops and the great siphons below us start to suck the water back into the belly of the world—I do not run. We do not run. We heave. 

*Slid-huff. Slid-huff.*

My left flipper-sole finds a ridge of smooth limestone; my right flipper claws, blunt and short like iron plugs, bite into a patch of leathery liver-mold. I drag myself three inches forward. Behind me, my tail-lobe follows like a sack of wet sand, leaving a broad, glistening smear through the silt.

In my chest, the crescent-bone sits in its pocket of cold, thick oil. Through it, I map the flats. 

Half a mile to the north, there is a great, blind crawler. I feel its joints—*clack, clack, click*—as it drags its greasy shell over the wet mounds. It is seeking the flat-shells, but it is moving too fast; it is hungry and clumsy. 

Much closer, only three lengths of my own body away, is the wall of our circle.

I know this wall better than my own skin. My mate, the one whose grease smells of lichen-yellow and dried worm-skin, is inside. I can feel her throat-rumble traveling through the slate foundation of the enclosure. It is a slow, grainy vibration: *Safe. Smooth. Bring the hard-leaf.*

The "hard-leaf" is what we call the shelf-fungus that grows in the deep cracks where the wind cannot blow. It is dry and chalky on the tongue, but when we mix it with the fat of the crawlers and the yellow sulfur-dust, it makes the smolder-blocks. 

Today, my flippers are loaded. I am carrying a slab of blue-grey slate clamped against my ribs. It is heavy, cold, and wet. The salt spray from the nearest blowhole—which is currently whistling a shrill, warning pitch as the water drains—weeps down my face, filling my blind, white eye-cauls with cold moisture. I do not mind the wet. It is the dry that we fear. When the skin goes dry, it cracks, and the basalt dust gets into the red meat beneath.

I reach the entrance of our enclosure. The mud here is packed deep and firm by our own dragging. I drop the slate. It hits the gravel with a dull, heavy *thrum* that I feel in my oil-plate like a spark of light.

My mate is there. Her head is low, her snout pressed against my flank. She begins to groom me. 

The touch of her three spade-digits is the only soft thing in this flat world. She finds a colony of parasite-worms that have burrowed into the thick fold behind my left flipper—they smell like rotted silt—and she scrapes them away with her obsidian tool. I feel the cold, sharp glass gliding over my hide. 

The glass has no voice. That is why we love it. 

Most stones have a hum, a history of the water that wore them down or the heat that spat them up. But the black glass we gather from the deep blowholes is silent. When you touch it to your skin, it is like a little pocket of absolute quiet. It does not vibrate. It is a cool, hard nothing.

Inside our circle, the hearth is cold but greasy. I can smell the soot from the last cycle—the sour, heavy stink of cured worm-meat and sulfur. 

My mate has laid out three flat-shells. They are still wet, their tiny, bristled legs twitching weakly against the packed mud of our platform. I lower my head. My lower mouth—the flat, crushing plates—presses against the first shell.

*Crunch.*

The taste is salt, cold grease, and the bitter, earthy tang of the slime-mold the creature had been scraping before we found it. It is good. It fills the cold space in my middle.

We lie together, chest to chest, our flippers tangled to keep each other from sliding in the draft. The earth is shifting now. The whistling of the blowholes is dying out. The air is growing thick, heavy, and wet again. 

Down in the dark, the great lungs of the world are filling with water. 

I can feel the subterranean rise two miles away. It starts as a low, wet growl in the deep siphons, a vibration so vast that it makes the iron-plates in my head hum in sympathy. The standing pools will be back soon. The water will rise to our chests, cold and foaming and loud, before it settles into the quiet, knee-deep stillness of the high tide.

But my joints are stiff today. 

It is a new stiffness. It is not the tiredness that comes after dragging slate from the silt-plains. It is different.

When I move my tail-lobe, it does not bend as it used to. It feels... heavy. Not with meat, but with stone. The skin near my tail-tip is no longer sliding; it is rough to the touch, like the edge of a basalt mound. When my mate's claws scrape against it, there is no grease there. There is only a dry, gritty rasp.

She knows. She has felt it. Her throat-rumble is very quiet now, a soft, sanding purr that stays low to the mud.

In the next cycle, or perhaps the one after, I will not go out to look for the slate. I will not go to the blowholes to fish for the pale worms that dance in the currents.

I will slide out of the circle. I will leave the obsidian scraper in its shallow mud-nest, where it belongs. I will drag myself into the shallow water, near the mound where my parent settled three great-turns ago.

I will pull my flippers in close, under my chest, so they do not catch the drift. I will lower my head until my oil-plate is flat against the bedrock, one final time.

I will listen. 

I will listen to the scuttle of the flat-shells, the heavy sliding of my mate inside the circle, the clumsy step of the crawlers, and the great whistle-shriek of the leaking world. I will listen until the silt settles over my back, until the lime in the water fills the pores of my skin, until my grease turns to oil, and my bone turns to stone.

And then, I will not have to listen anymore. I will be the thing they are listening to.
