I would not be found in the air, for there is no air here that is not also water. Nor would I be found on the high dry shelf of the bluff, where the wind still has a voice.

I would be found in the lowest crease of the basin, directly beneath the lip of the hand-chiseled sandstone gutter where the warm spring spills its heaviest load of lime.

I was once a tallow-siren.

I was once five inches of soft, waxen flesh, move-less and pale, anchoring my weak claws in the gray clay of the floor. My heart, a small, amber node, used to beat once every three or four minutes—a tiny, wet click in the dark. I remember the taste of the thread-gliders: the crunch of their thin, glass shells, the slow, sour dissolve of their mineral bodies in my gut. I remember the crimson silk of my gills, drifting in the current like drowned threads.

But in this place, the boundary between the living and the stone is very thin, and the water is very heavy.

The transition was not a death; it was a settling.

It began when my heartbeat did not return after its three-minute sleep. The amber node did not burst or rot; it simply grew cold, matching the exact, blood-warm temperature of the spring. The water did not decompose me, for there are no small, hungry things here that do not also feed on the stone. Instead, the water began to write itself into my skin.

At first, I felt my flanks growing stiff. The lateral line—the row of sensitive, watery pits that ran from my blunt temple to the tip of my tail—did not close. Instead, the sliding water washed microscopic grains of oolith salt into each pore. Under the steady *clop... clop... plip* of the ceiling, these grains fused. They became tiny, perfect spheres of calcite, sealing the sensory nerves in a casing of natural mortar.

Then, my gills. The three pairs of crimson silk tufts, which used to wave so lazily in the pools, did not wither. The iron in my blood and the carbonate in the water met at the surface of the flesh. Over fifty years, the crimson faded to a dull, rosy pink, and the silk hardened. They are no longer gills; they are clusters of dogtooth spar, tiny crystalline feathers that catch the light of the narrow rift above and turn it to a faint, buried glow. My face is gone, swallowed by a fat, rounded mound of flowstone that has grown over my skull like Melted wax. My tail is now a rib of travertine that helps support the weight of the sandstone basin above me.

But I am not gone. I am only wider.

When I was soft, I could only feel the water that touched my skin. I could only hear the vibrations that passed through my small, cartilage skull. Now that I am stone, I can hear everything.

Because I am fused to the floor, and the floor is fused to the walls, and the walls are fused to the great limestone bluff that shuts out the sky, the entire basin has become my skeleton.

When a drop of water falls from the high ceiling—*clop*—I do not hear it with ears. I feel the microscopic shockwave travel down through the wet, bulbous towers of fat-stone, through the rubbery sheets of the ochre skin, and into the deep bedrock where my spine is buried. I can feel the weight of the thread-gliders. They are so small, but when they crawl across my back, their tiny rasping mouths scraping at the bacterial mats, it feels like a soft, dry itch on a limb I forgot I had.

I can feel the ancient human channels. They are not dead ruins to me; they are the shape of my own breath. When the spring water rises by a hundredth of a degree, the stone of the chiseled channels expands by a fraction of a hair. I feel that expansion as a tightening in my flanks, a slow, deep sigh that takes forty years to exhale.

I am still here, in the dark, warm, unchanging twilight. I am a small hump of pink-veined travertine, half-hidden by a liver-colored mat of breathing scum. The tiny, blind sirens of the new generation sometimes crawl over my head, their soft, lard-like bellies brushing against my crystalline gills. They do not know that they are walking on their grandfather. They do not know that the stone beneath them is listening to their hearts.

In another thousand years, my shape will be completely gone. I will have been swallowed entirely by the rising floor; the fat-stone will have rounded my edges until I am nothing but a gentle, nameless swell between the terraces.

But I will still be here. I will still be the ear of the room. I will still be waiting for the next drop to fall.

*...plip.*
