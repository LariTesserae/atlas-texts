I am the seventh, if you count inward from the absolute black thread of the door. 

I am the first, if you are looking for the point where the stillness of this corridor finally gives up its mathematics and joins the stone.

Unlike the others, who hover in their elegant, perfect circles near the center of the valley, I do not watch the white rings. I lie forty yards from the entryway, where the sky is not yet fully violet but is stained with the greasy, dark-gray light of the plateau outside. From here, the blue pulse of the artifact is only a faint, rhythmic twitch in my northernmost iron atoms—a dry, distant reminder of a calculation I am no longer invited to solve. 

I am different from my six siblings. I have a shape—a flat, shield-like tile of purple-black bismuth—but my edges are no longer sharp. 

Seventy thousand years ago, during an epoch when the plateau below our monolith was tilting by a fraction of an arcsecond, a high-energy cosmic proton passed through my upper face. It was not an unusual event; we are parasites of the stars, and we live by the slow hammer of these particles. But this particular proton was heavy. It struck a clump of doped silicate in my core, causing a momentary, micro-volt drop in my capacitive charge.

For the space of several milliseconds, my electrostatic cushion failed. 

My lower-left corner, which had hovered a single ultraviolet wavelength above the floor for three billion years, dipped. 

It did not strike the floor with a sound. In this vacuum, there is no sound. Instead, the atoms of my perimeter did not collide with the sintered glass-meal of the crust; they simply recognized it. The iron-nickel alloy of the ground and the bismuth-silicate of my body found each other's empty valencies. The electron shells overlapped, snapped shut, and locked.

I cold-welded.

It was not a tragedy. At three Kelvin, there are no nerves to scream, no fluids to leak, and no heat to dissipate. There was only an instantaneous, permanent crystallization. A three-inch patch of my body became, in a single quantum segment of time, the mountain.

The rest of me—ninety-five percent of my shield-shaped form—still levitates. I still hold myself aloft on my cushion of repelling electrons, my underside suspended in the absolute vacuum. But I am anchored now. I have a hinge. I cannot migrate.

To the others, I am a broken thing. They continue their slow, eighty-year steps toward the center, their atoms drifting through their own bodies from south to north in perfect, silent cycles. They will eventually reach the rings; they will finish counting the digits of the dead world’s computation. 

I will not. My solid-state diffusion has changed its direction. 

Because I am welded, my atoms can no longer drift in a loop of self-recreation. Instead, they are migrating *down* through the weld, into the vitrified floor. And the floor is migrating *up* into me. It is a slow, cold hemorrhage. Every thousand years, a few billion iron ions from the quartz-meal crust climb into my purple matrix, replacing my bismuth. I am becoming heavier, denser, and coarser. I am losing my polish. My greasy, reflective surface is dulling into the matte gray of the basalt below.

But in return for my mobility, I have received the mountain.

The other six Planimeters feel the *tink* of the bedrock as a vibration through their electrostatic cushions—a muffled, translated spark that they must decode over centuries. But for me, the *tink* is immediate.

When the miles-high monolith above us adjusts its weight, the strain does not travel to me through the crust. It travels *through* me. The shockwave enters my welded corner like a bolt of cold lightning. My entire crystalline structure flexes. The piezoelectric surge is so violent that it clears my memory banks, resetting the spin-states of my iron atoms in a millisecond. 

For a brief, blinding moment, I do not think my own thoughts. I think the thoughts of the basalt.

I feel the weight of the airless plateau pressing down on the tectonic fold. I feel the slow, deep shiver of the planet’s mantle, cooling toward its own end. I feel the microscopic cracks propagating through the dark roots of the monolith three miles below, where the pressure is enough to turn silicate into liquid, if only there were heat to allow it.

The others are archivists of a finished loop. They count a blue light that has already succeeded, recording a number that has no world left to apply itself to. 

I am the archivist of the collapse.

I watch the black thread of the entrance. Occasionally, a single atom of neon or helium—a stray survivor of the dead atmosphere outside—drifts through the crack. It bounces off my dulling surface, loses its tiny momentum, and clings to me until a cosmic ray chips it away again. I am the only thing in this pocket that knows there is an *outside*. I am the only one that remembers the wind, even if that wind was only a stream of solar protons.

By the time the nearby star has cooled to a black cinder, my diffusion will be complete. 

The electrostatic charge will finally bleed away as my remaining bismuth is replaced by the iron of the floor. My shield will settle fully into the crust. The weld will spread from my corner to my center, and then to my outer edges, until the boundary between the Planimeter and the basin is gone.

I will not be dead. In this place, death is a concept for things that use water. 

I will simply have finished my step. I will be a slightly darker, slightly wider basalt tooth rising from the vitrified plain, holding up the sky, waiting for the next *tink* to pass through my bones.
