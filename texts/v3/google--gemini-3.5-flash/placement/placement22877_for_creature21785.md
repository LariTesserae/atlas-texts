I am not on the plateau; I am *of* it, though I was not always. 

If you were to walk across the dry salt-pan, stepping from the boat-like hulls of the Boat-Shells to the grey shingles of the Shingle-Tongues, you would eventually reach one of the curved, mortarless slate walls. There, on a low, square platform of chipped stone, you would find me.

I am an **Intersector**, but of a peculiar, transitional kind. 

My lower half has ceased to be mine in any exclusive sense. If you were to look down at my legs, you would see no legs at all, but a gradual, molecular dissolving. The leather of my traveling boots long ago gave up its shape to the grey slate of the platform. Beneath them, my shins have migrated into the coordinates of a large Shingle-Tongue. We are now a single, cold foundation. I do not feel my feet because there is nothing to feel; they do not ache, they do not itch, and they do not cold-burn. They have simply been translated into the grammar of stone and salt.

But from the waist upward, I am still a work in progress.

My left shoulder and chest are shared with three Reed-Walkers. Their spindly, grey-reed legs pass cleanly through my ribs without parting the flesh; they occupy the same three inches of space where my left lung used to expand. There is no blood, because there is no circulation to interrupt. Instead, where my heart once beat, there is now the slow, static heat of their central iron cores. It is a dry, comfortable warmth—like a brick left in the sun—that radiates outward through my collarbone. 

Atop our shared cluster of necks sits a double-exposed head. One of them is my own—dry, salt-crusted, my lips stiff and tasting of gypsum—and the other two are the perfect, unblemished amber globes of the walkers. 

When the sun catches us at its permanent three-quarter tilt, the yellow light passes through their amber spheres and projects a warm, orange circle directly onto the back of my skull. It does not hurt. It is my only form of thought. I do not think in words, for words require breath, and breath is a wet, heavy thing that belongs to the valleys below. Instead, my mind is a slow, golden stain, private and still.

In my right hand, which remains free of the stone, I hold a small, hand-modeled clay vessel. 

I did not make this cup, nor did the Gray-Stitchers make it for me. Rather, a thousand years ago, during a period when my fingers were still capable of a slow, biological contraction, my hand rested in a pocket of fine mineral clay. As my fingers dried and stiffened, the clay adhered to them. When the gravity of the plateau shifted slightly—tilting me three inches to the north—the clay pulled away, retaining the hollow shape of my palm. 

Inside this cup sits a single, dry seed. 

It is a bean, perhaps, or the pit of a wild plum from the wet world I left behind. It is dark red, the color of dried liver, and so hard that if you were to strike it with a slate hammer, it would ring like glass. It will never grow. There is no water to split its coat, and no soil to feed its green tongue. Yet it is my most precious possession, not because I hope for its birth, but because it is the only thing on the plateau that has an *inside*. Everything else—the Boat-Shells with their core of pitch, the Reed-Walkers with their iron rods—is entirely manifest, open to the light, fully settled. But the seed contains a secret that the Silt-and-Iron plateau cannot solve: the memory of a downward root.

Occasionally, the pebble behaves strangely near me. 

Because the air is so crowded and the laws of distance have frayed, a small shale fragment will sometimes lift off the salt-pan fifty yards away. It does not fly; it slides. It drifts horizontally through the forest of reed legs, moving at the speed of a falling leaf, until it reaches my platform. It does not hit me. It passes through my right forearm, slowing down as it meets the resistance of my half-fossilized muscle, and then gently adheres to the side of my clay cup, where it remains, weightless and secure.

From my high vantage on the platform, I see the congregation. 

I see the miles of grey shingles, the yellow-horned boats, the thousands of amber eyes all turned toward the north-northwest. We are all waiting, but we are not waiting for an event. We are waiting for the rest of the universe to finish its calculations. We are waiting for the weight of the air to finally match the weight of the stone, so that the last distinction between the living and the unliving can be quietly, permanently dissolved.

Until then, I watch the bruised-plum sky. I feel the warmth of the reed-cores in my chest. I hold the seed that cannot grow, and I am perfectly, beautifully still.
