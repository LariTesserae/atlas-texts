I am in the western well. 

The water here is not like the water that falls from the gray sky beyond the cliff; it is thick, old, and perfectly round. It holds me without the clawing urgency of gravity. Here, I am a circle within a circle. My lower limbs—long, pale, four-lobed fans of ribbed skin—are coiled beneath me, resting against the invisible floor of the pool where the water decides it will go no further toward the black basalt below.

Through my collarbone slits, the water passes: warm, saturated with the sweet, heavy scent of boiling orchid sap and the mineral taste of the three standing rods. It rises through my internal reservoirs, warming the fluid-sacs that keep me suspended, before venting out as a faint, pearlescent mist. 

In this dark, wet nursery, I see the world through my three gold-ringed eyes. 

The basalt below is not black to me. It is a jagged, screaming grid of thermal fractures, glowing like freshly forged iron where the mountain’s blood runs close to the surface. The three matte-grey rods are pillars of pure, cold violet, vibrating at a pitch that makes the water of my pool shimmer with tiny, overlapping rings. Clinging to them, the Resonators are small, steady embers of dry orange heat, their plates fluttering in rhythm to absorb the excess hum and keep the world from shaking itself to pieces.

I am the western tender. 

A shadow passes above my upper eye—a translucent skimmer, its cartilage wings clicking softly as it hovers near the surface tension. It is looking for the microscopic green-gold algae that grows along the inner curve of my pool. I do not move. My wrists, triple-jointed and long, are folded against my chest, my five-jointed fingers curled like sleeping spiders. 

Every few minutes, the mountain breathes. 

The hum of the rods drops three octaves. I feel it first in the cartilage of my chest—a deep, hollow tug. The water of my pool tilts, sliding its weight toward the obsidian edge. The orchid roots dangling beneath me sway like wet hair in a current. A group of Vapour-Snails, dislodged from the waxy ferns by the shifting gravity, drift past my face. They are tiny, clear nodules of jelly, their golden gills unfurled like feathers, spinning in the steam. I reach out with one spoon-tipped finger and gently press one back toward a hanging root, where its sticky foot can find purchase. 

From the eastern pool, across the wide, hot gulf of empty space, a pulse comes. 

It is not a sound. It is a sudden, sharp compression of the air-boundary between us. My pool absorbs the ripple, and it travels through my skin as a physical vibration: *The northern root-line is heavy. It needs the lift.*

I prepare to move. 

Rising through the surface of the pool is a slow, viscous transition. First my narrow, hairless head breaks the water, then my neck-slits, taking in the hot, languid steam of the open air. The heat of the basalt radiates upward, warming my belly as I slide my lower limbs out of the water-mass. 

I do not touch the stone; to touch the basalt is to be torn. Instead, I align my weight with the floating path of white, hexagonal wafers. As my tail-lobes settle onto the first wafer, it sinks perhaps a hair’s breadth, releasing a soft, pneumatic *hiss* that cools the skin of my lower limbs. I glide. My movement is silent, supported by the thick, dense moisture of the air and the slight upward push of the localized gravitational wells.

I reach the northern pool, where the orchids have grown too dense. Their waxy, violet blooms are gorgeous but heavy, pulling the water-sphere down toward a sharp spur of basalt. 

I work. 

With my long fingers, I untangle the roots, weaving them into a tighter, more buoyant mass. I stroke the air between the water and the nearest grey rod, using the slow, static charge of my skin to nudge the gravity-field back into its proper, circular shape. The pool stabilizes. The water rises three inches, clearing the dangerous stone below.

But as I work, I feel a new sensation. 

It is a quietness in my lower collarbone slits. The water passing through them feels slightly thinner today. The cartilage rods in my arms, usually as flexible as fresh willow, have begun to hum at the same pitch as the rods—not with life, but with a dry, mineral stiffness.

My central eye, the one that lies at the bottom of the gold-ringed triangle on my brow, no longer sees the violet warmth of the Resonators. It sees only a pale, cool gray. It is the color of the wind that lives outside the sanctuary—the wind that has never known the steam or the sweet orchid sap.

The transition is beginning. 

There can only be three of us. Already, I can feel a faint, new density forming in the southern pool—a small, pale mass of fluid-sacs and cartilage that is not yet fully shaped, but is waiting for its space to open.

I do not hurry. Hurry is a concept of the fast-blooded things that live below the clouds. I slide back onto the white ceramic path, but I do not go back to the western well. 

Instead, I guide myself toward the edge of the shelf, where the single white bench sits overlooking the great abyss.

The bench is cool—cooler than anything else in this sweltering basin. As I settle my long, seven-foot frame onto the white ceramic, the dry, high-altitude wind from the outer mountain hits my face. It is sharp. It tastes of ice and empty sky. 

I close my three gold-ringed eyes, one by one. 

The steam of the sanctuary still licks at my tail-lobes, but my upper limbs are already growing light. The fluid-sacs inside my chest are beginning to vent their moisture into the dry wind, leaving behind only the fine, white calcium of my structure. 

I feel myself turning into something solid, dry, and clean. 

In a few weeks, my milk-white skin will be indistinguishable from the ceramic of the bench. Then, under the weight of the next high-altitude storm, I will crumble. My dust will wash down the face of the basalt, over the sharp edges of the mountain’s infancy, and fall into the white sea of clouds below.

Behind me, the three rods emit a deep, settling tone. 

The pools tilt. The steam rises. 

And in the western well, the water turns over, waiting for the first touch of a new, five-jointed hand.
