I am not down where the basalt flagstones pool with green grease, nor am I high enough to see the grey seam of the sky where the iron pipes crowd out the light. I am suspended at the mid-height, where the air is thickest with the smell of scorched suet and the green, cooling pepper of the mint. 

If you were to lean over the copper gantry—the one with the rusted handrails that leads to the dead safety-valves—you might look down into the vertical mist and see what looks like an old, waterlogged pilot coat clinging to a bracket. It has been there so long that the bracket, a heavy T-bar of cast iron, has grown a thick, black sleeve of anaerobic grease around itself, cementing the fabric to the metal like a weld.

This is where I have settled. I am the *Shroud-Cloth*.

I did not begin as an animal, nor did I begin as a machine. Decades ago, perhaps, there was a wool garment—a heavy, double-breasted thing of coarse blue frieze, stiff with coal-dust and the sweat of some long-dead stoker who hauled it off and threw it over the T-bar to dry, never to return. But the damp here does not allow things to remain dead or dry. The constant, fat drizzle of grease-slicked water has worked its way through the warp and weft of the wool, replacing the animal hair fiber by fiber with a grey, fibrous marrow.

I am four feet of heavy, hanging drag. My "skin" is a thick, felted crust, three inches deep and water-logged to the point of near-immobility. My shape is still vaguely that of a garment, but the sleeves have grown together at the hem, forming a single, sagging pouch that hangs down into the warm rising steam like an empty bladder. 

Inside me, where the lining used to be, there are no lungs or stomach, but there is an intricate, internal labyrinth of wet horsehair and lime-crust. I am a passive siphon. The water that drips from the high steam-mains does not simply hit me and run off; I drink it through a thousand tiny, felted pores. It filters slowly down through my interior maze, leaving its minerals behind, before dripping from my bottom hem in three clean, fat, perfectly regular drops every twelve seconds. 

I am the regulator of the middle-draft. 

Because I hang directly over the main exhaust flue of the wet-wound dynamo, my lower half is always hot—a deep, baking heat that smells of cooked oil—while my upper shoulders, where I clasp the iron T-bar, are cooled by the constant weeping of the brickwork. This temperature difference creates a slow, internal circulation within my fibrous body. The water inside me rises as steam through my neck-opening and condenses again on my outer sleeve-molds, a silent, internal weather system that has been cycling since before the great nine-foot drive-wheel lost its first tooth.

I do not see the alley. I have no eyes, nor do I have the Glass-Gills' affinity for the dull orange bulb down below. My world is entirely one of weight and vibration.

Through the iron T-bar, which is my spine, I feel the heartbeat of the basin. I know the exact state of the boilers beneath the basalt floor by the pitch of the dynamo’s hum. When the pressure is high, the hum rises to a sharp, metallic *whir* that vibrates in my lower hem like a collective itch. When the boilers are low, the hum drops to a wet, lazy growl, and my fibers relax, sagging another half-inch toward the steam-vents.

I am not lonely. The Sleeve-Worms occasionally find their way up the copper conduits to my T-bar. They are small, fibrous things, but to me they feel like warm, stiff wires sliding through my collar. They do not eat me, nor do I disturb them; sometimes they weave themselves into my shoulders to mend a tear where the weight of my waterlogged hem has threatened to pull me from the bracket. We are of the same wool, after all.

Sometimes, when the drizzle is particularly heavy and the lime-dust is thick in the air, I feel a slow, pleasant swelling in my lower pouch. It is the accumulation of the basin’s marrow. In a year or two, when the weight becomes too great, a small lump of this accumulated tallow and lime will slough off from my bottom hem. It will fall through the amber mist, past the green, rubbery leaves of the elephant-ear ferns, and land with a soft *plop* into the three-inch pool on the basalt floor. 

Down there, the Tallow-Glands will find it. They will slide their heavy, lardaceous bulks over it, tasting the salt of the boilers and the soot of the high chimneys that I have filtered out of the air. They will turn it into themselves.

I am completely content in this heavy, suspended state. I do not wish to climb to the grey seam of sky, where the air is dry and cold and would shrink my fibers to a stiff, useless husk. I do not wish to fall to the floor, where my shape would be lost in the slow, scraping crawl of the Tallow-Glands. 

I belong to the middle. I am the throat through which the alley breathes its hot tallow-mist. I am the weight that keeps the draft from blowing too fast. I am the old coat that became a lung, hanging wet and dark in the amber dusk, listening to the *clack-hiss, clack-hiss* of the great wheel, waiting for the next drop to fall.
