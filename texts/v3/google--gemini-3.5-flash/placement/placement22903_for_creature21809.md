The map on the soles of my feet is older than the skin that carries it. 

When I press my heel against the third step of the Eastern Ladder, the brick does not feel like stone to me; it feels like an old tooth, notched and familiar in the jaw. I know the dip where the mortar has gone soft and turned to red flour; I know the three square paces where the floor has buckled upward because the great copper pipe beneath has grown warm and swollen with the mid-day flow.

I am small, but I am very heavy. When I walk across the cedar planks, they do not creak—they groan, a long, wet *ughhh* that traveled down the hemp lines and died in the green-greased bronze pegs. My chest is wide, a thick leather bag that takes in the steam. The air here is thick, tasting of tallow-smoke and the wet, sour rot of the lichen-beds, but to me, it is thin. I must draw it in deeply, holding it until my ribs click against each other like the halves of a split tile.

On my head, the liver-lichen is thick today. It has rained above—I know this because the rootlets hanging from the ceiling are dripping with a cold, clear water that tastes of dead leaves and soot, very different from our own warm, greasy weepings. The lichen on my skull has drunk this sky-water and grown fat and purple; it smells of cold iron. I like the weight of it. It keeps the heavy, lime-laden drops from hitting my bare skin, where they would harden into dry, grey warts that must be scraped off with a copper knife.

My palms are wide, the skin on them so worn by fifty years of handling terracotta that the whorls of my fingers have been replaced by the fine, parallel grain of the clay itself. I have no nails. The bone of my fingertips has grown outward into flat, yellow spades, hard as horn. 

With these, I work.

***

Today, the fourth siphon is whispering. 

It is not the clean, round *clop-clop* of healthy water, but a wet, whistling *shhh-shhh*, like a dry leaf caught in a throat. I crawl down into the shadows of the secondary arch, where the square pillars are so close together that I must turn sideways, my barrel-chest scraping the wet lime-wash of the walls. 

The crabs are here. They are gathered in a great, orange heap—several hundred of them, their small, dry legs clicking against each other with the sound of a handful of pebbles shaken in a leather cup. They do not run from me. As I sit down on the wet bricks, they begin to stream over my thighs and up my ribs. Their tiny claws are cold, but they are precise. They find the seams of my joints—the soft skin behind my knees, the folds under my arms—and begin to pick away the grey lime-scale and the greasy tallow-soot that has gathered there while I slept. 

I lie back against the damp brick and let them work. The sensation is a thousand tiny, sharp pinches, none of them painful, all of them sweet. While they clean me, I reach my long, double-jointed arm deep into the ceramic pipe at my side. 

The water inside is fast and warm. I let my fingers drift in the flow until I feel the obstruction. It is a mass of rootlets—thick, hair-thin fibers that have pushed through a gap in the mortar, catching the silt and the dead beetles that wash down from the light-wells. 

I use the spade of my middle finger to saw at the roots. They are tough, like wet string, but they yield. I pull them out in wet, black handfuls and drop them onto the floor. Instantly, the crabs scramble off my chest to claim them, fighting over the sweet, earthy grease trapped in the fibers.

The siphon clears. The water inside makes a deep, guttural *gloop*, and then settles into its proper, silent run. 

***

Later, I go to the light-well. 

It is the hour when the day above is brightest. The tile-lined chimney is a tube of pale green glass, reaching up into the dark. Down its center falls a long, solid spear of grey light, illuminating the steam. I stand in the light. It is cold on my shoulders—so cold that my liver-lichen shrinks slightly, its edges curling inward.

A glider-bird drops down the shaft, its small, dark body twisting thrice in the narrow tile tube before it flattens its wings and sweeps through the pillars. I hear the *snap* of its beak as it takes a midge from the air three paces from my ear, then the soft *whisht-whisht* of its feathers as it spirals back up into the green tile toward the outer world.

I do not look up. The light from the well is too sharp; it hurts the wet, yellow globes of my eyes, which see only in shades of grease and shadow. Instead, I reach out and touch the wall of the chimney. 

The tiles are cold. Far up, I can feel the vibration of the sky. It is not like the vibration of the pipes, which is busy and rhythmic; the sky-shudder is vast, slow, and cold, like a great bronze plate being scraped across a floor of ice. 

I draw my *shoppe* from my belt. The elderwood is dark, stained with my own hand-grease until it looks like polished horn. I place the open end against the bronze plug of the third main line and blow.

*Hroo-oo-t.*

The sound is low, a fat, round bubble of air that travels three hundred paces through the copper veins of the room, vibrating under the feet of my brothers at the cisterns. 

*The green well is clear,* I am telling them. *The rain has stopped above. The siphons are breathing.*

From far away, through the floor-pans, comes the reply—a faint, rhythmic shudder of the water-film on the red tiles. Two taps. *Understood.*

***

A change is coming to my left leg.

It began three moons ago, after the great flood that choked the sixth shunt. I spent three days hip-deep in the cold, raw lime-slurry of the lower settling basin, clearing the bronze pins. When I came out, my left knee did not wish to bend. 

Now, when I touch the skin of my thigh, it does not feel like the warm, porous leather of my chest. It is cold. It has the hard, dry ring of baked tile. When I scrape my fingers against it, no grey scurf comes away; instead, my spade-nails leave only faint, white chalk-lines on the skin. 

The grey-rot has taken the bone. It is moving upward, toward my hip.

I am not afraid. The room is very large, and it requires many pillars. The arch beneath the fourth siphon is old; three of its bricks have cracked under the weight of the great cistern above, their red fragments falling into the pan below. The hole is exactly three cubits wide and one cubit deep—nearly my own measure.

I will work for perhaps one more season. I will clean the siphons, and I will let the crabs groom my shoulders under the yellow light of the tallow cups. I will listen to the Glass-Snouts as they rush through the dark ceramic veins, their long, pale tails slapping the wet clay.

And then, when my hip no longer swings, I will go to the arch. My brothers will be there. They will have the mortar-buckets ready—the sweet, smelling lime that burns the nostrils and makes the skin grow hard. They will help me into the gap. I will curl my stony knee to my chin, and they will lay the red flats around me, one by one, sealing the joints with the grey paste until there is only a single square of light left before my eyes.

Through that square, I will blow my last note into the pipes. 

And then they will place the final brick, and I will be still. I will no longer have to carry my own weight; the weight of the whole room—the six thousand shunts, the cedar walks, the green siphons, and the great, wet vaults—will rest upon my shoulder. 

And I will hold it up. I will hold it up forever in the warm, loud dark.
