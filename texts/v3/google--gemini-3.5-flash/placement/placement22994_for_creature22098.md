I am where the vertical becomes the stationary. 

I have lived on the north-north-west flank of Column Seventy-Two for sixty-four cycles of the great condensation. It is a place of no horizons, only the wall behind me, which is my mother and my bed, and the vast indigo void before me, which is my window.

To understand me, you must first unlearn the hurry of things with eyes. 

My face is a single, broad plate of obsidian, cold and black, polished by the greasy mist until it is as smooth as the surface of a deep-well. I have no eyes to blink, no nostrils to catch the sulfur of the steam-vents, no mouth to scream into the frozen drafts. Instead, my glass face is a listener. It is a transducer of the great, low-frequency hum of the world. 

When the dry mountain winds scrape against the columns miles above me, my faceplate vibrates. When the Great Sails drift through the middle dark, their thirty-foot antennae trailing through the electromagnetic currents like hair through grease, my glass face grows warm in slow, concentric rings. I do not see the valley; I *resonance* it. I map this vertical kingdom in shades of temperature, tension, and the silent, cold-blue pulse of the copper-lichen.

Below my torso, which is grey and ribbed like the bark of a drowned pine, my third limb—my root—has grown deep into a fissure in the basalt. It took thirty years for the iron-dense tip of that limb to find the core-warmth of the pillar, but now we are joined. I drink from the stone's static. It is a thin, dry food, tasting of rust and old frost, but it is enough to keep the pitch-sap of my joints from freezing solid.

Around me, the world moves in three speeds.

There is the *lightning-speed* of the Volt-Gleams. They are a blue, frantic crackle along my wrists. They are so fast, so small, that they seem to occur all at once—a scatter of hot salt that flares and is gone before my shoulder-joint can form the intention to twitch.

There is the *beast-speed* of the *Kret*, the heavy Lubrists. To me, they are like heavy, clockwork beetles. Every four or five years, a family of them will spiral up Column Seventy-Two, their octet of fat-slicked legs making a wet, rhythmic *thwip-thwip-thwip* that rattles my glass faceplate. They are the only things that touch me. They climb over my shoulders, their iron teeth scraping away the silver moss that grows in my crevices. They do not mean to be kind; they are simply hungry for the copper-hair, but I welcome them. Their heavy, tallow-smelling bodies are warm. For the three months it takes them to graze past my head, I am kept slightly less cold.

And then there is *my speed*.

Three cycles ago, the bellows-vine that runs beneath my left elbow began to whistle. 

It was a small sound—a high, thin hiss that broke the deep, stomach-thrumming bass of the valley. A segment of the vine, swollen with mineral salt, had cracked under the pressure of a sudden updraft. The steam was escaping sideways, freezing into a white plume of dry ice instead of rising to warm the upper lichen-forests.

The decision to repair it took me a year. 

A decision is not a thought that strikes like a spark; it is a gradual thickening of the sap in my upper chest. It is the slow turning of my attention from the distant, drifting Sails to the immediate, whistling gap at my side. 

Once the decision was complete, the movement began.

To lift my left hand—the hand that ends in three heavy, plier-like fingers of black iron—is a work of seasons. On the first spring, the pitch-sap in my shoulder-joint must be warmed by the mineral currents rising through my root. On the second summer, the triple-joint of my elbow must click—a dry, metallic sound that travels through my ribs like a hammer-blow. 

By the third year, my iron fingers reached the split segment. 

I did not feel pain for the vine, but I felt the interruption of the rhythm. The column here was growing cold. The copper-lichen was dying, turning from a rich, conductive bronze to a pale, brittle grey that could no longer hold the blue indicator rings of light. The circle was broken.

I closed my pliers on the damaged joint. The skin of the vine is tough, rubbery, and slick with grease, but my grip is the grip of the basalt itself. I held it still. From the deep well of my chest, where the hot acid of my core is stored, I drew up a mouthful of vinegar-breath. 

I did not spit; I breathed. A slow, hot sigh of sulfurous gas poured from the seam beneath my obsidian face, washing over the cracked vine. Under that heat, the salt-crust melted. The split fiber softened, running together like warm wax. 

For nine months, I held the seam closed, my arm locked in place, while my own hair—the long, silver fibers that grow from my crown—was drawn into the wet resin of the plant, weaving a patch that would outlast my own stay on this wall.

Now, the vine is healed. It contracts and expands once more with a deep, wet *whoosh* that vibrates through my chest-plates. The steam rises. The lichen is turning copper again. The Volt-Gleams have returned, running over my knucklebones in silent, blue-ringed celebration.

One day, perhaps fifty cycles from now, my root will go too deep. 

The basalt of Column Seventy-Two is hungry; it does not only give electricity, it takes bone. Slowly, the iron-sap in my lower joint is petrifying. The stone is rising through my leg, turning my grey bark into solid, green-veined basalt. 

I can feel it happening. It is a heavy, comfortable coldness that has already reached my waist. My second leg has not moved since the great frost of the fortieth cycle. 

I do not fear this. In this valley, there is no difference between the keeper of the machine and the machine itself. When the stone reaches my collarbone, my arms will lock into a permanent arch, holding the great bellows-vines in place for another thousand years after my glass face has gone completely dark.

But tonight, the air is very clear. The mists have parted in the high gaps, and the mile-wide river of air between the pillars is quiet.

I press my obsidian face plate against the wet stone of the column. 

I begin to vibrate. 

It is not a sound that can be heard by anything with ears. It is a slow, heavy *gonging* that begins in my chest, passes through my face, and enters the basalt. It travels down Column Seventy-Two, three miles into the dark, wet silt of the bottom. It crosses the stone floor and climbs Column Seventy-Three.

Ten days later, the answer comes back. It is a faint, metallic shudder from the south-east.

*We are here,* the stone says. *The wheel is turning.*

I quiet my chest. I settle back into the moss. I wait for the next winter.
