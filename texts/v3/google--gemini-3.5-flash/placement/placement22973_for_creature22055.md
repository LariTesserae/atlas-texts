### The Wind-Scourer

The leather groin-strap of my harness is stiff with fifteen winters of beetle-grease, and it smells, as I do, of rancid wintergreen and cold steam. 

I am suspended forty feet out from the eastern flank of Tower Nine. My boots—heavy birch-soled things with three pounds of soft lead poured into the toes—hang down into three thousand feet of grey, wool-thin mist. If I look straight down between my arches, the mist does not move. It has the greasy, still density of lard that has cooled in the pan. But if I look out, toward the five-mile empty gap between us and Tower Eight, I can see the wind-tracks: long, shining rifts where the high-altitude gale is shearing the clouds into grey ribbons. 

I am a Wind-Scourer. My trade is the prevention of *sun-clove*.

On these high plateaus, the wood is ancient and petrified, but it is not dead; it still has a memory of water and a muscle of its own. When the sun passes over a tower, its light is thin but fierce, warming the great grey bark until the wood-cells swell. But where the shadow of an iron boiler-gantry or a crane-track lies, the wood remains cold, damp, and contracted. If a shadow stays pinned to the same spot of bark for more than twenty minutes after the machine that cast it has moved—held there by the stubborn, lazy light of this plateau—the wood beneath the shadow stays small while the wood around it grows. 

The result is a sound like a small cannon-shot. The bark splits. The six-foot iron spikes holding the houses above are spat out of their sockets like cherry stones. A whole tier of dwellings, with their canvas hammocks and brass boiling-kettles, will drop into the slow, heavy drop below.

My job is to make the wind "lick" the shadows away before the split happens.

### The Tool and the Scour

Beside me, held by three tarred hemp lines that lead up to the gantry-engine on the platform above, is my *shutch*. It is a twelve-foot funnel of heavy, oiled canvas, stayed with split-birch ribs like the wing of an immense, dead bat. The wide mouth of the funnel hangs out in the clean, forty-mile gale of the void, catching the wind and forcing it down through a tapering copper throat.

I hold the nozzle—a heavy brass pipe with two wooden handles, polished yellow by my palms. 

"More lash!" I click up the line. The sibilants of our tongue are short because the wind eats vowels before they can cross a gantry. *“Ts-ch. Ts-ch.”*

The Slack-Man three hundred feet above me, sitting in his oily harness with his ear pressed to the guide-cable, hears the clicks through the wire. He releases the brake on the counterweight. The shutch drops six feet, its copper throat groaning.

I lean my weight into the brass handles. I point the nozzle at a dark, vertical smear on the flank of Tower Nine.

It is the shadow of the great water-conduit from the sixth level—a thick, iron cylinder that was turned thirty minutes ago but has left its grey ghost behind on the petrified cedar-skin. The shadow is stubborn today; the air is cold and the light is thick as resin. The ghost-conduit looks so real you could reach out and try to knock your knuckles against it, though there is nothing there but the pale, cold memory of iron.

I open the brass butterfly-valve on the nozzle.

The compressed gale hits the wood with a sound like *shasssssss*.

It is not a heat-blast; it is a friction-blast. The wind at this height is full of tiny, hard particles of carbon-dust and petrified pine-needles, dried till they are sharp as glass. When this grit-laden wind hits the grey shadow, it "rubs." 

You have to watch the colors. First, the dark, waxy grey of the shadow begins to turn silver at the edges. Then, the grain of the ancient timber beneath begins to show through—the tight, circular growth-rings of a tree that was old before the first brass was melted. I sweep the nozzle in slow, rhythmic figure-eights, five inches from the bark. The wind-sore is loud; it fills my fog-cups with white dust until I have to wipe them with my greasy sleeve.

Slowly, the shadow fades. It does not disappear like a light turned off; it thins like grease on a hot plate. It smears sideways, drifting three inches to the left, then dissolves into the general grey-brown of the Spire’s flank.

"Done," I click into the wire. *“K-lick.”*

The wind-scream inside the shutch dies to a whistle as I close the valve. My arms are trembling. The vibration of the compressed wind has gone deep into my radius and ulna, leaving my elbows feeling hot and loose.

### The Flesh and the Lag

I am thirty-eight. My father was a Wind-Scourer before me, and his shadow is still on the workshop wall in Tower Nine. 

It is not a metaphor. He died twelve years ago when a guide-line snapped during a line-storm, but if you go into the oil-shed on the third platform at four o'clock on a Tuesday afternoon, you will see a dark, slim shape sitting on the cedar-chest where he kept his spare leather straps. It is his wash. It is faint now—a pale, grey watermark that smells of raw turpentine—and it will take another thirty years of the plateau's wind to scrape him completely from the wood.

My own hands are beginning to go.

When I hold my fingers up against the blue-grey sky of the void, the outline is no longer sharp. The skin between my thumb and forefinger has the grey, semi-transparent look of the Drift-Lace. If I move my hand quickly, there is a tiny, greasy lag—a second hand, very thin and pale, that follows my fingers three inches behind, like a wet thumb-print on a windowpane.

The doctors in the upper gantries call it the *Grip-Dwindle*. They say it comes from the constant proximity to the cold wood and the stubborn light. They say that if you spend enough time hanging against the three-mile columns, your body forgets how to be "here" and begins to be "then."

My wife, Mara, does not like to touch my hands in the dark. She says they feel like wet paper that has been left in the grease-pan. She works the lint-webs three levels up, her fingers always green-stained from the vinegar-acid they use to clean the hemp before it goes to the spindle.

We lived in a house of three spikes. 

It was a small place, no wider than the span of my arms, bolted directly into the bark of the southern cove. We had to pay the Spire-Master two feet of copper water-wire every season just for the right to keep our iron pins driven into the heartwood. 

We had a child there—a boy named Col. He was born with the long toes of the Spire-dwellers, his little feet curving naturally to clamp around the birch-mesh of our floor-gantries. 

Because of the lazy gravity, we had to keep him tethered. A baby here does not crawl; he floats if he is not weighted. We had a harness for him made of dog-skin, with a sliding brass ring that ran along a three-yard rope tied to the main floor-spike. If he slipped through the gaps in the birch-veneer while we were sleeping, he did not fall fast. He would drift down through the floor-grate and hang beneath our house like a grey bird, swinging slowly in the cold draught from the low plains, crying in his high, dry baby-speech until I could reach down with a long boat-hook and pull his harness-ring back up through the wood-lathes.

He died of the *lung-glaze* when he was three, his chest filling with the cold condensation-grease that drifts off the copper wires. 

We did not drop him into the void, as some of the lower-tier families do. We took him down on the coal-trolley to the plateau floor—my first time on the horizontal in ten years. My head swam; the flat ground felt as if it were tilting forty degrees to the left, trying to slide me into the sky.

We laid him in a shallow trench cut into the hard, charcoal-grey peat, five miles from the base of Tower Nine. We covered him with three layers of oil-soaked lichen and a heavy slab of fossilized bark to keep the wind from rolling him away. When we stood up, our boots felt like lead-weights that had been welded to the world. I did not look up at the Spire; I looked at my boots, which were sinking three inches into the dark, resilient moss that smells of old vinegar.

### The Void-Weaver’s Thread

A sound comes through the copper cable—not a click, but a long, musical *thrumm* that rises in pitch until it vibrates in the iron plates of my breast-harness.

*Thrumm-clink.*

It is the warning signal from the outer watch-gantry. A Drift-Lace colony is passing.

I look out into the grey-blue gulf. Five miles away, the outline of Tower Eight is disappearing behind a massive, semi-transparent sheet of silver-grey. It looks like a wedding veil that has been dropped from the stars, three miles high and two miles wide, drifting with the slow, liquid majesty of an iceberg in grease-water.

As it passes between the towers, the yellow steam from our lower boilers hits it. The Lace does not burn; it drinks. The millions of microscopic copper cilia on its margins begin to beat in long, green-glowing waves. The entire sheet lights up with a cold, chemical phosphorescence, transforming the dark void between the spires into a luminous, emerald hall.

In the green light, I see the spider.

It is a Void-Weaver, and it is huge—the size of an eight-oared land-boat, but its body is so thin and hollow it has no more weight than a dry dry-fossil leaf. It is clinging to the leading edge of the Drift-Lace, its six straw-like legs reaching out into the open sky.

As I watch, it lifts its abdomen and casts.

A single thread of silk—fine as a spider’s hair but shining like polished brass in the green light—shoots out from its spinneret. The wind catches it, carrying it across the five-mile gap toward Tower Nine. The spider does not hurry; it hangs in the lazy air, its blind, grey-scaled head turned toward our noise.

The thread hits the outer gantry of our tower, fifty feet above me, with a dry *snip*. The silk is sticky with some glue that smells of wintergreen and old fat. Instantly, the spider releases its grip on the Drift-Lace and lets itself go into the void.

It does not fall. It sails.

Using its long legs to ball the silk or let it out, it glides down the five-mile line with a jerky, slow-motion grace. To see a creature that large moving through the blue sky with no wings, suspended only by a thread you can barely see, is to feel the smallness of our brass engines and our iron spikes.

It passes fifty feet from my harness. I can see the grey-green scales on its flanks, the dry, blind pits where its eyes should be, and the tiny scutter-beetles that live in the hinges of its legs, scraping the grease-crusts from its carapace.

It does not look at me. To the Weaver, I am only a dark, vibrating spot on the petrified wood—a shadow that has not yet been rubbed away.

### The Shift-End

The sun is dipping now. It does not go down with a red flare; it simply sinks behind the level iron-line of the distant horizon, leaving the sky a cold, deep indigo that has no end.

The shadows of the towers are stretching out. Tower Nine’s shadow is forty miles long now, a dark road of grey light that lies across the Lichen-Plains like the finger of a giant.

On the platforms above, the steam-whistles begin to blow. Their scream is thin and dry in the high air, but they beat against the heartwood, setting up a rhythmic vibration that goes down into my heels. *Chug-shudder. Chug-shudder.*

My backup-man clicks the wire: *“Home. Home.”*

I hook my brass notch into the guide-cable. I release my waist-stay. 

As the steam-winch begins to pull my harness up the three hundred feet of dark wood, I look at my right hand. The grey lag is still there, trailing my skin by three inches in the cold twilight. 

I know what will happen. In ten years, the scrap-iron brush will feel lighter in my hand. In fifteen, the wind-nozzle will pass through my palm without my fingers closing. One evening, Mara will look at the canvas hammock and see only a grey, smudged shape that smells of wintergreen and coal-gas, fading slowly as the forty-mile wind licks the corner of the room.

But tonight, there is water.

I can hear it. On the copper wires hanging beneath our floor-gantry, the cold fog is condensing, bead by bead. Out of the darkness, five thousand wires are dripping into our green-stained cistern with a sound like a million small brass clocks, all ticking together, telling the wood that it is still alive, and telling us that we have another night before the iron forgets.
