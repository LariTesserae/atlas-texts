To find myself in this young, raw world, I must leave the hanging canopies and the clicking air. I must go down—past the grease-slicked trails of the Lithe-Greys, past the vibrating domes of the Marrow-Stones, and even below the melting, grey sheets of the Carrion-Mats. 

I am down where the basalt is cold.

I do not have a name that can be spoken, for there are no voices in this rift that use breath for words. If the Lithe-Greys have a tap for me in their finger-language, it is a single, heavy stroke: the sound of a wet stone settling into a deep slot. 

I am **the Wedge-Lurker**, or simply **the Squeeze**.

***

### The Shape of the Squeeze

I am not fast. In a valley where a plant can grow to the height of a man in an afternoon, my slowness is an ancient, deliberate heresy. I am built of grey, mineralized cartilage and dense, dry sponge, wrapped in a skin as tough and oil-less as cured steer-hide. My body is entirely flat—a broad, wedge-shaped spade, perhaps five feet long but barely two inches thick when my chambers are empty.

I have no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the black fractures of the basalt. I have no legs, for there is nowhere to walk where the walls are vertical scalpels. Instead, my undersurface is a solid, ribbed pad of sensitive pressure-receptors. I do not "hear" the din of the canyon; I feel it as a series of distinct pressures. 

To me:
*   The sub-bass hum of the Marrow-Stones is a warm, rhythmic massage that keeps my skin from sticking to the basalt.
*   The dry, metallic clicking of the Castanet swarm is a faint, prickly static, like hot sand blowing across my back.
*   The sharp *tink-tink-tink* of the Lithe-Greys' obsidian tools is a clear, ringing pulse that tells me exactly where the stone is weakest.

But my true sense—my only constant preoccupation—is the weight of the mountain. I feel the immense, silent tension of the canyon walls. I know when a cliff-face is tired. I know when the young earth is about to squeeze its jaws shut.

***

### The Drink and the Swell

My life is a cycle of thirst and pressure.

I spend my dry phases sliding. Coated in a dry, powdery dust of ground basalt, I search for the fresh, hair-thin fractures that open when the canyon shears. Because I am flat and flexible, I can worm my way into a crack that would pinch the fingers off a Lithe-Grey. I slide in, pushing deeper and deeper into the dark, where the air is silent and smells of cold iron and old steam.

Here, in the deepest wrinkles of the basalt, is where the sweet condensation collects. It does not pool in wide cups here; it runs down the inner seams of the rock in silent, icy threads. 

Once I am wedged deep into a fracture, I open my lateral margins. I have dozens of small, muscular siphons along my edges, like the gill-slits of a buried fish. I drink. I draw the cold, distilled water into my internal chambers.

And as I drink, the magic of my slow biology begins. 

The dry, compressed sponge of my interior does not merely wet itself; it undergoes a rapid, chemical hydrate-expansion. I swell. But because I am trapped between two walls of unyielding volcanic glass, my swelling becomes force.

I exert tons of hydraulic pressure per square inch.

***

### The Stone-Breaker

I am the silent partner of the rift’s transformation. 

As I expand, the groan of the basalt begins—a deep, low-frequency screech that travels through the bones of the mountain. The Lithe-Greys above cannot hear this with their nasal-slits, but they feel it through their fingers on the vine-mat. They know what it means. They gather above my crevice, chattering and drumming their pads in anticipation.

I push. The crack widens. A eighth of an inch. A quarter. An inch. 

The basalt, brittle and stressed, cannot tolerate this internal leverage. It resists, it groans, and then—*crack*.

The mountain shears. A three-ton slab of obsidian-sharp stone calves off the canyon wall and plunges into the abyss. 

Up above, the Lithe-Greys shriek and swing toward the sound. The collapse has exposed a fresh vein of sweet, starchy vine-roots, and more importantly, it has created a scree of brand-new, razor-sharp obsidian flakes. They scramble down to harvest the glass, tapping the vines, spilling the green sap, and dropping their tools when they are full.

But down in the newly widened fracture, I have achieved my purpose. 

The collapse has cleared away the old, dead block. The sudden release of pressure allows me to deflate, expelling the water—now thick with dissolved minerals—back into the deep channels. I contract, becoming thin and dry once more, slipping easily out of the expanding crack before the aggressive roots of the purple vines can reach down to find me.

***

### The Cycle of the Crevice

I do not die easily. The Carrion-Mats cannot dissolve me; my skin is too chemically inert, and I live too deep for their soft, larval bodies to follow. If a vine-root does find me while I am swollen, I simply release my water in a sudden, violent squirt, contracting instantly and falling deeper into the mountain's throat.

I have seen—or rather, felt—generations of the fast things pass. I have felt the Castanet swarms grow thick and die, their shells rattling down into my crack like dry leaves, providing a rich, acidic loam that I use to grease my next slide. I have felt the Lithe-Greys grow old, their tappings slowing down until they fall from the vines to be swept away by the Melt-Floor.

They are the foam on the river of this young world. I am the bed through which it runs.

Without me, the tectonic wounds of the rift would heal too quickly. The settling stone would lock together; the cracks would seal; the sweet water would find no path to the dark. I am the wedge that keeps the mouth of the earth open, ensuring the chaos remains loud, wet, and beautifully, terribly alive.
