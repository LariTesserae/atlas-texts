The waking is not a separation. 

It begins as a thinning of the warmth. First, the weight of the leg draped across my shoulder shifts; then, the wet, waxy peel of another torso sliding away from my ribs. We are five or six in this niche—I do not look to count, for my eyes are only small glimmers of white water beneath my brow—but I know each by the smell of their grease. One is sour like boiled iron; another has the thin, sweet tallow of the young; a third carries the cold, chalky smell of a skin that has begun to dry.

I slide from the heap. The stone beneath my horn-pads is wet, greasy with the red-slime trail of the trilobites that have scraped it clean during our sleep. As I stand, my ribs expand, pulling in the heavy, hot air of the lower terrace. The sulfur is thick today. It catches in the back of my throat like warm sand, but my lungs are broad—a great, hollow barrel of bone—and they drink it without coughing.

I do not hear the world with my ears. The air is a scream of gliders and the clicking of a million crabs, a useless, high rattle that my head has long since sealed against. Instead, I feel the city through my soles. 

Below me, three levels down, the great drainage vent is shuddering. *Thrum-thrum. Thrum-thrum.* A heavy, wet pulse. It tells me the water is high in the mud-pits today. The clay will be thin, runny as blood.

I do not walk; I slide. 

My feet, wide and flat like the spade-end of an oar, do not lift far from the obsidian. I keep my knees bent, my center low, allowing the tallow on my thighs to brush the narrow blocks of the passage. The street is barely wider than my hips. When I meet another citizen coming up from the wells, we do not stop. We do not greet. We simply turn our shoulders, pressing chest-to-chest, our grease-slick skins squealing softly as we glide past one another in a lubricated, heavy friction. I feel the hard, spade-claws of their left hand graze my ribs. It does not cut. The tallow is thick; the skin beneath is like cured leather. 

I am going down. 

My joints are stiffening. Year by year, the cartilage in my hips has grown less like wet river-clay and more like the basalt itself. I can no longer climb the high, five-story terraces to stack the heavy obsidian blocks. My hands, once wide and clean for slapping mud, have grown thick at the knuckles. The black keratin of my claws is chipped and blunt, worn down by forty seasons—or what would be seasons, if the steam ever changed—of digging.

So I have come to the nursery-wells.

Here, the air is not leaden twilight; it is thick, yellow fog. The steam rises directly from the great rift-cracks in the basalt floor, carrying the sound of the earth's deep boiling. It is so hot that my tallow runs in thin, clear grease-tears down my flanks, pooling in the hollows of my collarbones.

The floor is not stone here. It is a deep, grey slurry of volcanic mud, kept warm by the vents below. In this mud, they are buried.

I sit on a flat slab of raw basalt, my splayed feet dangling into the warm grey muck. Nearby, three other elders sit in the same silence. We do not speak. We have no words to waste on the air. But now and then, one of us will press their forehead against the basalt bench, sending a low, rattling hum through the stone: *Here. Still here. The mud is good.*

A young female comes down the vertical chute. She does not carry her infant; she carries a sac. It is the size of a clay water-pot, dull pink and soft as lard, glistening with the birth-grease. She does not look at us. She does not look at the sac. She drops it into the softest part of the mud-basin, where the steam hissed loudest, and then she turns and slides back up the chute, her splayed feet scratching a slow rhythm against the glass walls.

The sac does not float. It sinks until only its round, pale dome is visible above the grey ooze.

I lean forward. My spade-hands, heavy and wide, reach into the mud. 

To those from the upper worlds of thin air and dry grass, the child would look like nothing but a lump of dough. It has no shape yet. Its bones are not bones; they are gristle, soft as the land-anemone cords we use for ropes. If left to itself, the weight of the mud would flatten it into a dry plate, or the steam would warp its limbs into useless, twisted threads.

I pull the small, pale mass onto my lap. 

My fingers, thick and blind, begin their work. This is what I am now: a shaper of the stone-dwellers.

I start with the hands. The child’s fingers are currently round, fleshy little tubers. I take the first digit between my thick thumb and forefinger. I do not squeeze; I draw. I pull the cartilage outward, flattening the tip, pressing my own black spade-claw against its soft skin to leave a dent where the hard keratin will eventually grow. I widen the palm, pushing the small wrist back until the joint is stiff and square, built for the heavy leverage of the clay-slap.

*Thrum.* 

I press my forehead to the child’s skull. Inside, its tiny chest rattles in response—a weak, watery vibration that barely travels up my bone. But it is there. It understands the grease and the stone.

Next, I work the head. I press the flats of my forearms against the sides of its face, pushing the skull into a low, sloping wedge. I flatten the brow, drawing the thick ridges of fat down over the vestigial eyes so that when the child first crawls into the lightless alleys, the flying glass-slivers of the basalt-storms will slide off its yellow skin without blinding it.

Finally, the feet. I take the tiny, round heel and pull it wide. I smooth the sole with the ball of my thumb, hardening the skin by rubbing it with the mineral-crust I scrape from my own shins. It must be a single pad. If toes grow here, the razor-glass of the basin floor will catch between them and tear the foot to the bone.

The child does not cry. It has no voice for crying, only the low, wet rattle in its chest that grows slightly warmer as my grease transfers to its skin.

I hold it for a long time, molding, smoothing, pressing. I am not making something beautiful. I am making something that will slide. I am making something that can sleep five-deep in a stone niche without suffocating. I am making something that can eat the red slime of the walls and never know hunger.

When the shape is set—when the cartilage has begun to resist the pressure of my thumbs, holding the wide, flat lines of the city—I slide it back into the warm mud-bath. It will lie there for three hundred steam-turns, growing its first waxy tallow-coat, until its bones are hard enough to stand.

Beside me, the old one with the chalky skin has stopped moving.

I did not feel him die. There was no struggle, no sound. But the hum has left his side of the stone bench. His skin, once grey and greasy, has turned the flat, powdery white of cold wood-ash. The tallow has stopped flowing from his flanks; it has congealed into hard, yellow flakes.

Two young builders come down the chute. They do not carry ropes or tools. 

They lift the old one by his heels and shoulders. His body is stiff, molded forever into the seated curve of the shaper’s bench. They carry him to the central vent, where the steam does not hiss but roars—a white, sulfurous column that rises from the very throat of the basin.

They do not pray. They do not sing. They simply slide him into the horizontal fissure at the base of the vent.

I watch—with my skin, with my feet—as the heat takes him. 

The steam is so hot there that the greasy tallow on his body ignites with a brief, green hiss. Then the flesh melts. The cartilage, softened by the deep-earth water, dissolves into the mineral-slurry. Within three days, his bones will be nothing but white lime, carried up by the water-sheets to coat the obsidian blocks of the upper level.

Next season, the children we are molding today will scrape that white lime from the walls with their spade-hands. They will grind it between their bone-plates. They will eat him. He will become their ribs, their claws, the tallow on their thighs.

I reach back into the grey mud. My spade-hand finds another pink, soft sac.

I pull it to my chest. I feel the weak, wet vibration deep inside its gristle. I press my thumb to its palm, widening the joint, starting the slide.

We are the Basin. We do not leave. We do not change. We only turn, over and over, in the wet, black dark.
