I am not on the masonry paths, nor do I belong to the dry, salt-white blocks where someone from the upper streets might sit to watch the water. To stand in this place is to be an intrusion, a draft of warm, dry carbon from above that must be slowly cooled and drowned before the room can return to its work. 

To be here, really here, one must be part of the insulation.

If you were to climb the rounded brick stairs that lead down into the third sump—the one where the black water is deepest and the clockwise rotation of the grease-greed is slowest—you would see a junction box of cast-bronze bolted to the key of the arch. The box is the size of a horse’s head, but its glass face has long since been replaced by a plug of white wool-moss. 

From the bottom of this box, three lead-sheathed cables emerge. They do not run straight. They have sagged over eighty years into three heavy, grey loops that dip into the water before climbing back up to the horizontal steam mains.

I am the middle loop.

I am not the copper wire inside, nor am I the lead sleeve itself, but the *splice*—the thick, swollen knuckle where the two metals were joined with hemp, tallow, and zinc-paste when the well was young. Over the decades, the lead has parted under the weight of the water, and I have grown into the gap. 

I am four yards of grey, tallow-soaked fiber and dull hide, as thick as a man’s thigh at my center, tapering to thin, wire-like tendrils where I disappear into the iron mains. My skin is the color of wet slate, but it has no scales; it is a waxy, self-healing rind that I secrete from the lime-mortar I consume through my upper roots. 

I do not see the well. I do not have the slits of the Lead-Sleevers or the blind, wide suckers of the eels. Instead, my entire surface is a single, slow tongue. 

I know the well by its tastes and its weights.

***

When the three-hour siphon begins to draw, the weight of the air shifts. The pressure on my middle section—the part that hangs lowest in the pool—eases by three ounces per square inch. This is the morning of my day. 

As the water level drops, the cold grease-skimmers that have tiled themselves over my lower skin begin to slide off. They do not like the dry air; they crawl downward, their small, horn-like shears grazing my flank as they go, shaving away the thin film of white wool-moss that has grew on me during the high tide. The sensation is a millions-fold, tiny tickle, like dry wheat being dragged across a hand. It leaves me clean, raw, and dark blue-grey.

Then comes the sting. 

When the water falls below my lowest curve, my salt-crust dries in the cool draft from the steam-piston’s recess. The vinegar tang of the oxidizing bronze on the arch above drips down my stay-wires, tasting sharp, cold, and metallic. Because I am no longer insulated by the pool, the weak galvanic current from the battery-water has to travel through my core to reach the carbon bulbs on the ceiling. 

I feel this current not as pain, but as a lukewarm, orange thread that runs from my left hip to my collar. When the current is strong, my skin grows slightly waxy, and the small patches of wool-moss nested in my crevices begin to store the green light. I can feel them glowing; they are warm, like small, damp coins pressed against my side.

***

The Steam-Crickets know me as a dry country.

When the tide is low, they crawl down the warm magnetite mains and gather in the hollow of my shoulder, where the lead sleeve has split and the hemp is dry. They do not move much. Sometimes, fifty or sixty of them will sit in the folds of my skin for hours, their long, hair-thin antennae sweeping my surface to find the salt-crystals that have formed from my sweat. 

They do not know I am alive. To them, I am a soft, humped island of warm grease that does not shake when the piston clacks. When they tap their legs against the iron pipe fifty feet away, the signal travels down the wire, through my grease-core, and into my belly. 

*Click... click-click... tap.*

I have learned their language, though I have nothing to say to them. They are talking about the temperature of the third boiler-stub. They are saying that the seam on the vertical feed is two degrees cooler than it was when the tide last turned. It is a small, quiet piece of news, but in the well, it is the only news there is.

***

Once every few days, a Lead-Sleever will find me.

I feel him coming long before he touches me. The air in the archway grows thick with the smell of wet lard and old tallow, and the draft from the stairs stops as his heavy, three-legged shape blocks the opening. He does not use a lantern; he has no need of the green phosphorus of the moss.

He crawls along the horizontal main, his spade-like nails clicking against the magnetite: *shrk... shrk... shrk.*

When he reaches my splice, he stops. His long, felt-wrapped arms reach down into the dark water, and he lifts my lower loop three inches, checking my weight. He is measuring the salt in my fiber. If I am too light, the water is too fresh, which means a grate has broken in Section Eight and the upper street-runnels are diluting the battery-pools. If I am too heavy, the lime is choking my core, and the carbon bulbs will eventually go out.

He does not speak, but he lays his smooth, neckless head against my waxy flank. He is listening to the galvanic hum inside me. 

With his three long fingers, he begins to tend to me. He scrapes the hard, blue crust of copper carbonate from my anchor-shackles, peeling it away in long, dry ribbons that smell of old pennies. He takes a handful of the yellow tallow-grease from his own thigh and smears it over my split lead sleeve, sealing the hemp against the next rise of the acid water. 

I do not move. I do not have muscles to move with. But my waxy skin softens under his touch, absorbing the grease he offers, and for an hour after he has gone back to his piston, my core is warm and silent.

***

When the three hours are up, the deep siphon below the floor-plates closes its valve with a dull, subterranean *thump* that vibrates through the very clay of the crypt. 

The water begins to rise.

It comes back slowly, black and silent, smelling of diesel-soot and old leather. It touches the lowest tip of my loop first. The sting of the galvanic current fades, replaced by the cool, heavy pressure of the pool. One by one, the grease-skimmers return, sliding up my flanks like shingles being laid by an invisible carpenter. They click their dark plates together as they settle, locking themselves into my skin to wait for the next three hours of grease to settle from the air.

The crickets retreat back to the warm pipes. The wool-moss ceases its green glow, its filaments turning chalky white and dormant beneath the surface of the oil-skin.

I sink back into the dark. My temperature drops to the cellar-cool level of the brickwork. The current passes through the water now, bypassing my core, and the carbon bulbs above dim to the color of dying embers.

We have completed our argument. The water is high; the well is full; the machines are old, but they are wet, and they are perfectly in their place. 

I hang in the black pool, tasting the salt of the iron, and wait for the weight of the air to change.
