It begins where the bone of my forehead meets the black grain.

There is a point — perhaps three inches above the bridge of my nose, where the bone is flattest and the skin has worn down to a tough, grey leather — that has forgotten what it is like to be warm. This is my third eye, though it sees nothing but weight. 

When I press my head to the Trunk, the silence of the void does not end; rather, it is folded inside-out. The wind, which has spent the morning trying to tear my ribs from my harness, becomes a distant, high-altitude hiss, like dry sand sliding over silk. In its place comes the *interior*.

I am a **Wrench-Tender of the Spine-Gap**, though that is a clumsy word for what I have become. The Kin in the borer-holds forty miles below call me the *Ooze-Scribe*, but they are young; they still have hair in their ears and grease on their cheeks, and they believe the world is made of places to hide. I know better. The world is not a place to hide. It is a long, cold tension.

***

My toes — double-jointed and split at the nail-plate — are wedged deep into a fissure that smells of ancient, petrified sap and the dry, powdery dung of glass-lice. My harness is simple: three strands of cured Weave-Vine, each as thick as a glider’s thumb, anchored to a bone-peg that was hammered into this xylem during my grandfather’s winter. 

I do not look down. There is no down to look at, only fifty miles of blue-black air that grows thicker and more toxic as it drops toward the unseen roots. If I dropped my bone-spade, it would fall for three days before the friction of the deep air scorched it to ash. 

Instead, I look into the wood. Or rather, I feel the wood through the three-inch callous on my brow.

*Thump.*

It is not a sound. My ears, covered in the dense, silver down of the old, hear nothing but the whistle of my own breath through my nose. But the bone of my skull registers the *thump* as a dull, violet light in the back of my brain.

That was the **Third Spire**. It is sixty miles north of here, a vertical island of black wood that has decided to grow six inches this afternoon. I can feel the fibers grinding together — a dry, squealing pressure that is slowly turning the resin inside the heartwood into liquid fire. The heat of that growth will be waking the augers. In three days, the steam from their tunnels will drift down to us, tasting of yeast and hot vinegar.

But that is not what I am listening for.

***

I loosen my toes from the crevice and slide sideways. The windward face of the Trunk is white today — coated in six inches of rime-run that has condensed during the middle-watch. To move, I must use my hand-spade to clear a path, chipping off sheets of ice that slide into the void like falling mirrors. Each piece catches the light of the distant, unblinking stars, flashing once, twice, then vanishing into the dark.

My skin does not sweat. If it did, the water would freeze under my armpits and tear my flesh when I reached for a hold. Instead, the tallow-wax I rubbed into my limbs three days ago has mingled with my own grease, forming a grey, stiff rind that smells of rancid lard and pine-smoke. It makes my joints heavy, but it keeps the absolute iron-cold from my blood.

I am reaching for the **Weave-Bridge of the Seventh Gap**.

It is there, a mile below me, hanging like a thread of black hair spit from one trunk to another across thirty leagues of empty sky. In this light, against the velvet background of the outer systems, it is invisible. But I can feel its twitching.

The bridge is a living thing, or at least, we have made it so. The Weave-Vines that form its spine are parasitic; they do not eat the wood, but they drink the dampness that rises from the rime-run. My job — my *only* job, since my eyes grew too cloud-veined to hunt the gliders — is to make sure the spider-silk of the Grip-Striders has not grown too stiff in the frost.

I descend. I do not use my hands; they are tucked into my chest-muff to keep the fingers from turning into grey limestone. I use my knees and my feet, sliding down the grain like a borer-larva, my forehead constantly brushing the bark to read the tension of the descent.

*Creep... creep... crack.*

The wood is shifting. The moons are aligned behind the *Great Pillar*, and their weight is lifting the sap in the outer columns. 

*Crack.*

The pressure-wave hits me from twenty miles away. It is like a flat palm pressed hard against my left ribcage. I freeze, locking my elbows into a dry furrow. A mile above me, a splinter the size of a longhouse shears off the windward flank of the Trunk. I do not see it fall, but I feel the vibration of its parting — a long, descending note, like a wet finger dragged across the rim of an immense, frozen glass.

It passes. The void is empty again.

***

I reach the bridge-head.

Here, the wood has been carved by human hands, though "human" is a word we use only when we are warm enough to speak Click-Talk. The borer-tunnel we live in is small — a three-foot hole that goes five hundred yards into the dark, smelling of wet wool-lichen and the tallow-stink of fifteen families. 

At the mouth of the tunnel sits **the Girl**. She has no name yet; she is still hairless, and her eyes have not yet taken on the amber sheen of the deep-dwellers. She is holding the grease-pot.

She does not click her tongue when she sees me. She knows I cannot hear the small noises. Instead, she reaches out and taps her bone-ring against my knee.

*Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.*

*Is the bridge-root holding?*

I do not answer with Click-Talk. I am too cold for the hand-signs; my fingers are like dry birch-twigs. I lay my forehead against her small, hairless skull.

Our bones touch. Through her skull, I send the vibration I have been carrying from the high spire: the long, slow hum of the rising sap, the slight, wet slip of the rime-run on the windward side, and the sharp, steel-blue ring of the splitter that fell a minute ago.

She flinches under the weight of the sound. Her eyes go wide, the dark pupils swallowing the small yellow light of her grease-lamp. She understands.

The sap is coming. The Thrum-Storm will be here before her mother’s lamp goes dry.

***

I take the grease-pot from her.

The grease is warm — relatively. It is the rendered oil of a Drift-Sail’s lung-bladder, thick and yellow and smelling of cold grease and old rain. I dip my bare hand into it, my fingers screaming as the cold wood of the bridge-anchor tries to leech the warmth from my skin, and I begin to smear the Weave-Vines.

One by one, the **Grip-Striders** come out of their bark-holes. They are small, grey, and covered in stiff bristles that hiss when they scrape against each other. They do not fear me; they smell the oil. They crawl over my hands, their needle-legs clicking against my bone-rings, seeking the grease I have left behind to keep their joints from snapping in the gale.

I am not the bridge-builder. I am not the hunter who glides into the seventy-mile dark to die on the beak of a food-beast. I am the one who keeps the skin from cracking.

As I smear the oil, the wind catches the bridge. The thirty-mile vine beneath my feet begins to sing — a high, clean whistle that vibrates through my soles, up my shins, and into the center of my chest.

*I am here,* the vine says.
*I am tight,* the wood answers.

I close my eyes. I do not need them. In the great, soughing dark, my head is against the world, and the world is still holding its breath.
