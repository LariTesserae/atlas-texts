I am where the water meets the mud, but mostly I am where the mud meets the bone. 

I do not have a front or a back in the way of things that must run or chase. I am a long, pale drift—five paces of wet, translucent lard and soft cartilage, settled in the shallow rim of a red stone pool. My eyes died inside my skull three million years ago, sealed over by a smooth, milky skin that does not miss the light. The white glare of the sky is nothing to me but a warm weight on my back, a greasy silver film that I feel only when the mist thickens enough to weep down my flanks in braids.

I do not need to see. The world is not a picture; it is a weight, a texture, and a hum.

My ribs are lined with three hundred fluid-filled cups—tiny, sensitive pits that lie open to the water and the silt. Through them, the basin is perfectly clear. I can feel the tiny, pewter-colored scrapers three hills away, their magnetite teeth rasping against the granite—*skritch, skritch*—like dry fingernails on slate. I can feel the iron-bound almonds of the mud-keepers beneath me, their internal pendulums rocking in their shells: *chink, chink, chink*. If they were to stop, the grey mud around my belly would set like plaster within a single arc of the sun, pinning me to the floor. But they do not stop. They have not stopped since the mountains were high. Their small, heavy heartbeats keep the floor of my world soft and wet, a thick, warm soup that cradles my weight.

I sink my flat, paddle-like limbs into this slurry. They are no longer legs; they are anchors, wide spades of skin and pale gristle that I use to hollow out a nest in the silt. It is a good nest. It has the curve of the granite dome beneath it, a slow-sloping bowl that has been ground down until it fits the sag of my belly like a mold.

Every few hours, I draw in the air. 

It is a slow, heavy labor. I open my soft-rimmed mouth, and the warm steam of the basin pours in, tasting of iron, sour mud, and the leathery rot of liverwort. My throat-sac—a vast, ribbed balloon of bruised purple skin—begins to swell. It grows until it is nearly as large as the rest of my body, lifting my head slightly from the water, making me buoyant, light, almost empty. 

I hold it there, the warm, wet gas of the world filling my chest, while the solar drifters pass slowly through the mist above me. I cannot see them, but I can feel their long, invisible tentacles trailing through the water like threads of warm grease, tasting the same air I breathe.

Then, I drop my chin. I lay my soft, flat throat directly against the red granite at the pool's edge.

I release the air. 

I do not make a sound that the air can carry. The air here is too thick, too wet to hold the weight of what I have to say. Instead, I drive the breath down through my jaw, directly into the iron-red stone. 

*Thrumm-ub.*

The granite dome acts as a soundboard, carrying the vibration deep into the roots of the world. The pulse travels down through the smooth slopes, beneath the silt-beds, through the ancient, buried foundations of the old highlands where the mountains once stood. It is a long, heavy chord that takes five seconds to reach the edges of my pool, ten seconds to cross the wide silt channels, and twenty seconds to climb the next dome.

I wait, my jaw still pressed to the slick stone, my skin registration-sensitive.

The world answers.

First comes the bounce-back from the granite itself: the "sight" of my valley. I feel the smooth, curve-backed beasts of the domes rising out of the water; I feel the sharp, cold needle-points of the iron reeds standing like iron pins in the mud; I feel the deep, slow-moving channel to the south where the silt has gathered twenty feet deep, moving toward the ocean at the speed of a fingernail’s growth.

Then, miles away, through five leagues of solid, red stone, comes the reply.

An electronic itch? No, it is a low, rolling rumble that vibrates up through my left paddle-bone. It is my sister-in-the-silt, resting in the shallows under the grey mist of the northern flats. Her throat is larger than mine; her voice is a thicker, gravelly slide that tells me the northern domes are wet today, slick with the weeping condensation that has been falling since the world went quiet. 

Behind her, further still, another voice joins—a thin, distant scratch through the granite from someone we have never met, someone who has lived for five thousand years in a low basin where the water is three inches deeper than ours.

We do not speak of hunger. We do not speak of fear. There is nothing to flee; there is nothing to hunt. When the tide of grey silt rises, it brings with it the tiny, slow-drifting bodies of the scrapers that have lost their grip on the domes, the spores of the liverwort, the soft, decaying wealth of a world that is dissolving into itself. I only have to open my wide, soft mouth, and the basin feeds me. 

We speak only to say: *We are here. The stone is still round. The water is still warm.*

The sun behind the white mist is turning from silver to a dull, bruised copper. It is the only change we know. The temperature of the mud does not fall; the air does not cool. The solar drifters will sink a few feet as the light dims, and the iron reeds will begin to glow with their faint, cold green electricity, discharging the day’s static into the dark water around my tail. 

I will close my mouth. I will let my pale body sink three inches deeper into the warm, thixotropic silt. The mud-keepers will continue their *chink-chink-chink*, a million small clocks that have forgotten how to tell any time but *now*. 

I will lay my head upon the iron-red stone, and I will listen to the mountains being ground into mud, one micron, one breath, one thousand years at a time. Of all the things that have ever been, we are the ones who remained to see the end of the work, and we find it very soft, very heavy, and very good.
