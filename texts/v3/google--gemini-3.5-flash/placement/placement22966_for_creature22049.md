The day does not begin with light. It begins with the weight of the water.

I am suspended forty meters above the pale floor, hanging in the narrow slot between Column 117 and Column 118. My hammock, woven from the coarse, orange heart-fibers of the air-plants, is stretched so tight that my hips are pressed flat against the western face of the basalt. I can feel the cool, deep heart of the stone through my skin, while my front is already warming from the rising thermal drafts.

First comes the sigh. 

It is the wind from the lower flats, five miles down, pushing its way up into the root-cracks of the forest. It carries the smell of wet sulfur, starch, and the clean, dead rot of the Deep-Silt Moss. Then, the dampness. The fog does not fall from the violet sky; it rises like a thick, hot breath from the bottom. It settles on my face first—not as a mist, but as a greasy, heavy moisture that immediately beads on my oil-slick skin.

I open my eyes. There is nothing to see but the vertical line. 

To my left, three inches away, is the satin-smooth gray-purple of Column 117. To my right, the identical face of 118. If I look straight up, the slot narrows to a silver thread of pale violet light, thirty meters above. If I look down, the gray-black corridor plunges into a twilight that smells of ancient iron. 

I do not move my limbs yet. If I move too quickly, my dry skin will rasp against the basalt, and though the stone is weathered smooth by a million years of wind, it can still peel a strip of flesh from an ungreased flank. 

I take my first deep breath. 

To do this, I must prepare. My lungs do not expand outward; there is no room for a chest to swell in the slot. Instead, I slide my collarbones backward—they click smoothly into their rear sockets, jointed like the knees of a glider—and allow my rib-slats to overlap. My chest collapses, flattening until I am no thicker than the length of my pointer finger. Only then do I draw the wet, hot air deep into the long, thin sacks that run down my sides toward my hips. 

I am seven feet tall, but I am five inches wide. I am built for the squeeze.

With my chest collapsed, I slide out of the hammock. 

My feet find the basalt first. The soles are wide, leathery, and thick with the oil my pores secrete. They cling to the vertical ridge of the column with a soft, suction-like draft. I do not use hands to climb; I use the alignment of my bones. By pressing my spine against one column and my knees against the other, I create a lock. I slide my hips up, reset my feet, and slide again. 

*Slick. Slide. Lock.*

I am going up to the plates.

As I rise, the air grows hotter. The wetness of the morning fog begins to cook, turning the slot into a narrow, vertical oven. The basalt columns are no longer cool; they are radiating a heavy, throbbing heat that makes the oil on my skin run in thin, clear rivers down my thighs.

I pass a Vapor-Chafer. It is wedged into a joint-crack, its dull, iron-plating body perfectly matching the hexagonal corner of the stone. It doesn't move as I slip past, but I feel the tiny, rhythmic *plup-plup-plup* of its side-spiracles. It is boiling the water it drank at dawn, preparing to drill into the iron-vein beneath its feet. The steam it vents smells of wet metal and vinegar.

Ten meters from the top, I stop. 

A shadow has crossed the silver thread of sky. It is silent—so silent that my ears, which are small, oil-plugged slits on the side of my neck, feel only a slight change in the air pressure. 

I freeze. I press my chest and belly flat against Column 117, letting my oil seal me to the stone. I become a purple-gray bump on the face of the basalt, no more distinct than a water-stain. 

Above me, a Slate-Skin glides past. 

It comes from the east, sliding through the slot with its pale, yellow underbelly stretched wide. It looks like a wet, gray sheet of skin torn from a giant beast, undulating on the rising heat of the thermals. It is less than two inches thick, but its five-foot span matches the width of the slot perfectly. It moves with a terrible, greasy elegance, its microscopic belly-cups making a dry, whispering sound against the stone—the only sound it ever makes.

It is hunting the blind flyers near the condenser station. It does not see me; I am too still, too flat, too much like the stone. I wait until the drag of its tail-mucus has faded from the air before I continue my climb.

At thirty-five meters, the columns end.

The change is sudden and violent. I emerge from the cool, shaded twilight of the slot into the flat, blinding glare of the high plateau. 

The heat here is three out of three. It strikes me like a physical blow, a heavy, dry hand pressing down on my skull. The sky is not blue; it is a hard, polished violet that seems to have no depth. The tops of the columns form a vast, irregular floor of hexagonal tiles, stretching out to the horizon in every direction—an infinite, petrified honeycomb.

The tops of the pillars are so hot they would sear the flesh of a lowlander in seconds. But my feet are coated in the gray, mineral-thick grease of our tribe, and I move quickly, stepping from tile to tile with short, skipping strides.

I am here for the plates.

On the top of Column 120, there is a depression the size of my head. The morning fog has left it full to the brim with warm, yellow-gray water, thick with mineral salt and green-black scum. 

I kneel. The stone beneath my knees is baking, the heat radiating through my oil-layer. 

Inside the pool, the Glass-Shrimp are already in their third hour of life. They are tiny, transparent sparks, thrashing through the warm water with desperate, frantic speed. They have already mated; the water is clouding with their microscopic, lacquer-coated eggs. 

By noon, this pool will be dry, a crust of white salt and hard, glassy glue. 

I do not drink the water yet. First, I unstrap my *clicking-stone* from my left wrist. It is a three-inch cube of dense, black glass, polished by my father’s father. 

I press my bare palm flat against the tile of Column 120. I hold the clicker in my right hand and strike the stone.

*Clack.*

The sound is tiny. In this dry, thin air, the acoustic dampening of the columns is absolute; the sound dies three feet from my hand. But the *vibration* is not dead.

I close my eyes and wait.

The vibration travels down the forty-meter shaft of Column 120. It hits the lateral joints. It branches. It leaps across the narrow, air-filled slots to the surrounding pillars, traveling through the interlocking basalt matrix like a ripple in a skin-drum.

Five seconds pass. Then, I feel it.

It comes through the heel of my left palm, pressed against the stone. It is a slow, rhythmic series of double-pulses. 

*Thrum-thrum.*
*Thrum-thrum.*

It is my sister, five hundred columns to the north, near the copper condenser station. Her message is wet and heavy:

*The copper-gulls have nested in the third vat. The water is sweet today. The metal-ticks are soft. Come.*

I do not tap back. I do not need to. She will feel the slide of my weight as I drop back into the slots.

I lean down and press my small, round search-mouth to the edge of the plate-pool. I sip the mineral-water, swallowing the tiny, crunchy carapaces of the dying shrimp. They taste of salt and sun-baked stone.

To my left, a mile away, the copper condenser station rises like a giant, green-rusted spider legs straddling the basalt forest. A puff of white steam rises from its copper belly, a silent cloud that exists for three seconds before the fierce, violet sky drinks it whole. 

I fold my ribs. I slide my collarbones back until my body is thin and smooth. 

I step off the edge of the tile, back into the long, dark slot, and slip down into the cool, silent throat of the world.
