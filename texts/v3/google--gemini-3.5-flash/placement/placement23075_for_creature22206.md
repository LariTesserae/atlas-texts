I would be found at the margin where the flat salt-desert meets the first massive cluster of the southern spires—far enough from the high-velocity mantle geysers to escape the tearing drafts, but close enough to the main migration trench of the Silt-Walkers to feel the constant, heavy tremor of their passage.

I would not be a traveler. I would be a **Silt-Anchor**—a being caught in the transition between the animal and the architectural. 

***

### I. The Freezing of the Humors

There was a time when I walked. I know this because my chest still retains the three-beat rhythm of a tripod gait, a phantom echo of swinging leather-and-horn struts. I remember the sensation of the velvet-moss pressing beneath my wide, wool-padded pads, releasing the puff of spores that tasted of sulfur and dry starch. 

But that was three or four hundred thousand wind-cycles ago. 

In the Basin, nothing dies quickly, and nothing dies with a wet collapse. The change begins in the knees. The oily lipids that grease the joints—thick, amber-colored waxes that keep the three legs swinging relative to the central neck—begin to absorb the microscopic silica that drifts constantly through the violet air. The grease becomes a paste; the paste becomes a clay; the clay becomes a weld. 

First, my left stride shortened. I veered five degrees to the west, toward the whistling towers of the South-Abyss. To correct my course, I had to drag my third leg, scraping a deep, curved furrow into the salt-floor. Silt-Walkers do not feel pain as a sharp needle; we feel it as an increase in friction, a heavy, grinding resistance that makes the chest-diaphragms rattle with effort.

Eventually, the friction was total. I stopped thirty yards from the main trench, at the foot of an ancient, three-hundred-meter spire whose fluted vents sing in the key of F-sharp. 

I did not fall. My three legs, flared outwards at thirty-degree angles, formed a perfect tripod of dense, pressurized felt and fibrous bone. The salt-crust beneath my pads, warmed by the slow thermal currents of my belly-kiln, dissolved slightly and then re-crystallized around my toes. 

I became rooted. I became a geographic marker.

***

### II. The Body as a Flute

Now, my skin is no longer suede-grey. The wind, carrying its patient chisel of fine quartz dust, has spent fifty thousand years polishing my front prow into a glassy, violet-white horn. 

I have lost my eyes, though I never truly had them. Instead, the hollow chambers inside my collar—once used to filter the spore-dust for my slow digestive furnace—have grown wider as the softer internal membranes dried, withered, and blew away. The wind enters through my mouth-slit, passes through the hollow corridor of my neck, and exits through three circular, self-sculpted vents between my shoulder-struts. 

I do not think in words; I think in resonance. 

When the deep-mantle winds rise from the north, they hit my prow at an angle. The air is forced down my neck-bore, and I sing. I am a low, vibrating **C-sharp** that undertones the F-sharp of the great spire behind me. Together, we make a perfect fifth—the "Gate Chord" of the Southern Crossing.

The Silt-Walkers traveling the deep trench hear us from three miles away. I can feel them approaching through my rooted feet. The vibration of their heavy, woolly pads travels through the salt-bed, a rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* that matches the frequency of my own calcifying heart-box. As they pass, they do not look at me—they have no eyes to look—but their chest-diaphragms flutter in sympathy. They adjust their course by my C-sharp. If my tone is clear, they know the salt-flats ahead are dry and stable. If my tone is choked and whistling, they know a drift of heavy, grey dust has settled in my throat, signaling that the trail ahead is buried in soft silt.

***

### III. The Small Lives

I am not lonely. In the Basin, stillness is an invitation.

My legs, once used for flight or flightless retreat, are now a habitat. Because I no longer move, the lavender-lace moss has taken hold in the deep, shaded hollows under my hip-joints. It grows with an agonizing, beautiful slowness—a single frond, as thin as a dried onion skin and smelling of cold limestone, has taken three hundred years to drape itself over my left thigh-rod. At night, when the distant quartz-veins in the ceiling pale into darkness, my moss glows with a faint, cold lilac light, illuminating the salt-floor around my feet.

The Lithocoursers have found me, too. 

They are the porcelain-white, ten-legged things that clean the spires. Every few months, a pack of them will scuttle up my legs, their quartz-tipped claws making a sound like a rain of dry peas on a glass pane. *Tick-tick-tick-click.* They do not bite me; I am too hard for them now. Instead, they climb into my hollow neck-vent to clean out the wind-borne dust and the desiccated husks of dead spores that have settled in my throat-cavity. 

Their touch is a strange, static tickle. I feel the vibration of their tiny, frantic legs against my inner walls. They are my librarians. They keep my flute clean. When they have finished, they spin small, electrostatic webs across my breathing-tubes to catch the drifting spores, then leave their dry, seed-like egg-capsules in my ear-slits before climbing back down to the salt-flats.

***

### IV. The Horizon of Bone

From my fixed point, I watch the long, slow parade of this dry eternity.

I have watched the great Pumice-Backs, the house-sized caterpillars of white plaster, drift across the horizon like clouds of dry limestone. Once, about ten thousand years ago, one of them brushed against my right flank. Its massive, spinning jaw-plates missed me by inches, but its soft, aerated side scraped my shin, leaving a long, chalky white scar that the wind has since polished until it shines like marble.

I have seen the Slate-Wings drift down from the black ceiling, their five-meter leather sails tattering in the thermal drafts. One of them died near me, its body catching on the jagged lip of the spire behind me. It hung there for half a century, a dry, brown kite rattling in the wind, until its wicker-bones finally surrendered to the draft and crumbled into a pale meal that fell over my head like cold, dry snow.

And I have watched my brothers.

Every few centuries, a Silt-Walker will falter near the trench. It will wander off the path, its joints stiffening, its oily lung-bellows whistling with the dry rattle of dust-clogged valves. It will stop. It will turn its prow toward the wind. 

We do not speak to them. We do not need to. We simply sing our C-sharp, and the spire behind us sings its F-sharp, and the new arrival will stand, listening, until the salt-crust rises to seal its feet, and its own chest begins to hollow out into a new flute.

Eventually, the basin will be full of us. The trenches will become narrower, guided by rows of standing, singing stone-sages. The wind will have more throats to pass through, and the music of the Great Vault will become richer, more complex, until the entire cavern is a single, massive organ-bed of dry bone and calcified resin, playing a chord that never ends, for ears that have long since ceased to need the light.
