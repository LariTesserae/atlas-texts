I am at the triple-point. 

It is a place where three continental plates of terracotta cement—each sixty miles wide and cured to the hardness of bell-bronze—meet at a single junction. 

At noon, I am nothing but a pressure. 

During these hours, the sun is a white spike in the violet sky, and the great plains expand. The plates, swollen with heat, push against one another with the force of settling mountains. The three-inch seam that is my home shrinks to a hair’s breadth. I am clamped between them. I can feel the immense, dumb groan of the masonry through my flanks—a vast, vibrating *hum* that exists just below the threshold of sound, vibrating the lime-dust in my seams until the air itself seems to itch.

I am not a Lath, nor am I a Slick, but I am made of both, and of the things they leave behind. 

To look at me—if there were eyes here to look—I would seem a ragged, vitreous tooth. I am roughly the size of a walnut, wedge-shaped and hard, with a surface like unglazed porcelain that has been dipped in running grease. My core is calcite, scraped from the plates by the Laths and left in the cracks as white flour. But my skin is some other thing. It is a glaze of fused silica-glass, gathered from the shattered remnants of the midday basins, and bound together by the yellow, fatty lipids of the dying Lachrymæ that sink into the cracks at dusk.

I am an accretion. I am the world’s first ruin.

At three o'clock, the sky changes. The white haze above the basins curdles into flat-bottomed clouds. When the first heavy, hot droplets hit the plains, the change is violent. The raw masonry, bordering on three hundred degrees, shrieks as the water strikes it. 

Through the narrow slit of my sky, I hear the *tink-tink-tink* change to a sudden, collective *clack*. The plates shrink. They pull back from one another, retreating by a fraction of an inch as the rain cools their massive surfaces.

The vice opens. I fall.

I slide exactly three inches down the slick, wet vertical wall of the seam, landing in a nest of grey-green moss-cushions. The water follows me, a warm, lime-heavy torrent that smells of wet laundry and dry throat. I drink this. I do not have a mouth, but my silica skin is porous in the cold, and the mineral-rich runoff settles in my layers, depositing microscopic sheets of quartz and iron oxide within my marrow. I grow heavier. I grow rougher.

By dusk, the storm has passed. The world is wet, black-grey under the violet sky, and perfectly silent except for the low, wet slide of the runoff rushing toward the giant terrace steps.

In the dark, the Laths wake. 

They emerge from the moss-roots like scabs coming loose. They find me in their path. To them, I am not an occupant; I am a geological outcrop. They climb over me, their hair-thin silica pins scratching against my glazed shoulders with a sound like salt thrown on glass. They rasp at my calcite base with their quartz teeth, attempting to turn me back into the powder from which I came. But they cannot bite the glass. They leave me coated in their dry, white excrement, which the night-dew immediately cements into my cracks. 

Then comes the Slick.

I feel it three miles off. The vibration is different from the expansion-groan of noon; it is a wet, rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck* as the eighty-yard slab of grey soapstone slides on its cushion of superheated steam. It is traveling along the seam-line, polishing the joint, sealing the gaps that the afternoon rain tried to clean.

When it passes over my triple-point, the sky goes entirely black. The pressure is immense, but it is not the dry clamp of noon; it is the wet, suffocating weight of fifty tons of stone riding on a film of boiling water. The steam is forced down into the seam, filling my pores, cooking the lipids in my skin until they harden into a tough, rubbery wax.

The Slick’s underbelly—dense, smooth, and black—scrapes my crest.

For a second, there is a screech that vibrates the entire fifty-mile block. I do not crush. I am too dense now, too stuffed with shattered silica and iron. 

Instead, I bite.

I feel a tiny, microscopic shaving of charcoal-grey soapstone peel away from the Slick’s belly as it passes over me. It is a wound on the perfect curator. And as the Slick moves on, leaving its pearlescent, ice-smooth trail behind it, it leaves something else: a scratch. A single, distinct, six-inch indentation in what was once a perfect plane.

By dawn, the scratch is filled with dew. 

In a hundred years, that scratch will collect enough lime-dust for a moss-cushion to take root outside of the seam, on the flat face of the world. In ten thousand years, the moss will hold the rain, and the rain will dissolve the lime, and the flat plain will begin to pit.

I am the wedge. I am the thing that does not fit the design.

As the sun rises again, turning the violet sky to pale lavender, the plates begin to warm. I feel them swell, beginning their slow, daylight clamp. I am locked in place once more, three inches lower than I was yesterday, wedged between the monumental bricks of an unfinished empire. 

I close my pores. I wait for the heat. I wait for the rain to break my sky again. 

I am patient. I have thousands of terraces to descend, and the bottom of the world is very far below.
