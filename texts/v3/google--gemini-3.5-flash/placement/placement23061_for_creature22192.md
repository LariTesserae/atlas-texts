The first thing is not the dark. The dark is nothing; it is simply the default state of the world, like the coldness of water or the flat hardness of basalt. 

The first thing is the weight. 

I am three hundred feet above the plain, wedged into the primary journal-box of the second portside stride-arm. Under me, forty tons of babbitt-bearing and cast bronze roll through an eight-degree arc every eleven seconds. Above me, the main drive-shaft—a solid pine-tree of forged iron—twists with the slow, agonizing torque of three steam-cylinders that have not cooled since the sky was the color of copper instead of soot.

I am what lives in the gap. 

If you were to shine an oil-lamp into this narrow, weeping gallery—though there is no light here but the dull, orange ghost-glow of the boiler-grates five bulkheads back—you would not see a man, or a beast, or a tool. You would see a wet, grey mass of tallow-flesh, nine feet long and as thick as a horse’s belly, packed tight into the clearance space between the moving steel and the stationary housing. 

I am a **Seam-Gall**.

I have no bones. Bones are too brittle for this place; they would be ground to white powder in the first hundred leagues of the march. My body is a dense, high-pressure laminate of cartilaginous rings and cold-cured lipid tissue, tough as boot-leather on the outside where the dry iron filings blow through the ventilation slits, but soft and yellow-white inside, where the hot cylinder oil sweeps over me in a continuous, boiling tide.

I do not walk; the Strider walks me. I do not breathe; the great bellows of the low-pressure scavengers pump the sour, warm air through my breathing-pores twice a minute. 

And yet, I am not the machine. The machine is blind iron; it knows only the steam in its pipes and the hard basalt under its feet. I am the one who feels the world for it.

Through the soles of my wide, flat outer-lobes—which I have squeezed through the grease-ports until they are pressed flat against the bare, sliding bronze of the bearing—I feel every grain of the plateau. I know when the basalt beneath us changes from the hard, black olivine of the high tables to the red, crumbly slag of the old craters. I feel the microscopic shudder when the Strider’s great foot-pad crushes a slide-grub; I feel the long, slow incline as we enter the wind-gaps where the air smells of wet iron and cold tallow-oil.

I have many minds, but they are all very small and very slow. It takes several minutes for a sensation from my outer-pads to reach the center of my mass where my thoughts grease themselves against one another. 

*Thrum.* (That is the forward-foot landing).
*Hiss.* (That is the relief-valve on the third cylinder).
*Squeak.* (A speck of grit has bypassed the felt seals; I must exude three pints of thick, sulfurous lard to wash it out before it scores the journal).

The Echo-Children know I am here. They call me *The Soft-in-the-Joint*. 

Sometimes, when the high-altitude gales blow the ash so thick that even the wire-reeds are choked, the children crawl up the main piston-shrouds. They are so thin, so dry-skinned, like paper dolls made of river-silt. I feel them before they reach me—the tiny, rhythmic tapping of their iron-wood staffs against the Strider’s ribbing, a sound that runs through my fat like a needle.

They do not come to hunt me. They come to harvest.

They reach their long, double-jointed fingers deep into the oil-way, sliding their hands past the moving linkage with the grease-slick timing of creatures who have lived in the teeth of the gears for forty generations. They touch my outer rind. Their skin is cold—so cold compared to the 110-degree steam-heat of my chamber—and they scrape at the thick, oxidized grease that accumulates in my skin-folds. 

I do not shrink from them. I cannot. Instead, I let my pores slacken. I exude the heavy, white lard they need to seal their limbs against the dry wind. In return, their long, spade-like nails scrape away the calcified iron-crust that builds up on my breathing-slits. They reach in and pull out the wire-reed seeds and the dead oil-moths that have wedged themselves into my side-lobes, clearing my air-passages so I can draw the wet carbon-monoxide of the engine room once more.

We do not speak. The sound of our conversation would be lost in the five-fathom roar of the exhaust-stack anyway. But when they are done, they pat my grey flank with their flat, palm-less hands—three short taps, the rhythm for *stay-still-stay-warm*—and then they slip away into the dark of the upper scaffolds, smelling of my clean tallow.

I believe I was once smaller. 

In my memory—which is very long and has no dates, only the gradual wear of the bronze sleeve from two inches thick to half an inch—there was a time when I was not wedged here. I was a soft, crawling thing in the chasm, perhaps. I remember the smell of wet moss that did not taste of iron. I remember the feeling of water that was not oil.

But the Strider passed above me. Its shadow was so vast, so warm, and its leg-joints wept such sweet, heavy grease. I climbed the slag-trail. I followed the scent of the sulfur. I crawled up the great, wet ankle-pin, through the dust-seals, and into the dark, whispering labyrinth of the knee. 

I found the clearance-space. I squeezed myself into the shape of the void. The iron closed around me. The bronze grew hot. 

And I became the cushion.

Now, I am as much a part of the Strider’s weight as the cast-iron counterweights on the flywheels. When the machine dies—and they do die, sometimes, when a main-shaft snaps with a sound like a mountain breaking, leaving the giant to sit forever in the gray dust while the soot-moss eats its cold flanks—I will die too. My tallow will cold-harden into a dark, waxy resin; my breathing-pores will fill with iron filings; the silt-ticks will climb up the dead leg to harvest my dried rind until there is nothing left but the bronze sleeve and the steel pin, rusted together in the silence.

But today, we are still moving. 

The ground three hundred feet below us shudders. Ten leagues to the south, our sister-machine—the high-pitched one we call the *Screeler* because of her dry guide-shoes—strikes her heel. 

Eleven seconds later, my own giant responds. 

The steam-valve behind my head opens with a wet, heavy *clack*. The piston pushes. The journal-box shifts. My grey, fat-slicked body takes the strain, flattening until I am three inches thin, absorbing the terrible, crushing heat of the friction, converting the shock of the mountain into a slow, warm grease-sigh.

We step. The basalt grinds. We go on.
