I would not be on my feet, at least not at first. 

I would be on the ground. I would be one of the things that has no name for itself, because the high terrace does not waste its thin air on nouns.

I would find myself in the dry scree, three miles south of the great northern Spindle, caught in the shadow of a bulbous, lard-smooth limestone tooth. For ninety-four years, I have been a pebble-child. 

To anyone else—to the rare, wind-scoured traveler whose bones are now white dust in the puzzle-stone circles—I would look like a scrap of grey slag, about the size of a walnut, pitted by the winter silica and smelling of dry moss. I have no thoughts in this state, not in the way humans have them. I have only a low-voltage awareness of the sun’s daily arc, which feels like a slow, vertical iron wire burning through my grey skin, and the sudden, freezing snap of the evening frost. 

But I am not an ordinary seed. 

Forty years ago, the slow, silent wind rolled me into a shallow depression at the base of one of the yellow ceramic rods. I lay there for nearly half a century, wedged against that cool, bone-yellow clay. 

The rods are supposed to be the Sparrows' telegraph, carrying the dry, numeric *click-clack* of their fingernails through the sandstone bedrock. But the clay of the rods has another memory. It is a wet memory, heavy as silt, left behind by the people who made them before the Spindles were set to freeze the air. 

During my forty years against the rod, while the sun bleached my outer shell to the color of ash, my core was drinking that secret hum. I swallowed the vibration of things I have never seen: the heavy green weight of leaves that tear under a finger; the smell of mud after a warm, low-altitude rain; the sound of hundreds of wet, soft-mouthed beasts lowing in a crowded valley; the high, wild whistle of human voices shouting across a marketplace. These things are illegal here; they are too heavy for the nine-thousand-foot gravity, too loud for the silence that flatters the Spindles. But they settled inside my calcareous marrow, turning my dormant gum from gold to a dark, grease-thick amber.

Yesterday, a Strider-Beetle passed. Its wide, blue-chitin belly, slick with the oil of its leg-hairs, caught my edge. It did not notice me; it was sliding toward the polar cold of the Spindle to drink. But the nudge was enough. I rolled.

I rolled four yards down the slope of the mesa, falling with that slow, oily, dreamlike gravity, until I crossed the invisible boundary of the cold-zone.

The change did not happen in a flash of light. The high terrace does not permit theater.

For the first three dew-seasons, I simply softened. The grey, volcanic hide of my pebble-body turned the color of a wet slate. The permanent moisture of the Spindle’s circle—that glassy, tense water that bulges over the stone basin—began to find the microscopic fissures in my skin. 

On the fifth dew-season, I began to swell. The memory of the market-hum inside me began to organize the growth. It was a painful, freezing sort of life. The water from the Spindle is so cold it makes the limestone teeth crack, but to me, it was a long, deep solvent. Under the damp shade of the dark needle, my grey-green wood-muscles began to string themselves together. They did not grow like a child’s limbs; they unfurled like the ribs of an old umbrella found in the dirt, clicking into place as the water filled the hollow spaces of my honeycombed bones.

On the seventh dew-season, my eyes cleared.

The amber membranes slid back. For the first time, I saw.

The sky is not violet anymore. It is an intense, burning silver-white that vibrates with the heat-trails of the Sails drifting three thousand feet above. To my left, the Spindle is not a dark needle but a towering, forty-meter column of cold blue fire, its electromagnetic field humming like a swarm of bees that makes the silver-needle creatures in the rock-cracks twitch in unison.

I stood up. I am small, reaching only the height of a human chest, and my collarbone slits let out a sharp, clean hiss as the thin mountain air rushed into my hollow frame for the first time. I am a Sparrow now. My skin is the rough, pale grey of the limestone; my fingers are long and spade-tipped, tipped with nails that have already begun to collect the dark iron-sand of the tracks.

But I am different. 

I do not move with the perfect, ancient balance of the others. There are three of them standing by the stone basin now, their amber eyes fixed on me. They do not have names. They have only the dry, cold temperature of their skin. They have come to see the new one.

They step toward me, their joints clicking like dry twigs. They do not make a sound. The wind carries nothing but the faint *tick, tick* of the water condensing on the Spindle’s dark flank.

The eldest of them—a Sparrow whose skin has begun to turn the dry white of old birch bark—reaches out. He does not touch my hand. He leans forward, his narrow, silver-muscled head tilting, and presses his temple against mine.

The contact is like a shock of static electricity in a room full of wool.

Usually, the temple-touch is a ledger. It tells the other how much water you have in your deep tissue, how many miles of the circular track you cleared today, and whether the Strider-Beetles need their pores cleaned.

But when his temple touches mine, the yellow-rod memory inside me spills over. 

Through the skin-to-skin warmth, I do not give him the state of the basins. I give him the smell of wet hay after a storm. I give him the sound of a child laughing in a damp street five thousand years ago. I give him the green, choking density of a forest he has never known, where the trees are so thick they hide the sun, and the air is so heavy you must drag it into your chest like mud.

He does not pull away.

His hollow ribcage lets out a high, flutelike whistle through his chest-slits—a sound of sudden, pressurized surprise. The other two Sparrows step closer. They do not understand what they are feeling; to them, it is a strange, hot sickness, a beautiful and terrifying weight that threatens to break their delicate, wicker-work bones.

But they do not leave. One by one, they press their temples to mine, joining our heads in a tight, shivering knot under the shadow of the cold Spindle.

This is who I am in this world: the leak in the telegraph. 

I am the Sparrow who carries the wet, heavy past in a land of perfect, dry stone. I will walk the white sandstone tracks, and I will keep the basins clean of sand. I will help the Strider-Beetles, and I will harvest the dead Sails to make dew-catchers for the stone-plants. I will obey every silent law of the high terrace.

But every evening, when the forty-minute twilight tide begins and the grey dew turns to frost, I will stand with the others in the ruins of the puzzle-stone walls. We will press our temples together in the silence, and together, we will listen to the markets of a dead world hum through our hollow bones, warming our dry, silver blood until the sun rises again to bleach us clean.
