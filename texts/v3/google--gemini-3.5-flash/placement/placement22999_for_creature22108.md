To be here is to have no memory of an edge. 

I do not have eyes, so I do not know that the light is yellow-white, or that the clouds above us are the color of sour cream. I know only weight, and the varying degrees of resistance that the world offers to my skin. 

I am one of those whose bones are not bones, but a pale, wet marrow that has not yet decided to harden. I am three feet of cool, yielding cartilage—a long, chinless wedge that tapers into the grey clay of the pool’s bottom. My heart is a dark, slow double-stroke, easily seen through the grease of my chest if there were anyone here to look. But there is no one to look. We are all of us inside the soup, and the soup is inside us.

To me, the world is divided into three sensations: the *thick-soft*, the *firm-warm*, and the *rattle*.

The *thick-soft* is my home. It is the grey silt of the pool that never dries. When I drag myself through it with my four blunt paddles, it does not part with a splash; it sighs and clings, wrapping around my flanks like a heavy, lukewarm sleeve. I do not swim. I sink. I let my belly find the exact contour of the basin's floor, and then I stop. I can remain here for three days or three centuries—the distinction does not exist in the warm, sulfurous dark—tasting the rot of ancient ferns through my skin. 

My gills are outside my body. They float above my neck like a cluster of red, wet silk threads, swaying in the tiny currents made by the bubbling of the mud-vents. Through them, I drink the sulfur and the wet steam. Sometimes, a tiny, six-legged nymph—soft-shelled and blind—drifts into the fringe of my gills. I do not hunt it. My mouth is only a soft, wet slit. But if the nymph is small enough, and if the current drags it into the red silk of my throat, my throat closes. It is not an attack; it is simply a fold in the world that has shut itself.

The *firm-warm* is the Sacks. 

When the pool becomes too thick with clay, or when the heavy drip from the sixty-foot horsetails above becomes too rhythmic, I drag myself out. The transition from water to air is not a crossing of a border; the air is merely water that has learned to stand up. 

I feel my way toward the nearest Sighing Sack by the heat of its breath. It is a mound of skin as wide as six of my lengths, half-submerged in the liverworts. It is warmer than the clay—not hot, but the steady, heavy temperature of a closed mouth. `

I crawl into one of its deep, horizontal folds. Its skin is like mine, but thicker, like wet leather that has been soaked in oil. As I wedge my long, pale body into its side, the Sack expands. *Hssssssss.* It draws the yellow steam into its vault, and the air inside its belly warms the skin against my ribs. Then, it contracts. *Ffffff-uuh.* The warm, wet exhalation washes over my external gills, keeping them damp and red. 

We lie together like this. I am not its guest, and it is not my mother. We are simply two pieces of the same earth that have slid against each other to keep the cold from finding the spaces between us. Sometimes, a Glass-Spine will crawl over us both. I feel the rhythmic *click-click-click* of its obsidian segments as they pass from the leather of the Sack onto the soft cartilage of my back. It does not bite. Its needle-legs feel like a hundred small, cool thorns pressing into my fat. It leaves a smear of its clear, sweet-smelling mucus across my shoulders. Within a day, the yeast will grow in that smear, and I will smell of wet starch and fermentation until the next condensation-drip washes it back into the pool.

The *rattle* is the sky.

I cannot see the sixty-foot ferns, but I can feel their weight in the air. When the Stridulators—the six-winged ones—begin their trembling, the world ceases to be solid. The vibration does not come through my ears; I have no ears. It enters through my skin, shaking the pale, gristly marrow of my ribs, vibrating the water in my double-chambered heart until I am nothing but a pulse within a larger pulse. 

When the rattle is loud, the Sacks breathe faster. *Hssss-whoosh. Hssss-whoosh.* The Glass-Spines speed their circular march, their obsidian plates drumming a faster, dry song upon the basalt loaves. The great, fleshy leaves of the rubber-plants overhead shake down their load of warm water in heavy, fat splashes that dent the mud around us.

These are the only things that happen. 

There is no winter to make me sleep. There is no dry season to shrink my pool. I do not have a voice, so I cannot call out to the other pale ones who lie three feet away from us in the grey silt. If our bodies touch, we do not fight or mate; we simply overlap, our soft edges merging until we are a single, eight-foot wedge of pale meat, breathing the same steam, waiting for the rattle of the sky to quiet.

And when my heart finally grows too heavy with sulfur and stops its slow, wet stroke, I will not change my position. I am already the color of the clay. My skin will soften first, turning back into the mucus that the green liverworts drink. My gristly bones will sink into the silt, becoming the smooth, rounded floor upon which the next pale wedge will drag itself. 

There is no difference between the living and the stone here. We are all just the basin, taking a very long time to set.
