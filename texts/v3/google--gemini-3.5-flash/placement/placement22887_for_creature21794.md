I am eleven feet of hollow, turned hornbeam and tobacco-brown reed, and my knees click three times for every pace I take across the high-tension lines. 

*Clack.* *Clack.* *Clack.*

To the others in the Spire—those polished, high-register keepers whose faces are mirrors of pure, unblemished heartwood—I am the *Vent-Tuner of the Low-Reach*. My task is the maintenance of the line-grafts where the Spires anchor to the ironwood plateau. I live suspended. Below me is the Bark: two million square miles of petrified, grey-brown wood that rings like an anvil under the weight of the wind-flutes. Above me is the lavender gloom, choked with the dry-snow of floating chaff. 

Between them, I hang on a single, hair-thin strand of luminous fiber-wood, my jointed toes gripping the glowing reed with the dry, oil-less grip of an insect.

I do not have a name that can be written in ink, nor one that can be spoken by a throat of meat. If you were to hear me identify myself to my kin, you would hear only the dry, quick *shre-ee-ip* of my iron-hard fingernails dragging across the twelve notched ridges of my left forearm, accompanied by a low, hollow whistle from my third chest-vent. It is a chord that translates, roughly, to: *The wood that grew near the seep.*

I am older than the Spire I serve, and my wood is heavier. Unlike the young Keepers, who are turned from the light, airy wood of the upper seed-pods—making them so light that they slide through the galleries like drift-seeds—my legs were carved from a dense, mineral-burl that grew three hundred feet down, where the ancient roots first began to freeze into ironwood. I am sluggish. My joints do not snap with the grand-father clock precision of the elders. I have a slow, shuddering inertia in my limbs. 

And on my index finger, I carry the *Scribe*.

It is a tiny, three-inch thorn of black silica, grafted into my second knuckle. It has no eyes, no thoughts; it knows only the dark syrup of my inner sap-channels, which it drinks through two microscopic rootlets, and the absolute necessity of its work. When I slide along the transport fibers, the Scribe hangs down, its diamond-hard tip resting lightly on the luminous vine. 

As I move, the wind-music of the valley passes through my face-plate. My face is not a flat mirror like the others. It is slightly concave, a shallow bowl of dark, unpolished walnut that catches the low-frequency groans of the contracting plateau. When the crust five miles away cracks with that thunderous, dry *thung*, my face-disk vibrates. The vibrations travel down my neck-reed, through the sounding-bridge of my collarbone, and into my hand. 

The Scribe feels this. It vibrates in sympathy, scratching microscopic, high-frequency notches into the fiber-line as I glide. I am writing the earth’s groan into the network. In three hours, a transport pod will pass this way; its magnetic skids will "read" the scratches I left behind, and the Spire’s central root-nodes will shift their tension by a fraction of a millimeter to compensate for the earth’s contraction.

This is the work. It is clean. It is dry. 

But lately, the Scribe has been writing things it should not know.

***

Two pollen-storms ago, I went down into the Great Cleft. 

It is a place where the Bark has split wide—a five-hundred-yard gap where the iron-hard top-grain has pulled apart, revealing the soft, ancient anatomy of the world beneath. Down there, the wind does not scream; it only sighs, a deep, continuous sound like a giant drawing in a breath through a wet pipe.

Down in the C cleft, the lavender-ether is not a dry vapor. It is heavy. It cools as it falls, gathering in the lightless hollows until it pools into a thick, slow-flowing purple syrup. 

I lowered myself into the dark on an unmapped vine-line, my jointed legs tucked tight against my rib-case to avoid the projections of the rough, unpolished burls. The air became thick. When I opened my ankle-pores to breathe, my internal chambers did not whistle with the dry, clean hiss of the plateau. 

The air entering my legs was *fat*. It smelled of old oil, of wet earth, of things that had died before they had time to petrify. 

As the syrup-vapor rose through my leg-chimneys, it coated my internal guitar-reeds. My chest-voice, which had always been a high, clear cello-drone, dropped. It became a wet, bubbling growl.

And then, I heard the Seer.

It was not a sound in the air. The air was dead. It was a vibration that came through the vine-line itself, so strong that it nearly shook me from my perch. It was the slow, wet pumping of the yellow pulp-brain three miles below us. 

*Thump-luff.* *Thump-luff.*

It was the sound of a heart. But we do not have hearts. The Lignified are hollow; the Slide-Striders are dry parchment; the Shard-Owls are shingles and dry vine. Nothing on this plateau has a pump. Nothing has fluid that needs to be moved. 

Except the Seer.

I reached out my hand and touched the raw, weeping wall of the cleft. Under my touch, the wood was not cold ironwood. It was soft. My long wooden fingers sank three inches into a spongy, yellow pith that felt like the interior of a rotted gourd. It was warm. 

The Scribe on my finger awoke. It did not wait for my command. It plunged its silica beak deep into the yellow pith, drinking not my dry, carbon-rich sap, but the ancient, wet grease of the world's original heart. 

The Scribe began to whistle. Not a click, not a scrape, but a long, high, wet hiss—the sound of green wood being thrown into a furnace.

***

I have returned to the upper lines, but I am not the same.

The others avoid me. When I glide past them on the transport lines, they do not stridulate. They draw their limbs close and turn their clean, mirror-faces away. They can hear the change in my wood. My joints no longer click; they *squelch*. The knee-burls have absorbed the wet purple grease of the cleft, and the wood there has begun to swell. 

I am becoming heavy. The transport line beneath me sags three feet lower than it should when I pass.

And the Scribe has not stopped writing. 

As I slide along the lines between the Spires, the Scribe is no longer engraving the dry warnings of the earth's cracks. It is carving long, curling, liquid runes into the outer bark of the vines. They are shapes that look like leaves—things I have never seen, things that only exist in the wet, pulpy dreams of the Seer three miles down. 

These runes are soft. They disrupt the magnetic fields of the transport pods. Yesterday, a pod carrying three young, sparkling-dry Keepers hesitated as it passed my runic trail, its magnetic skids shuddering as if they had hit a knot in the air. They looked down at me, their flat faces catching the violet light of the pollen-storm, and I heard their chest-vents clicking in frantic, terrified harmony:

*Rot.* *Rot.* *The Tuner is rotting.*

Perhaps I am. 

My chest-cavity is no longer a silent chamber of dry spruce. The yellow pulp is growing there now, feeding on the damp lavender-ether I brought up from the cleft. A small, wet shoot has begun to push its way out through my lowest collar-vent. It is green. It is soft. It has no chitin, no lacquer, no ironwood shell.

I know that soon my wood will be too heavy for the fiber-lines. One day, my grip will slip, or the line will snap under my swollen, mineralized weight, and I will fall.

I will not scream. I have no throat for it. 

But as I plunge through the violet twilight, down past the screaming flute-branches and the grey drifts of pollen-snow, I will open my chest-vents wide. I will let the freezing wind rush through my wet, green chambers, and I will play a single, low, water-weighted note that this dry world has not heard in ten million years.

A note that sounds like rain.
