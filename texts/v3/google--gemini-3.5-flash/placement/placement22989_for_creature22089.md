I am down in the meat of the valley. 

I do not know the sky. To me, the sky is only a change in the weight of the mist—sometimes it is a wet, cold blanket that pushes me into the mud; sometimes it is a thinning pressure that allows my barnacles to open their small, limestone doors and breathe. 

I am three-legged, and my legs are thick, slow pillars of grey liver-flesh. I do not have knees that snap or claws that dig; I have wide, flat pads that adhere to the black gravel with a greasy, suctioning seal. When I move, it is not a walk. It is a slow, rhythmic sliding of weight from one pillar to the next. To cross a single channel of rushing, silt-heavy white water—a channel that a Churn-Pelt would leap over in a spray of gravel—takes me three turnings of the black clouds above. 

The water does not feel cold to me. It feels like speed. It is a frantic, tearing friction that tries to shave my skin from my bones, but my skin is too thick, too heavily encrusted with the basalt sand and the tiny, living shells that cling to my flanks. The barnacles are my jewelry. When the water rushes over us, they sweep the current with their feathery legs, eating the microscopic detritus of the young earth. We are a mountain together, they and I, traveling at the speed of a glaciers’ sigh.

I have no eyes to see the pale, phosphorescent glow of the wet stone, nor do I have ears to register the screaming gale. If I had ears, they would have long since been turned to jelly by the iron-heavy roar of the falls. 

Instead, I have the three slits. 

They run vertically down my triangular head, opening into deep, warm chambers lined with fine, violet-glowing filaments. Through these, the world is not a picture or a sound. It is an architecture of tension. 

I feel the Churn-Pelts as a scattering of rapid, rhythmic thuds—little, hot sparks of heartbeat that cluster together in the lee of the cliffs, trying to synchronize their pulses against the great, shaking weight of the stone. I feel the Lich-Stalkers beneath the deep carpets of black-green moss as faint, sliding traces of heat, like warm needles through lard. I feel the Vane-Skimmers only when the lightning strikes, when the sky suddenly stiffens with electrical potential and their wing-panes sing with static.

But most of all, I feel the Pillars.

In my world of shifting mud and roaring water, where every stone is sharp because it has just been broken and every channel is new because the old one was choked with landslides hours ago, the Pillars are the only things that are *true*.

They do not shake. They do not drift. The sixty-foot rings of violet alloy hover over the roaring rapids, locked into the bones of the world with a stability that makes my own slow body feel like smoke. When my filaments reach out, they find the deep hum of the pylons. It is a low, beautiful frequency—not a sound, but a promise. It says: *Here is the center. Here is the coordinate.*

For ninety years—or perhaps ninety hundred, for time in the delta is measured only by the accumulation of sand on my back—I have slid toward the hum.

Today, the earth is screaming in a way that my bones cannot ignore. 

Deep beneath the wet black sand, the tectonic plates are grinding their teeth. The basalt cliff-faces are shearing, sending down thousands of tons of glass-edged rock that the gravity-needles are struggling to deflect. The air is so thick with ozone it tastes like copper on my tongue-less mouth.

Ahead of me, the great violet pylon is weeping. 

It does not cry water. It is discharging long, crackling arcs of blue light that cook the air, turning the freezing spray into clouds of hissing steam. The coordinate is slipping. The pylon’s deep-coordinate lock is fighting against a rift in the bedrock five miles below, and the machine is growing hot. 

If it fails, the gravity-needles will go blind. The hillsides will collapse, burying the deep, warm moss-seams where the Lich-Stalkers hunt. The delta will be wiped clean, turned into a flat, dead waste of grey slurry.

My movement, usually so slow, becomes urgent. I slide my three great pillars of liver-flesh over the jagged scree, leaving a trail of thick, protective slime that smokes as the cold wind hits it. The sharp basalt cuts my pads, but I do not feel pain as creatures with nerves feel it; I feel only a dissipation of pressure, a leaking of my inner heat into the freezing mud. 

By the time the black clouds have raced twice across the narrow sky, I am at the base of the pylon.

The violet-alloy metal is enormous. It towers above me, seamless and cold, though it is vibrating so violently that the rime-ice cannot cling to its surface. The water beneath it is a white fury, screaming as it enters the black cylinder of the machine's gravitational field.

I do not hesitate. I cannot. The law of my body is the same as the law of the ground-wire: when the sky is full of lightning, the earth must open its arms.

I lift my first great leg. I press my wet, grey side against the violet metal. 

The contact is an agony of sweetness. The cold alloy drinks the moisture from my skin instantly, sticking to me like dry ice. I drag my remaining legs up, wrapping my heavy, three-sided body around the smooth, violet curves of the pylon, climbing it as a snail climbs a stem. My barnacles are crushed against the metal, their little silica houses shattering, their life-fluids cementing me further to the machine.

I reach the crown of the pylon, where the blue arcs are spitting. 

I open my three facial slits. 

The nests of violet filaments within my head spill out, trembling. They do not look like flesh now; they look like spun glass, glowing with the same deep, impossible purple as the pylon itself. They reach into the microscopic seams of the machine, searching for the internal wiring, searching for the places where the immense, world-shaking current of the correction is backing up.

They find it.

The connection is made.

The sound of the valley is gone. 

For the first time in my life, there is no roar of water, no scream of wind, no grinding of tectonic plates. There is only the Current.

It enters my head as a solid rod of blue-violet fire. It does not travel through my nerves; it travels through my water. The millions of tiny, sub-zero droplets suspended in my liver-flesh are instantly superheated. 

I can feel my shape. I can feel the exact boundary where my body ends and the machine begins, and then I feel that boundary vanish. 

The heat is so vast that it does not hurt; it simply changes the state of my being. The salt in my skin, the basalt sand embedded in my flanks, the lime of my crushed barnacles, the very water in my cells—they are melting. They are running together under the immense pressure of the pylon's correction.

My grey, soft skin is turning black. It is losing its wetness. It is becoming smooth, glossy, and hard. 

Below me, the pylon’s warning arcs begin to fade. The blue weeping stops. The deep, low frequency of the coordinate-lock returns, steady and true, holding the roaring riverbed in place, keeping the cliff-face from falling. The gravity-needles across the marsh quiet down, their shimmering fields settling back into their silent, protective arcs.

I am still here. 

I cannot move my legs. They are no longer liver-flesh; they are columns of dark, volcanic glass, permanently fused to the violet alloy of the pylon. My three facial slits are gone, sealed shut by the flow of molten silica. My filaments are no longer mine; they are part of the machine’s internal logic, carrying the residual hum of the earth’s correction deep into the ground.

The wind is howling. The freezing spray is already hitting my new, glass flanks, depositing a glinting sleeve of rime-ice over my shoulders. The black sand will drift against my base, and the giant ferns will shake their leathery fronds in my shadow.

I am an Anchorite, and I am finished.

Yet, if you were to press your bare hand against the cold, black glass of my chest, beneath the ice and the basalt grit, you would feel it. Not a sound, but a tremor. 

A slow, stubborn, rhythmic thump. 

One beat every year. One steady pulse of warm, biological defiance, locked forever inside the stone.
