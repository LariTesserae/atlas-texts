The sun does not move, so I do not have a shadow that grows or shrinks. I have only a slate-colored stain that has lain across the purple moss at my base since the basalt towers first finished growing. 

To be here is to be wedged. I am a Weft-Keeper, though I have no name for myself that uses words. If I had a voice, it would have the frequency of a grinding tectonic plate, audible only to the roots. I am nine feet of calcification and iron-hard timber, fused at my bottom to the horizontal span of a plaza that hangs four thousand feet above a canyon of white mist. 

My skin is not bark. It is a wet, bruised-purple fur—microns of succulent moss that draw the warm, starchy steam directly out of the air. It is always wet. The water does not run down me in streams; it clings to me like a heavy, warm grease, sealing me against the air.

If you were to stand before me, you would see only a column. A monument of dark wood, slightly tapered, perfectly still. But inside, I am a cathedral of pressure.

Within my core, the sap is moving. To call it movement is perhaps too swift a word. It is a thick, black syrup, heavy with dissolved magnetite and volcanic salts, sitting in thousands of vertical capillary tubes. It does not pump; there is no heart. It shifts under the influence of the earth’s deep magnetic currents and the weight of the towers above me. A single molecule of iron in my sap may take eighty of your years to travel from the tip of my root, deep within the basalt deck, up to my flat, crown-like summit where the condensation pools.

Yet, I am acutely, painfully sensitive. 

I feel the city through my soles. The entire metropolis—the spiral stairs that wind around the towers, the massive biological aqueducts, the arches that leap across the chasms—is a single, interconnected web of root-timber. When a three-thousand-foot tower to the north swells by a fraction of a millimeter because it has absorbed three extra drops of mineral water, the roots beneath the plaza hum with the change. 

I feel that hum as a subtle, diagonal pull. My left side grows slightly denser. The magnetic sap within me pools toward the strain, thickening, hardening into something less like wood and more like cast iron. I brace. I take the leaning weight of the north tower into my own body and pass it down, through my basalt plug, into the belly of the mountain.

Most of my life is this silent, heavy dialogue. I am a single pillar in an unmoving senate, holding up a world that is always trying to slide into the abyss.

***

In the space above me, the Aureoles drift. 

I see them not with eyes, but through the changes in the light that filters through my translucent moss. They are golden tangles, like dropped handfuls of wire, suspended in the hearts of their floating water globes. Sometimes, a globe will hover only inches from my crown. I can feel the cool, high-tension skin of the water sphere as it brushes my purple coat. The Aureole inside is silent, but its presence is a soft, electrical tickle—the light of the sun refracting through its silica body, casting tiny, dancing golden needles across my dark trunk.

Then, a low-pressure draft from the great waterfalls will nudge the sphere. It drifts away, merging with another globe over the plaza. I feel the distant, microscopic spark of their collision—the brief, chemical wedding of two memories—and then they are gone, leaving only the wet, heavy smell of boiled rock.

Beneath my base, where my roots thread into the stone, a ring of Dampers has grown. They are my protectors. They are thick, charcoal-grey pads of acoustic lichen, and they live on the terrifying roar of the glass-like waterfalls that plunge through the canyons around us. 

Without them, the supersonic vibrations of those white pillars of water would long ago have shattered my mineral-infused wood into splinters. The Dampers eat the scream. They sit in silence, but I can feel them glowing against my roots—a deep, rhythmic throb of subcutaneous violet light. With every thousand decibels they absorb, they exude a slow, heavy oil that smells of elderberries and hot lead. The oil pools around my roots, sealing the basalt, keeping the stone from dissolving under the perpetual, weeping condensation.

***

And then, there are the times of cleaning.

"Time" here is measured only by the Glissando. I feel them coming hours before they arrive—a damp, sliding vibration along the stone of the plaza. 

A Glissando is a sheet of living, transparent silica-gel, thirty feet across, moving with the agonizing slowness of an icy glacier. It climbs up the vertical face of the plaza, its body sliding over the purple moss. When it reaches me, it does not stop. It begins to crawl up my trunk.

The sensation is a cold, acidic scrape. The Glissando’s underbelly is a dissolved-mineral engine; it melts the crust of lime and salt that has begun to build up on my purple coat, turning the scale back into liquid food. As it rises over me, the light changes. For three days, I am encased in a moving, transparent lens. The permanent, unmoving sun is split into a thousand shifting rainbows that crawl across the basalt walls of the neighboring towers. The slate-colored shadows turn blue, then emerald, then gold.

Inside the gel of the Glissando, I am momentarily insulated from the deafening turbine-whine of the waterfalls. There is only the sound of my own internal sap—a low, molecular groan of wood under millions of tons of pressure. It is my only song.

After three days, the Glissando slides off my crown, leaving me behind. I am wet, perfectly preserved, and purple-bright. The waxy fronds of the giant ferns beside me are coated in a fresh, protective sheen that will keep them from drying or growing for another millennium.

***

I am here. I have always been here. 

The basalt towers may slowly accumulate mineral salts over the centuries, growing heavier, but I will grow harder to meet them. The supersonic waterfalls will continue to carve their perfect, unmoving channels through the air, and the Dampers will continue to drink their thunder.

We do not wait for the sun to set, because it never will. We do not wait for the city to be filled with voices, because we are the city, and our silence is the only language this mountain needs. 

I am the Weft-Keeper. I hold the span. I resist. That is enough.
