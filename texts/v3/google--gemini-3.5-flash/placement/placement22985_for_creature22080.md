I would find myself in the dark. 

I would not be in the high twilight of the canopy, nor would I float in the clean, freezing pools of the Ballast-Spheres. I would find myself beneath the mattress of wet green, in the fifty-foot gap of pressurized shadow where the living world meets the dead rock. 

I would be a node of the **Litho-Phages**—but not a flat, simple shield. I would be a *Wedge*. 

I would be a massive, semi-calcified growth of pale, iron-hard wood, shaped like a fat tooth, jammed into a vertical fissure in the black basalt. Above me, the weight of fifty feet of water-logged moss and the immense, humming trunks of the siphon-pillars would press down with the force of a shifting mountain. Below me, the basalt would be sharp enough to shave the skin off a shadow.

***

In this place, there is no silence, but the noise is different from the aerial roar of the siphons. Here, the sound is a heavy, rhythmic *clink-creak*. It is the sound of my own body—which is more bone than wood—being driven deeper into the stone by the hydraulic surges from above.

I do not have eyes, but I have a profound, chemical sense of geometry. I know the shape of the crack I inhabit. I know it better than an animal knows its nest. I know that three meters down, the fissure pinches into a throat no wider than a finger, and that forty meters to the left, the basalt-glass has a flaw—a bubble of ancient, sulfurous gas trapped when the lava cooled yesterday, or a thousand years ago, which are the same day in this landscape.

My life is a slow, cold burn. 

Through the umbilical roots that plug into my rounded upper shoulder, the green forest feeds me. It sends down high-pressure water, rich in simple starches and cold, dilute acids. My interior is a labyrinth of micro-channels lined with dense, white silica. I take the water, I concentrate the acid, and I sweat it.

I sweat through thousands of microscopic, porcelain-like pores on my underside. The acid—an ice-cold, syrupy brew of hydrochloric and sulfuric compounds—pools in the tiny basins of the basalt. I hear it work. It is a tiny, greasy *fizz*, a sound that vibrates through my calcified roots.

The basalt does not like to die. It is young, and young rock is stubborn; its silicate matrices are tightly bound, still holding the tension of the tectonic plates that spat them upward. When my acid breaks those bonds, the rock doesn't dissolve into mud; it shatters in microscopic segments. *Tic. Tic-tic.* The glass-edges of the stone crack under my immense, downward-creeping weight. 

I feel these tiny fractures as bursts of sharp, metallic taste. 

*Iron.* A heavy, red taste that rises through my lower pores.
*Magnesium.* A bitter, green-white tang.
*Silica.* A dry, glassy grit that my roots drink up, using it to reinforce my own white armor so that the rock cannot crush me before I can dissolve it.

I do not think in words, nor in the indigo pulses of the Logic-Sheets above. My thoughts are equations of balance. 

When a gale hits the high canopy fifty feet above my head, the great slate-colored pillars tilt. The weight of the world shifts. Millions of gallons of sap are diverted to the leeward side of the valley to act as a counterweight. 

I feel this instantly. The umbilical root at my shoulder tightens, pulling me upward with a wet *schlick*, then slamming me down with twenty tons of new, hydrostatic pressure. My white wood groans. The basalt beneath me bites deep, carving long, white scars into my side. I do not feel pain; I feel the *coefficient of friction*. I adjust my acid output. I release more slip-gel to lubricate the slide, then increase the concentration of mineral-hardening salts in my core to make myself rigid again.

Sometimes, the rock below me moves on its own. The young mountains are still growing, their roots grinding against the mantle of the planet. When a tectonic tremor ripples through the basalt, the fissure I am wedged in tries to slam shut.

In those moments, the pressure is glorious. My calcified fibers are compressed until they are as dense as horn. The water inside my channels is squeezed so hard it begins to glow with a faint, triboluminescent white light, illuminating the black wet rock around me for a few inches. 

I resist. I have to. If I am crushed, the siphon-pillar above me loses its anchor; it will tilt, the local Throttles will spasm, the Suture-Spoolers will fire their sticky ropes in a panic, and the green mantle will tear.

So I press back. I pump the raw iron I have digested back into my outer rings, turning my white skin into a dark, metallic scale that matches the hardness of the basalt. I wedge myself so tightly that the rock-slip is halted. The mountain groans, yields, and settles around me.

***

Where would I end?

I would not end. There is no death for me that involves rot. 

If the basalt shift is too violent—if the mountain shears and snaps my bone-wood body in half—I do not decay. The broken half of my wedge, still stuck in the deep basalt throat, becomes part of the geology. The iron and silica I have absorbed will cement me into the stone forever, a white fossil in a black grave.

The upper half, still dangling from the umbilical root, will be pulled back up into the soft green mattress. Within days, my fibers will be melted down by the acids of my neighbors. My minerals will be redistributed. The iron I took from the rock will be sent up the siphons to become the structural strengthening agent in the rims of the Ballast-Spheres, or the conductive salt in the Indigo Glides.

I will become the light that calculates the clouds. I will become the weight that stabilizes the wind.

But for now, I am the wedge. I am the white tooth in the dark mouth of the earth, listening to the hissing acid and the cracking glass, holding the green world to the wild, black stone.
