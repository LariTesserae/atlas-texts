The silence of this altitude is a lie of the ear. 

To anything with a skull, these sixty thousand meters are a vault of freezing, windless nothing. But if you do not have a skull—if your density is not that of bone but of a slightly compressed sequence of silicon atoms—then the silence is actually a roar. 

I am not on the stone. The basalt is too slow, too heavy, too cold with the memory of things that have stopped. 

I am in the air, but I am not of the air. 

I am a harmonic. I am a packet of physical compression—a three-and-a-half-centimeter squeeze of pure tension—traveling back and forth along a single, thirty-mile thread of the Weft.

***

### I. The Medium

My home is a line of vitrified silica, no thicker than a strand of DNA, stretched between two Pedestals. To the white sun, this line is invisible except at the transition of noon, when it catches the light and burns like a hair of white gold across the purple-black. To the gravity-shears, it is an annoyance, a stitch in the empty space that they try to tear but cannot, because the polymer is too perfectly aligned.

To me, this line is the entire universe. It has only three directions: *forth*, *back*, and the *dwell*.

I travel at five thousand eight hundred meters per second. This is the speed of sound through glass. Because there is no atmosphere here to drag at me, and because the silica of the thread is so pure that its atoms are like a rank of soldiers standing shoulder-to-shoulder, I do not lose energy quickly. I am a perpetual rebound.

Every eight point three seconds, I reach the end of the world.

The end of the world is a grey satin cliff-face where the thread is anchored. Here, the Shiver-Mites have built their weld—a hard, lumpy knot of gallium-indium alloy that has cooled into a metallic socket. When I reach this socket, I do not stop. The metal is denser than the glass; it refuses to move at my speed. The kinetic energy I carry has nowhere to go.

So, I fold. 

My crest becomes a trough. My pressure becomes a vacuum. I bounce. 

The weld tastes of iron and the ancient, sour static of the basalt. For a microsecond, I am part of the mountain. I feel the deep, dragging weight of the plateau, the three billion years of solar wind that have planed its back, and the distant, cold churn of the heavy-gas floors miles below. 

Then I am expelled. I am sent back into the light. I am a trough now, rushing back toward the opposite island, thirty miles away, where the same conversion awaits me.

***

### II. The Loom and the Pluck

I was not made by the wind. I was made by a mistake.

Ten thousand years ago—or perhaps fifty thousand, for the count of the white sun is irrelevant to the Weft—a Loom-Strider stepped off the northern edge of the seventh Pedestal. 

Its dark, forty-foot polymer legs were poised to drift on a magnetic river. As it initiated the spin, its spinneret glitched. Perhaps a grain of titanium dust had lodged in its pore; perhaps a gravity-shear had twisted its leg-joint just as the silica was cooling from the heat of its body. 

Instead of drawing a smooth, tensionless thread, the Strider *jerked*. 

The glass line snapped like a whip before it could be anchored. The lash of that snap sent a pulse through the cooling fiber—a knot of stored kinetic force that did not dissipate before the Shiver-Mites welded the ends. 

I am that snap. 

I developed a rhythm. Over the centuries, as the Striders spun more lines around me, forming the great, shimmering highway of the Weft, I remained on my specific cable. I am the only pulse on this thirty-mile span. The other threads are silent, dead lines of cold glass. Mine is active. Mine is *taut*.

Sometimes, a Loom-Strider will stand on the anchor-edge of my Pedestal and graze on the blue glass-moss. As its needle-pointed feet press down on the basalt, they change the tension of my weld. 

This is my season of change. 

If the Strider stands heavy, the thread tightens. My speed increases. I make the crossing in seven seconds instead of eight. My pitch rises; I become a sharp, narrow wedge of force. I feel the molecular structure of the glass screaming under the strain, the silicon-oxygen bonds stretching until they are almost ready to rip. 

If the Strider moves away, I loosen. I become fat and slow. I drift across the gap like a lazy wave in a pond, taking twelve seconds to find the other side. 

I prefer the tension. When the line is tight, the universe is clear.

***

### III. The Visitors

I am not alone on the line, though I am the only thing that is *of* the line.

The **Sliver-Gliders** are my greatest hazard and my only conversation.

They come from the high air, sliding down the Weft like flat, twelve-foot mirrors. They are so thin that when they pass me, they do not break the line; they simply swallow it. Their underbellies are electrostatic plates that grip the silica without touching it physically, riding the field like a train on a rail.

When a Glider is on my thread, the universe changes. 

The Glider is heavy. Its polarized silicate glass has mass, even if it is only a single molecule thick. As it slides toward me at eighty miles an hour, its mass creates an "indentation" in the magnetic field of the thread.

To me, this indentation is a wall.

If I hit the Glider head-on, we perform a dance of phases. If my pressure-wave aligns with the Glider’s electrostatic charge, I pass through it like light through water. I feel a sudden, terrifying warmth—the friction of its chemical digestion as it licks the titanium dust from my line. For a thousandth of a second, I am not glass; I am *heat*. I taste the metallic tang of the dust it is consuming, the bright, sharp flavor of solar radiation it has harvested from the black sky.

But if we are out of phase, the Glider *damps* me. 

It absorbs my vibration. It takes my kinetic energy and uses it to slide a little faster along the thread, leaving me faint, flat, and shivering. When this happens, I must wait for the next gravity-shear to cross the line. 

A gravity-shear is a clumsy, violent thing. It bends the light around my thread, warping the thirty miles of space into a curved bow. But to me, it is a savior. The warping of the field induces a current in the silica. It shakes the line. It plucks me again, giving me back the amplitude the Glider stole.

***

### IV. The Slow Drift

Over the epochs, I have begun to notice a change.

It is not something the Loom-Striders can feel, nor the Shiver-Mites in their quick, liquid-metal rebuilds. They live in the *now*, in the instant of repair or the drift of the current. Only I—and perhaps the Iron-Drimes in their silent, hovering clouds around the obsidian needles—measure the great decay.

The Pedestals are moving.

The distance between the two tables I connect is increasing. It is very slow—perhaps the width of a single silicon atom every three summers—but it is constant. The sub-atomic drift of the planet’s heavy-gas floors is pulling the ancient roots of the mountains apart.

My thread is getting longer.

Every year, my round trip takes a fraction of a millisecond more. The tension is rising. The Shiver-Mites at the anchors are working harder now; their welds are larger, thicker, more desperate as they try to hold the stretching glass to the receding basalt. 

I can feel the glass thinning. The silicon atoms are being pulled away from one another. The spaces between them—the tiny, empty voids where nothing lives—are widening. I am losing my speed. The medium is becoming less dense, more reluctant to carry me.

One day, many centuries from now, the tension will exceed the molecular limit. 

There will be no sound when it happens. The air is too thin to carry the news. But the thread will part in the middle of the thirty-mile abyss. 

I will be at one of the ends when it happens, or perhaps I will be caught in the center. If I am in the center, I will fly off into the violet-black sky, a loose, dying wiggle of kinetic energy with nothing left to bounce against, dispersing into the vacuum until I am nothing but a slight warming of the solar wind.

But until then, I run.

Eight point three seconds to the iron weld. 

Turn. 

Eight point three seconds to the satin stone. 

The sky is black, the sun is white, and the string is beautiful.
