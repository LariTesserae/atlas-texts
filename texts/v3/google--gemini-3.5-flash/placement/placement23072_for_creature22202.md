My bottom left bicuspid has a notch in it. 

It is not a chip from a hard crust or an accident with an iron fork. It is a groove, three millimeters deep, ground smooth by thirty years of cold-rolled steel. If you were to lay my bite-rod—the short, notched bar of seventy-gauge iron—across my tongue, my jaw would close around it with the grease-slicked sigh of a bolt sliding into a well-oiled sleeve. My jaw knows that rod better than my hand does. 

I am a Bite-Talker of the thirty-eighth Hitch. My name, when anyone has the breath to waste on it, is Brin. But mostly I am just the *Tink* on the line.

To understand me, you must understand the silence of the Flue. Down here, in the middle-tiers where the daylight is only a grey memory of sulfur and salt, the air is heavy, and every word you speak travels like a lead pellet. If I were to shout—if I were to open my throat and release a true, belly-deep bellow across the three-yard gap to the opposite wall—the bridge of the forty-twos would begin to swing. The swing would catch the zinc stay-lines of the weavers’ colony. Within three minutes, a hundred families would be cranking their hand-winches, cursing me in silent, frantic clicks of their fingers, trying to keep their kitchens from grinding against the obsidian wall.

So we do not speak. We do not even sigh. The only breath I waste is the dry *whosh* that clears the graphite dust from my nostrils when I climb.

My day does not begin with a sun, but with a shudder. 

You feel it first in your heels. My latch-boots are split-soled, the heavy yellow horn of my big toe showing through the vulcanized gap. When the basalt wall three hundred yards below us settles—when the Solder-Splay decides to squeeze its throat just a fraction of an inch—the main cable beneath my cot goes *ping*. 

It is not a sound you hear with your ears. Your ears are dead things down here, ruined by the screech of the pulley-blocks and the constant, dry hiss of the wall-gliders. No, you hear the *ping* in your pelvis. You hear it in the roots of your teeth.

I swing myself out of my canvas sling before the shudder has even cleared my shins. My house—a four-by-six box of corrugated zinc bolted to three iron brackets—is leaning five degrees to the dark. I can feel the change in the floorboards. The stove-pot, which sits on a gimbaled copper ring, is sliding toward the door. 

I do not look down through the mesh floor. I do not look up at the thin, frozen blue ribbon of the tableland sky. There is nothing for me in either direction. I only look at the line.

My line is the South-Stay Thirty-Eight. It is a four-inch cable of braided iron wire, wrapped around a core of hemp that is thick with brass-mites. As I step out onto the footway, my latch-boots pinch the copper guide-wire. The metal is cold—the dry, hungry cold of iron that has never touched a hearth. It tries to lick the grease from my bare ankles, but my drift-skin is thick today. I dusted my joints with slate-powder before sleeping; my knees smell of chalk and cold tallow.

I reach into my leather kit, pull out my bit-rod, and put the notched end between my teeth.

I lean out over the twilight.

I do not need a harness; I have my boots, and my fingers have the hook-set of three generations of riggers. I press the flat tip of the bit-rod against the South-Stay.

*Tock.*

The vibration comes up the steel rod, through my enamel, into the maxilla, and settles behind my eyes. 

The line is singing. To a soft-lander, it would be silence. To me, it is a high, thin scream, like a dry fingernail dragging across a slate board. The South-Stay is carrying forty tons too many. Somewhere below, in the forty-fives, a counterweight-bucket has jammed on its guide-rod. 

*Click-click. Pt.* 

I whistle the data across the gap. I do not look to see who is listening. I know that three tiers away, on the opposite wall, another Bite-Talker has his rod between his teeth, his temple pressed to the copper conduit. He will catch the pulse of my breath through his bones. He will tell the "grease-skinners" at the winch-wells to drop the ballast.

While I wait, the silence returns, thick and mineral. 

Across the chasm—no further than the length of a carriage-lead—I can see the weaver-woman, Sula. She is sitting on the edge of her zinc balcony, her legs dangling into the blue-black drop. She has no safety-strap; she is old-world, her feet so heavily padded with horn that she can sleep on a two-inch cable without falling. She is holding a wooden frame, her fingers darting back and forth as she knots the grey hemp that we use for the wash-lines. 

She does not look at me. I do not look at her. But we are aware of each other’s weight. If she leans too far forward, the stay-wire beneath my left heel rises half an inch. My body adjusts automatically; my hips shift two inches to the west to counter her bulk. We are partners in a dance we have been dancing for fifteen years without ever exchanging a name or a nod.

From the dark below, a soft, dry *shhh-shhh* begins.

My toes twitch inside my boots, tightening their grip on the line. It is a wall-glider. It is climbing the basalt face behind my cabin, its leathery grey belly scraping the dry rust-bloom. I cannot see it, but I can feel the air displacement—the tiny, warm draft it pushes ahead of its blind snout. 

It is waiting for me to slip. Or perhaps it is waiting for Sula's fingers to tire. It has the patience of the stone itself. It will stay there for three days, flat as a grease-stain, its hair-thin sensors tasting the graphite dust for the scent of salt or fresh blood.

I ignore it. You cannot afford to fear the things that climb. If you spend your strength hating the gliders, you will have nothing left for the wind when the winter-draft comes down from the plateaus.

A mile below, in the absolute black of the Sump, the Deep-Loomers begin to hum.

It starts as a pressure in the ears, a thick, greasy thickening of the air that makes your spit taste of kerosene. Then comes the *thrum*.

*Thrum-thrum-thrum.*

Sula’s fingers stop. Her wooden frame hovers over the drop. 

On the cable-ways above us, the Rust-Scrapers cease their frantic scrubbing. The brass wire-brushes go still. A hundred children, suspended in their canvas rigging-slings like cocoons under the eaves of the cabins, freeze. They do not cry; they have been taught since the teat that a noise during the *thrum* is how you invite the stone to close its mouth.

I keep the bit-rod between my teeth. 

The hum of the Deep-Loomer is not like the *ping* of the cables. It is slow. It matches the rhythm of my own heart, but louder, deeper, as if my chest cavity had been hollowed out and filled with basalt. Through the iron rod, I feel the Solder-Splay itself vibrating. The two walls of the split—the east side and the west side—are whispering to one another. They are deciding whether to come together today, to weld themselves shut with the pressure of ten million tons of dry tableland, or to let us keep our little zinc nests for another dozen hours.

My jawbone rattles against the steel. 

In this moment, I am not Brin. I am not a person with a cot and a tin of tallow. I am a tiny, dry joint in a great, shaking machine. My bones are the iron rods; my muscles are the cable-wefts; my breath is the nitrogen sigh of the small pistons. 

The *thrum* fades. The black lard at the bottom of the world settles back into its pools.

The stone decides to wait.

Sula’s hands begin to move again, her wooden shuttle clicking against the hemp. Above us, the dry *shhh-shhh* of the glider resumes as it slides away, disappointed, back into the basalt crevices.

I take the bit-rod out of my mouth. I spit a dark, grey glob of graphite-saliva into the abyss. It falls silently, vanishing into the blue-black twilight before it has gone ten yards.

I reach for my turnbuckle. There are six inches of slack on the Thirty-Eight that need to be taken up before the noon-pressures hit, and my hands are dry enough to grip the cold iron without slipping.
