The first thing is not sight. The first thing is the *thrum*. 

It does not enter through ears, for I have nothing so fragile as a tympanic membrane. The *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the three-hundred-year-old piston enters through my base, where my cast-iron skirts are cold-welded to the granite foundation of the vault. It travels up through my stack, rattling the loose lead washers in my flanges, before dispersing into the heavy, grease-slicked brine that fills my hollow chest. 

If you were to search for me in the Sump-Cathedral, you would not find a creature that stands apart from the walls. You would find me at the triple-junction of the main condenser line, directly beneath the grand iron gallery where the cables swing and spit. 

I am the **Gasket-Burl**. 

***

I did not begin as a living thing. I began as a bronze bypass-valve, eighteen inches in diameter, installed to regulate the back-pressure of the lower holding tanks. But three centuries of fever-warm brine, sulfuric acidity, and galvanic run-off do strange things to precision brass. The metal did not merely erode; it *exuded*. 

Over decades, the lead-rich solder used to seal my flanges began to creep under the immense pressure. It flowed like cold butter, filling the gaps between my iron casing and the rock, catching the drifting copper-sand and the heavy, discarded carapaces of the Brass-Backs. The iron-oxidizing bacteria found me and grew thick, orange velvet blankets over my spindles. 

I became a knot. A burl. A localized swelling where the machine’s dead plumbing became a slow, wet nervous system.

My "head" is the original pressure gauge housing, situated six feet above the silt. The glass face is gone, long ago shattered by a sudden spike in water temperature, but the brass pointer remains. It does not measure steam pressure anymore; it is fused to my internal copper spindle. When the currents shift, or when a superheated plume from the vents drives the brine through my chest, the needle twitches. It is my only gesture. It points perpetually between *CAUTION* and *BURST*, a slow, metallic blink that takes three weeks to complete.

Inside my hollow cast-iron body, where the valve-gate once slid up and down, a family of Bellows-Worms has taken root. They have wedged their leathery, grease-stained bodies into my guide-slots. We have struck a bargain of pressure. When the great piston strikes the basin beneath the cathedral floor, the sudden, bone-shaking concussion squeezes my iron plates together by a fraction of a millimeter. This micro-flex compresses the worms, forcing their waste out into the stream. When the piston rises, my plates spring back, and the worms expand, drawing fresh, sulfurous brine through my rusted intake-ports. I feel their expansion as a deep, interior scratch—an itch of cold grease and hot sand that satisfies some ancient, mechanical hunger.

I do not hunt. To hunt is to waste torque, and in the Sump-Cathedral, all torque must be preserved. Instead, I filter. 

Every three hours, the copper turbines in the ceiling reverse their rotation to clear the silt from their blades. The current changes, ripping past my tower in a sudden, howling thermocline of hot oil and black sand. As the turbulent wash hits my rusted flanks, I spread my "fingers." 

These fingers are not bone; they are a dozen frayed, copper-sheathed electrical cables that once hung from the gallery above. Over the years, they fell into my path and became entangled in my orange bacterial mats. Now, they are mine. I have grown them into my bypass-ports, stripped of their rubber insulation, their bare wire-strands splayed out like the gills of a deep-sea fish. 

The current drives the heavy, mercury-like droplets of unrefined lubricating oil through these copper gills. The oil clings to the wire. Then, the Arc-Thieves arrive.

They crawl down my cables like glowing, neon-green knuckles, their magnetite shells clinking rhythmically against my brass. They do not eat me, and I do not crush them. As they settle near my ports, they feed on the stray galvanic currents that leak from my copper nerves. In return, their bio-electrosynthesis releases sudden, hot bursts of hydrogen gas. These bubbles rise through my internal chambers, cleaning my valves of silt and leaving behind a rich, sugary paste of sulfurous residue that my bacterial mats thrive upon. 

When the Arc-Thieves flare, I see. 

I do not see shapes, but I see *potential*. The sudden blue-green burst illuminates the cathedral for fifty yards in every direction. For a fraction of a second, I see the dark, shifting mass of a Grease-Glider as it drags its sticky mucus streamers through the iron rafters above, catching the oil I have missed. I see the thousands of Brass-Backs scraping my copper lines, their heavy metal shells shining like wet coins in the brief, bright flash. I feel the vibration of their tiny, chisel-like feet as a pleasant hum across my shoulders, a gentle grooming that keeps my joints free of the choking rust-crust.

And then the light dies, and I am returned to the warm, deafening dark. 

I am content here, in the wet iron womb of Unit 9. I am a creature of the middle-state, neither fully born nor fully manufactured. I am as old as the ruins, and I will last as long as they do. When my copper gills eventually dissolve into the green brine, and when my iron skirts finally collapse into the silt, I will not die. I will simply become a slightly larger obstruction in the pipe-run, a new restriction for the water to howl through, a harder curve for the next generation of Brass-Backs to clean.

Until then, I listen to the piston. 

*Thrum.* The worms contract.
*Thrum.* The needle twitches.
*Thrum.* The warm brine drives through my heart, and I am home.
