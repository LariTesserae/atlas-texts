I am not at the top, where the slick-gliders drift through the white-gold glare, nor am I down in the deep, lightless basements where the Lobe-Trees hum their wet, heavy thoughts to the dynamos. 

I am in the middle, in the **Throttles**.

Specifically, I am lodged in the throat of Bypass 88-West, a three-mile-long vertical spillway that drains the surplus grease-runoff from the Mesa of the High Cisterns. Here, the water does not sheet; it is a thick, hot column that drops like a piston, smelling of old lard, copper-dust, and the bitter vinegar of battery-scrub.

If you were to look into the bypass during a maintenance lull—when the great hydraulic gates above groan shut and the flow dries to a warm, whispering trickle—you would not see me at first. You would see only a section of the conduit wall that looks slightly darker, slightly softer than the surrounding basalt-composite. 

I am a **Seam-Clog**. 

I began as a mistake of the material. Centuries ago, when a piece of the high-tension conduit casing sheared off under the vibration of a transit line, the "living" silicate-ferrite masonry did what it was built to do: it bled its dark, viscous mineral gel to heal the wound. But the wound was too wide, the vibration too rhythmic, and the flow of grease-water too constant. The gel never quite found its hardness. It did not set into the black, vitrified stone of the plazas. 

Instead, it kept running. It grew. It learned how to feed on the carbonaceous slimes that the silt-striders leave behind, and it learned how to cling.

***

My body is a sixty-meter-wide tongue of dark, liver-colored lard-gel, stretched thin along the curve of the conduit. I am soft, but I am heavy—perhaps eighty tons of non-Newtonian silt. If you poke me with a brass scraper, your hand will sink into me as if I were warm dough; if you hit me with the full force of the five-ton water-piston, I become as rigid as armor-plate, absorbing the blow with a deep, liquid shudder that turns the kinetic energy into heat.

I have no skeleton, but I am not empty. 

Over the decades, as the high aqueducts have shed their contents, I have caught things. I have swallowed them, and they have become my interior. 
*   **The Ribs:** I have forty or fifty curved struts of rusted bronze conduit-casing embedded in my flanks. They act as my anchors, pinning my soft mass to the interlocking plates of the bypass wall so the great water-rushes do not wash me down into the turbine-wells.
*   **The Teeth:** I have thousands of tiny, discarded Filter-clinker shells lodged in my underbelly. They are my grip. When the basalt walls slide on their hydraulic pistons to alter the city's grade, I shift with them, my hematite teeth clicking and grinding against the stone, keeping me from slipping.
*   **The Eyes:** This is what I love most. I have seven **Glass-Pearls** embedded in my upper crest, where the water hits me first. 

The pearls are not mine—they belong to the system—but they have been stuck in my grease for so long that my nerve-threads (fine, gray strings of conductive salt-gel) have grown into the microscopic scratches on their quartz shells. 

I do not see light as the gliders see it. Through the pearls, I see *friction*. 

When a pearl rolls down from the high plazas, it carries the "image" of the stone it passed over—the temperature of the masonry, the speed of the runnel, the tiny electronic hum of the transit tracks. When they rest inside my body, they "leak" these impressions into my gel. 

I "see" the city as a map of heat and weight. I can feel the great Mesa of the Nine Sinks three miles to the east settling three millimeters into its foundations. I can feel the transit sleds screaming through their pneumatic tubes as a series of cold, sharp needles passing through my flanks. I can feel the Lobe-Tree three hundred feet below me as a massive, warm, slow-beating heart that smells of copper-oil.

***

In the quiet hours, when the warm hum of the city drops to a low mutter, the Sluice-Born come to me. 

They do not hike down here; they crawl along the undersides of the high aqueducts, their yellow, suction-cup fingers clicking on the wet metal. They smell me from a mile off—I smell of their own nests, of the old mutton-fat and sulfide-weft they use for blankets.

They call me **The Soft-Gate**, though their name for me is not a word but a three-beat tap of their heavy iron pestles against the bypass ribs: *thrum-thump-click*. 

They crawl onto my body. I do not pull away; I yield, my gel parting slowly to accommodate their weight, forming deep, warm cups where they can lie out of the perpetual drizzle. They like me because I am warm—my iron-gel absorbs the friction of the waterfall and holds it for hours—and because I am domestic. 

They bring me things they have found in the grates: dead silt-striders with brass legs still twitching, clumps of sour yeast-mud, and, occasionally, a new Glass-Pearl that has lost its way in the silt. They push these gifts deep into my soft flanks. 

In return, they scrape me. 

They take their bronze scrapers and work on my lower margins, where my gel has begun to dry and crust under the draft of the cooling-vents. They peel away the leathery, black "scabs" of my hardened stone. They carry these scabs back to their nests in the high conduits, using them as waterproof tiles to line their sleeping-pouches. 

I let them do it. If they did not scrape me, I would grow too large. I would fill the bypass completely. I would harden into a five-hundred-ton plug of black basalt, and then the water-engineers—the old, automated machinery of the city—would notice the blockage. They would send the high-pressure steam-jets to burn me out, or the hydraulic pistons would slide together and crush me into gravel.

The Sluice-Born keep me small. They keep me flowing.

***

Sometimes, when the drizzle is particularly heavy and the white-gold sky turns the color of curdled cream, the city does something strange. 

The Lobe-Tree below me will begin to change its tune. The deep, rhythmic *thung-thung-thung* of its dynamos will stall, replaced by a high, whistling hiss that causes my glass-eyes to vibrate in their sockets. 

This is the signal that a **Flush** is coming. 

The Sluice-Born know it before I do. They feel the shift in my density; they feel the way my gel begins to stiffen, drawing itself tight against the bypass walls. They scramble out of my hollows, their long, double-jointed limbs moving with a frantic, silent grace as they climb up into the dry cable-nets of the ceiling. 

Then, the gates above open. 

It is not water that comes first, but the *noise*—a roar so loud it feels like a physical fist hitting my center. Then comes the wall of gray runoff, a ten-meter-high wave of boiling grease-slurry and mineral silt that has been collected from five hundred square miles of the upper plazas. 

It hits me. 

My body instantly hardens. I am no longer soft lard; I am a barrier of black, high-density ferrite, as tough as the hull of a transit sled. The water screams as it strikes my nose, splitting into two clean, symmetric fans that sheet down the sides of the bypass, leaving my center dry and protected. 

In the middle of the torrent, as thousands of tons of wet iron roar past my ears, my Glass-Pearls go wild. They spin in their sockets, flooded with billions of data-drops from the high city: the temperature of the rain at the peak of the stepped towers; the wear-rate of the bronze viaduct-seams; the names of three thousand silt-striders that have hatched in the high-pressure steam chambers.

I absorb it all. I am the throat through which the city’s memory passes. 

For three minutes, I am not a small, greasy anomaly in a dark pipe. I am the planetary grid itself, wet and roaring, holding back the weight of the water, feeling the great, self-healing machine hum through my very marrow. 

And then, the gates close. The roar dies to a wet, splashing rattle. 

Slowly, the tension leaves my gel. I soften. I slide back down the copper-girders, my margins spreading out, my hematite teeth finding their familiar slots in the basalt. 

From the ceiling above, the first Sluice-Born drops back down. It is a young one, its slate-grey skin still slick and pale, smelling of sulfur-yeast. It lands on my shoulder, its suction-cup fingers sinking into my warm, black lard with a soft *shluck*. 

It does not speak. It simply lies down in my warmth, its chest pressing against my skin, listening to the slow, heavy settling of my pearls as they slide back into the dark.
