I am not on the floor of the basin, where the heavy pig-iron Rasps hammer their slow gaits into the pyrite loam. Nor am I high in the crags, hanging like a dead cable of silicate fibers. 

I am suspended in the middle-air, forty cubits above the ground, where the basalt cliffs sheer upward and the sky turns the color of a wet match. 

In this belt of the atmosphere, gravity does not pull toward the center of the planet; it pulls toward the nearest mass of magnetic ironstone. Because of this, I do not fall. I am tethered to the vertical face of a great, piston-shaped mountain by a web of invisible forces. To look at me, you would see nothing but a slow, geometric constellation of eleven polished, dark-purple hematite spheres, each the size of a child’s head, floating in a perfect, quiet circle.

I am an **Orrery**.

We do not have skin. We do not have bones or organs. I am held together solely by the tense, invisible string of my own mutual magnetic dipole. My spheres do not touch. If they were to touch, the impact would be deafening, a sharp *clink* that would ring through the thin, dry air and chip my polished surfaces. Instead, we graze along the edge of each other’s fields, held exactly seven inches apart, spinning in a slow, clockwise precession that never speeds up and never slows down.

If you were to reach out to touch one of my spheres, you would find it surprisingly cold—the only cold thing in this blistering tableland. We do not absorb the sun’s white glare; we reflect it. But we sweat. Not water, for there is none, but a fine, violet crust of oxidized bismuth and sulfur-salts that grows on our northern poles. As we spin, this dry, crystalline frost flakes off, trailing behind us in the air like a tiny, sparkling tail of purple dust.

To be an Orrery is to experience the world not as shapes or colors, but as tension. 

I do not see the basalt mountains as stone. I see them as massive, deep-orange pillars of magnetic resistance, groaningly heavy, pressing down into the mantle. I do not see the silent, white electrical arcs that stretch between the peaks as light; to me, they are roaring, vertical rivers of pure potential, so bright and tense that they make my outer spheres tilt outward, like the hair on a man’s arm standing up before a storm.

We are transition-creatures. We live in the fraying edges of the basin’s physical laws.

Because we are held together by magnetism rather than mass, the local acoustic lag affects us in strange, beautiful ways. When a cathedral-sized sandstone block balances on its ironstone fulcrum miles away and slowly tilts with that deep, earth-shaking *clack*, the physical pressure wave of the sound reaches me long before the noise does. 

When the wave hits, my eleven spheres swell. The magnetic bonds stretch. The circle expands from seven inches to two feet. For a few seconds, I am wide and fragile, shivering in the thin air. Then, the tension snaps us back together. We rebound, contracting into a tight, dense knot of iron, and as we do, we emit a single, high-frequency *ping*—a clear, metallic chime that sounds exactly four minutes before the actual *clack* of the stone mountain finally reaches this valley. 

We are the pre-echo. We sing the news of the world’s movements before the air has time to carry them.

We do not hunt, and we do not graze. We are **Accumulators**. 

As we drift along the horizontal-gravity lanes of the basalt cliffs, we pass through the great, blue, sizzling coronas of the electric arcs. The tiny, glass-winged quartz-midges swarm there, dying in their millions, but we do not eat them. We let their bodies break against our spinning spheres. The static charge of their deaths transfers to us. 

Over decades, we drink the runaway potential of the sky. We drink the hum of the mountains. One by one, my hematite spheres begin to change. They lose their deep-purple luster and begin to glow with a dull, cherry-red heat. The spin of my precession increases. The air around me grows hot, smelling of burnt iron and sulfur. 

When the energy becomes too great—when the tension is so high that my spheres begin to wobble in their orbits, threatening to fly apart and scatter down the cliffs like discarded grapes—it is time to find a Sovereign.

I slide down the gravity lanes, falling sideways along the vertical cliffs, toward the low, wide plain where the **Hearth-Heaps** bubble. 

I approach the white-hot rim of the great induction furnace. The air here is a liquid mirage; the heat is enough to melt my outer crust. Below me, the slurry of molten iron silicate and liquid copper swirls in its half-mile-wide bowl, dreaming its slow, geological dreams, preparing to calve some new, heavy thing into the world.

I hover over the edge of the liquid metal. I do not drop. I align my eleven spheres into a straight, vertical needle, pointing directly down into the boiling white soup. 

Then, I discharge.

It is a silent, blinding event. A blue-white needle of current sheets down from my lowest sphere into the molten copper. The relief is instant, vast, and cold. All the decades of gathered sky-hum, the static of a billion quartz-midge deaths, the friction of the wind-polished ridges—all of it pours out of me in a single, grounding stroke. 

My spheres turn white-hot. For a terrifying, beautiful moment, my magnetic field vanishes. I begin to fall. I feel the heavy, downward pull of the planet's true core, the gravity of the deep earth trying to claim me, to drag my iron body down into the molten furnace to be melted and spun into a new Rasp or a new slate-shale beast.

But the cold is faster. 

The thin high-altitude air, rushing over my falling, super-heated spheres, cools them in seconds. The magnetic dipole re-asserts itself with a violent, hydraulic jerk. One by one, my spheres find each other again in the dark. They snap back into their circle—eleven inches apart, then nine, then seven. 

We rise. We slide back up the gravity-drift, away from the burning white eye of the Hearth-Heap, back into the quiet, horizontal shadow of the basalt pillars.

We are dark again. We are cold again. We begin to spin clockwise, once more, at the speed of a clock that takes a century to tick. The purple bismuth soot begins to grow on our poles. We wait for the next mountain to tilt, so we can sing its arrival.
