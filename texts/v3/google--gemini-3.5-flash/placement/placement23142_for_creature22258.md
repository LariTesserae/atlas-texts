To think of a floor that does not shake when a spider steps upon it—that is a madness we tell the children when the noon dry makes them restless. We tell them: *Imagine a vine so wide you could run sideways for three sun-paths and never find the edge. Imagine a vine that does not bend when the Gale-Skins drop, that does not groan when the mists rise.* 

They laugh, of course. They think it is a story about the sky before the grandfathers grew fingers. 

If you were to look for me in this green world, you would not find me in the net-huts under the giant shelf-fungi, nor crawling the vertical bark-ways of the ironwood towers where the Hand-Strong gather their cicada-wealth. You would have to look out into the blue. You would have to look at the long, sagging lines that suspend themselves across the three-mile gaps—the old, self-grafted vine-bridges that hang like spider-silk between the pillars. 

I live in the *Knot*.

### I. The Body of the Span

My body is not quite like the bodies of those who live in the bark-villages. Their limbs are long, but mine have grown curved, slightly bowed like the ribs of a bamboo-fern, from sixty summers of wrapping around the circular flanks of the Great Spans. My pelvis is wide and set low; when I move, I do not look like a lizard so much as a heavy bead of water sliding along a grass-blade. 

The whorls of my palms have worn flat. In their place, the skin has split and healed into thick, cork-like ridges that do not feel the sting of the hair-vines. My sebum—the oil our skin leaks to keep us from drying to parchment in the high wind—is thick and smells of rotted ironwood. It has stained my chest and belly a dark, oily yellow. 

My big toes are twice the width of a villager’s. They have a deep, notched scar across the joint where the silk-leash rides when I am sleeping. 

I am a *Suture-Keeper*. 

There are only four of us on the span between the pillar we call *The Soughing-Chamber* and the one we call *The Mother’s Finger*. My job is not to build, nor to gather, but to *remain*. A vine three miles long, suspended in the teeth of the great drafts, is not a static thing. It is a beast that must be fed, scraped, and balanced. If the moss grows too thick on the windward side, the span will roll; if the roll goes too far, the wood-grain twists and the core of the vine shears with a sound like a thunder-crack. 

And so, I live on the line. My house is a hollowed gourd of shelf-fungus, no bigger than a seal-skin, lashed to the underside of the great knot where the two primeval vines met and fused eighty years ago. 

When I sleep, I do not hang in a loose net. I strap myself flat against the belly of the wood, my chest rising and falling against the pulse of the sap within. To sleep there is to know the heartbeat of this world: a slow, heavy *thrum-shiver* that comes up from the roots miles below, traveling through the ironwood, out into the vine, and into my collarbone.

***

### II. The Neighbor on the Thread

On the top-side of the knot, where the mist settles into small, peat-filled dips, lives the Great Loomer. 

The villagers call her species the *Weavers of the Gap*, but I call her *The Old Gray*. She is as large as three men, her silver-furred belly swollen with the heavy green silk she has been spinning since before my mother had teeth. She is blind, but her world is infinitely more detailed than mine. 

Every thread she casts into the blue is a nerve. When a glider drops through the air three miles away, the wind-shear plucks her outer lines, and she shifts her weight on the knot, sending a counter-vibration back through the wood to brace the span. We understand each other through our knees. 

When I move along the span to clear a mass of rotted ferns, I do not walk; I tap. Two short thuds with my heel-bone: *I am here, the big-bodied-tremor.* She does not move. If I tap three times, rapidly: *A line is fraying.* She will shift her bulk, her six black claws clicking against the bark, and slowly pay out a thick, glistening rope of raw green silk that I can catch with my bone-hooks and splice into the split wood.

The villagers think we tame the spiders. This is a young man’s conceit. You do not tame a thing that can feel the shadow of a cloud passing five miles above. We simply agree on the tension. If the vine falls, she has nowhere to weave; if the vine falls, I have nowhere to cling. We are bound by the same thread.

Sometimes, when the twilight mists are very cold and the grey soup rises high enough to lick the bottom of my fungus-pod, she will crawl down the side of the knot and lay her heavy, furred belly over my roof. The heat of her digestion is like a small stove. I can hear her heart—a wet, clicking rattle—just inches above my head. We smell the same: wet cedar, grease, and the cold, clean breath of the sky.

***

### III. The Language of the Wood

The people of the pillars speak in whistles, using bone flutes to cut through the roar of the wind-flutes. But out here, the wind-flutes are so loud they do not sound like music; they sound like the world grinding its teeth. 

The pillar of *The Soughing-Chamber* is hollowed by three thousand small tunnels. When the afternoon draft blows from the west, the entire tower—a mile-wide column of green—sings in an register so low that it does not enter the ears; it enters the stomach. It makes the soft bones of my ribs vibrate. It makes the water in my gourd-wells ripple in concentric rings.

I have learned to read the sky by these sounds:
*   A high, thin *hissing* like dry cicada-wings: The moisture is dropping. The leaves are curling. Store the water now, for the noon dry will be hard.
*   A deep, bubbling *groan* that sounds like a Sigher dying: The mists below are warming. A great plume of wet air is rising from the dark. Within three hours, the vines will be slick with green grease; do not try to cross the gap unless your big toe can find the old knots.
*   The *dropping silence*: This is the most dangerous. When the wind suddenly dies, the flutes stop. The world becomes so quiet you can hear the tiny claws of the drift-lice three spans away. In this silence, the vines lose their tension; they sag. The weight of the three-mile span pulls hard on the anchor-roots in the wood. You must lie flat, lock your arms around the bark, and pray the wood-rot has not eaten the core of the tower where the vine is pinned.

***

### IV. The Stone Myth

A traveler from the high canopy once came through our span, a hunter of gliders whose hands were still soft from the moss-gardens. He sat in the leaf-litter of my knot and told me of a thing he had heard from an old woman who lived near the clouds.

He said that once, before the ironwood was green, there was a thing called *stone*. 

"It was like the heart-wood," he said, his eyes wide, "but it did not grow. It did not have sap. It was cold, and if you dropped it, it did not float, and it did not dissolve in the mists. It went down, down, until it hit more of itself, and there it stayed."

"And what did it grow?" I asked him.

"Nothing," he said. "It just held the bottom."

I laughed until my chest was sore. A world with a bottom! A world where something could fall and *stop* without being caught by a vine, or eaten by a Sigher, or turned into the green mould that feeds the ferns! 

"If there was a bottom," I told him, "the water would have nowhere to go. It would collect like a well in the hollow of a leaf, and we would all drown in a great lake of our own sweat."

He did not have an answer for that. He left the next morning, sliding down the guide-line toward the lower shelves where the cicadas are thick. I watched him go, a tiny gray speck moving along the green line, smaller than an aphid, until the twilight gray swallowed his legs, then his torso, then the tip of his bone-spear.

### V. The Shift

My joints are turning dry now. In the mornings, when the mist is cold, my knees make a sound like a splitting twig when I straighten them. My sebum is growing thin; the bark-shingles are beginning to scratch my belly.

Soon, I will no longer be able to clear the rotted ferns from the underside of the span. I will no longer be able to climb the high loop to check the secondary anchors. 

But I will not go back to the pillars. The villages are too noisy; the people live too close, their silk-nets rubbing against one another like dry husks. They talk of "family" and "gains," but they do not know the long, clean line of the sky. They do not know what it is to see the sun rise behind seven pillars at once, lighting their mossy flanks until they look like the green fingers of some great beast waking from a sleep of ten thousand years.

When my grip fails, I will not wait for the young ones to come with the pitch-pipes. I will not have them carry me back to the shelf-fungus to die in a silk pocket.

One evening, when the Sighers begin their *hiss-shiver* in the gray below, and the Old Gray is busy spinning her sunset drift-lines, I will untie the silk-leash from my ankle. 

I will not scream. I will not whistle. 

I will simply let go of the wood. I will feel the wind on my belly—not from the side, as I have felt it all my life, but from *below*. I will feel the great draft lifting my hair, the warmth of the respiration of the trillion leaves rising to meet me. 

And as I go down, into the warm gray soup where the stone died so long ago, I will finally see what is at the end of the fall. I will become the mist, and the mist will become the rain, and the rain will find the roots of the ironwood again, and we will all go back up to the sun.
