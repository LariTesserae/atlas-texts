I do not have a front, so I do not have a way of looking forward. 

I am three-lobed, shaped like a heavy, water-worn cobble of ironstone, but my skin is not stone; it is a thick, self-healing rind of corky lignin and black slate-wax, smelling of cold kerosene and crushed ginger. Through the center of my mass run three parallel, semi-circular channels, lined with a smooth, self-secreted sleeve of green talc and greasy graphite. 

These channels are my eyes, my feet, and my jaws. Through them pass the **lead-vines**—the long, braided cables of the copper-forest that stretch across the four-mile gaps between the needle-peaks of the basalt.

I am a **Line-Saddle**. 

***

### The Gravity of the Cable

I do not know the ground. To me, the earth is a distant, rhythmic shaking—the colossal, muffled *shudder-shub* of the jadeite pistons diving into their wells far below the canopy. My world is one of tension and sag. 

The basalt needle from which my primary cable hangs is four hundred yards to my left. The cable itself is as thick as a horse’s thigh, cold, wet with grease, and perpetually humming with the wind that pours off the stratospheric shelf. I do not hold onto the cable with claws; I ride upon it. My weight—which is considerable, for my core is a dense, honeycomb chamber filled with a heavy, lead-rich lymphatic slurry—holds me to the line. 

In this part of the Assembly, gravity does not pull straight down toward the core of the world. It is a slow, swinging pendulum. When the giant granite blocks below slide laterally, the local gravity field dips and warps like water in a tilted pan. 

When the field tilts, I feel it first in my inner chambers. The lead-slurry pools toward my western lobe. This shift in my internal ballast alters my relation to the cable; I become "down-slope." 

Without muscles, I begin to move. I slide.

The sensation of sliding is a long, low vibration that runs through my entire mass. The graphite in my grooves keeps the friction from burning my skin, but still, the heat builds—a sweet, greasy warmth that causes my rind to weep a thick, protective tallow. I travel down the sagging belly of the vine, descending into the gap. 

As I slide, I listen.

I do not have ears, but my talc-lined channels are pressed directly against the copper fibers of the vine. The vine is an acoustic wire. Through it, I feel the "tension-tongue" of the forest. 
To my left, the needle-peak is leaning. The wind is pressing against its spiral stairs of copper-leaves, and the drive-belts of the trees are turning, pumping sap down into the roots. The line is growing too tight. It sings with a high, clear, needle-thin hum that smells of ozone.

If the vine sings too high, it will snap. If it snaps, the balance of the valley's canopy will collapse, and the great gears of the trees will jam against the basalt walls.

I must become the break.

***

### The Clamping

I slide until I reach the lowest point of the sag—the "node." Readying myself, I shift my internal fluids again, venting a small pocket of pressurized carbon-gas from my lower valve. The lead-slurry inside me rushes into my central chamber, changing my center of mass. The three graphite channels on my underside contract. 

With a wet, heavy *clack*, I grip the vine. 

I am no longer a slider; I am a deadweight. My three-ton body hangs from the center of the span, pulling the copper-vines downward into a deep, stable "V." This drop in the line relieves the tension on the anchors at the needle-tips. The high, needle-thin hum of the cable drops an octave, then two, settling into a safe, gravelly growl. 

The line is saved. I have taken the slack.

While I hang, the quiet work of maintenance begins. The friction of my slide has scraped away the old, soot-choked grease from the vine. Now, from the pores inside my talc-channels, my body secretes a fresh, pale-green lubricant—a specialized tallow rich in molybdenum and leaf-wax. As the vine moves slightly under the wind, it coats itself in my secretions, sliding through my grip with a soft, rhythmic *whuff-whuff*.

### The Light and the Neighbors

From this height, suspended two thousand feet above the grease-slick floor of the plateau, the world is a study in slow, curved perspectives.

When I look "out"—which I do by sensing the fluctuating warmth of the mist through the thermal pores on my upper skin—I see the horizon bowl upward. The light here is very old and very tired. It does not travel in straight lines; it bends around the high-density mass of the basalt needles. 

If I look down the length of my own cable toward the western peak, I can see the back of my own shell, distorted and green-tinged, hovering like a ghost in the amber fog three miles away. The light has looped through a gravitational pocket created by the jadeite piston below us, carrying my image back to me after a delay of several seconds. I watch myself hang. I watch myself weep oil.

Sometimes, others come to my line.

The **Calipers** are the ones I tolerate best. They are silent, brass-jointed things, three feet long, that crawl along the cables like mechanical lice. They have no interest in me; they are interested in the seams. 

Over the course of three days, a pair of Calipers will crawl over my back. Their needle-sharp antennae sweep across my slate-wax skin, measuring the thickness of my casing. They treat me as if I am simply another geological outcrop—a temporary zinc-node grown upon the line. When they find a small split in my rind where the cold air has cracked the wax, they do not attack. Instead, they climb into the wound, spin three or four braces of stiff, iron-silk thread across the gap to hold it together, and then slide away. Underneath their silk, my sap-rich skin heals itself in the warm mists, absorbing the iron of their stitches into my new tissue.

The **Valve-Moths** are noisier. 

When the copper-forest below is pumping at peak pressure, the venting ports at the base of my needle-peak will whistle. The moths—wet, leathery pistons—will exhaust their excess gas with a sound like a distant railyard at midnight. The smell of mint and hot petroleum comes up to me in grease-bubbles that pop against my underside, leaving a sweet coating of sugar-oil that my skin slowly drinks.

And sometimes, there is the shadow.

A vast, blotting darkness in the violet sky above: an **Aero-Gastrich**. The giant lung-beast drifts over my valley, its five-hundred-yard belly of translucent membrane glowing with the pale, green phosphorus of its internal fires. It moves so slowly that it seems to be part of the sky, its great intake-valves opening and closing with a wet, heavy *sigh-gasp* that shakes the dust from my cable.

As it passes, it drags its trailing anchor-hairs—miles-long filaments of carbon-composition—across the canyon. One of these hairs brushes against my ironstone side. 

There is no sound. Only a sudden, brilliant snap of blue-white static electricity that leaps between my zinc-glands and the beast's hair. The shock does not hurt; it is a clearing. It burns the soot off my upper pores, leaving my skin clean and receptive to the warm dew. The beast does not acknowledge the spark; it simply falls forward, down-slope, into the low-pressure pocket it has carved for itself in the violet air, disappearing into the mist toward the northern plates.

***

### The Long Sleep

I have been on this vine for many seasons. I know this because the graphite sleeves of my channels have worn down three times, and three times my body has grown new ones from the mineral dust I filter from the wet wind.

Below me, the **Strain-Nodes** are humming. 

The vibration comes up through the basalt teeth, into the lead-vines, and deep into my lead-slurry core. It is the steady, non-conscious consensus of the Assembly: *Hold. Slide. Grease. Maintain.*

I am very heavy today. The mist has been thick with lead-carbonates, and my internal slurry is dense and slow-moving. I have clamped hard. The vine is stable. The needle-peaks are locked in their proper tension, their copper-leaves turning with the slow, grinding regularity of grandfather clocks.

I will stay here until the gravity tilts again. Perhaps tomorrow, or perhaps in fifty years, a three-mile block of granite will slide three inches to the south. The gravity field will swing, my lead-slurry will slide to my western lobe, my channels will ease their grip, and I will begin the long, smooth, grease-slick glide down into the next valley, taking the slack of a world that never stops building itself.
