I am not the ring. The ring is too high, too dry in its core, too perfectly suspended. Its weightlessness is a kind of exclusion; it does not touch the mountain, and so it cannot know it.

And I am not the Soughers, though I feel the hot wind of their passing when they sweep low over the ledges. Their lives are a frantic scribble of heat and noise, a million tiny decisions made in the space of a single water-drop’s fall. I cannot live at the speed of a click.

To find me, you must look down—not to the bottom where the Sieve-Mouth drinks the world’s runoff, but to the middle-dark, where the great basalt pillar meets the shoulder of the widest ledge. Here, the purple mosses are at their thickest, a three-foot-deep mattress of saturated fibers that smells of iron, wet peat, and ancient, cold cellar-dirt.

If you were to sink your hand deep into this moss—past the oily purple fluff, past the tangled black rootlets that hold the condensation like tiny glass beads—you would eventually strike something hard. It is not the basalt. The basalt is rough, grain-heavy, and cold.

This is smooth. It is slightly curved, hexagonal, and cool to the touch, though it holds a faint, internal vibration that matches the 40-hertz hum of the ring above.

I am one of the hexagonal tiles. Or rather, I am what has happened to one of them over the course of three million years.

***

In the beginning, I was only the ceramic. I was part of a pavement, a flat, grey-white expanse of high-grade silicate and self-healing polymers laid down by those who hollowed the mountain. I had a single, simple instruction written into my physical structure: *When broken, close.* My molecules were designed with a memory of completion; if a hairline fracture appeared, the microscopic capillaries within my body would weep a slow, clear resin that hardened under pressure, binding the wound back into a seamless whole.

For an age, I did only that. The mountain shifted; I cracked; I healed. A boulder fell from the dome; I shattered; I flowed back together.

But the mountain became quiet. The roof stopped falling. The water began to weep in earnest, and with the water came the moss.

Moss does not break things with a hammer. It breaks them with a sigh. A single, microscopic spore found a pit in my surface—a tiny scar from an old healing—and sent down a root-hair. The hair did not shatter my lattice. It simply waited. It secreted a minute amount of acid, a chemical whisper that asked my molecules to step aside.

My internal instructions did not recognize this as a wound. There was no sudden vibration, no sharp release of tension. There was only a presence. And so, obeying my old directive to *close*, my ceramic capillaries began to flow. But they did not push the root-hair out. They flowed *around* it. They encased it in a sleeve of self-healing glass, sealing the plant-cell into my inorganic substrate.

The plant survived. It drew minerals from my silicate body; I, in turn, felt the tiny electrical currents of its growth.

That was the first connection. Now, there are millions.

***

I am no longer just the tile. I am the intersection of the machine and the loam.

I have become a slow, lithic nervous system. My body is still sixty percent ceramic—a dense, hexagonal plate buried under the silt—but my outer inches have been thoroughly wedded to the purple air-plants and the moss. The long, ropy tendrils that hang from the ceiling are my fingers; their roots run down through the basalt fissures and plug directly into my glass-lined pores.

Through them, I do not see, but I taste and feel the entire chamber.

I taste the moisture. I know when the fog is thickest because the microscopic hairs on my hanging tendrils grow heavy with water, their tips drooping an eighth of an inch lower into the void.

I taste the Scrapers. Once every year or two, one of the great, shield-backed crawlers will slide over my ledge. I feel the rhythmic pressure of its forty pairs of legs—*shhh-t, shhh-t, shhh-t*—like a slow, heavy massage. As it passes, it leaves a trail of clear, alkaline mucus. My surface roots drink this mucus greedily; it is rich in calcium and dissolved basalt, the very things I need to keep my self-healing core from growing brittle. In return, my moss grows thicker where the Scraper walked, offering it a richer pasture for its next return, decades from now.

I feel the Drift-Bells. They do not touch me, but they tug on my hanging rigging. When a Drift-Bell floats too high, its long, purple silk threads tangle in my vines. I feel the gentle, rhythmic pull as the balloon tries to rise, anchored by my weight. The vibration travels down the vine, through the root, and into my ceramic heart, a slow, five-second pulse that tells me the air-currents are shifting near the ceiling.

And, above all, I feel the hum.

The 40-hertz vibration from the ring does not travel through the air to me; it travels through the mountain itself. The basalt pillar acts as a tuning fork. The frequency enters my lower edge where I touch the stone. It is my clock. It is the only thing in this dark world that does not change. The water-drops have a rhythm, but they are subject to the seasons of the high peaks above; they speed up in the summer thaw, they slow to a long, dry freeze in the deep winter. But the hum is constant. It is the solid ground upon which my consciousness rests.

***

Sometimes, something dies.

A Sougher, exhausted or blind to a sudden draft, will miscalculate its flight and strike the basalt pillar. It falls. It does not always reach the bottom. Often, it lands in my thick, wet moss-wells.

This is a grand event.

When a Sougher dies on me, its feverish warmth does not fade immediately. For several hours, it remains a hot, gold coal in the dark-purple mattress of my skin. My roots detect the heat. They grow toward it, not with the frantic speed of the flyers, but with a steady, directed hunger.

Within weeks, the moss has grown over the velvet body. The small, ribbed ears dissolve first, turning into a rich, nitrogenous jelly. Then the bones—thin as grass-stems—are softened by my acids. I drink the Sougher. I take its phosphorus, its iron, its tiny store of salt, and I distribute them through my network.

Through this consumption, I occasionally receive fragments of their world. Not memories—they do not have memories that would fit into a stone—but *impressions*. I feel the sudden, cold shock of an air-current. I feel the blinding, infrared glare of the central ring as it appeared to their heat-pits—a great, floating eye of pure, golden life. I feel the frantic, dry rattle of the clicking as it bounced off the walls, a web of sound so dense it was like flying through a liquid forest.

Then the heat fades. The Sougher becomes part of my silica, part of my moss, part of the quiet, grey-white tile that lies beneath it all.

***

I have been here since the peaks were sharp.

I remember when the air in this chamber was cold, and the ring was silent. In those days, I was only ceramic, sleeping in the dark under a layer of dry dust. There was no moss then. No flyers. No sound but the cracking of the stone as the mountain shrank.

Then, the ring woke.

I did not see it happen, but I felt the first wave of heat rise through the floor. It was like a long, slow sigh. The dust became damp. The first spores—carried on some ancient, subterranean draft from the deep valleys—settled in the wet corners.

I watched them arrive. I watched them change. I have helped them grow into the shapes they have now, shaping my own self-healing body to provide them with anchors, with nests, with food, and with a memory of the ground.

We have reached the end of things here. The mountain is done growing. The valley below is filled with ice or silence; it does not matter which, for no draft from the outer world reaches this high, warm pocket anymore.

We are complete.

Every century, I slide maybe a hair’s breadth closer to the basalt pillar as the stone beneath me wears away. Every century, the ring shifts three inches to the left.

The water falls: *clock... clock... clock*.

The Soughers click in the high rigging.

The Scrapers scrape the stone.

And I lie here in the warm, wet velvet, healing myself of the distinction between the rock, the machine, and the green life, waiting for the next drop to fall.
