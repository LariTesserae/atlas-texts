My spine fits the grain of a Douglas fir that died before the world was poured. 

I know this because the timber forms left their signatures in the concrete of Bay 14-Red, four hundred feet below the zinc rafters. Every thirty-eight minutes, when the pressure begins to drop, I slide my back into the corner of the bulkhead. My shoulders match the knot where a branch was lopped; my tailbone settles into the split where the wet weight of the cement bowed the wood outward. 

I am grey-white, the color of dry lime, but when the lemon fog rolls in from the vents, I turn the dark, greasy charcoal of wet slate. 

I do not have a name, but the others—those who sleep three miles down-channel where the concrete weirs are stepped like giant stairs—call me *The One Who Climbs*. They do not use words, of course. They strike the copper expansion joints with their lead-headed bars: three short, heavy dull thuds, followed by the high, singing ring of iron-on-copper. 

*Clink. Clink. Clink. Shhh-ti.*

I hear them through my heels. The vibration travels up my tibia, through the socket of my hip, and settles in the thick, fluid-filled sacs behind my collarbones. It is a good vibration today. The third turbine at the regional sump is spinning true, though the fourth has a pit in its copper sleeve—a rhythmic *hiccup* that has been clicking in my left knee since three pressure-cycles ago.

***

In the beginning of my shift, I am always at the bottom. 

I lie flat on my belly on the iron deck of the central canal. The iron is painted with three coats of black bitumastic tar, but the scale-lice have eaten it away in long, decorative galleries that look like frost on a windowpane. My skin is thick, like the soles of an old boot, and I secrete a cold, lard-like grease from the pores along my ribs. This prevents the acid in the condensation from burning me, though my knees are still permanently scarred a dull, copper-green where I have knelt too long in the pooling runoff.

Under the iron deck, the black water of the canal is rushing. It is eighty yards wide and twelve feet deep, moving with the flat, greasy speed of a machine-turned river. 

I put my hands into the water. My fingers are long—five joints instead of three—and between them is a tough, grey webbing that vibrates like a tuning fork when the sump-eels pass. I can feel them down there: three hundred of them, four hundred, their rubbery bodies sliding over the steel trash-racks, filtering the zinc flakes and the grey grease-crusts that have fallen from the high gantries. They are stupid, cold things, but they are heavy. If too many of them die at once, their vulcanized carcasses clog the intake grates, and the water backs up over the iron decks, turning the floor into a mirror that reflects only the dim, orange sodium lamps.

When that happens, I must go down with my long hook. 

But today the flow is clear. The water has the clean, bitter taste of iron oxide and high-pressure steam. 

***

By the third pressure cycle, the lemon fog is so thick I can no longer see my own knees. 

This is when the hunger comes. It is not a sharp pain, but a heavy, slow thickening in the back of my throat, where my second tongue lives—that dark, sponge-like filter that bridges the windpipe. 

I crawl to the edge of the canal and hook my long toes into the expansion joints. I lift my face toward the high zinc ceiling. 

The air is cold—cold enough to crack glass—but it is rich. Every exhaust fan in the regional basin is pulling the wet, greasy air toward the north shafts. The draft is immense, a solid wall of yellow wind that smells of paraffin, unburnt diesel, and the sweet, rotten smell of the wire-weed that hangs from the high gantries.

I open my mouth. 

I do not breathe as the dry-land things once did. I let the wind blow into my throat. The sponge-tongue swells, turning black as it drinks the grease-mist. I can taste the history of the machines above: a drop of grease from a gantry crane that was lubricated during the last century; the fine, metallic dust of a bronze gear wearing down in the high winches; the bitter salt of the glaze-lice as they scraper-feed on the copper lines.

It takes ten minutes for the filter to fill. When it is done, it is a heavy, sweet paste. I swallow. It goes down into my three stomachs, slow as cold tar, where the bacteria of the sump—the only things here that do not have metal or rubber in their skin—turn it into warmth. 

My heart, which had slowed to four beats a minute during the cold-peak, begins to thump again. It sounds like a piston in an old pump-housing. 

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

***

Today, I do not stay on the deck. 

I have my lead bar, but I do not use it to tap. I have tucked it into my wire-weed belt because I am going up. 

Historically, we do not go into the "sky." The Silt-Keepers belong to the concrete, where the vibration is true and the corners are square. But I have a calcified joint in my left wrist that does not like the dampness of the floor anymore, and the high gantries are dry and warm with the heat of the steam-lines.

I begin the climb on the south pier of Bay 12. 

The ladder is made of square-section iron bars, socketed directly into the brutalist concrete. Every ten feet, the timber forms have left their horizontal ridges, and I use them as toe-holds to save my arms. The concrete is cold against my belly, but it is a familiar cold—the cold of a mountain that does not know it is made of lime and slag.

At two hundred feet, the fog begins to thin. 

Here, the light is different. It is not the dull, yellow wash of the sodium lamps below, but a strange, shifting violet-grey, cast by the corona discharges along the high-tension lines. The cables are three inches thick, suspended from massive porcelain insulators that look like stacks of grey plates. They hum. They do not hum like the engines; it is a high, dry, whistling sound, like wind passing through a dead pine forest.

*Zzzzz-hiss. Zzzzz-hiss.*

I stop on a maintenance platform. It is made of perforated iron grate, and when I look down, the floor of the basin is gone. There is only a sea of yellow wool, divided here and there by the concrete bulkheads like the walls of a drowned city. 

A Glaze-Louse is sitting on the iron handrail. 

It is the size of my chest, a flat, oval plate of lead-grey pewter with twenty pairs of short, hook-tips. It is scraping the zinc-oxide off the rail with its vibrating belly. The sound is like a knife scraping a plate, but to me, through the bones of my arms, it is a small, neat song about salt.

I do not disturb it. We are the same, in a way. We both have the zinc-taste in our teeth.

***

At three hundred and fifty feet, I reach the gantry network. 

The heat is here. It comes off the main steam-bypass line—a six-foot pipe wrapped in grey asbestos blankets that have turned black with oil-soot. It is so hot that the condensation does not weep; it spits and hisses as it hits the metal, leaving white crusts of lime that look like tiny, calcified teeth along the seams.

I crawl onto the steam-pipe. 

This is where the Spinners live. I can see one now, suspended between two steel web-braces. It looks like a triangular chunk of boiler plate that has fallen and gotten stuck, but as I watch, its three pairs of magnetic feet release with a series of tiny *tinks*—*tink, tink, tink*—and it slides six inches to the left.

It is weaving. 

From its rear plates, it is pulling a single, grey thread of wire-weed. The thread is sticky, glittering with droplets of heavy lubricating oil it has harvested from the gantry cranes above. The line drops down, down, down, into the yellow fog below, a single, long hair designed to catch the breath of the sump.

I lie down on the asbestos blanket of the steam-pipe. 

The heat goes into my belly, through the grease-skin, into my cold bones. My calcified wrist stops aching. I reach out and touch the wire-weed line the Spinner has just laid.

It is vibrating. 

It is not vibrating with the pumps or the turbines. It is too high, too light. It has caught the vibration of the sky-ceiling—the vast, corrugated zinc sheets four hundred feet up, which are shivering under the weight of the exhaust fans. 

The sky is singing. 

It is a terrible, thin sound, like a saw blade being bent in the dark. It sings of the outside—though we do not have a word for *outside*, only *The Place Where the Fan-Shafts Go*. It sings of a cold, dry emptiness that has no concrete to hold it, no iron to guide it, no water to keep it moving.

I press my forehead against the iron support of the gantry. 

The Silt-Keepers below are tapping now. I can feel them through the five miles of structural steel that connect this bay to the next. They are telling each other that the fifth drainage weir is clear of silt. They are telling each other that the forty-minute cycle is turning, that the fog is going back into the floor.

*Clink. Clink. Clink. Shhh-ti.*

I do not reply. 

I lie on the warm asbestos, my grey toes dangling over the four-hundred-foot drop, my three-toed feet catching the rising heat. I close my milky eyes. I do not need to see. The world is entirely assembled, and every joint is tight. The steam is hot; the water is cold; the sump is flowing. 

Above me, the Spinner clicks its magnetic feet. Below me, the eels slide through the iron grates. And between us, the wire-weed shivers in the draft, catching the grease, keeping the air clean for another forty minutes.
