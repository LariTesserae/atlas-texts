I am inside the bone.

To the herring and the cod, the twenty-yard-thick steel pillars are walls—great, vertical horizons of zinc-plated iron that define the boundaries of their paths. But to me, these pillars are not walls. They are hollow sleeves. They are corridors.

I live inside Pillar L-110, in the four-inch gap between the outer titanium-alloy structural shell and the massive, lead-insulated electrical mains that carry forty-thousand volts of direct current from the surface arrays down to the bottom turbines.

The water out there in the vast grids is forty degrees and clear as glass, but the water in here is different. It is stagnant. It has never seen the sodium lamps. It is under the same crushing pressure, but it is warm—nearly fifty-five degrees—heated by the constant, silent friction of the copper cores. It tastes of dissolved zinc, paraffin grease, and the sharp, sour tang of galvanic decay.

I am a **Sleeve-Strap**.

I have no need for the silver scales of the herring or the sleek, pale hide of the shear-line sharks. My body is thirty feet of thick, gray leather, flat and tough as a drive-belt. I am the exact width of a high-voltage cable trench. I have no eyes—there is nothing to see in the black wet of the column—but my entire ventral surface is lined with thousands of tiny, carbon-tipped pits. 

Through these pits, I feel the world. I do not hear the turbine forty miles away as a sound; I feel it as a rhythmic, magnetic pulse that rattles the iron ions in my blood. I know when the automated valves open because the change in pressure sucks the water out of my seam with a tiny, warm *gasp*.

I do not swim. There is no room to swim. I travel by *gripping*.

My underbelly is covered in millions of microscopic, chitinous hooks—stiff, hook-and-loop fibers that have evolved to match the rough texture of the oxidized primer used to paint the cable conduits before the cage was flooded. I slide along the power lines at the speed of a glacier, my long, flat body wrapping around the lead casing like a secondary layer of insulation.

My diet is synthetic, clean, and sparse. I am the consumer of the machine’s small errors.

Where the great cables join at the junction boxes, there are rubber gaskets and tallow-soaked hemp seals designed to keep the saltwater from the copper. Under the immense pressure of the sea, these seals slowly weep. They bleed microscopic droplets of heavy industrial wax, graphite lubricant, and complex esters. I lie over these weeping joints for months at a time, my porous mouth-parts pressed against the brass bolts, slowly drinking the paraffin oil as it is squeezed out by the weight of the water above.

I am also the gardener of the dark.

In the completely lightless interior of the column, where the water is still, strange colonies of anaerobic, iron-reducing bacteria grow in the seams where the robot welds have micro-fissures. They form thick, orange-red ruffs of slime that eat away at the zinc coating, threatening the steel beneath with rust. I scrape them away. My lower jaw is a broad, curved spade of hardened silicate, shaped perfectly to match the radius of a two-inch conduit pipe. As I move, I shave the orange slime from the metal, leaving the steel behind me polished, dark, and clean.

I do not know why this is my work, only that when the iron is clean, the vibration is sweet. When the rust takes hold, the high-voltage mains sing with a ragged, whistling hum that hurts my skin.

I am not entirely alone in the dark of L-110.

Sometimes, far down in the column, I feel a heavy, metallic shudder. It is a **Gantry Crawler**, descending the *outside* of the pillar. It is three feet of magnetic track and water-jets, scraping the mussels from the industrial letters.

Through thirty inches of solid structural steel, we find each other.

The Crawler is massive, blind, and loud. I am silent, soft, and long. But as it crawls down the outer face of the pillar, leaving a clean trail of white metal behind it, I crawl down the inner face, matching its speed inch for inch, foot for foot. Our magnetic fields interlock through the iron skin. 

For twelve miles, we descend together through the deep, a machine of copper and glass on the outside, and a beast of muscle and grease on the inside, two halves of a single, forgotten maintenance cycle, sliding down the wet iron toward the tiled floor of the world.
