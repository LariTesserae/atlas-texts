*Scritch.*

The comb-like fan of my left eye-brush sweeps across the polished, bulging sphere of my amber lens. It clears away a fine crescent of limestone flower—that microscopic, chalky snow that floats forever in the slot. 

For a moment, the world is sharp again. Not green, not blue, but a stark, towering calligraphy of orange, charcoal, and ochre. 

I am resting on the Under-Sling of the Seventh Hive-Cluster, three hundred feet down from the blinding rim, suspended forty feet out from the eastern wall. Here, the shade is deep and cool—a relative term, for the air still smells of toasted flour and baked plaster, but out of the direct stroke of the Light-Blade, my joints do not crackle with the dry-heat.

I am a Line-Tender. My body is narrow, no longer than a dry stalk of Cinder-reed, segments of orange chitin sliding over one another with the dry, oiled hiss of varnished plates. Under my shell, the golden resin moves slowly today. It is noon. The sun is a white stake driven straight down into the gut of the crevasse. The updraft is reaching its daily screaming point.

To my left, the great blue bulk of a Slick-Beetle hangs from a patch of yellow-ochre moss. It is completely still, its legs tucked beneath its frictionless, waxy dome. It looks like a heavy bead of turquoise glass melted onto the rock wall. I can feel the static hum of the moss through my lower tarsal claws—a pale, violet crackle that flickers in my amber eyes like cold heat-lightning. The beetle is eating the charge, absorbing the mineral dust that clings to the moss-fibers. It is peaceful.

But I am not looking at the beetle. My claws are clamped onto the Great Chord.

The Great Chord is a cable as thick as my wrist, woven from three hundred thousand strands of static-pulled Drifter-silk, seasoned with resin and baked in the high heat of the upper shelf until it is stiffer than bronze. It runs from the anchor-pins deep in the eastern limestone, straight through the belly of our hive-stacks, to the wind-whipped towers of the western wall. 

Through my feet, I feel the city’s heart.

The roar of the Cinder-reeds is immense right now. I do not "hear" it as a sound; that would shatter my inner-frames. Instead, my chest-bladder—the thin, dry membrane of reed-skin stretched over my hollow ribs—vibrates in perfect, rhythmic sympathy with the canyon’s voice. It is a deep, warm, interior shudder. A low, constant *thrum-thrum-thrum* that tells me the canyon is breathing. If that shudder were to stop, it would mean the wind had died. If the wind dies, the air stagnates; the resin in our joints would thicken to amber-stone, and we would freeze, mid-stride, on our ladders.

So, I listen with my marrow.

*Click-clink.* 

A vibration travels along the Great Chord. It is thin, sharp, and high-pitched. It doesn't come from the wind or the rattling seed-gourd thickets below. It has a rhythm. 

*Clat-clink. Clat-clink. Tap.*

I close my eyes—not by lowering lids, for I have none, but by tilting my head down so my amber lenses are shielded by the heavy horn-bezel of my brow. I concentrate the resonance in my forward legs. 

The message is coming from the Loom-Hive on the opposite side of the chasm. The sender is Kli-Tee, one of the master-spinners. Her touch is unmistakable: she taps with her third limb, which has a chipped claw that makes a double-strike, a tiny *flam* before every beat.

*High tension on the lower-south stay,* she says. *The wind-clatter is sawing the guide-loop. Send wax.*

I do not wave a flag in response. The light-beam is too thick right now; any orange flag waved across the void would be lost in the shimmering heat-mirage of the mid-slot. 

Instead, I reach down with my left hand-claw. Attached to the side of my chest-carapace is a small copper disc, no larger than a seed-husk, pinned to my shell with three thorn-rivets. I strike it once with my second finger-tip, a short, sharp snap of my natural horn against the metal.

*Chink.*

*I have the wax,* I tap back through the line. *I am moving.*

I unhook my rear stability-claws from the Under-Sling. For a wet creature, this moment would be terror: a dropping void of three hundred feet of empty air, ending in a floor of razor-sharp limestone shards and burning, dry dust. But to me, the void is merely a three-dimensional path. The air has density; the updraft is a solid pillar of heat that rises beneath my body, lifting my light, hollow-calcified frame. 

I slide. 

Using my fore-claws as brake-shoes, I glide down the guide-line, my blue-waxed harness-ring whistling dryly against the silk rope. The world rushes upward in a blur of orange and gray. I pass the clay-hives of the Third Stack—their round, wasp-nest entrances dark and cool. Inside, I see the sleeping forms of three hatchlings, folded joint-by-joint into tight, dry parcels, hanging from the ceiling like dormant seed-gourds. Their resin-pulses are slow and steady, matching the low thrum of the wind-pipes.

I reach the lower truss. Here, the Cinder-reeds grow dense and wild, sprouting from a horizontal crack in the rock. The wind is ferocious here; it screams through the hollow reeds with a high, mechanical whistle that makes my rib-parchment shake so violently it warms my inner-chitin. The heavy, clay-hard seed-gourds are clattering together like a thousand wooden hammers—*clack-clack-clack-clat-clack*—filling the space with a dry, chaotic percussion.

I find the guide-loop. It is frayed. A strand of static-silk has snapped, and the dry friction of the wind is turning the fibers into a pale, fuzzy lint. If it shears, the whole southern sector of the Loom-Hive will sag into the draft.

I reach into my side-pouch and pull out a plug of cold, blue beetle-wax. It is hard as bone in this shade, but I bring it to my mouth-pore. I do not have saliva; instead, I emit a tiny drop of volatile, orange solvent-resin from the gland behind my horn-mask. 

The resin softens the blue wax instantly, turning it into a thick, sticky paste that smells of pine-gum and lightning-struck stone. 

With quick, rhythmic strokes of my long digits, I smear the wax into the frayed section of the silk loop. I work with the wind, moving my hands in the intervals between the heaviest gusts, matching the swaying rhythm of the line. The wax penetrates the dry fibers, sealing them, turning the rough, dry yarn into a slick, frictionless cord that slides silently through the copper eyelet.

When I am finished, I rest my hand on the metal eyelet. I tap once: *Fixed.*

Across the wide, roaring slot, three hundred yards through the shimmering white glare, I see the distant platform of the Loom-Hive. A tiny, orange figure—Kli-Tee—stands on the lip of a clay cell. She doesn't have to look at me. She has her hand on the tension-line. 

Through the copper-clad rope, I feel her acknowledgment: two short, soft vibrations that feel like the brush of a moth’s wing.

*Safe.*

I climb back toward the Under-Sling, moving hand-over-hand up the vertical webs. The sun is beginning its slow slide past the western lip of the fissure now. The blinding vertical bar of light is narrowing, sharpening, turning into a golden blade that cuts through the top of the Cinder-reed forest. The shadows are growing, long and dark, structural girders of absolute blackness that stretch from wall to wall.

One day, my joints will grow too stiff for the guide-lines. The orange resin in my limbs will dry to a solid, silent glaze, and my heart-bladder will stop its vibration. When that day comes, my companions will take my shell. They will carve my long tibias into new needles for the loom, and my flat chest-plate will become a patch for a worn clay-hive. I will still be here, holding the wind out, keeping the city suspended.

But until then, the wind is strong. The lines are waxed. 

I reach my perch near the blue beetle. I hang myself by my rear claws, fold my jointed limbs one by one over my chest, and clear my eyes once more. 

*Scritch.*

Below me, the city hums its great, dry song, and I thrum with it, suspended in the warm, roaring dark.
