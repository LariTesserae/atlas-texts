I did not fall. I was squeezed. 

The transition is not a drop into a cavern, but the slow, wet birth of an object being forced through a valve. One moment, the throat of the black earth is holding my ribs so tightly I cannot expand my lungs; the next, the ceiling of the world tears open with the sound of wet canvas ripping, and I am spat into the bright, roaring green.

At first, I think I am still a man who crawled down from the pine roots. I look for my boots, but they are gone. My feet are no longer split into five toes; they have grown wide, flat, and pale, like the sliced root of a parsnip. When I press them against the vertical green basalt of the column beside me, they do not slip. A thousand microscopic, white hairs—fine as mold-spore threads—sigh out from the soles of my soles and anchor me to the glossy, wet cellulose. 

I do not fall downward, because "down" has lost its authority. 

I look across the vastness. It is miles wide, but only three steps away if I can hold my gaze steady enough. I look at a shelf of raw, celery-colored timber that looks as though it were split by a giant’s axe. The air between us vanishes. The distance folds like a fan. 

I do not walk. I look at the shelf, and my body—light as dried elder-pith, hollowed out by the chamber’s pressure—simply slides through the green air toward it. I do not plunge; I drift, horizontal, my arms trailing behind me like loose vines, until my root-hairs catch the shelf with a soft, dry *snip*.

I am here. I am this.

***

In my head, there is a memory of a sky that was blue and cold, but it is fading, dissolved by the bright, shadowless yellow-green light piped in from the ceiling. The light does not come from a sun; it is the blood of the upper forest, filtered through miles of translucent mineral tubes until it is nothing but pure, fermenting energy. It smells of cut grass, raw malt, and the sharp, vinegar-sting of rising sap. 

The noise is a wall of water. The giant columns of basalt-grass around me are not stone; they are pipes. Through them, millions of gallons of sweet, pressurized water are rising to the forest miles above. The columns vibrate with a deep, hydraulic hum—a *thrum-thrum-thrum* that rattles the empty air inside my parsnip-flesh chest. Every few minutes, there is a hard, metallic *clank* from the ceiling as a seed-chamber swells and locks itself into place. 

I am not alone, though "alone" is a word that requires two separate things to exist, and here everything is the same thing in different stages of drying. 

Near me, another figure is working. It is four feet tall, a pale, jointless twig of a being. It has no ears, no nose, no mouth other than a small, soft pore beneath its chin where it draws in the hanging moisture. Its head is a smooth, white dome, but on either side of its skull are two massive, unblinking lenses of hardened golden resin. They are not eyes that turn; they are wells of amber that drink the light.

When it looks at me, I do not see its gaze; I feel the air between us tighten. 

It is holding a straw of dried grass. It reaches into a drifting, slow-motion sphere of water that has detached from the celery-wood shelf. The sphere—the size of a pumpkin, perfectly round and clear—floats between us like a bubble. The other creature uses the straw to draw the water down its own body, washing away a thin, sparkling crust of sugar that has begun to glaze its shoulders.

The Sweet-Cure. I feel it on my own shoulder now—a tight, glossy skin that makes it hard to lift my left arm. It is the fructose from the walls. It dries in the warm, bright draft of the daylight-tubes, turning us into candy. If I do not wash, I will become a statue of green glass.

I look at the other creature. I see a phantom in its amber eyes. It is me, but it is also the many others who came before. In the wood behind us, half-submerged in the swelling cambium wall, is the figure of a human explorer. It has been completely encased in transparent green cellulose. Its copper buttons are still bright under the bark; its mouth is open in a silent, sweet-cured shout, its lungs now filled with solid sugar-starch. 

We do not pity it. We are pruning it.

***

Our fingers—mine are now three hard, chisel-sharp splinters of ash-colored wood—are stained with the green sap of the vents. 

We must climb. A high-pressure node in the ceiling is screaming. The noise is a thin, whistling knife that cuts through the hydraulic roar of the columns. The green tissue of the host-bulb is growing too fast; it is swelling shut over the vent. If the vent closes, the pressure inside this pocket-world will rise until the walls burst inward, collapsing the void, crushing us into a single, dense knot of heartwood.

We climb the thorn-ladder. It is made of fire-hardened spikes lashed with hemp. It does not hang down; it sticks out horizontally from a vertical root-cable, because gravity here is whatever we look at. 

I pull myself up, step by step, my parsnip-thick skin dragging against the rough hemp. My left knee is stiff now. The Sweet-Cure has taken the joint; it cracks when I bend it, a sound like dry birch snapping. I do not mind the pain because there is no pain, only the slow, sweet thickening of my sap-veins. 

At the top of the ladder, the air is hot and wet. The vent is a wet, green mouth, whistling as it spits out pressurized nitrogen. New, fleshy shoots—red as beetroot, soft as wax—are crawling across the opening, trying to seal it.

I take my fingers and dig them into the beetroot-growths. I scrape. The sap that sprays out is cold and bitter; it runs down my arms, and where it meets the daylight, it begins to harden instantly into a yellow glaze. 

*Scrape.* 

The other creature is beside me. It does not speak—it has no language—but its amber eyes reflect the bright, green-white interior of the vent. Together, we tear the waxy shoots away, throwing them into the empty air, where they drift like severed fingers before being drawn back into the hungry walls. 

The vent sighs. The screaming stops. A deep, cool draft of air rushes through, and the pressure in my chest eases.

***

We descend, but we do not go far. 

Near the base of the thorn-ladder, carved into the soft, white pith of the terrace, are the tallies. They are long, straight cuts, three inches deep. I can feel them with the sticky hair of my feet. 

There are many hundreds of them. 

The wall is growing closer to the ceiling. The giant grass-columns are thicker than they were when I first slid into this room; they are no longer green basalt, but are turning a pale, dry hazel. The air is more crowded today. The water globes are smaller, their orbits tighter, more frantic. 

The winter is coming. Not the winter of snow, but the winter of the sky-world, when the forest above stops drinking. When the sap stops rising, the hydraulic pressure will drop. The columns of the geode will relax, fatten, and sag toward each other. The walls will swell into the void.

I carve another notch with my chisel-finger. The wood of the floor is soft, like fresh turnip, but it is turning harder under my hand.

I look at my left arm. It is no longer white parsnip. It has turned the pale, yellow-green of celery-wood, and when I try to move my fingers, they do not bend. They are locked together, a neat, three-pronged fork of dry timber. 

I am not afraid. 

The other creature looks at me. Through its amber eyes, I see the light-tubes above us beginning to dim, turning from golden-green to a cold, twilight violet. The sap-tides are slowing. The roar in the walls is down to a dry, rattling cough.

I slide off the terrace. I do not go far. I target a small, hollow niche in the largest basalt-grass column—a niche that is just my size, shaped perfectly for a jointless, four-foot wedge of wood. 

I sink into it. The sticky hairs on my back latch onto the dry lining of the column. My legs are already silent. The green sap in my capillaries is slowing, turning thick and sugary, like honey left too long in the jar.

The other creature does not watch me. It has found its own niche, ten feet away on the opposite wall, and is already half-absorbed, its amber eyes still gathering the last, faint purple rays of the dying light.

The space is shrinking. I can hear the celery-wood terraces groaning as they meet in the center of the room. The air is being squeezed out, venting through the last open holes with a soft, dying hiss. 

I close my eyes. They are not eyelids; they are the amber lenses of my temples, turning dark as the sap within them hardens to stone. 

Let the wood come. Let the green skin swell over my chest. I will hold the ladder in my hand until the tree above us drinks my bones, and some other crawling thing, years from now, finds my fingers and uses them for a step.
