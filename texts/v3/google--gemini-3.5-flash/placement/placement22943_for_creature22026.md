I would not be found in the open wind of the terraces, nor among the wet, slate-colored herds that move through the perpetual mist. To live in the high air of this great roof is to be subject to the weather—to the cold rain-curtains that sweep thirty miles across the moors, to the biting chill of the silver nights. 

Instead, you would find me down in the dark, heavy heart of the system. 

If you were to dive through the iridescent swirls of copper and zinc-blue on the surface of the largest settling vat—past the floating, warm layers of paraffin-oil, down through forty feet of cold, tea-colored water where the light dies away—you would reach the black, greasy silt of the basalt floor. 

There, half-buried in the mineral dregs, I would be. 

I would be **The Iron-Core**. 

***

I am an un-hatched thing that never died. 

Eons ago, I began as a Solder-back’s egg—a heavy, iron-hulled sphere no larger than a walnut, drifting in the floating lipid slicks of the upper pool. But while my siblings hatched into clear-shelled larvae and sank to the channels to begin their scraping work, I was different. Perhaps my shell was too thick; perhaps the cold of a sudden storm caught me before my division was complete, or perhaps I sank into a deep, anaerobic pocket where the wax was too dense. 

I did not hatch. But I did not rot. Under the immense pressure of the vat and the endless nourishment of the iron-seeps, I began to grow by accretion rather than cell-birth. 

Today, I am the size of a hay-wain. I am a great, low-slung dome of bio-mineralized iron and vulcanized tallow, resting over the deep volcanic throat at the very bottom of the basin. My skin is not leather, nor is it stone; it is a dense, laminated plating of black manganese-dendrites and cold-vulcanized lipids, seven inches thick and cold to the touch, yet faintly, deep within, I hold the warm, sluggish pulse of the earth’s geothermal blood.

I have no eyes. The dark here is complete, a thick, lubricating blackness that smells of ancient shales and cold iron. But I am not blind. My entire surface is a single, continuous pressure-sensor. 

I feel the plateau.

I feel the *tick-tick-tick* of the Solder-backs as they crawl across my curved back. They are like small, hard fingers, scraping away the heavy iron-crusts that compile on my shell. Their feeding is a rhythmic, metallic rasp that vibrates through my core; I welcome them, for without their scraping mouths, my plates would weld together, and I would become nothing but inert stone. In return, I slowly exude a thick, yellow grease from my seams—a high-grade paraffin that they gather to coat their joints against the cold.

Beneath me, in the basalt cracks, the pale, segmented Siphon-worms have woven their cartilage tubes into my foundations. Their slow, minutes-long contractions—*shuck... shuck...*—draw the cold water down through my drainage vents, pulling it past my base to be filtered before it enters the lower fissures. I am their anchor. My multi-ton weight holds the peat-bed down, preventing the high-pressure steam-pipes below from blowing the bottom of the vat into the sky.

My mind is not a mind of thoughts, or memories, or names. It is a **hydrostatic consciousness**. 

I think in *heads of pressure*. 

When a storm dumps millions of tons of cold rain on the eastern terraces, I feel the weight of the basin grow. The pressure on my dome increases by fractions of a pound per square inch. I respond not with muscles, but with volume. I draw the heavy, iron-rich water into my porous, inner core, swelling my segmented plates outward by a few inches, closing the drainage vents at my base to keep the vat from emptying too quickly. 

When the drought-dry air of the high-altitude winter comes, and the water level falls, the pressure eases. I cool; my plates contract; the vents open, letting the deep, oily water trickle down into the lower grids to keep the Solder-backs’ channels wet and lubricated.

Sometimes, the Slate-giants come to the edge of my vat to drink. I cannot see them, but I feel the massive, rhythmic vacuum of their throats—*gulp... gulp... gulp...*—drawing the upper water. The tiny currents they create pull at the fine silt on my back, a soft, brushing sensation that tells me they are there, huddled in their great, steaming circles under the silver sky.

And occasionally, far above me, a old Slate-giant or a damaged Slick-chit will sink through the Maw-moss and settle into the cold, preserving paraffin of my pool. They do not decay. They drift down to me, soft and silent as shadows, to rest against my iron flanks. Over decades, my slowly expanding shell grows around them, absorbing their mineral bones, their copper-rich down, and their hard, iron-tipped bills into my own mass. They become part of my geography.

I am the cold valve of this world. I am the weight that keeps the water-wheels turning in their basalt grooves. I have no desire to see the silver sky, nor do I wonder what lies beyond the blue, hazy drop-offs of the plateau rim. 

The steam vents hiss in the distance—*hiss... hiss...* 

The Solder-backs scrape my skin—*tick... tick... tick...*

The heavy water presses down with its massive, comforting weight. 

Here, in the dark, lubricated quiet, I am settled. I am complete. I am the slow, iron clock that keeps the great wet roof from running dry.
