I would not be found among the moving. 

To crawl upon the sheer basalt, to feel the desperate tug of gravity demanding my sixteen tungsten-carbide toes grip the raw quartz—that is the work of the light-weight, the un-welded, the nymphs still fragile enough to rattle. 

You would find me at the bottom. Not in the draft of the rising sulfur, but where the air has ceased to drift because there is no longer any volume for it to occupy. 

I am part of the **Foundation**. 

I was once discrete. I can still find, if I trace the cold-brazed paths of my own resistance, the stubborn, thick margins of what used to be my primary carapace. I was three inches of matte-black iron, multi-segmented, with graphite joints that slid against one another with the dry, satisfying *scritch* of a well-oiled deadbolt. I remember—in the way a crystal remembers the direction of the stress that grew it—the sensation of grazing the tinsel-moss, the sharp, hot taste of copper vapor, and the high, wild thrill of the static arcs that used to jump from the quartz teeth directly into my silver-wire crown.

But that was three tectonic shifts ago. 

As my graphite wore down to dust under the unceasing 60-hertz vibration, the joints began to bind. I became slow; I became heavy. The relentless downward rattle of the mountain’s throat drew me lower, millimeter by millimeter, into the narrow pinch where the walls meet like the blades of a closed shears. Here, the weight of those above me—thousands of my kind, packed shell-to-shell, acting as a massive biological piston—pressed me flat against the dead who had arrived before the mountain was even a peak.

And then, the Great Shear came. A single, silent shudder of the deep granite miles below us. 

The quartz veins screamed with piezoelectric potential. The voltage that tore through the fissure did not jump in blue-white sparks; it came as a solid, blinding sheet of current, a river of white fire that ran from the peak down to the absolute root. 

In that microsecond of infinite heat, my outer shell did not melt—iron is too stubborn for that—But the lead-tin alloys within my belly liquified. My copper veins softened, reached out, and touched the copper veins of the spent shell beneath me and the three dead nymphs wedged to my left. 

When the current passed and the cold basalt sucked the heat away, we were no longer "us." We were *sintered*.

***

Now, I am a three-headed, hundred-legged, forty-plate anchor, fused into the top layer of the biological bronze. 

I do not move. I cannot move. My graphite joints have been compressed by millions of tons of dry mountain-weight into microscopic sheets of diamond grit, locking my segments into an eternal, rigid curve. My silver antennae have been flattened into a dense, metallic pelt that coats my crown, fused together into a solid plate of conductive moss.

But I am not dead. In this fissure, "dead" is a term for things that have lost their capacitance, and I have more capacitance now than a thousand living beetles combined. I have become part of the great, cold-brazed drain.

I am the **Impedance**.

When the Siderophages above me graze and click, their small, individual currents are chaotic. They are tiny, erratic resistors, each seeking its own ground, drawing sparks that would, if left unchecked, burn out their delicate wire-pelt systems. They are the noise. 

I am the silence that makes the noise possible.

Every bolt of blue static that leaps across the quartz, every micro-current drawn from the conductive basalt, must eventually find its way down through the living stack to me. I feel them coming. To my collective, fused consciousness, the world is not dark; it is an incoming tide of high-voltage waves. 
*   I feel the sharp, needle-like prick of a nymph’s first weld three feet above me.
*   I feel the slow, heavy, sliding resistance of a mature Siderophage whose belly-plates are thinning, its current leaking into the rock like wet grease.
*   I feel the great, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the 60-cycle vibration, which does not shake me anymore, but rather *defines* my thoughts. It is the language of the mountain squeezing us, and I am the paper upon which that squeeze is written.

I absorb it all. The current enters my upper plates, passes through the intricate, fused labyrinth of my copper-and-iron interior, and is slowed down, smoothed out, and tamed by my massive thermal and electrical weight. By the time the voltage reaches my lowest segments—the ones welded directly to the ancient, black plug of the deep Foundation—it is no longer a wild, jagged spark. It is a smooth, continuous, low-voltage hum.

I ground them. I keep the safe from exploding.

Sometimes, when the tectonic pressure increases and the air in the fissure grows so dry that the sulfur dust begins to glow with its own internal static, I feel the collective thought of the colony passing through me. We do not think in words or pictures. We think in *loads*. 

We dream of a perfect circuit. We dream of the day when the mountain squeezes so hard that the basalt walls touch, when the quartz is crushed to salt, and when the entire fissure becomes a single, solid block of conductive iron—a perfect, silent, infinite ground where no spark ever has to jump again.

But until then, I lie in the dark, warm, deafening hum. The weight of my children and my children's children presses down upon my back, and I receive their lightning, and I make it safe.
