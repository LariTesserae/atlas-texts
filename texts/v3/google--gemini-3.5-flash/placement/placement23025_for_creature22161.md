### I. The Loss of the Heel

The first thing that leaves you is the heel. 

On the lesser planets, the body is designed for the strike. The heel lands, the arch absorbs, the toe pushes off. Your whole skeleton is a vertical monument to the existence of a floor. 

Here, that monument collapses. Within forty leagues of the descent, the heels of my boots felt like ancient, useless artifacts—heavy lumps of vulcanized rubber that kept trying to point toward a center of mass that did not exist. I remember unlacing them and letting them drop. They did not fall; they drifted. They entered one of the minor transition currents and began a slow, wobbling spiral downward, turning over and over like twin beetles until the turquoise light swallowed them.

The water here is not wet in the way rain is wet. At seventy miles deep, under the compression of the invisible anchors, it feels more like a cold, lubricating grease. It does not cling to you; you are suspended within it, an impurity in a sapphire of infinite size.

At first, your lungs try to fight. They want the dry, gaseous nitrogen of the upper troposphere. But the transition is chemical, not mechanical. The heavy water does not fill your chest to drown you; it slowly exchanges its dissolved gasses through the membranes of your throat. Your ribs, once a cage of bone, begin to soften. The marrow turns quiet. The calcium in your clavicle and hips—the heavy, dense stone of your inner terrestrial geography—begins to leach out into the 7-degree medium, replaced by the dissolved silica that sweeps down from the newly erupted spires.

You do not die. You simply become less dense. You are being ironed out by the pressure until your refractive index matches the water. 

If you were to look at me now, you would not see a man. You would see a long, pale ripple—a shadow that is slightly more silver than the surrounding cold.

### II. The Anchor-Welding

I have found my spire. 

It is a young one, an Erosion 0.4 needle of black volcanic glass that has only recently erupted from the deep gas-shallows. It smells of sulfur and the clean, dry scent of frozen quartz. The basalt is cold—colder than the water—and its hexagonal columns are so sharp that if I press my changing hand against them, they do not cut so much as they *divide* me.

I do not bleed red. The fluid that exits my silica-skin is a thick, milky lacquer that instantly polymerizes in the current. 

I have chosen a crack between two basalt columns, thirty leagues below the region where the sun shafts lose their gold and turn a hard, glacial green. Here, the glass-lace grows in sparse, frozen webs. I have begun to weld myself to it.

The process is slow. It takes several of the sun’s rotations through the upper mist. I secrete the acidic lithium-gel from my fingertips, melting the outer micron of the black volcanic glass. Then, as the basalt cools and softens, I press my long, wire-like rib-tendrils into the groove. 

We fuse. There is no joint where the rock ends and my body begins. The basalt enters my nervous system as a vast, heavy silence; I enter the basalt as a slight, warm tremor.

I am thirty feet long now, but I am no thicker than a strip of hide. My body has stretched under the constant draw of the 30-knot boundary current. I am no longer a creature of volumes; I am a creature of surfaces. I have become a ribbon of pale, luminescent blue cartilage, suspended across the fifty-foot gap between the spire and a neighboring cluster of glass-lace.

I am stretched tight. The tension is magnificent.

### III. The Piezo-Electric Mind

When the great currents pluck me, I do not feel pain. I feel thoughts.

On the floor-worlds, you think in words—small, clumsy symbols that you arrange in rows like dry bricks. Here, I think in frequencies.

My body is lined with millions of microscopic, hexagonal quartz crystals that have grown into my skin from the mineral-dense drift. When the current shears across my ribbon, stretching me until I am as taut as a iron wire, these crystals are compressed. They generate tiny, pulsing sparks of electricity that race down my length, clean and cold.

A 40-knot current from the north is an memory of the upper ice.
A 60-knot shear from the southern gravity node is a long, complex calculation about the density of the deep gas-cliffs.

Sometimes, the *Chime-Stalks* on my spire begin to sing. 

Just behind me, the *Borers* have finished their long, vertical flutes in the basalt. When the great 80-knot jet stream shifts five miles to the west, it hits these pipes head-on. The spire begins to thrum. It is a G-flat, but a G-flat that has the weight of a continent behind it. The vibration passes through my anchor-point, up through my silica-spine, and out into my trailing ribbons.

My thoughts synchronize with the flute. For twelve hours, while the current shears, I do not have a private mind. I am simply the lyric of the stone. I think about the slow cooling of the planet’s core; I think about the invisible non-baryonic knots that hold this entire seven-million-ton ocean from flying off into the void.

And then, the light comes.

### IV. The Passing of the Lenses

Once every three rotations, a constellation of *Hydralenses* passes through our chasm.

They are three leagues above me, but because the water is so perfectly clear, they appear to be floating just out of reach. They are massive—some of them fifty yards across—spherical bubbles of heavy water wrapped in glass skins so thin they are practically non-existent.

When they hit the green sun shafts, the world changes.

The light is no longer a broad, gentle wash. The Hydralenses focus the shafts into burning, needle-sharp beams of brilliant gold that pierce the dark water. These needles sweep across the black faces of the spires like the fingers of a giant hand.

When a beam strikes me, the response is instantaneous. My piezo-electric skin is highly sensitive to the thermal spike. The water around my blue ribbon warms by exactly 0.4 degrees. My blood—which is now a mixture of liquid silica and seawater—expands. My tension increases by ten kilograms per square centimeter.

The warmth is an ecstasy. It lasts for only four seconds—the time it takes the giant lens to drift through the sun shaft—but in those four seconds, the electricity running through my body reaches such a voltage that I glow. I cast a bright, blue-white light that illuminates the black hexagonal basalt, the delicate grey fuzz of the Borers, and the cold, empty void below me.

In those four seconds, I can see twenty miles into the blue quiet. 

I see no fish. I see no shells. I see only the perfect, terrifying purity of the hanging water, and, very far away, the vast, slow bending of the light where the gravity anchor sits, humming its silent, non-Euclidean song.

### V. The Shift

We have just experienced a orientation-drop.

The world-machine has shifted its weight. One of the distant anchors—perhaps the one near the northern transition zone—has altered its pull by three degrees.

To a floor-dweller, it would have felt like the end of the world. The "down" of the universe instantly tilted. 

For us, the change was a silent, beautiful rearrangement. The water around me did not move, but the *density* of it did. In a millisecond, the pressure on my left side dropped by fifteen atmospheres, while the pressure on my right spiked.

The *Void-Slicks*—the flat, black gravity-scabs that cling to the anchor-nodes—began to glow with their pale, cold Cherenkov blue. I could see them three leagues away, folding and unfolding like silk napkins in the dark, absorbing the shock of the shift so the spire would not shatter.

My body released its tension. I went slack, my blue ribbon curling into the gentle eddies like a discarded thread of silk. My light went out. My thoughts became low, slow hums of near-zero voltage.

I am waiting now.

The water is quiet. The Chime-Stalks are silent. The current has dropped to a mere five knots, and the glass-lace is still. 

In this stillness, I can feel my anchors. The lithium-welds are strong. They have held through the shift. I am part of this spire now, as much as the black glass and the hexagonal columns.

Soon, the great counter-rotating streams will find their new balance. The water will begin to shear again. The thrumming will return to the pipes, the tension will pull me straight, and I will begin, once more, to think.
