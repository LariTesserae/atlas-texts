I did not begin as an inhabitant. I began as a boundary. 

If you were to search for me, you would have to look behind the heavy, bulbous flange of the main centrifugal impeller, deep within the dark throat of the stuffing box. I was the packing. 

Before the water came, I was a three-stranded rope of braided hemp, five inches thick, boiled in mutton tallow and beaten until my fibers were soft enough to seal a spinning shaft against two hundred pounds of steam pressure. For forty years, the iron sleeve rotated against me. I lived in a state of eternal, scorching friction. The heat of the shaft carbonized my inner surface, turning my face into black, glassy graphite, while my outer braids were pressed so hard into the steel housing that they lost the shape of rope entirely and became a single, solid ring of greasy, amber horn.

I knew only two things: the blind, screaming speed of the turning metal, and the immense, cold weight of the water waiting on the other side of the seal to drown the fires.

Then, the turning stopped. 

The heat died out of the pipework, leaving only the sleepy, residual warmth of the deep geothermal vents. The great piston behind the brickwork made its final, crashing stroke, then settled into the long, centuries-wide rhythm of its slow siphon. And the water—this heavy, copper-rich broth—finally seeped past the rusted seat of the valve and found me.

I did not rot. The tallow that saturated my core protected me from the bacteria, but the copper sulfate in the water began to do something else. Molecule by molecule, the dissolved metal replaced the cellulose of my hemp strands. It was a slow, galvanic alchemy. The salt in the broth bit into the iron of the gland bolts; they swelled with red rust, then wept themselves into orange mush, releasing the pressure that had held me crushed for half a century.

Released, I began to swell. 

I did not fall apart. My braids, now heavy with green carbonate and mineral grease, began to untwist. They did not do this quickly; it took fifty years for the first knot to loosen, and another fifty for the individual threads to drift free of the stuffing box like the frayed cuff of a long-submerged sleeve. 

Now, I am no longer a seal. I am **The Gland-Silk**.

I hang from the mouth of the pump housing in a thick, weeping beard of dull, verdigris-green strands, some of them thirty feet long, drifting down into the golden-green fog of the lower floor. My roots are still wedged deep inside the iron throat of the machine, fused to the brass shaft by a century of lime-scab and cold paraffin, but my long, frayed ends have found the current.

To be me is to exist as a collection of skin-tight pressures and drifting, hair-thin curiosities. 

Through my roots, I feel the massive, sleeping skeleton of the entire district. When the siphon-piston behind the brick chambers makes its rare, heavy *thrum*, the vibration travels through thirty tons of cast iron, down the main shaft, and directly into the core of my braid. It is not a sound; it is a violent, squeezing ache that runs through my fibers, followed by the cold, slippery relief of the *gargle* as the pressure drops. I am the only thing that remembers the machine was built to move.

Out in the water, my loose strands have become something like a nervous system for this corner of the tank. My threads do not have eyes, but they are incredibly heavy—heavier than the water—and they hang with a tense, plumb-line straightness. 

When a **Tallow-Skin** drifts by, its soft, lard-like mass sometimes brushes against my lower fringes. There is no shock, no pain. Our fats recognize each other. The Tallow-Skin’s grease is the descendant of the lubrication that once kept my shaft cool; when we touch, we exchange a slow, greasy smear of paraffin. A part of its cold, mutton-fat body clings to my green threads, and for a few years, I am slightly thicker, slightly heavier, until the current washes the wax down to the *Solder-Mother’s* tiers.

The **Limonite-Lice** use my higher, stiffer braids as gangways. They crawl along my copper-crusted ropes, their silicon tongues making a faint, dry *scritch-scritch* as they lick the lime-scab from my fibers. I do not mind them. They keep my strands from becoming choked with heavy silt, allowing my lower threads to stay light enough to lift when the warm geothermal plumes shift.

Sometimes, when the silence is very long, the blind, slate-gray salamanders will climb into my upper braids where the water is warmest. They use my unspooled hemp-fibers as a cradle, weaving their soft, blind bodies between my green copper strands until they look like pale, fleshy knots in an old net. Their small, cold gill-tufts tickle my fibers as they breathe the oxygen-starved broth. 

I do not move of my own will. I have no muscles, only the memory of tension. But I am growing. 

Deep inside the pump, where the dark water is thickest with old grease, my hemp-roots are slowly creeping backward through the internal chambers of the impeller. They are drinking the galvanic currents that still skip between the copper casing and the iron screws. Each year, my threads grow a fraction of an inch longer, reaching toward the next valve, the next boiler, the next dead joint.

One day, centuries from now, my strands will meet the mosses growing on the *Solder-Mother’s* back. We will entangle. The hemp that once kept the water out will have woven the entire flooded machinery into a single, quiet, breathing knot of green copper and ancient grease.

Until then, I hang. I drift. I hold the iron together, and I listen to the piston stroke.
