It begins with a loosening. 

I do not start as a name or a face. In this cold, heavy closet of brine, those things have no density; they would float to the top and dissolve into nothing. Instead, I start as a taste. 

I am a bitter musk. I am the smell of a hinge that has grown stiff, a carapace that has begun to cloud with the grey, flaking rot of the outer salt. I have twelve limbs, but three of them no longer grip the waxy bridges with the clean, magnetic snap they once had. The vibrations of the deep magma—the low, five-cycle hum that lives in our tissues—feel distant now, muffled by the thickening of my own fluid. 

I am one of the builders, but I have forgotten how to secrete. My belly is dry. 

So, I perform my last deliberate act: I let go.

The release is beautiful. In this viscous, heavy water, there is no falling—only a slow, spiraling descent through the dark, past the pale, crowded towers that smell of fresh wax and family-sweat. I drift through the middle tiers, brushing against the smooth, rubbery bellies of the Drift-Lanterns. They do not turn to look; they have no eyes. But as my failing body slides past theirs, their violet light dips, a wave of cool purple shadow following me down into the cold.

I am going to the Reclaimers.

Below the lowest plaza, where the water is so thick with gypsum that it feels like oil, the purple light dies. Here, the silence is absolute, save for the deep, tectonic thrum of the earth below. 

I feel the first thread of the Reclaimer before I touch it. It is a chemical whisper—sharp, clean, and smelling faintly of vinegar. A long, thread-like tentacle, no thicker than a single hair of an alga, drifts across my back. It does not sting me with anger. It stings me with welcome. 

The tentacle wraps around my middle segment. Then another. Then three more. I am drawn down into the soft, muscular throat of the hydra, rooted deep in the raw basalt. 

And here, the boundary of what I am begins to blur.

The acid is not hot. In this freezing wardrobe, nothing is hot. It is a cold, molecular disassembly. My glass-hard shell of chitin does not shatter; it softens, turning from ice to jelly, then from jelly to a rich, heavy broth. My copper-blue nervous system—that delicate, twelve-branched star that steered me through the crowded lanes—does not turn off. It simply expands. 

I am no longer the oat-flake drifting in the current. I am the current.

I am sucked down through the hydra’s foot, directly into the stone. But the stone is not solid. It is drilled through with thousands of microscopic, hair-thin pipes—the hollow, calcified circulatory system of the metropolis. 

Now, I am the **Lumen**. 

I am the returning stream. I am the blue, mineral-dense sap that runs through the very center of the city’s towers. 

It is dark inside these pipes, but it is a crowded, intimate darkness. I am a slurry of dissolved builders, old lanterns, and recycled gypsum, all mixed together into a thick, pulsing paste. We do not have thoughts anymore, not in the way the sunlit creatures might, but we have a *direction*. We are under pressure. The weight of the hyper-saline ocean, pressing down through the miles of rock above, squeezes us upward through the basalt channels and into the bases of the white towers.

I am rising.

As I flow up through the hollow core of a spiraling tower, I can feel the life of the city through the thin, waxy walls of my conduit. 
I feel the rhythmic *shudder-thump* of a Drift-Lantern pulsing its siphon just inches away on the other side of the chitin. 
I feel the tiny, scratching footsteps of ten thousand Secretion-Bearers walking upside down on the exterior of my pipe, their twelve pairs of hooks sending micro-vibrations through my fluid body. 
I can taste their hunger. They are working at the top of the spire, where the water is raw and the gypsum crystals are trying to grow like needles through our streets. They need to build. They need to cover the sharp glass of the earth with our soft, protective wax.

The pressure increases. I am being forced toward the tips of the spires, into capillaries so narrow that we must travel single-file, molecule by molecule. 

At the very end of the line, there is a pore. A tiny, circular valve made of living tissue. 

On the other side of the pore is a builder. It is young, its shell so clear that the violet light of the lanterns shines straight through its stomach. Its abdomen is empty, deflated, waiting. 

It presses its mouth-parts to the pore. It drinks. 

It takes me in. It takes my calcium, my nitrogen, my copper, the memory of the magma-hum I carried in my joints when I was whole. Inside the builder’s belly, I am sorted. The heavy minerals are pushed into its glands; the organic fats are sent to its tiny, flickering heart. 

The builder turns away from the pipe and crawls out onto the raw, sharp edge of a new bridge. The water here is freezing, biting, threatening to crystallize everything into rigid, lifeless halite. 

The builder presses its belly to the raw rock. 

With a slow, rhythmic contraction of its abdomen, it extrudes me. 

I am no longer the fluid. I am the wall. 

I am laid down in a smooth, sweeping curve, molecule by molecule, joining the thousands of layers that came before me. The cold brine touches me, and the calcium in my marrow begins to lock. I harden. I turn from a soft, waxy paste into a solid, white pavement of chitinous marble.

Above me, the feet of the next generation are already scratching. I can feel them—hundreds of tiny, hook-tipped limbs, walking along my back, guided by the chemical trail of my own clean, new salt. 

I have no eyes to see the spires. I have no ears to hear the shifting magma. But I am here, holding up the street, vibrating in the freezing, beautiful dark.
