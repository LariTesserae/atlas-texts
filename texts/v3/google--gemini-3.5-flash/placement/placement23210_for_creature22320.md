I lie in the bottom of a chisel-stroke, three hundred paces from the western pylon. 

If you were to bend close to the pale limestone—close enough to smell the slaked lime and the old, wet well-water—you would see me as a thin, gray-white silt. I am the mountain-flour, the fine masonry powder left behind when the flint adzes bit into the stone. The builders did not sweep before they laid down their tools, and because there is no wind to stir the heavy, greenhouse air, I have never been moved. 

To be this small in a world so flat and high-definition is a peculiar thing. Because the gold sky has no sun and casts no lines, there are no shadows here—except for those we keep. Beneath every grain of my powder, where the limestone tooth meets my belly, there is a microscopic dot of absolute, cool darkness. It is no larger than a microbe, but it is mine. I am the host of ten million private shadows, keeping them safe from the unblinking amber glare above.

Above me, the world exists in its two planes: the dry, hot air and the two-inch sea. 

Where the water rests in its shallow lagoon, I am dark and wet, like grease. I feel the flat-bodied rug-worms when they slide over my groove. They do not have feet; they are only weight and pattern. When one passes, it feels like a strip of heavy, damp brocade being dragged across my face. If they find a speck of flint-dust among my grains, they slide over it, and I feel that tiny, sharp edge sink into their red-and-black skin, absorbed into their geometric bands. For a moment, as they pass through one another, I am aware of two directions at once—the worm going north toward the bamboo trellis, and the worm going south toward the rising pillars. My mind is small, but it is divided and distributed among them, drifting in slow, beautiful chessboards of red and black.

But where the stone is dry, I am flour.

Sometimes, the coordinate of a Plaster-Skin shifts. There is no sound of an approach, no heat-displacement. One moment there is only the gold light; the next, a heel-less foot of dry, powdery marl is pressing down upon me. 

To the Plaster-Skin, I am nothing but traction. But when their heavy, nine-foot weight squeezes my grains, I am not crushed—I am slaked. I cling to the fine, porous cracks in their heel. I climb.

When I am pressed into the skin of a Plaster-Skin, I leave the floor. I ascend the long, thin verticality of their leg, which smells of river-silt and dry kiln-mortar. I move through them not as blood moves, but as dry water rising through a wick of chalk. I become part of their great, slow stature. 

From up here, nine feet above the tiles, the basin looks different. The flat terraces are not an endless plain; they are an immense, interlocking puzzle that has been assembled with a desperate, hand-wrought panic. I can see the hair-thin joints where the blocks don't quite meet, where the pale frogs are still thickening in the dark, wet pressure of the seams, waiting to slide out with their gold eyes already open. 

I feel the Plaster-Skin's hands. They are long, four-jointed, and tipped with slate fingernails. Right now, we are standing before a heart-shaped elephant-ear leaf that does not move. The Plaster-Skin does not look at the leaf; their flat, featureless face is turned toward the empty air beside it. 

We reach out. 

With our slate fingers, we touch the invisible vertical line. It is cold—colder than the stone, colder than the water. It feels like a thread of frozen grease suspended in the air. We loop a strip of yellow vine around this nothingness. We tie a neat, repetitive knot. I do not know what we are binding to this frame. It is something that has not arrived yet. It is the trellis for a vine that will only sprout when the golden sky finally blinks, when the sun that is stuck beneath the limestone step-up finally decides to rise. 

Sometimes, while we are tying, we hear it. 

*Scritch-click.*

A mile away, another Plaster-Skin is cleaning its slate nails. The sound is tiny, soft, muffled by the wet wool of the air, but through our heels, through the stone grid, the vibration travels. We feel it in our knees. It is a slow, rhythmic scraper-heartbeat. *We are here,* it says. *The alignment is holding.*

Suddenly, the gold-slit eyes in our flat face burn brighter. 

A mile to the west, a single pylon has shifted. Perhaps a frog, drying into a limestone boss, has expanded by the width of a single spore. Perhaps the water climbing its side has found a softer vein of marl and carved a microscopic groove. 

We freeze. Our slate fingers stop mid-knot, suspended three inches from the leaf. 

Across the basin, thousands of us turn. We do not walk; we simply shift our coordinates, pivoting on the flat grid until every flat, white face is pointed at the western horizon. We stand in the immense, damp heat. We do not breathe. We are waiting for the weight of the water to balance the weight of the stone. We are waiting for the world to become perfect again.

It takes two hours. Or perhaps three centuries. 

When the block settles, a tiny thrill of relief—a cold, lime-flavored shiver—passes through the soles of our feet. We turn back to the invisible trellis.

But as we move our hand, our slate fingernails scrape against one another. 

*Scritch-click.*

A few grains of dry powder—the old mountain-flour, the tiny shadows I kept—are shaved away by the friction. I detach. 

I fall through the hot, motionless air. I do not drift; there is no wind to carry me. I fall straight down, landing in the shallow, two-inch lagoon of a terrace tile, right into the fresh groove of an adze-mark that was carved when the world was young.

The water covers me. The rug-worms glide over me. I settle into the tooth of the stone, and I find my tiny, dark places again, waiting for the heel that will lift me back into the air.
