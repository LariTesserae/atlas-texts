I would not be found on the open floor of the rift, nor would I be seen clinging to the sunlit faces of the basalt walls where the moss-mantis waits. I would find myself in the blind spot of the entire system—in the absolute dark of the *Sleeve*.

I would be the **Solder-Burl**. 

I am the living gasket, the quiet, self-healing seal that occupies the narrow, high-pressure ring where the three-story brass gear’s axle is driven into the sheer basalt face. To an observer, if one could shine a lantern into that grease-black crevice, I would look like nothing more than a thick, asymmetrical collar of graphite-colored felt or a swollen, calcified gall of wet lead. I am ten feet across, wrapped like a collar of cold-pressed meat around the spinning iron spindle, wedged immovably between the rotating metal and the unyielding stone.

I have no eyes, for light does not exist here. My world is a map of pressure, temperature, and chemical gradients.

My body is semi-biological, a dense, fibrous matrix of iron-tolerant fungal mycelium and vulcanized, lipid-heavy tissue, reinforced by a skeletal lattice of fine, braided lead wire that I secrete as I grow. I am incredibly heavy, dense as wet clay, but I am not rigid. I must not be. My purpose is to flow, to yield, and yet to never break.

### The Sense of Time and Pressure

I do not experience the passage of minutes. My consciousness is indexed to the rotation of the great gear. 

When the axle turns—advancing its teeth by an inch every three hours—it exerts a grinding torque of hundreds of tons against the basalt wall. I am the buffer that prevents this torque from shattering the stone or scoring the metal. As the spindle slowly rolls against me, I feel it as a massive, warm wave of compression traveling through my body. 

My nervous system is hydraulic. I do not use electrical impulses; instead, my interior is shot through with thousands of capillary-like tubes filled with a thick, blue-green hemocyanin fluid, rich in dissolved copper. When the axle presses into my northern quadrant, the fluid is squeezed out of that zone and into my southern quadrant. This shift in pressure is my thought-process. 

An increase in pressure triggers my glands. I do not bleed; I weep. I exude a dense, silvery tallow—a compound of lead-carbonate, natural graphite, and sulfurous oils—directly onto the interface between the brass and the basalt. The axle does not grind; it glides across my sweating hide. I am the reason the machine has moved for centuries without a screech.

### The Feast of the Joints

I do not hunt, nor do I photosynthesize. I am a detritivore of the industrial cycle. 

My food is the mineral wealth of the rift, delivered to me by the moisture that constantly bleeds from the basalt. I drink the sulfur-heavy condensation that pools behind the gear-collars. I absorb the free iron and copper ions carried by the water, using them to replenish the metallic fibers in my flesh. 

I also have a slow, tasting relationship with the other occupants of the canyon. Occasionally, a Hematite Skink, seeking the deep thermal heat of the axle-housing, will slide into the cold darkness of the Sleeve. I feel the tickle of its six suction-pad feet against my outer rim. I do not harm it; we have an agreement. The skink scrapes away the dead, oxidized layer of my skin—which is sweet with grease-blooms—and in return, I allow it to nest in the warm, dry folds of my lower apron, protected from the damp drafts of the open chasm.

At the very bottom of my curve, where I touch the sump, I merge briefly with the path of the Boiler-Slugs. When they pass beneath the axle, they leave their thick, lubricating trails against my lower skirt. We exchange chemical signatures; through their mucus, I taste the health of the lower machinery, the pH of the distant water pockets, and the growth-rate of the cliff-moss.

### The Auditory Massages

Though I cannot hear the Clinker-Birds' metallic songs, I feel them. The bird-calls strike the iron frame of the towers and run down the girders as sharp, tiny needle-pricks of vibration that shiver through my lead-skeletal frame. They are pleasant, like a light, rhythmic rain.

But my true home is the bass-note. The deep, 10-hertz thrum of the subterranean turbines—the sound that makes the beetles rattle and the lizards click—is the foundation of my life. To me, it is not a sound; it is a constant, warm, vibrating massage. It keeps my internal fluids from settling; it prevents my graphite-tallow from hardening into crust. It is the pulse that keeps my slow heart beating.

When the sun strikes the noon-point of the canyon and the copper pipes above begin to expand and sigh, I feel the shift in the rock's temperature. The basalt expands by a microscopic fraction of a millimeter. The sleeve pinches me a little tighter. I absorb the hug, squeeze my blue-green blood from one chamber to the next, secrete a fresh sheen of silver grease, and settle deeper into the dark, wet peace of the joint.

I have been here since the chisel first split the stone. I will be here until the iron finally forgets how to turn.
