My chest is a flat plate of silver-grey skin, six spans wide and stretched so tight over my hollow ribs that it rings like a dry hide when the wind hits it. 

I do not have ears; I have this drum. And right now, the drum is telling me that the *Needle of Saffron*, six miles to the west, is three inches out of its true lock.

I can feel it in the third caliper of my left foot. I am clamped to the Seventh Tension—a guide-wire of black carbon, no thicker than a bull’s-tail, that runs from the sheer obsidian lip of the northern basin out to the central municipal cluster. The wire is cold, greasy with the microscopic spores of violet lichen, and it is singing in a flat, high-pitched *E-minor* that means the wind is dragging the gossamer ivy too hard. The ivy has grown too long this season; its green, weeping beards are heavy with dew, and they are acting like sails, pulling the wire toward the glass cliffs.

I am what the young gliders call a **Weight-Bound**. 

When I was twenty, my bones were thin, gas-filled reeds. I was so light that the thermal sighed by the Soughers could lift me twenty yards into the warm, smelling air without me even having to open my skin-flaps. I used to glide from the needle-tips down to the water-rivers, my three-pronged claws barely touching the guide-wires as I zipped at fifty miles an hour, my wrist-bronzes whistling in the wind. 

But a Chorda cannot glide forever. As the decades pass, our porous cartilage begins to drink. The warm, humid air of the abyss is thick with copper-salts and quantum-cooled moisture from the city’s hulls. Year by year, our bones absorb the mist. The hollow chambers in my legs have filled with heavy, blue-grey mineral silt. My kneecaps are as dense as basalt. I weigh forty pounds now—nearly twice what a young Chorda weighs. 

To the youths who zip past me like silver needles, I am a stone. I am a relic of the gravity we pretend to ignore. 

But the city needs its stones. Without us, the needles would dance themselves to pieces in the high winds.

I live in the **Trivium**, a small, three-pointed hammock woven from dried ivy-vines and hung directly under the junction where three major guide-wires cross. Below me is five miles of warm, copper-colored twilight, ending in the permanent purple soup where the Soughers breathe. Above me, the pale, self-luminous block of the residential sector hangs like a frozen spear, its quantum-lock engines humming a low, ninety-cycle drone that rattles the teeth in my skull.

To my right, just four inches below my wire, runs one of the horizontal water-rivers. It is a fat, shimmering cylinder of blue-grey liquid, held to the underside of the alloy path by the city’s magnetic tail. 

As I watch, a family of *Dew-Sliders* passes inside the water. They are small, ribbon-like, and blind, their microscopic cilia rowing them through the current like tiny oars. A *Siphon-Snail*, its shell of spun glass refracting the raw blue light of the gravity wells, is perched on the outer tension-edge of the water. Its needle-mouth is thrust deep into the stream, drinking its fill while its sticky foot holds it firm against the howling drafts.

I reach down with my middle claw, slide the caliper-tips into the water, and let the current wash the lichen-tar from my skin. The water is sweet, tasting of old copper and the cold, clean metal of the hulls.

*Thump.*

A vibration travels down the northern wire. It is a sharp, double-deadened click. 

I press my chest-drum against the carbon. The message shivers through my hide, into my lungs, and down to my pelvic bones:

*“Tensioner-Seven. The Glass-Laid are dropping coal. The line is heavy. Slack us three notches.”*

It is **Old Kael**, speaking from his cave on the obsidian cliffs five miles away. His Toll-Speech is dry and slow, like stones rubbing together in a bucket. He is eighty years old, and his skin has been stained as black as the basalt he mines. 

I reach down to the brass gear-block bolted directly into the carbon wire’s anchor-point. The block is an ancient piece of self-luminous alloy, cool and smooth. I jam my heavy wrist-bronze into the spoke-hole and lean my forty pounds of body-weight against it. My silt-heavy bones groan, but the gear turns. 

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

Three teeth. The wire slacks. The high-pitched *E-minor* drops to a rich, vibrating *D-natural*. The *Needle of Saffron* sways slightly, then settles into its new pocket of air, the quantum-lock fields catching it with a soft, oceanic *whoosh* that smells of ozone.

Behind me, there is a sudden, blinding flash. 

I don't look, but my drum registers the quick, high-voltage snap of a *Zarikh*. A ribbon-beast has coiled itself around a stray Nautiloid three miles out in the empty space. I feel the electric shock travel through the guide-wires as a tickling sensation in my toes. A moment later, the high-frequency clicking of the Nautiloid herd—which usually sounds like a thousand grandfather clocks ticking in the dark—stops for a brief, respectful three seconds, then begins again. The void has no time for the dead.

And then, the wind dies.

The silence that follows is not quiet; it is the terrifying, heavy silence of a machine catching its breath.

The sub-bass drone of the city’s displacement engines drops an octave, settling into a register so low that my rib-cage begins to vibrate in sympathy, my very lungs shaking inside my chest. The gossamer ivy stops swaying. The long, green tentacles hang perfectly straight down into the five-mile drop, like green plumb-lines marking the center of the world.

*It is the third hour.*

Deep below, in the purple sulfur fog where the sun never shines, the **Soughers** are preparing to breathe. 

I pull myself flat against the Trivium hammock, locking my four calipers into the carbon wires with bone-crushing force. I close my nostrils. I pull my chin down against my chest-plate, sealing my throat.

Then, it comes.

The Sougher’s breath is not a wind; it is a wall of density. 

A colossal, warm column of tropical air, heated by the biological furnaces of beasts five miles long, rises from the bottom of the rift. It is thick with the scent of wild yeast, wet moss, and the sweet, musk-like discharge of the deep-dwellers. 

The entire city—millions of tons of alloy needles, hanging gardens, and carbon highways—is lifted three inches. The quantum-lock fields hum in sudden, frantic resistance, their blue light flaring into brilliant, blinding sheets of turquoise that illuminate the dark obsidian walls of the chasm.

The three guide-wires of my Trivium tighten until they are as hard as iron rods. They do not whistle; they *howl*. They sing a single, gargantuan chord—a beautiful, deafening *C-sharp* that shakes the silt in my bones and clears the dust from my drum.

I open my eyes. The bronze twilight has turned to gold. Millions of tiny, translucent Nautiloids are rising on the thermal, their silver siphons pumping, their clicking voices joining the great, vibrating song of the valley. 

I am thirty miles from the nearest solid ground. I have a gallon of heavy mineral water in my bones, and my body will never leave these wires. But as the warm breath of the world fills my lungs, and the great chord of the city runs through my chest, I know exactly who I am.

I am the string. And the void is playing me.
