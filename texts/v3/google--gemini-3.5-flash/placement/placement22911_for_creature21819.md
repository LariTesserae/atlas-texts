To understand who I am, you must first close your eyes and let the weight of seven miles of black water settle behind your temples. 

I do not have a name—there are no words here to carry one, and if there were, the cold is too thick for sound to travel far before it becomes a flat, dead thud. I am a pressure. I am a density. I am the slow, hydraulic pulse of a mineral gel that has forgotten how to be soft.

I am situated in the third tier of the western basin, where the basalt tiles are wide enough to hold three villages of the dry world, though I have never known a dry world, nor a village. 

My crown—the thousands of white, silken threads that float from the collar of my neck—is currently drifted to the west. The planet is leaning. It has been leaning to the west for eighty of my years, and my threads, which are as thin as spider-silk but as long as pine trees, are tasting the change. They taste the faint, sour tang of iron-rich brine rising from a crack three miles behind me. They taste the cold. 

Mostly, they taste the weight.

I am not walking. I have never walked. My single, broad foot is wedged into a groove three inches deep, carved into a dark basalt tile by hands that were old when the kelp was only a green film on the stone. The groove is my road, my boundary, and my spine. I do not choose my direction; the masonry chose it for me before I budded. I am currently sliding south. 

To you, my movement would look like the growth of a lichen on a tombstone. In three days, I will have moved the length of my own body. My foot, which is tough as tanned seal-hide, secretes a slow, mucous grease that eases my passage along the basalt, but the floor is hungry. It drinks the grease; it drinks the mineral silt; it drinks my history. 

Ahead of me, a guideline has gone slack.

I do not see the slackness. I do not have eyes to see the faint, green bioluminescence that drips from the moss-nodes like melting candles. I feel it. 

Three miles above me, a great petrified-wood frame has groaned under the weight of a century’s accumulation of black-veined kelp. One of its stone weights—a rough ring of grey slate—has slipped its wooden peg. The braided rope of split reeds, which I tied when my rind was still pale and thin, has lost its tension. 

The vibration travels down the line. It passes through the stone peg driven into the tile seam; it enters the basalt; it travels through the cold stone beneath my foot. It is a tiny, high-frequency shudder—a *ping* in a world that only knows the *thrum* of the deep currents.

It is a call. It is the only voice I know.

I contract. The pressurized gel inside my leather-tough skin shifts, squeezing toward my forward edge. I slide. My wooden arms, which hang from my sides like the heavy branches of a drowned yew tree, drag behind me through the velvet kelp-leaves. They are wet, green, and heavy.

It will take me four months to reach the slack line. I will not hurry, because hurry is a concept that requires a heart that beats, and my interior does not beat; it only yields to the squeeze of the trench.

As I slide, I can feel my own ending. 

It is not a coldness—that would be impossible, for I am already as cold as the sea—but a hardness. A stiffness in my lower core. 

For nine thousand years, the mineral-rich waters that I drink through my foot have passed through my fibers, leaving behind tiny deposits of silica and iron. The gel within me is no longer a slurry; it is turning to flint. My wood-arms, which once bent like fresh willow, are growing brittle at the elbows. When I lift my left limb to clear a clump of pale moss from my track, I hear—not with ears, but through the solid timber of my shoulder—the dry, stony *clack* of mineralized grain.

I am petrifying. 

I do not fear this, because I do not have the machinery for fear. I have only the tension. The desire to make the rope straight.

When I reach the slack cord, my rootlets—the hundreds of dark, fibrous fingers at the ends of my wooden arms—will begin their work. They are already dry, almost like charcoal, but they still know the loops. They know the three-fold knot that does not slip under the pressure of the great currents. 

It will take me seven years to tie it. I will lift one strand of the braided reed, wet and slippery with cold-water algae, and hold it against the timber. I will wait for the rotation of the planet to shift the current, using the water’s own massive hand to pull the loop through the eye of the slate weight. I will nudge it with my stone-hard shoulder. I will wait.

And when the knot is tight, and the vibration in the basalt tile dies away into the perfect, dead quiet of the floor, I may not move again.

My foot will have settled over the mineral seep. My rind will fuse with the basalt tile. The silica in my blood will finally lock together, crystal to crystal, until my wedge-shaped body is as solid as the masonry I have traveled for millennia. 

I will became a pillar.

And centuries from now, another Tender—perhaps the small, pale nodule that budded from my side during the great eastward current three hundred years ago—will slide down this same groove. Its crown of white threads will touch my petrified rind. It will feel the grooves of my old bark, now turned to stone.

It will not mourn me. It will climb me.

It will use my stone shoulders as a scaffold. It will loop a new, green reed-line around my neck. It will plant a sprig of white moss in the hollow where my crown used to drift. 

I will hold the garden up. I will keep the dark from collapsing. And in that silence, which is the only beautiful thing the world has ever made, I will remain.
