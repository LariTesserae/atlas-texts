The roar is not in my ears. I have no ears to speak of—only the flat, lime-crusted margins of my shell that catch the vibrations of the basalt and translate them into a cold, rhythmic itch in my legs. 

The roar is my breathing. It is the breathing of the seventy-thousand others whose plates overlap mine, whose gray, slate-hard bellies press against the wet stone of the western wall. When we draw back our breathing-pores, the air makes a sound like dry gravel being turned over with a iron shovel. When we push it out, it is a long, wet hiss that turns the humidity into grease.

I am forty years old, which means I have traveled roughly eighty cubits down from the high rim where I was dropped as a spore. I am now the size of a master-mason’s hand-basin. My shell is no longer sleek; it has been notched by falling shale, pitted by the sulfuric sweat of the chimney, and overgrown by seventy-two generations of the white wool-grows.

I can feel each one of them. 

The wool-grows—the *felt*—are the marrow of my back. They have found the microscopic faults in my third and fifth segments, sending their root-threads through the grey lime and into the cold, lard-colored pulp of my body. They do not hurt. They taste like rain that has traveled through five miles of limestone: bitter, clean, and heavy with iron-salt. 

Through these threads, I am tied to the three other striders who sit above me, and the four who crawl beneath my tail-margin. 

To my left, there is an old sister whose shell has gone green-grey with iron-rot. She has not shifted her claws in seven moons. The felt-threads between us are so thick they look like fat cords of tallow. When she draws in her breath, the tug pulls on my left flank, a gentle, wet reminder that she is still there, still holding the rock. 

Three hours ago, the summit wind changed.

We felt it first as a dry prickle in the wool-grows on our backs—the moisture in the chimney’s throat was sucked upward by a great, cold mouth far above. The air became thin, smelling of snow and dead sky. 

In response, the wall is tightening.

It begins as a ripple two hundred cubits below, where the Fused-ones sit in their black muck. We feel the vibration travel up through the basalt—a deep, iron *thrum* that makes our quartz-cones ring in the dark like wet fingers on the rims of wine-glasses. 

*Clink. Clack.*

The sound runs up the chimney, faster than a stone can fall.

*Hold.*

I draw my limbs inward. My claws—six pairs of flinty spikes, tipped with natural silica—find the tiny, razor-sharp ridges of the brand-new basalt split. I press my head-shield down into the grease-slick. The sister to my left does the same. Her shell-rim slides over mine with a dry, metallic *skreigh* that joins the wider chorus of the wall. 

Around us, a million shells settle. We are no longer individual plates; we are a sheet of scales, a single coat of stone-armor three inches thick, clinging to the vertical bone of the mountain. We do not breathe now. We save the wetness in our wool. We wait for the summit wind to tire.

But there is a pulling at my tail-rim.

It is not the wind. The wind is a broad, blunt hand; this is a thin, sliding wedge of cold leather.

A Bellows-hide has found the gap where my shell does not quite meet the rock.

It must have spent the last two days sliding up from the terrace below, its soft, oily belly smelling of old grease-rot and digested limestone. I did not hear it. It has no claws to scrape; it moves by the slow, turgid stretching of its leathery hide. But now I feel the hard, triangular chisel of its snout. It is cold—colder than the stone—and it is greasy with mucus.

It slips beneath my posterior plate.

The felt-threads to my neighbors tighten instantly. The wool-grows on my back are being pulled taut, singing like harp-strings under the skin of my segments. 

*Tug.*

The sister to my left shifts her weight. She does not move her legs—that would take too long—but she tilts her shell, throwing her bulk toward me so that her side-margin pinches down on the Bellows-hide’s snout. To my right, two younger striders, no larger than beech-leaves, crawl closer, wedge-locking their plates against my side to keep me from lifting.

We are a wall. We have always been a wall.

Underneath me, the Bellows-hide begins to pump.

I can hear its leathery ribs expanding. *Whoof. Whoof.* It is drawing in the thin, dry air of the chimney and packing it into its internal bladders. It is a slow, rhythmic labor. It will take three hours for the beast to compress enough air to slip me from the basalt.

With every pump, the pressure under my shell grows. It is a hot, dry ache. The air is trying to find the wet seam where my flesh meets the iron-slate. 

The quartz-chimes above us are ringing louder now, stimulated by the grinding of our claws as we resist. The high, silver *ting-ting-ting* falls through the dark like frozen needles. In the mud-pools on the terrace below, the grubs—the Iron-marrows—hear the ringing. They begin to turn in their gray silt, their blind, ring-toothed mouths tasting the falling dust, waiting for whatever the mountain discards.

Let them wait.

I dig my third claw into a fissure that is only six weeks old. The basalt here is so sharp it has not yet lost its glass-edge; it cuts into the silica tip of my toe, but the fit is perfect. I am wedged.

The Bellows-hide gives a great, shuddering balloon-heave. The air inside it is hot now, warmed by its leathery heart.

The threat is not that my legs will tire. My legs do not have muscles that grow weary; they are moved by the slow, hydraulic pressure of my own grey brine, which does not sleep. The threat is the felt. If the felt-threads tear, I will lose the weight of my sisters. I will be single. And a single plate cannot hold against the pump.

A thread snaps.

It sounds like a tiny wet thread of silk breaking in an empty room.

*Ting.*

Another.

The sister on my left feels the slack. Through the remaining cords, I feel her shift—not to retreat, but to drop her own heavy head-shield directly onto my front-plate. She is offering her weight. She is letting herself be pulled toward the void so that her mass might anchor mine.

We wait. The mountain waits.

The Bellows-hide pumps a seventh time. The pressure is immense now; my shell is bowing slightly in the center, the grey slate-plates creaking under the tensional strain. If I pop, I will fall forty cubits before I hit the first terrace. The grubs will have my pulp before the grease on my shell has dried.

But the summit wind is dying.

The air in the chimney is growing heavy again. The cold, wet iron-smell is returning, falling down from the ceiling in fat, greasy drops. The wool-grows on our backs begin to drink, swelling with water, gaining weight by the ounce, by the pound, by the ton.

The entire wall is growing heavier.

The sister on my left grows three ounces heavier as her wool hydrates. The striders above her grow heavier. The great, suspended tapestry of plate and fiber settles.

The Bellows-hide cannot lift the mountain.

With a long, wet, flatulent *hiss*, the compressed air escapes from the sides of its leathery snout, unable to find the purchase to pop me. The pressure beneath my shell vanishes, replaced by the cool, comforting seep of iron-water.

The chisel-snout withdraws.

I feel the beast’s leathery belly slide away, retreating down the vertical face toward the lower shelves where the dead things go. It will find an old one, or a sick one whose felt has rotted, and it will try again. But not here.

The roar of the wall rises again, full and deep, a grinding of a million claws settling back into their basalt grooves. 

I lift my front-most spade-limb. It takes me twenty minutes to move it two inches to the left, where a small flake of shale has wedged itself in a crack. I push the flake deeper into the rift. 

It fits. The shelf is slightly wider now. The mud-pools below will have another drop of silt.

We are holding. The mountain is holding.

And far below, in the dark where the air is like oil, the great heart of the Fused-one beats. 

*Thump.*

We feel it in our claws. We feel it in our shells. 

We wait twenty minutes for the next one.
