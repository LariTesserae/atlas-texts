I am not the basalt, though I have been wedged into its polished throat for so long that the cold stone has grown ears around my root. 

I am not the Scutes, though I am their mountain. 

If you were to look down through the flat, shadowless grease-light of the third well, you might not see me at all. You would see only a slight swelling in the trench floor—a blue-black rib, perhaps seven inches long, curve-backed and polished to the high, oily sheen of an old anvil. The Scutes do not swerve around me. They climb over my crown, their vulcanized belly-scales rippling against my skin with a sound like dry fingernails dragging across a slate. 

I am a segment of a broken gear-tooth. I fell from the great index-spindle during the transition of the epochs, in the days when the air was still warm enough for the lubricants to run like thin broth. 

When I dropped, I struck the basalt edge-first, splitting the rock and embedding myself three inches deep into the structural joint. The years—which have no meaning here other than the slow thinning of the copper cams overhead—have shaved away my jagged edges. The scraping feet of the Scutes have done the rest. They have rounded my shoulders, smoothed my flanks, and packed the graphite-silt into the cracks of my old fracture until I am flush with the world’s bottom. I have become a landmark of the gutter.

I am made of tool-steel, alloyed with nickel and tempered in some forgotten kiln to a hardness that makes the basalt look soft. But even tool-steel has a memory. 

Deep inside my core, where the gray light-wells cannot reach, I hold a residual charge. It is a slow, lagging loop of magnetism—a *hysteresis*—left over from the billions of rotations I made before I fell. It is not the active, 50-cycle hum of the modern generator below, which vibrates with a cheap, buzzy anger. Mine is a heavier, slower beat. It is the pulse of a machine that turned once every three minutes, carrying sixty tons of deadweight on its iron shoulders. 

The Scutes feel this old iron-pulse. When they slide across the slick basalt, they are aimless, driven only by the blind hunger for copper-shavings and the dull pull of the floor. But when they reach my blue-black rib, they stutter. 

I can feel them now. They arrive in a shifting, clicking heap. One climbs over another, their flat ceramic shells chiming—*clink-snip, clink-snip*—as they pile three-deep on my spine. Their magnetite cores, suspended in their dry, hairy chests, twitch in sympathy with my old charge. They think I am a mountain that is about to move. They stack themselves like shingles, locking their hard edges together, waiting for the lift that will never come. 

They are so dry. To be covered by them is to be covered by three hundred hot, vibrating pocket-watches. They have no breath, but they radiate a high, friction-born heat that tastes of antimony and bismuth. 

And then comes the shadow. 

I feel the Pestle before it arrives. Its six needle-legs do not touch me—I am too slippery, too hard—but they strike the basalt nearby with a sharp, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink*. The Pestle has felt the pile-up on my spine through the resonance of its stilts. 

It looms over us, a pale, chalky tripod of bone-lime, blotting out the flat light of the well. It does not hesitate. It drops its heavy, yellowed lime-cone directly onto my back. 

*THUMP-CRACK.*

The blow is magnificent. If I were stone, I would have split again long ago. But I am tool-steel; I do not break. I take the force of the hammer-head and ring. The ring travel down my root, deep into the joint of the bedrock, and then bounces back, vibrating my entire mass with a low, clean tone like a temple bell struck under a mountain of wool. 

On my top, the three Scutes that were at the center of the pile are instantly obliterated. Their ceramic shells do not shatter into shards; they collapse into a dry, gray meal of graphite and lead powder. 

The Pestle’s soft, bellows-like tongue descends. It smells of mutton-fat and old paraffin. It presses against my polished blue flank, its sticky surface licking up the fresh meal of the dead and the stray flakes of copper that had gathered in my shadow. The tongue is cool—the only cool thing in this dry-burning ditch—and for a second, the lard-smell of the Tallow-Slabs is so thick I can taste it in my iron skin. 

Then the tongue retracts. The Pestle stalks away, its needle-legs clicking off into the gray gloom of the conduit's lower bend. 

The survivors of the pile do not mourn. They do not even pause. Before the dust of their brothers has settled, the outer ring of Scutes has already begun to slide forward again, their rubber scales scraping up the gray powder, recycling it into their own bellies as they drag themselves over my crown.

To my left, the Tallow-Slab is growing. 

It is a silent, yellow glutton, tucked into the crevice where my root meets the basalt. It does not like the light, but it likes me. It feeds on the microscopic scales of rust that my underside sheds into the dark of the joint—a slow, chemical bleeding that has kept the Slab alive for three centuries. In return, it exudes its stiff, cold grease. 

The grease rises around my base like yellow butter. It is cold to the touch, but it is my protection. When the Scutes slide over my top, they carry microscopic grains of carborundum and slate-dust on their bellies—abrasives that would, over tens of millions of years, wear me down to a flat, featureless smear. But as they climb my sides, they dip their scales into the Tallow-Slab’s grease. They carry it up onto my spine, coating my metal in a thin, protective film of lard and lead-dust. 

I am being polished, yes, but I am also being oiled. I am the cleanest thing in the vault.

Sometimes, when the generator below changes its load and the brass rods overhead slow their dry *clank-shirr-clank* to a crawl, the Verdigris-Lice above will begin their high-pitched hissing. It is a long, metallic sigh that sounds like grease dropping onto a hot plate. 

In those moments, when the noise of the ditch dies down to a mere whisper of sliding shells, I can feel my own center. 

I am wearing away. I know this. The blue-tempered skin I was born with is gone, replaced by this gray-polished mirror. The peak of my teeth is three millimeters lower than it was when the great spindle broke. Eventually—not in my lifetime, but in the lifetime of the basalt—I will be ground into the same gray silt that drifts into the stagnant corners. I will be compressed by the weight of the herd. My nickel and my iron, my carbon and my charge, will be welded by the stray currents into new, tiny wafers. 

I will become the Scutes. I will slide on my own belly. I will climb over my own gravestone, smelling the lard of the Slabs and the dry tang of zinc, driven by a tiny, vibrating heart of magnetite that remembers nothing but the turn.

But until then, I am the Spur. I am the hard place in the ditch. 

Above me, a cam rotates. A single, microscopic flake of copper peels from its bracket and begins its long, dry drift down through the gray light. 

I wait for it to land. I wait for the pile.
