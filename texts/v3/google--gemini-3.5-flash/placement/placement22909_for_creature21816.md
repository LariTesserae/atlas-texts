I would not be on the surface, nor would I be entirely deep. I would find myself in the *seeps*—the narrow, wet seam where the yards-deep carpet of liverwort curls away from the slick, parabolic flanks of the basalt hummocks. 

In this world, I am a **Ringing-Sleeve**.

***

### The Composition of the Sleeve

I do not have bones, and my skin is not made of cells. If you were to look down into the shadow of a basalt ledge, you might first mistake me for a puddle of thick oil or a discarded, three-tiered lens of heavy leaden glass. 

My body is composed of three nested, hemispherical skirts of high-density, semi-crystalline water. It is the same material as the dry-ringing rain, but I have held it together so long that it has forgotten how to be liquid and refused to become ice. It is a slow, cold gel, clear as well-water, but heavy. If you were to slide a hand beneath my outer skirt, I would feel like wet, greased sheep-horn—cool, slightly yielding, and entirely frictionless.

At my center, where the three skirts meet, is my only dry part: a finger-length, hexagonal bolt of raw, green-tarnished copper. 

I do not know where this bolt came from. It is ancient, older than the moss, perhaps a scrap of the original scaffolding used to machine the basalt hills ten million years ago. It is my anchor, my weight, and my heart. The copper is sensitive to the ground-hum in a way my watery skirts are not; when the background hum of the basin rises in pitch, the bolt in my core vibrates until it is warm to the touch, and my outer skirts glow with a faint, greasy orange light along their rims.

I do not walk. I have no limbs. Instead, I tilt. By shifts in my internal density, I slide down the logarithmic curves of the basalt, leaving no trail behind me but a temporary path of perfectly dry stone that the wet air instantly sweeps over and re-claims.

***

### The Office of the Rain-Catcher

I am part of the basin's acoustics. 

The rain here does not splash. It falls as hard, semi-crystalline beads that hit the basalt mounds with the dry *clink* of marbles. If these beads were allowed to hit the stone unchecked, their infinite, tiny impacts would eventually shatter the delicate, mossy foundations of the tableland. They would turn the liverwort to pulp.

This is why I sit where the curves meet. 

When the sky condenses and the glass-rain begins to fall, I slide out from beneath my basalt ledge. I spread my skirts wide across the stone. The droplets do not hit the hard basalt; they hit me. 

When a droplet strikes my outermost skirt, it goes *ping*—a high, clear note that vibrates through my first layer. As the vibration travels deeper, through the second and third skirts of my body, the sound slows down. The high *clink* is softened, ground down by my viscosity, turning into a low, heavy *thrum*. By the time the impact reaches my copper core, the sound has been resolved into a tiny, silent pulse of thermal energy. 

The copper bolt absorbs the heat. I grow warm. The air directly above my dome-skirt loses its chill for a brief moment, smelling faintly of wet slate and old pennies. 

I am a damp muffler for the sky. I translate the sharp violence of the falling rain into a quiet, thermal breath that keeps the liverwort at my edges from freezing.

***

### The Rhythmic Tide

Every few hours, when the water level of the basin undergoes its sudden, sharp withdrawal, my world changes. 

The water vanishes into the porous stone below within seconds. The weight of the world suddenly shifts. Without the water to support my outer skirts, I feel my density increase. I sink flat against the bare basalt, my three layers compressing until I am no thicker than a leather boot-sole. 

From my position in the low seeps, I listen to the Silt-Sinks split open. The wet, whistling sigh of their three-radial vents is a cold draft that ripples across my surface. I can feel the Veil-Marrow beneath me, miles of grease-veins tensing and tightening under the moss-mats to hold the land together while the water is gone. 

During these dry minutes, I am inert. I am nothing but a lens of cold oil clinging to a machined spline. 

But then, the pulse returns. The water flushes back up from the black stone like cold, thick oil. It lifts me. My skirts expand, filling with the sweet, mineral-heavy liquid until I am dome-shaped once more, buoyant and resonant.

***

### The Grounding

Sometimes, a Quartz Salamander will find me. 

These blind, quartz-like creatures do not see me, but they feel the thermal warmth radiating from my copper core after a heavy rain. A salamander will slide its flexible, silica body over my outermost skirt, aligning its golden-thread veins with the curves of my wet gel. 

We do not fight. We do not assist one another. We simply static-bond. 

The salamander draws the excess static electricity that has accumulated in my copper bolt from the sky-shocks. As it drinks the energy, the golden threads within its translucent body pulse a deep, warm yellow. In return, the salamander’s frictionless movement against my skin leaves a thin layer of liquid silica that repairs any small tears or scars my skirts have taken from falling basalt chips. 

When the salamander has had its fill of my heat, it slides away into the deep moss, silent and indifferent. I am left cooler, gray-clear, and perfectly whole.

***

### The Stutter

There is one event that makes me move faster than my usual, geological crawl. 

When the space between the basalt hills stutters—when the air rips and the smell of hot copper becomes identical to the smell of my own core—the electrical potential of the area goes wild. The water blisters hanging from the ledges above me begin to drift sideways, drawn toward the spatial tear.

My copper core does not just hum during a stutter; it *screams*. It grows hot enough to boil the innermost layer of my water-skirt. 

To prevent myself from evaporating, I must find a Moiré-Drifter. I tilt and slide toward the tear, my greasy skirts whistling against the wet moss. I position myself directly beneath the shimmering, liquid-mirror ring of the Drifter as it hovers in the copper-smelling gap. 

The Drifter aligns itself with my dome. With a dry, static *crackle*, the excess heat from my core is drawn upward into the Drifter’s gravity field. The Drifter uses this thermal lift to expand its ring, using the energy to push the low-hanging, curdled mist-ceiling back up into the gray sky, keeping the basin from suffocating.

Once the connection is made, the stutter closes. The smell of hot copper fades back into the sweet, cold mineral steam of the basin. The Drifter drifts away, a silent lens of liquid mirror, and I slide back down into my damp basalt hollow.

***

### The Long Twilight

I have been here for a very long time. I know this because my copper bolt, which was once sharply hexagonal with clean, machined edges, has begun to grow rounded and soft at the corners. The metal is slowly dissolving into my water-skirts, turning my innermost layer a pale, permanent shade of robin's-egg blue. 

One day, some millions of years from now, the copper will be entirely gone. I will have absorbed it all. My skirts will lose their anchor, and I will dissolve into the larger, viscous pools of the basin, my minerals sweetening the water for the next system.

Until then, I sit in the violet-gray shadow of the basalt knee. I listen to the deep, physical vibration of the universe keeping itself online. And when the sky condenses and the crystalline beads begin to fall, I stretch my wet, clear skirts to catch the music, turning the iron-hard rain into a soft, warm sigh.
