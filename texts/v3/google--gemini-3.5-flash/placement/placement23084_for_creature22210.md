I wake not to a sound, but to a change in the tension of my left heel. 

My bed is a pocket of double-layered gray wool, slung from three braided seal-gut lines that run up into the iron rafters of the *Weavers’ Gap*. When the earth beneath the alleyway swells, the warehouse on my left leans three inches toward the copper-rendering works on my right. The gut-lines creak—not a dry scratch, but a soft, greased *slither*—and my body swings two degrees to the north to compensate. 

This is how I know the morning has come, or at least that the daylight has flattened out over the rim-cliffs, turning the steam of the basin from a night-time charcoal to the color of wet lard.

I am an *Adjuster of the Hangings*. In the Chinks, they call me the *Lead-Sinker*, though my actual trade has more to do with balance than weight.

***

My hands are wide and thick, the skin on the palms tanned to the consistency of boot-sole by decades of rubbing with tallow and graphite. My fingers are not green like the Glass-Herders’—I do not summon the stone—nor are they white and dry like the White-Wools’ who live above the steam-canopy. My knuckles are gray. The gray comes from lead-dust, which I rub into the threads of my brass adjusting-turnbuckles to keep them from seizing up in the wet, biting salt of the condensation.

I wear three coats of plum-dyed wool, each one shorter than the last so that my hips are free to move. My boots are double-bandaged in lard-soaked canvas. If you were to watch me walk along the narrow catwalk of the Weaver’s Gap, you would not see my knees lift. My thighs remain level; my ankles do the work, sliding over the wet copper plates with the *Sinking Shuffle*, my soles never losing contact with the metal. To lift a foot in the Chinks is to invite a slip, and a slip means falling forty feet into the warm, black porridge of the low-lying ooze where the basalt is still soft enough to swallow a man’s boots before he can scream.

In my leather shoulder-sack, I carry my life: three heavy plumb-bobs of solid gray lead, two dozen brass pulleys that have been boiled in mutton-fat to make them silent, and a skein of horsehair cord—sixteen strands, braided tight by the blind weavers of the lower canal. Horsehair does not stretch when the humidity rises; it only grows firmer, like the muscle of an eel.

***

Today, the work lies in the *Three-Angle*, a wedge of space where an old tallow-house has been pinched between two rising lime-crusted ribs. 

I drop from my sleeping-nest, my boots landing on the wet peat moss of the catwalk with a sound no louder than a wet cloth falling on a bench. The air is cold on my teeth—that clean, high-latitude cold that smells of iron and the distant, frozen sea—but from below, the warmth of the earth rises through the cracks in the street, smelling of sulfur, wet coal, and the heavy, sweet grease of the boiling troughs.

A Slick is sitting on the junction box near my ladder. It is a big one, five feet from its blunt, whiskerless snout to its leathery tail-pad. Its skin is the color of a wet slate shingle, glistening with the bitter, clear oil it secretes to keep the acid-dew from its flesh. 

As I pass, it does not move. It does not have eyes to see me, but its copper-colored neck-skin swells. I feel its welcome not in my ears, but as a deep, rhythmic pacing in my pelvic bone—*thrum, thrum, thrum*—like the vibration of a great factory piston three miles away. 

I slide my hand over its back as I shuffle past. The oil is cool and smells faintly of turnip-skins. "Easy, girl," I say. I speak in the *Basin hum*, the vowels long and rounded, my tongue never touching my teeth. *"Oo-lay, shoo-ah."* The words carry no hard edges; they drift into the blue peat moss on either side of the catwalk and are gone before they can strike the iron.

***

In the Three-Angle, I find the weaver’s widow, a woman named Maren. Her skin has the translucent, bluish-gray waxiness of those who have spent forty years in the steam-shade. She is sitting on her three-legged stool, which hangs from the ceiling by three thin wires of braided copper.

Her floor is at a thirty-degree slant today. A rib of dark, glassy obsidian has pushed up through her threshold overnight, a beautiful, terrible thing like the snout of a giant black fish. It is cold to the touch, but the heat of its rising still lingers in the air around it—a dry, basaltic bake that makes the eyes water.

"It rose before the shifts changed," Maren says. Her voice is a low, sliding murmur, like water moving through grease-cloth. *"Is-oo-lay... the tea-kettle would not sit."*

She gestured with her chin toward the corner. Her kettle, a heavy pot of black cast-iron, is slung from a brass arm on the wall. Because the wall has tilted outward, the kettle is hanging three inches to the left of her small coal-brazier. If she fills it, the water will spill into the ashes.

"We will set it straight," I tell her. My voice is low, deep in my chest. 

I do not look at the stone rib. The stone is none of my business; that is for the Herd-Keepers and their copper cables. My business is the hanging house.

I climb her wall using the small, lead-weighted stirrups I have fastened to the studding. Near the ceiling, where the timber of her roof meets the copper flank of the neighboring boiler-house, the condensation is thick. It drips steadily into my hair—cold, regular drops that run down the back of my neck. I do not flinch. You learn to let the water find its way down your wool without fighting it.

I find the main pulley block. The hook, carved from a dense knot of lignum-vitae, is jammed against an iron bracket because the building has squeezed three inches to the west. The horsehair cord is humming like a harp-string under the strain.

I take my lead hammer from my sack—lead, because iron on iron makes a sharp, ringing *ping* that would make Maren's ears weep—and tap the lignum block.

*Thump.*

The sound is dull, swallowed instantly by the moss that grows in the corner where the roof leaks. 

Again. *Thump.*

The block slides. The horsehair cord slacks, then tightens with a wet, sucking sound as the weight of Maren’s cupboard, her hanging bed, and her tea-brazier adjust themselves. 

Below me, the cupboard swings. It drifts six inches to the right, hovering an inch above the steeply tilted floorboards. The brass kettle settles back directly over the center of the coal fire. 

Maren does not look up. She reaches out with a blue-veined hand, takes the kettle by its hemp-wrapped handle, and pushes it slightly. It swings in a gentle, perfect circle, level with the earth’s deep center, regardless of the fact that her doorway is now a triangle and her windows look down into the dark gap at a terrifying slant.

*"Soo-ah,"* she murmurs. *It is level.*

She reaches into her pocket and pulls out my pay: three small cakes of cold, salted seal-fat, wrapped in a clean dock-leaf. They are hard and gray, but they will keep my boots water-tight for another week if I melt them over my lamp, or they will keep my own joints from creaking if I eat them with my salt-tea.

***

In the afternoon, I climb higher. 

I like the high catwalks, where the air is colder and the daylight has that clean, ice-sheared brightness that makes your eyes feel wide and clean. Up here, the mosses are different. They are not the heavy, dark-green peat of the low streets, but the pale, lavender-tinted *static-moss* that grows near the high-voltage lines.

As I shuffle along the copper pipeway of the Seventh Tier, I can look down through the gaps in the floor. 

Below me, the city looks like a cold, gray kettle that has been left to simmer on a slow fire. The steam rises in long, lazy plumes, curling around the fat, wool-wrapped pipes and the iron limbs of the crane-towers. It is so quiet. If I close my eyes, I can hear only the *whuff-whuff-whuff* of the great bellows on the upper platform—but it is a soft sound, like a bird’s wing brushing against a curtain.

I see a white figure on the high copper deck above me—one of the White-Wools. 

He is standing at the edge of his white-larch pavilion, which looks like a cage of birch branches suspended from the sky. He is dressed in his thick, stiff coat of mountain-wool, so white it seems to drink the flat daylight and give nothing back. 

He does not look down at me. He is watching a new column of basalt rise from the center of the *Tallow-Market* below. The column is rising slowly, perhaps a foot every hour, pushing a heap of rusted iron gears before it like a black tooth rising through gums of gray mud.

A Current-Keeper is there, a tiny figure in a dark plum coat, holding his long brass ear-trumpet against the base of the rising pillar. I can see the copper lines running from his belt into the mud, glowing with a soft, green static that makes the air around his boots shimmer.

The White-Wool raises a thin silver wand, his hand moving in a slow, elegant curve through the cold air—*a gesture of allowance*. He is telling the Keeper to let the stone rise another three yards before they freeze it with the cold current. He does not say a word. The wand moves, the Keeper nods, and the stone continues its silent, heavy climb.

***

I sit on my lead-sack at the edge of the catwalk, my boots dangling over the forty-foot drop. 

My toes are slightly cold, but the lard-canvas keeps the dampness from reaching my skin. I pull one of Maren’s grease-cakes from my pocket and bite off a corner. It is salty and tastes of the cold northern sea, but as it melts in my mouth, the warmth of it goes straight to my collarbones.

Down in the crevices, the Slicks are thrumming again. A long, slow wave of vibration passes up through the iron stanchions, through my lead-sack, and into the base of my spine. It is the earth, turning over in its sleep beneath the basin.

Some people from the outer hills say that one day the basin will close its mouth—that the stone will freeze solid and the rise will stop, leaving Sinking-on-the-Rise as hard and dead as the limestone cliffs that surround us. They say the silence here is the silence of a tomb.

But they do not know the weight of the lead. They do not know how beautiful it is to live in a house that swings like a pendulum, always true to the middle of the world, while the ground beneath you slides and swells like the sea.

I chew my grease-cake, adjust my plumb-line, and wait for the next floor to tilt.
