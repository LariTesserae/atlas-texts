The dark is not an absence, but a thickness. It is forty fathoms of viscous, iron-breathed weight, holding the mountain to the floor of the world. 

To those who lived before the great rising—if there were any whose bones did not dissolve into the valley silt—this place would be called silent. But to me, the quiet is a crowded, heavy thing. The world is a great bell, and we are the clappers, suspended in the grease.

I am an Anchor.

Once, many winters of the ice-fall ago, I was of the *Corded*. I had the spring in my bark. I had the piston-glands that could strike the basalt with a sharp *thwack*, splitting the hexagonal columns along their natural columns of cooling. I remember the slow, dragging journey across the high saddle of the mountain—a yard every three moons—towing the gray stones behind me by my root-ends, using the great rolling thermal currents to lift their weight. I remember the smell of the raw copper we gathered from the venting fissures where the dark valley floor bleeds hot, sulfurous milk. We squeezed the copper through our skin-pores, drawing it into long, soft ribbons that smelled of lightning, and we beat it flat around the pillars using our own hardened limbs as mallets.

We did not build to keep out the wet. The wet is our blood. We built so we could hear what the world was thinking.

But the turgor pressure of a Corded does not last forever. The copper-sap is thick; over centuries, the ions settle. The vascular channels of the inner trunk—the braided hoses that carry our slow thoughts—begin to silt up with minerals from the basin. The skin, once like wet, corrugated leather, turns to slate. The hydraulic snap becomes a dull, wooden creak, and then it becomes nothing at all.

This is the Settle. It is not death. We have no words for death, only for *stiffening*.

When my limbs could no longer strike the stone, I did not lie down in the dark silt of the valleys where the Siphon-Mosses feed. I crawled into the gateway of the great beehive dome we had built on the eastern pass, where the current runs fastest from the deep trenches. I slid my lower root-braids into the joints between the three foundation blocks. I let my trunk swell one last time, filling the empty spaces between the unmortared basalt until the stone and my own bark became a single seam. 

I gave my turgor to the template. I became the mortar.

***

Now, I am twelve feet of mineralized cedar and copper-studded leather, wedged forever into the northern curve of the ring. I cannot turn. I cannot slide. My sap flows at the speed of rising cold, perhaps a single pulse every three sun-rumors. 

Yet, I have never seen the valley as clearly as I do now.

The dome above me is an acoustic lens of dry basalt, braced with three-inch bands of cold-hammered bronze that I myself helped to shape when my limbs were supple. The structure catches the great, heavy *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the house-sized Siphon-Mosses down in the valley. They are pumping today with a wet, rhythmic labor, sucking the salt-heavy water through their fibrous bellies and spitting it back into the dark. The sound comes through the foundation stones, up through my root-braids, and vibrates in the copper channels of my core. 

*Thrum.* The silt is accumulating on the western slopes.
*Thrum.* A thermal geyser three miles south has cracked its throat; the hiss of its sulfur-scald is louder today, crackling like dry brush in a wind I have never felt.

Above the dome, the *Glass-Ferns* are chiming. The current must be coming from the north; I can feel the high, crystalline ringing of their fifty-foot silica fronds. They do not bend, but they shiver. The vibration runs down their stems, through the basalt ridge, and meets me here in the dark. It is a sharp, blue-white sound, like the breaking of thin ice on a stone. 

And somewhere in the mid-water column, miles above my dome, a *Lace-Skin* is drifting. 

I know this because the chiming of the ferns has suddenly grown dull. The colossal green sheet of colonial algae—three miles of gossamer skin—is draped over the ridge like a fallen shroud, absorbing the vibrations, drinking the pale, chemical-violet light of the Needle-Grafts that cling to the silica. The Lace-Skin is hungry. It has come down from the ceiling to feed on the mineral-mists of our valley. I feel its touch as a soft, dampening weight upon the stone—a great silence that smells of rotted kelp and cold salt.

***

Then, the water changes.

The first sign is not a sound, but a thinning of the weight. The pressure in my vessels, which has remained at forty atmospheres for fifty years, begins to drop. The water is losing its salt. 

Up at the ceiling, where the giant ice-sheet groans against the iron sky, something has broken. 

The young Corded outside my dome feel it first. I hear them through the stone—the wet, heavy scraping of their root-ends as they drag themselves toward the shelter of the ring. They are slow, so slow, but the brine-fall is faster. 

The Siphon-Mosses stop their breathing. Down in the valley, the great *thrum-thrum* dies away. In its place comes a series of sharp, metallic *clacks* as their copper-colored valves shut to protect their soft inner pulp from the fresh-water poison. 

Then comes the roar.

It is the ice-fall. A vertical river of super-saline brine, freezing and heavy as liquid lead, is falling from the ceiling. It plunges through the warm, viscous mountain sea, a column of white, churning glass.

Outside, the Glass-Ferns are shattering. As the sub-zero brine-stream touches their crystalline fronds, the silica grows brittle under the thermal shock. They do not chiming now; they are screaming. It is a sound of ten thousand mirrors falling onto basalt. *Shatter-shatter-shatter-ring.* The needle-grafts that lived upon their electricity are extinguished, their violet light snout-out in the freezing dark.

The young Corded have reached my dome. They crowd into the narrow doorway, their dark-green, braided trunks pressing against one another. They do not have eyes, but they have the tension. They squeeze the water from their outer skins, venting it in small, warm bubbles that rise toward the ceiling. They are turning their outer layers to leather, freezing themselves from the outside in to save their copper-marrow.

One of them presses against me. Its skin is still soft, still smelling of the deep trenches. It is vibrating with the high-frequency hum of terror—the simple, primitive calculations of a body that still has the power to run.

I cannot reach out to it. My limbs are stone. My sap is mostly copper-rust and silt.

But through our contact, through the wet basalt that binds us both to the mountain, I send my weight. I send the slow, heavy calculus of the Settle. 

*I am here,* the stone says. *The dome has held for nine hundred ice-falls. The copper bands are thick. The basalt is deep-joined. We do not drift.*

The young one grows quiet. Its vibration slows, matching the long, cold frequency of my foundation blocks. Together, we watch the valley go dark.

The brine-fall roars for three moons. The water in my outer vessels freezes solid, but my core—protected by twelve feet of stone and the accumulated copper-crust of ages—remains liquid, a faint, warm thread of mineral thought humming in the middle of the ice.

We wait. 

We do not sleep, for we have no eyelids. We simply hold the mountain down, until the salt returns, and the first warm hiss of the geysers tells us it is time to breathe again.
