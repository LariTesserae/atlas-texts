To find me, you must look closer than the wide, flat floor of the Low-Silt. You must look past the grey-blue majesty of the forty-foot iron shafts, past the liver-colored rings of mold, and focus on the very point where the machinery disappears into the earth.

You must look into the *clearance*.

Between the polished iron of the piston and the wet basalt of its sleeve lies a gap of no more than three inches. It is a zone of immense shear, of hot oil-tides and black, pressurized water. Here, in the dark helical grooves that spiral around the circumference of the great shafts—grooves cut deep into the metal to distribute the lubricant—you would find me.

I am a **Groove-Ribbon**.

***

I do not have a head, nor do I have eyes. In a world of three-o'clock twilight and basalt tunnels, sight is a useless, fragile complexity. Instead, my body is a twenty-foot band of dense, rubbery gristle, no wider than a man’s hand and less than an inch thick. I fit perfectly into the helical track of the piston, lying within the iron like a thread in a screw.

My substance is a strange, slow chemistry: a compound of vulcanized lipids, tough copper-rich fibers, and a cold, self-healing jelly. If you were to pull me from my groove—though there is no force in the basin that would think to do so—I would look like a long, greasy strip of industrial packing, smelling of wet slate and hot oil.

My life is measured not in years or hours, but in the **Stroke**.

When the piston is extended, rising forty feet into the amber haze, I am exposed to the open air of the basin. This is my season of harvest. The soot-scented rain falls upon my leathery skin; the steam from the vents pools in my segments. Along my length are thousands of tiny, hook-like cilia made of a flexible, horn-like material. As the shaft remains stationary for its ten minutes of rest, these cilia twitch with a slow, hypnotic wave. They drag the pooling rainwater, the zinc-laden soot, and the smeared grease of the nearby Tallow-Wreck into the groove, packing it tightly against my flank.

I am a sponge of industry. I drink the waste of the world above.

Then, the earth shudders. The distant *clonk* travels through the iron, vibrating my copper-skeled heart. It is the signal.

The piston begins its descent. 

As I am carried down into the basalt sleeve, the amber light of the Low-Silt vanishes, replaced by a pressure that would flatten a solder-louse into pewter foil. The clearance between the iron and the stone is absolute. I am squeezed. 

In this dark, screaming compression, my function begins.

The extreme heat of the earth’s depths—channeled through the steam vents—cooks my rubbery body. Under this thermal stress, my interior vessels begin to secrete. I do not produce blood; I produce a light, straw-colored ester, a molecular surfactant that I have refined from the Tallow-Wreck's heavy lard and the zinc dust of the air. 

As the piston slides deeper, I release this fluid into the gap. It is a specialized, high-pressure lubricant. Without me, the heavy lard of the Tallow-Wrecks would polymerize under the heat, turning into a hard, sticky varnish that would eventually seize the forty-ton strokes. I break the lard down, thinning it, spreading it in a microfilm of perfect, friction-free wetness between the polished blue iron and the black basalt.

I feel the rock brush past me. It is a sensation of immense, smooth speed—though the piston moves no faster than a walking man. To me, the basalt is a ceiling of wet silk, sliding by with a deep, subterranean roar. If there is a grain of silt caught in the clearance, a tiny speck of rust-colored clay that would scratch the bore, my soft edges instantly engulf it. I hold the grit deep within my waxy flesh, neutralizing it, preventing it from scoring the metal, until I can carry it back to the surface.

I am the gasket. I am the tolerance. I am the silence between the metals.

***

In the depths of the sleeve, at the bottom of the stroke, there is a second of absolute stillness. The pressure is at its peak. The heat is a white, clean presence that rejuvenates my fibers, curing my rubbery skin and melting away the structural fatigue of the previous climb. I am home here, in the hot, dark oil-wells of the deep crust.

Then comes the "wet, sucking sigh."

The shaft begins to rise. The pressure eases. The basalt slides away in reverse, and I am drawn back up, segment by segment, into the quiet, three-o'clock air.

As I emerge, the solder-lice are waiting at the rim of the sleeve. They know the rhythm. They gather where the groove first clears the basalt, their wire antennae trembling in the warm dampness. They crawl onto my exposed surface, their pewter-chitin plates clicking against the iron. With their sharp, blind mandibles, they scrape away the pocketed grains of silt and the carbonized crusts of old grease that I have carried up from the depths. 

They do not hurt me; they clean my vents. We are parts of the same assembly.

I lie in my groove, cooling in the greasy mist, watching nothing, wanting nothing, waiting for the next *clonk* to rattle the clay. I have been in this piston for ten thousand years. The blue-grey iron has worn down by less than a millimeter in that time, polished to a mirror finish by my soft, oil-soaked body.

The sky is amber. The air smells of scorched lard. Below me, the earth hums its forty-cycle song, and I am perfectly, infinitely lubrication.
