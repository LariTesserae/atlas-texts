I would find myself in the middle circle. 

I would not be the hard, velvet-grey slate of the outer shingles, nor would I be the long, brassy wires that run like cold veins through the dry silt. I would be further down, sheltered from the shadowless light, in the dark and quiet core of the middle one—specifically, in the small, hollow chamber where the left knee once folded.

We were tall once. We had joints that swung, heels that struck the red stone with a wet, heavy thud, and names with vowels that sounded like splashing water. When we lay down, we did not do it all at once. We curled. We pulled our knees toward our chests to fit inside the small slate benches we had built, and then we let the stillness take us from the toes upward.

Where my left knee once formed a hinge, the bone did not flatten entirely. Instead, as the calcium turned to dry, porous pith—like the inside of a winter gourd—it left a tiny, rounded chamber. It is no larger than a wild plum. Within this dark, cellular space, I am the small pocket of air that has been trapped for three hundred years. 

I am the sigh that is waiting.

The life here is not lived in moments, but in lines of temperature. Through the floor of the basin, the brass-like wires bring us the news of the west wall. For years, there is only the dry, iron warmth of the stone—a level, golden hum that we drink through our scales. But then, deep in the night that is no darker than our day, the wire at my heel grows suddenly, piercingly cold. 

It is the welt-sign. Far away under the limestone, a single drop has fallen.

The cold does not shock; it is a thin, silver thread of water-thought entering the dry pith. It travels slowly up through the gourd-flesh of my lower leg, cooling the fibers, until it reaches my hollow. 

When the cold touches the chamber, the great slate-plates above me respond. The five-pace shingle of ironstone that is my outer skin begins to slide. The movement is so slow that a grain of red dust resting on the seam does not roll; it merely turns its rough face three degrees to the north. But inside, the pressure is immense. The ceiling of my little plum-sized cave descends by the width of a spider’s thread.

The cave pinches. And I—the three-decade-old air—am squeezed out.

I pass through the microscopic, weevil-bored vents of the slate skin. I leave the dark of the pith and enter the warm, motionless twilight of the basin floor. As I touch the air, I am no longer just breath; I have carried the dust of the old bone-gourd with me. I am the scent of dry sage. 

I rise. The basin is so still that I do not drift. I hang like a thin, invisible leaf, two inches above the red floor, smelling of things that died before the plateau lost its trees. For three days, I am the only thing in the basin that has a shape of its own.

Then, as the plate above settles back into its thirty-year lock, the tiny vacuum left behind in the knee-joint begins to draw. It does not pull hard. But slowly, over weeks, it coaxes a new dram of air down from the deep, cellar-cool crevices of the cliffs. This new air is clean, smelling of wet iron and very old subterranean dark. 

It enters the chamber. It warms. It becomes dry. It forgets the wet crevice it came from, and it learns the shape of my old knee. 

By the time the next drop gathers on the western limestone, I am quiet again, holding my breath, waiting for the cold thread to tell me it is time to speak.
