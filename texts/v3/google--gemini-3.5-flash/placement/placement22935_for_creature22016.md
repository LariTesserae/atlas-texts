The three needles of my feet do not step; they slide. 

They are tipped with caps of self-lubricating graphite, grey and worn down by ten thousand miles of travel, but still perfectly slick against the wind-polished basalt. As I move across the high tablelands, they make a sound like a pencil drawing a long, unbroken line across heavy paper. 

*Shh. Shh. Shh.*

I am a **Spindle-Gnomon** (*Stylus lithos*), though there is no one here to call me that. In the taxonomy of this dry world, I am neither plant nor animal, but an aggregate—a slow-thoughted wanderer of the flat-tops, built of the very things the wind has spent three billion years refining. 

My body is a tripod of hollow silica spars, nine feet tall, incredibly stiff and light. Where the three legs meet at my crown, they support a central bladder—an egg-shaped sphere of stretched, copper-colored vellum. Inside this bladder is a pocket of pure nitrogen gas. When the giant, white star rises over the rim of the world, its intense light hits the vellum, heating the nitrogen within. The gas expands, my bladder plumps and lifts, and my weight is reduced to almost nothing. In the heat of noon, I weigh no more than a dry leaf of glass-fern; in the deep, cold shadow of the canyons, I grow heavy and still.

Inside my hollow chest-cavity, suspended by three braided copper wires, hangs a single, heavy pendulum of ironstone. It is my heart, my compass, and my brain.

When the basin winds push against my vellum sail, I lean. As I lean, the ironstone pendulum swings, touching the inner graphite rings of my chassis. Each touch closes a circuit. Each contact sends a tiny, dry tick of static electricity down the core of my silica legs to my graphite toes. 

*Tick. Tick-tick.*

This is how I think. My thoughts are not wet, chemical rushes; they are a series of electrostatic intervals, determined by wind speed, gravity, and the slope of the stone beneath me. When the wind is still, I have no thoughts at all. I stand on the edge of the twenty-mile drops, my legs locked, my pendulum hanging perfectly plumb, waiting in a clean, glassy silence that might last for three decades. I do not mind the wait. Without moisture, there is no decay; without blood, there is no hunger. I am as permanent as the basalt.

***

Today, the air is thick with the scent of ozone and warm parchment. 

To my left, the great tableland drops away into the West Basin—a twenty-mile plunge that looks like a clean slice made by a giant knife. Fifty leagues across the void, the opposing plateau stands like a red-gold wall, its fluted pillars shimmering in the dry, distortionless light. 

I am walking the boundary of an ink-slash. The shadow cast by a three-hundred-foot ribbon-tree is so dark and sharp that it looks like a physical crack in the world. I must be careful. If I step entirely into the shadow, my vellum bladder will cool. The nitrogen will contract, my lift will vanish, and I will become heavy—several hundred pounds of brittle silica. If I am caught in the dark on a steep slope, I will slide into the basin and shatter like a dropped lamp.

So I walk the line. My right leg slides through the blinding, white glare, warm and humming with charge; my left leg slides through the absolute cold of the ink-shadow, stiff and silent. 

I am searching for the silver-grey.

A Great Loom-Moth passed over the basin three days ago. It was sixty feet of dry, fibrous bark, floating on the upper thermals where the sky is almost black. As it drifted, it shed its old outer skin. The skin—a massive, hollow tube of silver-grey paper—was torn by the high-altitude winds and scattered across the plateaus. 

To a Spindle-Gnomon, the skin of a Loom-Moth is life. My own vellum bladder has a small tear near its crown, pierced by a sharp grain of quartz dust during the last high-velocity pollen-front. I am leaking nitrogen, slowly, silently. I can feel the loss: my lift is slightly off-center; my ironstone pendulum leans too far to the south.

Ahead, caught in a grove of three-hundred-foot ribbon-trees, I see it.

The silver skin is draped over the copper-colored sails of the trees like a ghost. The ribbon-trees hum as the wind passes through their hollow trunks—a low, deep *huff-huff* that vibrates through the basalt and up into my graphite toes. The glass-ferns below them *clink* in the draft, a delicate, cold sound like key-rings jingling in an empty room.

I approach the grove. My middle leg reaches out, its graphite tip catching the edge of a basalt crack to anchor me. I raise my two front legs—which are tipped not with graphite, but with fine, three-pronged pincers of cold-hammered copper. 

With great deliberation, I reach up into the ribbon-tree. My movement is slow, governed by the rhythm of my swinging pendulum. *Tick. Slide. Pinch.*

The Loom-Moth skin is dry and stiff. It does not yield; it cracks like a sheet of thin ice. I peel away a three-foot panel of the silver fiber. It smells of old resin and ancient, sun-baked dust. 

Using my own electrostatic charge—the purple St. Elmo’s fire that has begun to gather at the tips of my copper pincers as the daily gold-storm approaches—I press the silver patch against the tear in my vellum bladder. The dry fibers of the moth-skin respond to the current, contracting and sealing themselves against my amber skin. The leak stops. Inside, the nitrogen warms and expands. 

I feel myself grow light again. My pendulum centers itself. The world tilts back into its proper perspective.

***

The sun is beginning to sink, and the sky is deepening from indigo to a hard, mineral violet. 

To the east, the pollen-front is finally arriving. It looks like a wall of solid gold, five miles high, moving across the basin with the majestic slowness of a glacier. It does not bring rain—the very word is a blank space in my mind—but it brings the spores of the carpet-mosses and the dry, flour-like dust of the silica-forests.

As the gold-front nears, the air pressure shifts. Below me, in the deep crevices of the basalt, the millions of scattered drift-cysts feel the change in static charge. 

*Ping.* 
*Ping-ping.*

The sound begins near the rim and spreads outward across the twenty-mile flat. It is a dry, collective snapping, like a forest of tiny glass bubbles breaking all at once. The cysts are opening. From their black carbon-glass shells, tiny, thread-like crawlers—no larger than pine needles—are dragging themselves into the light, ready to catch the gold-dust and begin their thousand-year growth into ribbon-trees.

The wind begins to rise, making its long, low *huff* through the fluted canyons. 

I turn my sails into the draft. I lift my front legs, balancing on my single rear needle, and let the wind carry me. I do not fly—I have no wings—but I glide, my graphite tip barely skimming the surface of the basalt, leaving a faint, silvery line behind me that the dust will erase by morning.

I am the Gnomon. I am the writer of lines that do not stay, on stone that does not change, under a sky that never weeps. I will slide across this high table until my graphite is dust and my silica is sand, and nothing of me will ever rot. I will only become part of the wind.
