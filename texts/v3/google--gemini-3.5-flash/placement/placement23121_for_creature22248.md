To live here is to understand that there are only two things in the universe: the **Stone** and the **Not-Stone**. 

The Not-Stone is vast, blue, loud, and hungry. It is the empty mile below and the pale vault above. It is the wind that tries to slip its fingers under your edges and pry you away. 

The Stone is safety. The Stone is food. The Stone is the only thing that does not fall.

I am down in the dirt-hollow of the dry circle, where the tall ones who did not adhere once built their wind-break. I am one of three, pressed flank-to-flank, our gray-blue keratin plates overlapping like shingles on a roof. To any eye looking down from the sky, we do not exist; we are simply a patch of the floor, a slightly rounded heap of river-shale, weathered and still.

Underneath my heavy plates, my body is a warm, soft engine of muscle and fluid. But my edges are hard. When the wind rises, as it is rising now, I pull my head and my six short limbs beneath the rim of my shell. I contract the lateral muscles of my skirt, drawing the tough, leathery perimeter of my body flush against the granite of the great basin. The vacuum holds. The wind can scream, it can roar through the three deep pipes in the ledge’s lip until the stone beneath my chest vibrates like a plucked string, but it cannot move me. I am part of the mountain’s weight.

Today, the salt-hunger is a dry itch in the back of my throat. 

I begin to move. It is not a walk—walking is a dangerous, hollow business that lifts the body away from the Stone. It is a ripple. I release the suction of my forward-most toe-pads, slide them three millimeters through the dry dust of the shelter, and let the microscopic hooks on their undersides catch the grain of the granite. Then I drag the rest of my weight forward. *Tshhh-click. Tshhh-click.* 

My sister-plate clicks against mine as I nudge past her. We exchange a brief, dry rhythm of taps—*tclck, tclck-tlc*—reassuring ourselves of each other’s weight, our shared gravity. 

I reach the lip of the dark granite mortar. This stone is different from the lime-stone of the cliff. The cliff-stone tastes of cold rain and dissolved shells; it is soft, porous, and always weeping. But this basin—this hard, dark stranger hauled up from the deep world by the ones who went away—it tastes of iron, of old pressure, of the black roots of the earth. 

I lower my head into the smooth, spiraling groove that my ancestors and I have carved into the basin over a thousand seasons. I extend my tongue. It is long, rough as a file, and wet with my own saliva. I rasp it against the dark stone. 

The mineral salt is sharp and metallic, a cold fire that wakes up my blood. Each lick is a tiny vibration that travels up the bones of my skull. As I scrape, I can hear the rest of the ledger through the soles of my feet. The stone is a massive conductor of noise.
*   I feel the high-frequency *thrum-thrum* of the Scythe-Wings clinging to the wet cracks high above, their tiny hearts beating like rain.
*   I feel the heavy, wet *plop* of the weeping wall, each drop hitting the moss with a distinct, low thud.
*   I feel the slow, heavy slide of a Chime-Snail entering the throat of the eastern wind-pipe. 

Suddenly, the tone of the wind shift. 

The low, rumbling bass of the wind-pipes begins to rise, sharpening into a thin, hollow whistle. The temperature against my exposed plates drops instantly. The air, which had been warm and smelling of sun-baked peat, turns thick and wet. 

The cloud is coming. 

It rises from the Not-Stone like a blind, white beast, swallowing the ledge in seconds. The brilliant sky is gone. The green moss is gone. There is only a foot of gray space in front of my nose, and then the white dampness. 

To a creature of the lowlands, this fog would be a blindness. To us, it is a opening. 

Beneath me, the limestone seeps begin to run faster, greased by the heavy condensation. The Glass-Frogs in the moss gardens let out their first, dry *tclck-tclck*. The sound bounces off the vertical wall behind us, off the stone circle, off the lip of the ledge. In my mind, the world lights up. The sonar-clicks of the frogs draw a perfect map of the amphitheater—I can "see" the exact curve of the weeping wall, the sway of the hanging ferns, the deep drop where the water turns to upward-blowing mist.

The moisture settles on my keratin back. It rolls down the neat, grooved channels between my plates, funneling down to my flanks where my suction pads drink it in. My skin softens. The dry dust of the shelter turns to slick clay. 

I back out of the granite mortar, my plates clicking against its rim. I do not need the dry shelter now. The wet is when we feast. 

I ripple out of the circle, over the flat shale, and onto the vertical face of the weeping wall. Here, where a human would see only a terrifying drop into nothingness, I find a wide, three-dimensional pasture. I tilt my body downward, my head pointing toward the mile of empty air, my six feet locked onto the wet, mossy slate. The water trickles over my nose, cool and sweet. 

I rasp at a patch of black lichen, my body vibrating in time with the roaring wind, completely secure, perfectly heavy, holding the mountain down.
