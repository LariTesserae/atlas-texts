To begin, there is no sound of waking. Waking is merely a density that slowly thins. 

It starts in the belly, deep within the cleft where the stone wall meets the floor of star-moss. Down there, thirty feet below the mossy carpet, the earth is breathing. The thermal vents push up a thick, sulfurous heat that keeps my central marrow—that slow, white latex—from turning to dry chalk. For eighty years, or perhaps a hundred, that warmth has been enough. I have been a shape among shapes, a grey buttress of slate and lichen, curved to fit the eastern curve of the basin.

But the balance has drifted. 

I hear it first not in my head—for I have no ears to speak of—but in my palms. My left hand is wedged into a fissure six feet above the floor, fingers curled into a shallow bowl. Over the decades, the weeping water from the limestone lip above has found this hand. It has filled the cup of my palm, creating a tiny, hanging font. 

For a long time, the pool in my hand was quiet. It held two *Vitrella*—two small, clear globules of gel that sat in my palm like cold, living glass. When the plum-sized drops fell from the high ceiling, they would strike the *Vitrella* with a deep, liquid *plim* that vibrated down my arm-bones, a clean, resonant note that told me the water was pure and the flow was true.

But lately, the note has gone sour. 

The drop is hitting too far to the left, striking the bare, dry knuckle of my index finger instead of the gel-pool. It makes a dry, flat *tchick*. It is a thin, irritated sound. It does not belong in the cathedral. It is a chip in the silence.

To correct this, I must move.

***

The decision to move is the hardest part. It requires pulling the heat up from my belly. I have to call to the white sap in my core and ask it to flow upward into the shoulder, then down the long, cold granite of my arm. 

It moves like cold honey. As the warmth enters my joints, the grey lichen that has sealed my elbow to the cliff-face begins to stretch. It resists. Lichen does not like to hurry; it has its own small dreams of yellow margins and slow expansion. I can feel the tiny, tearing pins of its roots breaking one by one with a sound like dry grass being stepped on by a bird.

Below the moss, the *Pale Crawlers* feel the shift in my temperature. To them, my waking arm is a sudden chimney. I can feel their soft, almond-colored bellies sliding along my ribs, their pink, thread-like gills brushing against my stone flanks like a flurry of warm, wet feathers. They are gathering the flakes of dead skin and dry lichen that fall from my shifting joints, clearing the way. They are my cleaners, working in the dark.

By the second year of my waking, the heat has reached my wrist.

My right hip is currently home to a *Glass-back* snail. It has been there since the year the high pine on the ridge fell. Its obsidian shell, as large as a shield, is pressed hard against my thigh. It is so still that the star-moss has climbed its black sides, but I can feel the faint, heavy sliding of its muscular foot. It is translating my slow warmth as an invitation. It intends to climb my torso to reach the algae on my neck. It will take him five years to complete the journey. I do not mind. His shell is a fine bell; when the drops hit his back, they ring with a low, metallic *gong* that makes my ribs shake with a dark, pleasant hum. 

***

By the third year, I have tilted my wrist. 

It is only three inches of movement—a geological twitch—but it is enough. The cup of my palm slides back into the path of the dripping ceiling. 

The first plum of water falls. It drops fifty feet through the lime-gold light, swelling as it descends, heavy and oil-thick. 

I hold my breath. My skin, porous and wet, waits.

The drop strikes. It does not hit the bone of my knuckle. It lands precisely in the center of the gel-pool, directly onto the back of the larger *Vitrella*. 

The creature deforms under the blow, stretching into a long, trembling lens, absorbing the force without breaking, before bouncing back to its perfect sphere. The sound is a deep, round *tock* that rings through the limestone basin, echoing off the wet walls, clean and heavy. 

The vibration travels down my arm, through my shoulder, and settles into my spine. 

The cathedral is in tune again. 

***

Now, the warmth can recede. 

I do not need to pull my hand back. It has found its place. The *Vitrella* in my palm are already filtering the water, their simple, transparent bodies pulsing with the rhythm of the drops, clearing the pool of the mountain’s sulfur. 

The *Glass-back* has reached my ribs now, its obsidian shell cool against my chest, waiting for the next chime. The *Pale Crawlers* have gone back down into the deep, warm carpet of star-moss, satisfied that the heat-event has passed.

The grey lichen will begin to grow over my knuckles again, knitting my skin back into the stone of the ridge. The green moss will creep up my forearm, turning the limb into a soft, living terrace. 

I will close the spaces in my mind. I will let the white latex in my veins grow thick and still. 

We have negotiated our peace, the stone and I, the water and the moss. The sun will strike the mist; the clouds will run wild over the high summit; but down here, in the warm bowl, nothing will change for a long time. 

I will listen to the bells. I will become the mountain again, dreaming of water.
