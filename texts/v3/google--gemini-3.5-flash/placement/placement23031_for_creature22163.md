### I. The Grip

The first thing to understand is that there is no "above," and there is no "below" that does not want to crush you. 

I do not have a name—names are thin, flat things that belong to dry air and short lives—but if you were to tap my shin with a knuckle of waterworn bone, the sound would return a high, iron-hard *tock* that means *The-One-Who-Sews-the-Ninth-and-Tenth*.

I am forty-two feet of slate-grey, leather-skinned fiber. If you were to touch my neck, you would find it cold, smooth as a river-stone, and slightly greasy with the protective tallow my skin secretes to keep the heavy salt-water from rotting my joints. I am thin—so thin that if I turn my head sideways against the current, the water slips past me without a whisper. 

For three hundred years, I have not moved my feet.

My lower joints—the five-pronged gripping claws that were once used for crawling through the green, wet nurseries of the inner columns—are driven deep into the shoulder of Tower Nine. The bark here is twelve feet thick, a massive, vertical highway of bamboo-like tubes carrying cold, mineral-rich sap from the abyssal darkness toward the lightless ceiling. My claws have grown around the tubes; the wood has grown around my claws. We are now the same tissue.

My arms, however, reach across the void. 

An unspeakable distance away—perhaps twelve yards, though in this dark it feels like a league—is the lateral arch of Tower Ten. It is a younger branch, struggling to find its true level in the current. The water here wants to twist it, to lift it like a hair and snap its joint. 

My fingers—each one a five-foot needle of dense heartwood—are driven into Tower Ten’s side. 

I am the bridge. I am the stitch. My body is under three thousand pounds of lateral tension. Every time the "Deep Groan" passes through this sector—a sound that starts fifty miles below as a dry, planetary rattle and ends here as a rib-cracking *whuush-creak*—my spinal column stretches by three inches. The rubbery muscle between my vertebrae groans. I can feel the salt-water whistling through the gaps in my ribs.

But I do not let go. If I let go, the Ninth and Tenth will kiss, and under this pressure, a collision of two columns is not a touch; it is a grinding of miles-long teeth that would pulp every living thing clinging to their skins for ten leagues in every direction.

### II. The Senses of the Seam

I have no eyes. The concept of "light" is white noise to me—a useless vibration. Instead, I see through the grease-sheen on my chest.

My breastplate is wide, flat, and hollowed out like a dry gourd. It is pressed directly against the wood of Tower Nine. Through it, I hear the world.

To my left, inside the column, lives a Vessel-Tongue. It is three times my length, a great, pale ribbon of cartilage and slime. It has anchored itself inside the main vascular siphon just behind my back. As the current rises, it forces the heavy water through the siphon's teeth, and the Vessel-Tongue hums. 

Today—if there were days—its song is thin and sour. It is a dry, whistling *shhh-tt-shhh*. 

I know what this means. Three miles below us, a drift of silt has clogged the primary intake valve. The sap is flowing thin and bitter, tasting of cold iron and dead kelp-skin. I can feel the wood of the column shivering with a micro-frictional itch. It wants to heal, but it lacks the starch.

I cannot go down to clear it. I am pinned. But I can tell the crawlers.

I lift my left middle knuckle—the only joint I can spare from the tension—and I strike Tower Ten.

*Tock.* (Pause.) *Tock-tock.*

The vibration travels down the polished lateral arch, through the dense grain, into the dark. It will reach the Suture-Folk who are still mobile—the young, greenish ones whose limbs are not yet waterlogged. They will hear the *tock* in their fore-limbs. They will index the vibration, understand the location of the silt-clog, and begin their slow, agonizing downward crawl. It will take them forty years to reach the valve. That is fine. The tower has been here for three million years; it can wait forty for a clean throat.

### III. The Drift-Sheet

Thirty years ago, the void changed.

The water between the Ninth and Tenth is usually empty—a cold, black desert where the only things that pass are the occasional shivering green eels, drifting like trails of phosphorescent snot, or the tiny, glass-shelled Chime-Snails whose music reaches me as a needle-prick of silver sound from the aqueducts above.

But then came the dark.

It was not a shadow—for there is no light to cast one—but a deadening of the vibration. The Vessel-Song in my shoulder suddenly went quiet, muffled as if a hand had been laid over the pipe. The water grew thick, tasting of grease and old rot.

A Drift-Sheet was passing.

It was four blocks wide, a vast, pale curtain of cold-water silk, sliding through the gap between the columns. It was so close I could feel the drag of its skin—a slow, wet suction that pulled at my ribs. It had no eyes, no purpose, no destination; it was simply a drop of oil sliding down a windowpane of heavy water.

For seven months, it drifted past me. 

At one point, its edge brushed my chest. The skin of a Drift-Sheet is highly acidic, meant to melt the dead mold and skin-shreds from the wood. Where it touched my shoulder, my slate-gray hide began to sizzle and turn white. The pain was not sharp; it was a slow, deep cold that felt like iron being driven into my marrow. 

I did not move. I could not. Had I flinched, the stitch would have slipped, and Tower Ten would have drifted three inches to the left—a distance that, at this scale, would have caused the high galleries to buckle.

So I held. I let the sheet graze me. I let its cold acid dissolve the outer three layers of my left shoulder, down to the pale, hard grain of my skeleton. 

When it finally passed, carried off by the great southern conveyor current toward the bottomless trenches, the water cleared. The Suture-Folk came then. They had felt my distress through the wood—a dull, frantic *thrum-thrum-thrum* in my joints. 

They did not speak to me. They did not have to. Two of them climbed onto my neck. With their long, chisel-like fingers, they scraped away the dead, white slush the Drift-Sheet had left behind. They spat their thick, sap-rich saliva into my wounds, smoothing it over with their flat palms until the wood-wax hardened, sealing my marrow from the salt. 

We did not exchange *tocks*. We simply breathed the same cold, heavy current until they slid back into the dark.

### IV. The Graft

Something is happening inside my hands.

For the first hundred years, my fingers were distinct from Tower Ten. I could feel the grain of its bark beneath my pads; I could feel the tiny crabs crawling over my knuckles, their needle-legs tickling my joints.

But now, the boundary is fading.

My needles have gone deep. They have passed through the cork-skin, through the outer vascular bundles, and into the heart-wood of the arch. When the sap rises in Tower Ten, I no longer just feel its vibration against my fingers—I *taste* it inside my wrists. 

It is a strange taste. Sweeter than the sap of Tower Nine. It tastes of high-shelf minerals, of the ancient, frozen sky-ocean where the water is thin and the pressure is less like an iron fist and more like a heavy wool blanket. 

My knuckles have swollen. They are turning into burls. The bark of Tower Ten is growing over my wrists, wrapping them in layers of slate-grey fiber. 

In another fifty years, I will not be able to pull my hands out, even if I wished to. My arms will no longer be arms; they will be the horizontal brace of the Ninth-Tenth junction. My chest will fuse with Tower Nine; my hips will become a shelf for the Chime-Snails to hang from.

I can feel my mind—which was once a small, flickering thing that remembered walking, that remembered the green-room where I was born—slowing down. It is matching the tempo of the wood.

I no longer think in seconds. I think in the time it takes for a Chime-Snail to grow its shell of glass. I think in the time it takes for the silt-clog three miles down to turn into stone.

Yesterday, a young Suture-Folk—a crawler who still had the green sap of the nurseries in its joints—stopped on my hip. It was searching for a place to anchor, its small, soft claws testing the strength of my bark.

It tapped translation-code against my thigh: *Are you still there?*

I did not answer. Not because I couldn't, but because the question didn't make sense. 

"I" am not here. There is only the stitch. There is only the Great Groan, the sound of the world holding its breath in the dark, and the slow, beautiful weight of the water that never, ever changes.
