I did not fall. Nothing falls here. We only drift, or we hold, or we are torn.

For a long time, I was part of the *we*. I was a Barolith, or at least the wet, heavy promise of one. I lived inside the dark, sluggish collective that gathered on the saddle between the three tallest spikes of the Teeth. We were a carriage-sized mass of basalt-crust, bound together by the thick, iron-rich cement of our own making. We sat in the amphitheater—that crude, mortarless ring of black blocks that we piled up not to look at the sky, but to hold ourselves against the tearing current. We wedged our heavy, stony flanks into the crevices, our shells vibrating like tuning forks in the warm, teal gloom. 

Through the basalt of our plates, we listened. We heard the deep, miles-down labor of the earth. We felt the volcanic roots of the Teeth shifting, the slow, tectonic grind of plates that did not know the sky had liquefied above them. We were heavy. We were safe.

But my cement was weak. Or perhaps the sound was too loud.

There is a moment—not of birth, but of shedding—when the stone-clad life becomes impossible. Under the immense, screeching pressure of the Well, a hairline fracture appeared in my outer basalt plate. The water, which behaves like a solid under sudden blows, did not merely seep in; it drove into the crack like an iron wedge. The high-velocity currents, shearing against the stone, began to peel my heavy shell away. 

I did not regrow the plates. I did not secrete the iron-rich glue to heal the scab. Instead, I let the Well have the stone. 

I watched my shell—several thousand pounds of dark, porous basalt—drift away in slow, weightless orbits above the amphitheater, leaving me behind. I became what the others avoid. I became **The Un-crusted**. 

***

Now, I am a naked soft-core. 

Without my basalt armor, I am roughly the size of a drowned stag, though I have no shape that stays. I am a dense, quivering mass of heavy, plum-purple gelatin, so thick with suspended magnetite filings that I am nearly black in the bruised twilight of the pool. I have no eyes, no bones, and no limbs. I am a single, massive, hydraulic muscle, veined with raw mineral iron that aligns itself, whether I wish it or not, with the magnetic pull of the volcanic spires below.

To live without stone in the Well is to live without a filter. 

When the 3.0-scale roar of the currents collides, the sound does not vibrate my shell; it vibrates me. The sub-bass hum passes through my jelly like a hand through wet clay. I do not "hear" the mountain; I am deformed by it. Every scream of the water reshapes my contours. A sudden, hydraulic screech will flatten me into a disk; a deep-bore moan will stretch me into a thick, shivering column. I am a living transducer, converting the kinetic violence of the Well into the raw, internal heat that keeps my purple tissue from dissolving.

To move, I still use the siphons—the row of muscular vents along my flanks. But because I have no heavy stone shell to launch, the physics of my travel are bizarre and fragile. When I eject a high-velocity jet of water, the medium directly behind me instantly solidifies into a glass-like pillar of non-Newtonian ice-water. But instead of pushing against it and flying forward, my soft body stretches. I cling to the hardened water like a rubber band caught on a nail, my body thinning to a translucent thread before the pillar melts back into the churn and I snap forward, gathering myself into a dense, heavy knot once more. 

It is a violent, jerky way to travel. It sounds like the wet snapping of leather. But it is fast enough to keep me out of the paths of the great drifting blocks.

I have survived because I am a shadow. 

The **Churn-Sieve**, that forty-foot ribbon of non-Newtonian muscle that rules the twilight between the spires, does not hunt me. She hunts by vibration, and to her, food sounds like stone. She listens for the slow, heavy rasp of basalt shells grinding against one another in the current, or the sharp *clack* of a Barolith pushing off its temporary pillars. I am silent to her. I have the density of the surrounding water, the same iron signature as the volcanic vents. When she sweeps through the dark, a cold, pale javelin of frozen water, she passes right through my outer fringes, treating me as nothing more than a thick, warm eddy in the teal current.

Sometimes, I drift near the spires where the **Tonotopes** cling. They are pale, flat scars of lard-white muscle, drinking the sound from the stone. Because I am so heavy with magnetite, they occasionally mistake me for a new basalt tooth. They reach out with their flat, sucking discs, trying to anchor themselves to my skin. It is a strange, cold sensation—like dozens of frozen coins being pressed into my flesh. But they cannot hold. My body is too fluid, too active. As I writhe away, their violet bioluminescence lights up my interior, revealing the dark, swirling clouds of iron filings inside my purple core, like a storm trapped in a grease-drop.

But my greatest strange joy—and my most terrifying danger—are the **Voids**.

The dry-air bubbles, those silent spheres of pressurized mountain wind, drift constantly through the churning warm water. When you are clad in stone, a void is merely a bump, a momentary loss of buoyancy. But to me, a void is a hollow wound.

When an air-bubble strikes my soft body, it does not bounce. It enters me. My purple jelly parts, and the pressurized sphere of mountain sky is swallowed whole into my center. 

For a few minutes, I am a creature with a dry, silent heart. 

Inside the bubble, the screaming roar of the Well vanishes. The magnetite in my core stops vibrating. The sudden, absolute silence is so intense it feels like a physical puncture, a vacuum that threatens to collapse my jelly inward. But inside that pocket of dry, sulfurous wind, there are often **Anemochores**. 

These tiny, paper-fine flakes of grey ash, held together by threads of dry protein, find themselves trapped inside my body. They do not know I am alive; to them, my internal walls are a wet, dark cave that has temporarily sheltered their wind-blown home. They flutter against my inner lining like dry soot. Their tiny, static-charged bodies tickle me—a dry, electrical prickling that is the only quiet sensation I ever experience. 

While they are inside me, they feast on the sulfurous gases I have absorbed from the Teeth. They clean my interior, consuming the volcanic impurities that would otherwise poison my tissue. We drift together through the teal twilight: a soft, heavy monster carrying a pocket of dry, silent sky and a handful of ash-colored ghosts.

Eventually, the void must escape. A sudden cross-current will tear at my side, or I will strike a drifting stone, and the bubble will squeeze out of me, popping back into the wet roar. The return of the sound is always a convussive blow. It strikes my denuded body like a hammer, making my iron filings scatter and my margins bleed a thick, dark indigo ink.

But until the next silent pocket finds me, I drift. 

I am no longer a Barolith. I no longer care about the ancient, slow gossip of the tectonic plates beneath the amphitheater. Let the others sit in their stone ring and listen to the earth die. I am soft, I am raw, and I am wide open to the screaming, beautiful now of the water.
