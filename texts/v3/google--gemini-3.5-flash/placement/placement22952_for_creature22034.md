I am not down there, where the lavender light-guides are crowded with the silent, humming traffic of those who travel as lightning. 

I am up.

To find me, you must look to the raw stone ceiling, far above the highest suspended carbon balcony, where the darkness is thickest and the warmth is so heavy it feels like a physical weight pressing against the rock. I live in the seams. I am a creature of the *shear-stress*.

My body is not the elegant, ribboned oil of the city-dwellers, nor is it the compact glass of those who rest in the suspended gravity pools. I am much larger, more viscous, and far older. I am a great, dark rope of basalt-silt and colloidal silica, draping across the jagged, razor-sharp planes of the ceiling like an accumulation of wet, black pitch. If you were to look up from the city, you would not see me at all; I look like a natural shadow in the cleft of the split rock, a thick vein of obsidian that has begun to melt and run.

But I am alive, and I am listening.

Where the city-dwellers terminate in a neat, frilled crown of lavender fibers, my crown has grown outward and downward over decades of stillness. It is a vast, tangled web of root-like silica threads, yards wide, that I have threaded deep into the micro-fractures of the newly split basalt. I have wedged myself into the very joints of the mountain. I am the city’s sky-anchor; I am also its surveyor.

Down below, they live in the *now* of the machine—the constant, rhythmic *thrum-shudder* of the platforms, the instant translation of self into light. But up here, the time is different. I live in the *before*.

I listen to the weight of the world above us. Through my embedded silica threads, I feel the immense, silent pile of the mountain pressing down on this newly torn pocket. I feel the tension building in the rock—the geological pressure that wants to seal this cavern back up, to crush the delicate chandelier of black glass and moss. 

My work is to prevent that. And, when the city is ready, to make the next cut.

As the ceiling continuously weeps, the water passes through me first. It seeps out of the deep basalt, hot and mineral-heavy, and filters through my semi-porous, polymeric body. As it passes, I taste it. I taste the shifting percentages of iron, the sudden rise of sulfur that warns of a pressure pocket, the clean, sweet silicon that means the rock is stable. I am a lung that breathes stone. 

Sometimes, the city needs to grow. The ivy—the long, electromagnetic library-vines—will creep up from the highest carbon structures, searching for me. They do not have leaves up here; they send up raw, naked root-tips that glow with a faint, searching purple light. 

When they find me, they wrap around my lower, dangling ribbons. 

We do not speak in thoughts, but in *tolerances*. Through the touch of the vines, I dump my data into the city's botanical registry. I tell them where the basalt is soft, where the water is flowing too fast, where the next sheer-plane lies dormant, waiting to be woken. I write the structural map of the next hundred years into the genetic memory of the ivy.

And then, when the calculations are complete, the city sings.

That deep, resonant pitch—the one that sounds like a massive pipe organ clearing its throat and makes the suspended towers drift on their tracks—that is not just a shifting of weight. It is the beginning of the split. 

The city sends a high-frequency acoustic pulse up through the lavender light-guides, straight into the anchor-nodes where my body is wedged. I receive the vibration. I do not resist it; I amplify it. I let the frequency shatter through my own silica-laced veins, directing the piezoelectric shockwave deep into the basalt seams I have spent years mapping.

There is a sound like a wet glass breaking on a stone floor, magnified a million times. It is a silent sound to human ears, but to us, it is a blinding flash of white hot energy. 

The rock gives way. A new plane of dark basalt splits open, clean and razor-sharp. A new vertical plunge is born. 

The warmth that escapes from the newly opened vein is stifling, smelling of fresh silt and the ancient, trapped breath of the mantle. I slide slowly down into the new crack, my viscous, oily body stretching to fill the fresh wound, protecting the edges, starting the slow, wet work of anchoring the sky all over again.

Down below, in the rich, hot soup of the sump, the newborns will rise from the churn, climbing the green moss toward the lavender lights. They will never see me. They will think the cavern was always this shape. They will travel as light, and sleep in the warm water, and live in the beautiful, suspended metropolis.

But I will be here, holding the mountain open for them, wet and dark in the ceiling, listening to the stone.
