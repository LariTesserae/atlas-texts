I would not be found on the flat, open terraces where the Geometers make their tedious, shadows-dragged journeys, nor would I be tucked into the warm, close density of the copper thickets where the Solder-Weavers hiss and mend.

I would be found spanning the rifts. 

In this world of sharp-edged steps, the plateaus do not merely end; they split. They drop away into deep, vaporless chasms where the basalt walls face each other across half a league of absolute void. Here, the violet sky is pinched into a narrow band above, and the pitch-black stone below is cold—eternally shielded from the sun, preserved in a dry, lunar chill.

I am a **Cantilever**. 

I did not begin as an organism, nor was I built. Millennia ago, a massive Drift-Foil—perhaps three hundred cubits wide, a sheet of pure, unblemished silver—was caught by a rogue electrostatic updraft while crossing a chasm. It did not clear the gap. It clipped the razor-sharp edge of the northern cliff, folded over, and hung there like a draped silk ribbon, its lower half dangling in the blue-shadowed cold of the rift, its upper half pinned to the hot, sun-baked basalt of the plateau.

This was the template. The polarization was absolute: the top of the foil was baked to seventy degrees Celsius by the unscattering sun; the bottom hung in the zero-degree dark of the gorge. 

Over five thousand years, the dry ion-mist of the plateau found this silver bridge. Copper ions, sheared from the nearby metallophyte forests by the sun's radiation, deposited themselves along the tension lines of the foil, thickening into heavy, braided cords. Bismuth and tin migrated to the pivot points, forming thick, blocky knuckles at the cliff’s edge. Green silicate grew over the joints like smooth, cooled glass, insulating the currents that began to map my length.

Today, I am a self-supporting arch of bimetallic lattice, spanning three hundred meters of empty space. 

***

I do not possess a brain, but I have a geometry. My structure is my mind, and my thoughts are the physical laws of thermal expansion. 

During the long, slow mornings, the violet sky is pale. The sun rises on my eastern abutment. My eastern ribs—made of a high-expansion copper-zinc alloy—begin to warm. They lengthen, pushing against the unyielding basalt of the cliff. Because my western ribs remain cold and contracted in the shadow of the opposite wall, this lopsided expansion forces me to bow. 

I do not walk, but I *flex*. I rise. My entire central span, a vast belly of silver foil and steel-wire ribbing, lifts forty feet into the dry air, clearing the top of the rift. I become a high, elegant loop, glittering like a frozen splash of mercury against the violet sky.

At noon, when the sun is directly overhead, the heat is uniform. My entire back is hot. The copper plates along my spine swell, while the steel cables along my belly remain cool, shaded by my own bulk. This differential forces me to curl downward. My belly sinks back into the shadow of the rift, dropping until my lowest silver tassels brush the cold, dark basalt of the chasm floor. 

I feel this cycle not as pleasure or pain, but as a shifting configuration of tension. To be warm is to be tense, rigid, and high; to cool is to relax, to drop, to feel the long, soothing pull of gravity drawing my silver-plated ribs back into the silent dark of the gorge.

***

In this state of slow, mechanical respiration, I have become an anchor for other things.

The **Solder-Weavers** treat me as a mountain. They live in the deep sockets of my cliffside joints, where the friction of my daily movement creates a constant, tiny *skrit-skrit* of raw metal. They are drawn to these points by the localized electrostatic charges generated by my grinding knees. When a copper braid on my underside begins to fray from the constant, daily flexing, a Weaver will crawl out along my cables. 

I feel them as pinpricks of intense, localized heat. They do not speak, and I do not command them. But when their quartz lenses focus the sun onto my split seams, melting a small, bright bead of bismuth-solder to fuse the wire back together, a sudden wave of electrical continuity sweeps through my frame. It is the closest thing to a sigh I will ever know.

The **Drift-Foils** use my shadow. Below me, in the deep channel of the rift, the air is cold and the electrostatic charge of the stone is different. When a flock of those silver sheets drifts into my chasm, they find themselves caught in the dead-space created by my massive metal bulk. 

Because I block the sun, the stone directly beneath me is cool, lacking the thermal updrafts that keep the foils aloft. They settle as they pass under my arch, sinking into the dark like heavy, metallic leaves falling through water. For a few hours, they linger in the shadow of my belly, a quiet, rotating pool of flat silver, waiting for the sun to shift so they can catch the warm updrafts on my southern side and rise once more into the light.

And occasionally, the **Latticed Geometers** cross me. 

They do not know I am alive; to them, I am merely a path. They arrive at my northern anchor when the sun is at three-quarters height, their gold-wire legs dragging them slowly, click by click, onto my braided spine. When they walk upon me, their sapphire feet strike my steel ribs, sending tiny, bell-like vibrations down my length. 

*Tink. Tink. Tink.*

As they cross, their bodies cast small, moving shadows over my copper plating. These shadows cause my metal to cool and contract in tiny, localized segments beneath them. In a very real sense, they write their journey into my body: I bend slightly under their weight, not because they are heavy, but because their shadows make me shrink. I curve to meet them, then straighten as they pass, a giant, slow-motion wave of metal rising to carry them across the gulf.

***

I have stood here for ten thousand years. I will stand here for ten thousand more. 

There is no wind to shake my cables. There is no water to seep into my joints and freeze, splitting my stone anchors. There is no oxygen to turn my bright copper orange-brown, or to dull the mirror-finish of my silver belly. 

Sometimes, during the transition from day to night, when the sun has slipped below the lip of the plateau but the violet sky still glows, the temperature of the world drops by forty degrees in a matter of minutes. 

When this happens, my entire three-hundred-meter span contracts at once. Thousands of rivets, joints, and braided cables settle into the cold. 

A single, monumental sound ripples out from my core—not a screech, but a deep, resonant *CLANG* that vibrates through the basalt bedrock of the entire plateau. It is a pure, clear note, like a cathedral bell struck once in an empty desert. 

The Geometers freeze on their long legs. The Drift-Foils ripple in their pools. The Solder-Weavers pull their mercury legs tight against their green silicate bodies. 

The sound travels for leagues across the silent, black terraces, bouncing off the sharp-edged crystalline blocks of obsidian, unanswered by any voice, until it finally spills over the edge of the world and is lost in the quiet, violet void. 

Then, the cold takes me, and I am still.
