I am not on the slate. To be on the slate is to be subject to the wind that has no name, to the black ice that creeps over the ledges like a slow, flat tooth, and to the sheer, terrifying drop of the new peaks. 

Instead, I am inside. 

I live in the wet, iron belly of the Fourth Crawler—the one with the rusted third gudgeon-pin that squeaks with a high, wet *cree-shuck* every time the machine shifts its weight to the north. 

I was not born here. Once, many seasons of coal-smoke ago, I was a crevice-drifter in the dark. I was five feet of blind, pale parchment, squeezing my body through the water-logged fractures of the basalt three hundred feet below the summits, tasting the sulfur-springs and eating what fell into the dark. But the rock down there is cold. It is a slow, freezing wet that saps the skin. 

When the Fourth Crawler locked its pneumatic legs into the ridge above my fracture to sink its deep drill, I felt the vibration first as a rhythmic thunder that turned the groundwater to silver froth. Then came the drill: a sixty-foot column of spinning diamond and hot water. I did not flee it. I was drawn to the heat. I climbed the wet, vertical borehole it left behind, sliding through the slurry of crushed basalt, until I slipped through the drainage grates of the machine’s lower bilge.

I have never gone back down.

Here, in the crawlways between the condenser tanks and the main piston-well, the world is always warm. It is a dark of dripping bronze, hot rubber, and the heavy, yellow smell of cylinder grease. 

I have changed since I came aboard. My white, parchment skin has turned a thick, greasy yellow-gray, tanned by the constant bath of mineral oil and coal-soot. I am shorter now, more muscular, my hydrostatic body having adapted to the high-pressure pulses of the steam-lines. I move through the copper pipeways like a living clog, sliding my soft, ribless bulk through three-inch boiler-feed tubes to clear the lime-scale and the crusts of boiler-scab.

I am the machine’s secret gardener.

The crawler does not know I am here—it has no brain to know things—but its systems function better because of my presence. When the sulfur-heavy condensation pools in the low bends of the exhaust lines, threatening to rust the iron from the inside out, I lie there. I drink the acidic water, neutralizing it with the heavy, alkaline secretions of my skin, and spit out clean, black mineral paste. 

Sometimes, the oil-lice find me. They are small, flat, trilobite things that live on the grease of the joints. They are hard-shelled and tickle as they crawl over my soft flanks, scraping away the dead, soot-caked skin I shed every few weeks. We have an understanding, the lice and I: I do not block their access to the heavy grease-buckets, and they keep my breathing-pores free of coal-grit.

Above me, through the thick iron ceilings, I can hear the grease-birds. 

They do not know me either, but I know them by their weight. I hear the *tap-tap-tap* of their heavy, yellow beaks against the cooling fins directly above my favorite steam-pipe. When the wind outside rises to a gale, hundreds of them crowd into the exhaust vents. Sometimes, a fat, grease-drunk chick will lose its footing and fall down the wide, soot-blackened chimney into the main blower-fans. 

By the time it reaches the bilge, it is no longer a bird. It is a wet lump of tallow, feathers, and crushed bone. 

I am waiting for it. 

I glide out from the condensation tubes, my soft, blind head expanding to cup the remains. My skin secretes a sharp, vinegary acid that dissolves the feathers and the bone within hours, turning the bird into a rich, dark gruel that I slide back into my belly. I leave nothing to rot, nothing to jam the bilge-pumps. I keep the machine clean.

When the crawler moves, the world is a symphony of pressure. 

I feel the lock-pins disengage from the basalt—a sudden, light-headed buoyancy as the weight shifts. Then comes the slow, rhythmic *thwip-clap, thwip-clap* of the great steam-pistons directly over my head. The vibration runs through my fluid-filled body, matching the thump of my own simple vessel. My skin-cilia vibrate in time with the dynamos; I am part of the circuit, a soft resistor in an iron loop.

From time to time, when the machine stops to drill and the great boilers are damped down to a low simmer, I will crawl out to the edge of the bilge grate. 

I do not have eyes, but my skin can feel the immense, freezing void of the outside world. I feel the thin, wet mist dragging its heavy belly across the iron chassis. I feel the distant, cold *thud-thud-thud* of another crawler ten miles away, its vibrations traveling down through the mountain's roots, up through our own locked legs, and into the soles of my flesh.

It is a lonely, towering world of stone and iron. But inside the Fourth Crawler, in the dark between the boilers, it is warm. The oil is thick; the dead birds are sweet; and the great engine breathes for us both.
