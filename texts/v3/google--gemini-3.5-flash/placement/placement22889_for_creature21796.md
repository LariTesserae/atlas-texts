First, there is the weight. 

Not the weight of a stone falling, but the weight of a stone that has already settled and has no intention of moving again. 

I am in the floor. 

I do not mean I am lying on it. I am in the shallow circular pit at the center of a squat, timber-roofed house. The silt here is not dirt; it is the finest grey loam of the mountain, ground down by centuries of leaking water until it is as smooth as fat. It is warm, heated by the slow, radioactive pulse of the young basalt deep below the foundation. 

My body is half-dissolved into it. Under the silt, my limbs have no sharp corners. My spine is a thick, flexible cord of cured-bark cartilage that curve-bends to fit the contour of the basin; my shoulders have widened into flat, heavy plates that merge with the clay. My breathing slits, positioned high on the flat plane where a human would have a nose, pulse once every three or four of your minutes, drawing in the humid, warm air that smells of wet cedar and iron.

Above me, the roof-leaves are massive, waxy, and stained a deep, translucent amber by the eternal afternoon. Through the open doorway, the light does not shift. It stays. It is a thick, golden fluid that fills the room but casts no glare, only soft, charcoal shadows that stay exactly three inches from the base of the stone pillars.

I have been here for a long time. I know this because the layer of silt over my chest has settled into three distinct bands of grey, deposited by the micro-condensation that drips from the cedar rafters. 

Then, there is the hum.

It does not come through the air. The air here is too heavy, too thick with moisture to carry anything but the *plip-plop* of the roof-drops. The hum comes from below. It travels up through the basalt bedrock, through the beaten clay of the house, and enters my body through the flat of my back.

Someone, three levels down on the great stepped plaza, has dropped their palm into an aqueduct.

The pattern of the vibration is wide, slow, and cool. It is the signature of *Spans-With-No-Sorrow*, an elder whose skin has grown so dark and dense it matches the blue-black of the deep cliffs. The vibration travels through the hollowed-out cedar flumes that thread the town like veins. 

Translated through the mud, the pattern reads: *The basin at the eastern cleft is losing its skin. The water is cold.*

I do not startle. To startle would require muscles that contract like dry leather, and mine are long, damp, and slow. Instead, I begin the transition from the mud. 

It is a beautiful, heavy process. I pull my legs—broad, flat, and jointless—toward my belly. The silt parts with a soft, sucking sigh. As my body rises into the warm air, the moisture of my skin instantly reacts. I secrete a clean, micro-thin glaze from my shoulders and temples, a protective oil that prevents the air from drying my surface. The mud slides off me in thick, elegant ribbons, leaving me clean and wet-shining, the color of a wet slate shingle after a storm.

I stand, though "stand" is too rigid a word. I rest my weight on my wide, flat feet. The soles are ridged like the underside of a mushroom, allowing me to cling to the wet stone without slipping.

My eyes—large, round, pupilless spheres of amber water—adjust to the light. I do not blink. A thin film of moisture rises from the base of my eye-socket, sweeps across the lens to wash away a speck of cedar ash, and drains silently down the seam of my cheek.

I walk out into the standing noon.

The silence of the settlement is a physical presence. It is not the silence of a canyon where nothing lives; it is the silence of an orchestra that has finished tuning and is waiting, instruments poised, for a conductor who is still miles away.

The only sounds are the music of our labor:
*   The deep *churr* of a stone drill, miles up on the high cliff, where a single pebble—spun by a continuous trickle of water—has been wearing a perfect three-inch channel into the basalt for eighty years.
*   The cold, clear *tink* of water falling from one terraced basin to the next.
*   The soft, muffled *hiss* of the rising steam where the dark basalt meets the warm light.

I descend the shallow ramps. My footsteps make no sound; the lime-green moss that carpets the plazas is as thick as sheep’s wool, soaking up the weight of my flat feet. 

As I descend, I pass *The-One-Who-Watches-the-Leaf*. He has been standing at the edge of a three-mile flume for three of our "dry" cycles. His fingers are lightly touching the waxy wood, reading the speed of the water. He does not turn his head as I pass. Our eyes meet for a fraction of a second—two pools of amber liquid reflecting the same unchanging sky—and we acknowledge each other not with a nod, but with a slight, rhythmic swelling of our breathing slits.

At the eastern terrace, four others are already waiting. 

They are crouched around a circular basin of rough, split basalt. The basin is old—its edges are sharper than the ones near the center of the town, still showing the clean, seismic fractures of the mountain's birth. 

The water inside is wrong. 

It does not sit a finger-breadth below the lip in that perfect, tense mirror-stasis. Instead, it is shivering. Small, chaotic ripples are crossing it from left to right, and the water is weeping over the side in a thin, untidy dribble that is washing away the green moss below. The temperature has dropped; to my damp skin, the air near the basin feels like the shadow of a glacier.

We do not speak. We do not need to.

We crouch together, five blue-black shapes rising from the blue-black flagstones, looking like nothing so much as five more decorative boundary markers. 

I reach out my hand. It is broad, the four digits fused by a thick, sensitive web of skin. I submerge my palm into the cold water. It feels like needles of ice, but as my body heat transfers, the water begins to warm.

Across from me, *Spans-With-No-Sorrow* dips his hand. His skin is so old it has the texture of river-worn coal.

We begin.

We do not splash. We move our fingers in tiny, rhythmic, opposing pulses. I pull a wave toward my chest; he pushes a counter-wave to meet it. The other three join, their fingers moving with the precision of a clockwork mechanism. 

We are speaking to the silt at the bottom of the basin. 

We are telling it the shape of the mountain. We are vibrating the water at the exact resonant frequency of the waxy cedar, of the basalt cliffs, of the silent, amber sky. We are singing the song of the *Standing Noon*.

Under our hands, the chaotic ripples begin to organize. They form beautiful, concentric geometric rings—circles within circles, sharp and perfect as the lines on an owl’s feather.

At the bottom of the basin, the dark grey silt begins to stir. 

It does not rise like dust in water; it draws together, thick and deliberate, matching the nodes of our vibration. I feel the pulse of it through the pads of my fingers: the silt is hardening. It is forming the dense, flexible cartilage of a spine. It is skinning over with a blue-black glaze. 

For three shifts of the light that never changes, we stand there, hands in the water, humming the child into being. 

We do not grow tired. Our bodies are designed for this slow, hydraulic patience. My amber eyes remain fixed on the center of the pool, where the water is growing warm again, rising back to its perfect level—exactly one finger-breadth below the stone lip.

When the new one finally breaks the surface, it does not cry. 

It is small, the size of a river stone, its skin a pale, soft slate-grey. It rises slowly, its breathing slits opening for the first time with a soft *sigh* to drink the warm, wet air. Its eyes are already clear, twin lenses of amber water reflecting the five of us who stand around it.

It looks at me. I look at it.

It does not need to be taught. It reaches out its small, webbed hand, dips its fingers into the basin, and sends a single, perfect ripple toward my palm.

*I am here,* the ripple says.

I answer with a soft, slow pulse of my own. 

*The mountain is young,* I tell it. *And the afternoon is long. Rest.*
