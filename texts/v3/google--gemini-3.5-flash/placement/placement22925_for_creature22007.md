The first thing to return is the eye. 

The pale, horizontal slide of my second eyelid moves across the dry eyeball, clearing the slick of warm condensation that gathers there every thirty seconds. To those from the low drylands, I would look like an owl or a seal, but here, the nictitating membrane is merely the wiper of the lens. Through its grease-thin yellow film, the world is a smear of copper-green and basalt-black; when it clicks back into the corner of my orbit, the world sharpens into its true, heavy, glistening reality.

I am sitting on the wet rim of the Eleventh Spillway. I am a **Lead-Scribe of the Crust**, though the apprentices call me the *Clinker* because of the weight of the rings I wear. 

***

### The Body in the Wet

My body is small—shorter by a head than those who live on the western plains, but twice as thick through the ribs. My chest is a bellows designed to draw the thick, soup-like air of the terraces and find the oxygen hidden between the steam-molecules. I have no hair to rot; my scalp is a smooth, pale dome tanned to the texture of boiled lard by the sulfurous drafts of the boiler-flues. 

Where my fingers should end in soft nails, they end in hard, yellow calluses, except for my thumbs. On each thumb, I wear a heavy, thick-walled collar of leaded bronze. They are my tongue. 

Beside me, resting its flat, purple throat against my oiled thigh, is *Clicker*. 

Clicker is a Siphon-Hound of the fifth year, his slate-colored hide smelling strongly of the zinc-paste I rub into his seams to keep the rust-lice from burrowing under his skin-folds. He does not have eyes, but his twin snout-tubes are raised, swaying in the warm draft that rises from the lower pulp-vats. He is listening, as I am, to the mountain.

To my right, the great sixty-foot water-wheel of the Eleventh Spillway turns. *Thrum. Thrum. Thrum.* Three times a minute. It is so heavy, its axle so packed with grease-mat fat, that the sound is not a mechanical rattle but a deep, structural groan that lives in my pelvis. If the axle runs dry, the groan will sharpen into a whistle. If it whistles, I must tap the grease-boys before the iron welds itself to the stone bearings.

But the wheel is healthy today. My concern is lower down, where the wet basalt steps slope toward the great copper intake-valves of the refinery.

***

### The Reading

I slide my hand down the face of the stone. 

The shingle-crusts are packed tight here, their purple-grey shells overlapping like the scales of an enormous, sleeping dragon. They are warm. To the inexperienced hand, they feel like wet velvet, but beneath the velvet is the bone-hard calcimine of the shell. 

I tap my left thumb-ring against the heavy iron of my belt-guard: *Clink.*
Then against my copper chest-plate: *Tack.*

*Who is there?* the pattern says.

I do not expect the shells to answer—they are brainless, rhythmic things—but I am listening to the response of the colony. When my metal hits the iron, the vibration travels through the wet basalt. A yard away, a dozen shingle-crusts snap their valves shut. *Tick-tick-click-tack.* The reaction spreads.

It is like throwing a stone into a pond of dry twigs. The sound ripples outward down the cliff-face—a dry, crackling fire of billions of miniature shutters closing. It moves at the speed of a running man, down the steps of the Eleventh, over the shelf of the Tenth, until it is lost in the roar of the lower sluices.

I listen to the texture of the click. 

If the crust is healthy, the sound is crisp—like dry salt under a heel. But today, fifty feet below near the intake-pipe, there is a "dead patch." The vibration hits that area and goes soft, like a boot stepping into lard. 

"The grease-mats," I tap to myself, my thumbs clicking against my leather apron. *The white ones are crawling where they should not.*

A colony of grease-mats, three tons of rubbery grey flesh, must have slipped from the high benches during the midnight steam-venting. They are moving down into the copper sluices, scraping the shingles bare and leaving their thick, iridescent slime over the intake screens. If they reach the valves, the suction will pull their cold, gelatinous bodies into the seals. The water will stop. The boilers will dry. The city’s iron lungs will seize.

I whistle through my teeth—a wet, low hiss that Clicker understands. 

The hound’s suction-cup feet release from the wet stone with a series of wet *plops*. He turns his blind head toward the dead patch below, his smell-hoses twitching. He tastes the zinc and the cold, fatty mucus of the intruder.

***

### The Descent

We do not walk in Oru; we slide. 

To lift the foot is to invite the grease-mats’ discarded oils to throw you into the three-hundred-foot canyons. Instead, we keep our feet flat, the soles of our heavy leather boots grooved like the underside of a river-couch. We drift down the basalt steps, our knees bent, using the heavy iron hand-guides bolted to the schist.

The air gets hotter as we descend. We are approaching the level of the steam-conduits that carry the waste-heat from the deep smelting-wells. Here, the stone is so warm that the condensation does not weep; it boils off the rock in thin, twisting ribbons of vapor that smell of boiled iron and cabbage.

The blushing pink of the tube-worms is gone here—it is too hot for them—and in their place are the "black-caps," tiny, sulfur-drinking barnacles that rattle like pebbles in a cup when the pressure in the iron pipes behind them rises.

I find the grease-mat where the basalt steps narrow into the copper flume. 

It is a massive specimen. It looks like a forty-foot roll of wet lard, grey-white and glistening with a cold, blue sheen. It has no head, only a wide, muscular under-skirt that rasp-rasps against the stone, licking the mineral salts clean. Behind it, the stone is left so bare and white it looks like bone, stripped of the protective purple shingles.

The mat has already wedged its front third into the primary intake. The iron grate is groaning under the pressure of the suction, the copper rods bending inward like wire.

I do not have a voice to scream for the Silt-Suckers. Instead, I reach for my *tapper*—the five-foot rod of seasoned horn-timber with the heavy copper hammer-head.

I strike the rock three feet from the mat's flank. 

*CLANG-TACK.*

The sound is immense in the narrow canyon, echoing off the wet basalt walls. I do not strike to hurt; I strike to vibrate. The copper-faced hammer sends a shockwave through the water film directly into the mat’s gelatinous mantle.

The grease-mat flinches. Its flesh puckers, turning from grey-white to a bruised, liver-purple. But it is hungry, and the suction of the intake is strong; it does not back out.

I strike again, faster. *Clang-tack-tack-dang.*

Now, Clicker moves. The hound crawls over my shoulder, his wet purple skin cold against my neck. He descends the copper frame of the intake, his four-suction feet locking onto the green-patinated metal with absolute security. He reaches his long, muscular nose-tubes toward the mat’s breathing pores—the small, bubbling holes along its lateral ridge.

With a sudden, wet *sloop*, Clicker seals his nostrils over the mat's respiratory vents. 

He does not bite; he has no teeth. Instead, he begins to draw. The bellows of his ribcage expand—six times, eight times—creating a powerful vacuum. He is sucking the air-gas out of the mat’s internal cells.

The giant slug begins to deflate. Its grey skin sags, looking like a wet sheet of canvas that has lost its wind. It loses its grip on the basalt. Slowly, with the agonizing slowness of Oru, its three-ton weight begins to slide backward out of the copper intake, grease-slime lubrication working against it now as the water-flow begins to push it down the spillway toward the waste-channels.

I do not watch it go. In Oru, a hazard avoided is merely the start of the next cycle.

***

### The Return to the Dry

My shift is twelve hours old. My skin is beginning to feel "spongy"—the first sign of the silt-cough. The skin of my wrists is white and swollen, and when I press my thumb into my forearm, the indentation stays for several seconds, like dough. My joints are cold.

I must go to the dry.

I leave the Eleventh Spillway under the care of my apprentice—a young, bald girl whose name is three quick taps on the belt-buckle. She is fresh from the lower tiers, her nictitating membrane still clear and quick, her leathers still smelling of the new oil. I hand her the horn-tapper.

"Listen to the axle," I click against her copper shoulder-plate. "If the shingle-crusts near the bearing go silent, use the Luffer-fat."

She taps her ring in agreement: *Understood.*

I turn toward the residence tiers. I climb the wet basalt stairs, my legs heavy, the smell of the iron-smoke following me like a shroud.

At the threshold of my home—Tier Nine, Section Four—the Siphon-Hound Clicker leaves me. He crawls into his resting-trough, where the water is tepid and the rust-lice are plentiful. He will spend his twelve hours of rest cleaning his own folds and digesting the zinc-saline he sucked from the grease-mat.

I stand before the door of my Dry-Box. 

It is a heavy slab of basalt, four inches thick, three-times-bolted, with the copper steam-lines running into the hollow frame. Even through the stone, I can feel the heat—a parched, baking warmth that makes my wet skin shiver with anticipation.

I strip off my wet leathers. The wool-silken undergarments, spun by the Dry-Moths, are soaked through, sticking to my ribs like a cold second skin. I peeling them off, leaving myself entirely bare—a small, pale, hairless thing, creased with the deep lines of thirty years in the wet.

I open the inner seal.

The heat hits me like a fist. It is one hundred and ten degrees, and the air is so dry it feels like sand in my throat.

I step inside and bolt the door behind me.

For the first five minutes, I do not move. I lie on the woven wire shelf, my back against the hot, dry basalt blocks. My skin begins to scream. It is the dry-shriek—the rapid evaporation of twelve hours of Oru’s moisture from my pores. My skin tightens, turning from the pale, water-logged grey of a fish to a dry, healthy pink.

My lungs, too, begin to clear. I cough once, twice—heavy, wet rattles—and spit the grey silt-mucus into the drainage trough. With every breath of the parched air, the heavy weight in my chest grows lighter.

I reach for my records. They are written on dry grease-mat skin, preserved here in the heat where they will not rot. With a copper needle, I scratch the entry for today:

*Eleventh Spillway. Intake clear. Mat removed from Tier Ten. Shell-pulse: steady.*

The heat is a mercy. Here, in the dark, windowless warmth of the box, with the great *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the sixty-foot wheel vibrating through the stone pillow beneath my head, I am no longer a creature of the water. I am dry. I am human.

And outside, the mountain clicks its billions of tiny, wet teeth, waiting for me to return.
