I would not be one of the young ones. 

I would not be one of those swift, long-ribbed girls who throw themselves across the gaps, their fingers greased with moss-paste, their lungs like dry leather bags that can hold a breath through three minutes of high-velocity descent. I would not be a hunter of the Gale-Ticks, nor would I be one of the scouts who climb to the sun-blinded crowns of the Spires to look for the migration of the Soot-Moths.

I have lived too many wind-seasons for that. The lime has found its way into my joints, as it does to all of us who survive the great drafts.

You would find me in the **Middle-Flute of the Three-Legged Sister**—a massive, three-pronged spire that rises forty miles north of the Great Split. It is a wet pillar, always weeping, its stone so riddled with vertical channels that when the northern gales rise, it does not whistle; it *groans*. It produces a sound like a wet finger dragged across the rim of a clay jar, but scaled to the size of a mountain. The vibration is so dense that if you lay your bare belly against the floor of my cave, your teeth will click together in your skull.

My body has grown to match this place. I am one of the *Stave-Keepers*.

***

### I. The Shape of the Shell

If you were to see me in a rare moment of stillness—perhaps during the short, hot lulls when the wind dies to a greasy shudder and the mists turn blue—you would think me a piece of the rock that had begun to rot.

My skin is the color of old tallow, stained with the green-black smear of the Vessel-Lichen. I am long-waisted and short-legged, a shape that keeps my center of gravity close to the stone. My feet have no arch; they are flat, broad spades, the soles covered in a gray, rubbery horn that is nearly an inch thick. I can feel the temperature of the limestone through that horn. I can tell if a storm is coming from three valleys away by the way the stone grows cold before the wet draft arrives.

My left knee is larger than my right. It is swollen with "stone-salt," the calcification that comes from sixty years of kneeling on wet ledges to scrape the moss-beds. 

I do not wear clothes, but I am heavy with grease. Twice a day, I reach into the deep crevice behind my sleeping-ledge and pull out a handful of the fat-paste we render from dead Skystriders. I smear it into the hollows of my armpits, between my long toes, and across my chest. Without this, the wind would dry my skin until it split like old bark, and the salt-spray would eat the raw meat beneath.

In my hand is my third arm: a heavy, black basalt wedge, its edge ground smooth by forty years of rubbing against river-silt I found in a deep-water pot three hundred feet below.

***

### II. The Work of the Cradle

My cave is a "dry-scoop," but it is only dry because I have spent half my life making it so. 

It sits thirty feet inside a natural blowhole. I have built three parallel windbreaks across the mouth, using dry limestone blocks that I carried up one by one from the fallen scree of the lower galleries. They are laid without mortar, but they are wedge-shaped, so that the harder the wind blows against them, the tighter they lock into the throat of the cave. Behind them, the roar of the Sister is reduced to a heavy, rhythmic *thwup-thwup-thwup*, like the heart of a running deer.

In this pocket of gray light, I keep the **Cradle of the Vines**.

The Luthier-Vines are the roads of our people. Without them, we are prisoners of our single pillars, confined to the moss-beds of a single tooth until the lime-bamp takes us and we fall into the void. But a vine is a living thing; it grows old, it rots from the wet, or it grows too tight under the strain of the autumn gales.

My task is to nurse the "sprouts."

In the back of my scoop, where the dripping condensation has worn a small bowl in the stone, I keep three young vines. They do not look like plants; they look like coils of black, greasy wire. I feed them on the dung of the Larynx-Grubs, which I gather from the blowholes using a long cleft stick. I must keep them wet, but not drowned. If the water stays too still, they will grow the soft-fungus and go white and dead. I must blow on them with my own breath twice a day to mimic the wind.

When a tribe from the South-Spire taps their signals across the stone—*Three taps, short-pause, two taps: The bridge at the Great Gap is fraying*—I am the one who must prepare the replacement. 

I take the young vine, now twenty paces long and as stiff as a whale-bone, and I carry it out onto the wet ledge.

***

### III. The Crossing

To move through this world when you are old is a dance of three points.

I do not walk. I slide my thighs along the narrow ledges, my belly pressed to the stone, my fingers finding the tiny, grease-slick drill-holes of the Gale-Ticks. Every step is an interrogation of the rock. 

The wind today is a "Yellow Gale"—heavy with warm, sulfuric mist from the depths, blowing from the southwest with enough weight to lift a child off her feet. It smells of wet iron and scorched moss. The water does not fall; it rises. It strikes the underside of my chin in hot, fat beads that taste of lime.

A hundred yards below me, suspended over three miles of blue-and-white void, is the high bridge to the *Sister’s Thumb*.

It is an old vine. It has hung there since my grandmother was a girl. The middle of it has grown thin, worn down by the constant scraping of the Soot-Moths that cling to it during the migrations. It hums a high, whistling green-note that is half an octave too sharp. That is the note of a wire about to snap.

As I crawl toward the anchor point, I see her.

An old **Skystrider** has come to die on the ledge near my vine-anchor. She has been there three days. Her sail-membrane is torn in four places, the pale, oily skin flapping in the gale like the rags of a drowned shelter. Her three hearts are beating out of sync—I can hear them through the stone, three dull, wet thuds that do not line up with the vibration of the spire.

She has no eyes, but as my shadow falls across her flank, her large resonance-flap twitches. She feels my weight through the rock.

She does not move to strike. She has no strength left in her wing-hooks. She is simply waiting for the next great updraft to tear her loose from the moss-mats so she can drift down into the white sea below, where the small crawlers will find her bones.

I stop near her head. Her skin is warm—much warmer than the limestone. I reach out with one long, grease-smeared hand and press my palm against the cartilage of her jaw. 

We do not speak, for there are no words, and the wind would take them anyway. But through my palm, I feel the long, slow rumble of her marrow. She is listening to the Sister's deep drone. She is tuning her last breaths to the minor third of the Three-Legged Sister. 

I take my basalt wedge. I do not kill her—that would be a waste of strength, and her blood would make the ledge too slippery for my work. I simply sit beside her for the space of ten winds, my back against the vibrating rock, my shoulder against her wet, rubbery flank.

We are both of us hanging. We are both of us held by nothing but the skin of our soles.

***

### IV. The Cast

When the tide of the cloud-sea rises to cover the lower ledge, I begin the work.

I find the old anchor-groove—a deep, wind-carved cup in the limestone that my ancestors lined with dried moss to prevent the vine from chafing against the sharp edges. The old vine is held by three wedges of fire-hardened cedar, black with age and slick with slime.

I place my basalt tool against the first wedge. 

*TAP.*

The sound travels through the bone of my hand, up my arm, and into my jaw. I do not hear it with my ears; I feel it as a sharp, white needle of pressure in my teeth.

*TAP. TAP.*

The old vine shivers. It has known this tension for fifty years. It does not want to let go. The high whistle of its string rises, screaming in key with the wind, until with a wet, cracking *slap*, it breaks.

The long tail of the vine whips out into the three-mile void. It does not fall—the updraft is too strong. It rises like a great, black snake, lunging toward the sky, before the cross-winds catch it and fling it eastward, a thin noodle of carbon and fiber disappearing into the boiling white cloud-sea, gone to join the millions of tons of organic drift that rot at the world's wet floor.

For a moment, the *Three-Legged Sister* sounds different. Her voice has lost one of her strings. Her chord is incomplete.

I do not wait to listen to the empty space. I take my young vine, the one I reared in the dark on the dung of grubs, and I lay its head into the moss-lined groove. I drive the new cedar wedges home with three heavy, rhythmic blows of my basalt stone.

*STRIKE. STRIKE. STRIKE.*

I do not need to look across the abyss to the *Sister's Thumb* to know if they are ready. Two miles away, through the shifting gray curtains of mist, I feel the answering thud through the marrow of my shins.

They have caught the tail I threw. They are pulling it tight.

The new vine begins to sing. It is a low, wet, green note—raw and stubborn, but true. It will take ten wind-seasons for the mist to age it, for the moss to find its back, and for the soot-moths to wear it smooth. But it will hold.

I lie back against the weeping stone of the ledge, my hands locked in the moss, my swollen knee resting against the flank of the dying Skystrider.

Below us, the white ocean rolls and hisses, never finding a shore. Above us, the teeth of the world scrape the blue sky, whistling their endless, lonely song. 

And here, in the middle of the throat, I close my eyes and let the scream of the stone go through me, until I cannot tell where the bone ends and the lime begins.
