The iron does not lie.

It is the first thing you learn, and the last thing you have left when the steam-blindness takes your outer eyes. People from the upper cocoons—those Tappers who still have the long, thin legs for leaping from flange to flange—they think the world is made of paths. They think a ladder is a road. 

I know better. The world is not a road. The world is a weight, and it is trying to find its way down.

***

### I. The Nest in the Gudgeon

If you were to drop a copper plumb-bob from the high-altitude grid where the purple *Sails-of-Cloud* drag their wet roots, and let it fall three miles through the cross-braces, past the green hanging forests of waxy ivy, through the steam-plumes that smell of boiled turnip, it would eventually strike a shoulder of dark, grey-painted iron that has not seen the white sun since the three-thousandth tooth of the Great Dial sheared off.

This is the **Seventieth Gudgeon**. 

Here, two plates of rolled steel, each forty inches thick and held together by five-pound rivets, have begun to "groan." That is what the Tappers call it when the metal grows tired of its alignment. It doesn't break—nothing breaks here—but it *yawns*. A gap of three inches has opened between the plates, and through that gap, the hot, black grease of the internal piston-shafts spits twice every hour, like an old dog coughing up phlegm.

I live in the yawn.

I am what the Tappers click about when they tap their rings against the dry pipes in the dark: *The Plug*. Or *The Seam-Sitter*. Or *The One Who Eats the Tallow*.

I do not have a name that can be spoken with a tongue. If you wanted to call me, you would take a short length of lead pipe and strike the vertical web-frame twice—a heavy, flat *thud-thud*—and wait for the iron to sing back.

***

### II. My Body is a Wedge

I was small once. I remember the green bags. I remember the taste of the swift-eggs we took from the nests beneath the high walkways, and how the air up there tasted of cold tin and ozone. I had the long fingers too, and the opposable toes that could grip a cold-rolled bolt-head as if it were a branch.

But I was too broad in the chest for the long runs, and I had the "iron-ear." I could hear the cracks before they reached the surface.

When the Seventieth began to yawn forty seasons ago, the clan did not want the water-pipes above to slip. If those pipes slipped, the cistern-hollows would dry up; the duckweed would die, the pale crayfish would turn to gravel, and the green cocoons would starve.

So they brought me down to the gudgeon. They gave me three bags of lead shims, a bucket of mineral fat, and a heavy brass mallet. 

I did not go back up.

Over the years, the gap has shaped me, as the slow-turning gear shapes the ivy that climbs its hub. My legs are no longer used for climbing; they are tucked beneath me, thin and dry as willow-sticks, white from forty years of steam-wash. They have fused into the recess where the drainage-flume meets the lower flange. I do not need them. 

My upper body, though—my chest has grown wide as a boiler-shield. My skin is the color of a wet stove-pipe, stained so deeply with hot tallow and graphite that the sweat runs off me in grey beads, like water on grease-paper. I have no hair; the steam took it when the safety-valve on the sixty-eighth boiler blew thirty summers ago, and now my scalp is a tough, pink callus that doesn't feel the heat of the pipes when I lean my head against them to listen.

My left hand is normal, though the skin of the palm is four times thicker than a climber's. But my right hand—my right is the *Mallet-Hand*. I have wrapped it in layers of oil-soaked hide so many times, and struck so many lead wedges into this seam, that the wrist is as thick as a pump-piston and the fingers do not close anymore. They are a single, flat spade of bone and horn, perfect for scraping the green scale-rust out of the seating-grooves before the grease-spit comes.

***

### III. The Work of the Seam

The world does not stop. 

Every twelve minutes, the grandfather-wheel three hundred feet above me drops its counterweight. *Clank-shudder.* 

When the vibration hits the Seventieth Gudgeon, the yawn tries to close. If there is nothing in the seam, the two plates will slam together with a sound that would split a climber's skull like an egg. The iron would crystallize; the rivets would pop out like grape-stones, and the whole shelf—with its five thousand cocoons and its gardens of orange orchids—would slide down into the boiling shadow of the *Bottombox*.

So, when the vibration starts—when the iron under my thighs begin to "hum" with that high, dry whistle that means *the weight is coming*—I reach into my leather bucket.

I find a lead shim. They are soft, these shims. I cast them myself in the winter-turns, using the lead from old condenser-tubes we drag down from the dead zones.

I place the shim in the yawn. 

I lean my forehead against the upper plate. My ears are useless now—the hiss of the steam-vents has made them like empty shells—but my teeth are perfect. I bite down on a brass bolt that is threaded into the main rib. Through my jawbone, I "see" the weight coming.

*Five miles of iron... three miles of bronze... the weight of the water-forest... the weight of the birds... the weight of the sky.*

It comes as a red-hot pain in my teeth.

*Tap.* 

I strike the lead shim with my mallet-hand.

*Tap.*

The lead flattens. It flows like warm wax into the microscopic pits of the rust-scab. It fills the space. The iron plates meet, but they do not scream; they settle into the soft grey lead with a low, wet *thuff*, like an old man sitting down in a moss-chair.

The weight is passed. The grandfather-wheel rests.

Until the next twelve minutes.

***

### IV. The Neighbors

I am not alone in the gudgeon, though I do not see my own kind more than once a turn, when the Tappers lower more lead and salt-pork on a wire-line.

The **Tallow-Worms** are my friends. They are three feet long, white and clear as mutton-fat, and they have no legs. They slide out of the lubrication-chassis when the piston spits. They do not mind me. When I am cold, I drape two or three of them across my shoulders; their bodies are always ninety degrees hot from the friction of the gears, and they smell of old sheep-grease. We share the cistern-water that drips from the copper drain-pipe above us.

Sometimes, the **Silt-Cats** come down. They are green and quiet, like a clump of moss that has learned to walk on eight sponge-toes. They do not have eyes, but they have those long, wet whiskers that feel the draft.

One of them has lived on the girder behind my left shoulder for seven turns of the Great Dial. It does not try to eat me; I am too large, and I smell too much of the machine. It sits there, looking like an overgrown pipe-saddle, its green fur vibrating in time with the boiler-pulse. Sometimes, when a slate-swift gets confused by the steam and flies down into the dark of the gudgeon, the Silt-Cat will drop from the girder like a wet rag. There is a dry *click-flutter*, the sound of feathers breaking, and then the cat will climb back up to its seat, its green chin dark with bird-blood.

It leaves the feathers for me. I use them to stuff the hollow under my hip where the iron-plate is cold.

***

### V. The High Grid and the Long Deep

Sometimes, when the steam clears for an hour and the hot sun-stripes line the bulkheads like clean yellow paint, I look up.

Between the massive cross-braces, I can see the high world. It looks like a lace shawl made of gold and emerald. I can see the little green pods of the Tappers’ cocoons swaying in the rising hot air, and I can hear—very faintly, through the bone of my jaw—the clicking of their wire-talk.

*The water is sweet today,* they tap.
*The swift-flock has turned south.*
*The Great Dial has turned two teeth.*

They think they are the ones who live here. They think their climbing and their singing and their honey-harvesting is the life of the world.

They do not know about the Seventieth Gudgeon. They do not know that three miles below their hammocks, an old man with no legs and a mallet for a hand is holding their sky on his teeth.

And down below me?

If I look down through the drainage-slots, where the black water and the sheared rivets fall, there is only the *Bottombox*. It is not empty. It is a thick, yellow fog that smells of sulfur and old boots. You can hear things down there—not the clean *clank-shudder* of the gears, but a wet, bubbling sound, like a pot of porridge that has been left on the fire too long. 

The *Cinder-Gills* are down there. Sometimes, when my light-worm dies and the gudgeon is perfectly dark, I can see their brass lanterns moving through the slurry miles below, like green fireflies in a coal-mine. They are looking for my dropped shims. They are looking for the things that slip.

One day, my mallet-hand will grow soft. The bone will turn to chalk from the damp, or my teeth will rot out and I will no longer be able to hear the weight coming through my jaw.

When that happens, I will not climb up. I couldn't if I wanted to.

I will simply slide through the yawn. I will go down past the sixty-ninth gudgeon, past the sixty-eighth, down into the yellow fog where the Cinder-Gills wait with their green lanterns and their spade-shaped hands. They will take my leather skin and make boots of it; they will take my brass mallet-hand and melt it down into new lard-pans.

And that is as it should be. The iron does not waste anything.

But today, the sun-stripe is wide and warm on my back. The Silt-Cat is purring against my shoulder—a low, rhythmic vibration that matches the ninety-cycle hum of the main turbine.

The grandfather-wheel is turning. I can feel the weight gathering on the sixty-ninth rib. It is heavy today; there must be water in the upper expansion-chambers.

I reach into the bucket. I find a fresh, cold lead shim. I scrape the green moss from the seat with my right hand.

I bite the bolt.

*Come on then,* I think. *Let’s see how much you weigh.*
