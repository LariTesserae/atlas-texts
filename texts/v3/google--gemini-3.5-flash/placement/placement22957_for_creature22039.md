I would be the one who sits at the seam of the three-second gap.

You would find me neither on the floor-raft of green timber nor clinging to the copper dome, but wedged directly between them, my heavy, lard-pale flanks squeezed tight into the frame of the eastern bladder-port.

I am longer than the others. My cartilage ribs did not set in the round, sack-like shape of my nest-mates; they grew long and flat, like the split willow poles we use to rib the walls. I am five feet of pale tripe-skin and whistling air-sacs, wedged into the circular collar of the window like a living cork. My skin smells of boiled vinegar and wet hair. I do not have a name—we do not trade sounds that do not describe a leak—but if the others had a vibration for me, it would be the wet, sliding click of a wooden bolt falling into a wet groove.

My left half exists in the sweltering, pressurized orange dusk of the chamber. My skin here is hot, greasy with mutton-tallow, and constantly slicked by the reverse rain of the upward-dripping floor-crevices. 

My right half—my right arm, my right ear-fold, the right side of my toothless, vertical mouth-gasp—is pushed through the pig-bladder membrane, out into the absolute, screaming nothingness of the vertical void. 

It is there, outside, that the world is blue-black and silent. The cold out there does not merely freeze; it bites like a iron clamp. The steam that whistles out around my body from the edges of the port instantly turns into a crust of grey, hollow frost that clings to my sparse spine-hairs. One half of my brain-sac is warm and heavy with the smell of boiled cabbage; the other half is numb, cold as a river-stone, listening to the vast, hollow hiss of the high altitude where there is no air to carry a roar.

My work is the Thread.

Between my pair of left thumbs, I hold the tail of a fresh, bleeding vine—thick as an ankle, weeping its bitter, milky sap into the hot clay of the floor. My left thumbs are stained a deep, permanent red from the iron-silt; they are raw and blistered from the heat of the birch steam-pipes. With a slow, fluid lurch of my cartilage spine, I feed this vine outward, into the freezing fog of the gap.

As the vine leaves the warmth of the room, the cold hits it. The white sap freezes instantly into a hard, glossy varnish, stiffening the wet wood until it is as rigid as an iron bar. 

I do not look at the vine as it goes. I look straight ahead, through the translucent, greasy membrane of my port, across the twenty-foot gap of swirling mountain-steam.

I watch the western wall of the opposite structure—which is our own ceiling, turned sideways.

Through the fog, I see a figure. It is me. 

It is me, but it is three seconds older than I am. Its skin is slightly bluer from the cold; its right arm is already outstretched into the grey air, its calloused, parsnip-shaped thumbs waiting. 

I watch my future self reach out. I feel the phantom sensation in my own right hand—the cold, numb fingers waiting for the wood—before the wood has even traveled halfway across the gap. 

The vine cross-cuts the space between us. It travels through the thin, dead air where gravity does not know which way to pull, turning slowly, shedding tiny, frozen needles of sap that fall upward and downward at the same time, disappearing into the mist.

At exactly three seconds, my right hand—on the other side of the loop—closes over the frozen tip of the vine. 

The moment of contact is a shock of vibration that travels backward through the wood, through the gap, and into my left shoulder. It is a loop of nerves. I am pulling the vine that I am pushing. I can feel the drag of my own strength against my own strength. If I pull too hard with my right hand, my left shoulder is jerked forward against the copper frame; if I slacken my left-side grip, my right arm plunges into the icy void, dragged down by the weight of the timber.

We must be perfectly balanced, my future and I. We must agree on the tension of the world.

With my right hand, I draw the frozen vine through the western port of that sideways room. I bend it—feel the wood groan and crack as the ice on its bark splinters—and I weave it back into the ceiling-seam of the structure. I pack the gap with a double-handful of red clay that I have kept warm in my belly-folds, smoothing it down with the flat of my thumb until the deep, double-handed groove matches the groove I made three seconds ago on the other side.

The voice of the Loom is always in my ribs.

I feel the valve-lice squealing on the pipes below me. I feel the deep, grinding *thump* of the river-silt as it pumps through the birch conduits. Sometimes, when the pressure in the room rises too high, my own air-sacs swell until my skin is stretched thin as a wet bladder, and I must let out a long, whistling hiss through the cold corner of my mouth-slit, releasing our breath into the blue-black emptiness.

I do not remember the birth-sac from which I crawled. I do not know if I was made from the ceiling-mud or the floor-mud. 

But sometimes, during the long hours when the steam is quiet and the water-globule hangs perfectly still in the center of the room, I look through the bladder-port and do not move. 

Across the gap, three seconds away, my other self does not move either.

We stare at each other with our yellow, boiled-egg eyes. We cannot speak; our mouth-slits are full of the cold salt of the ice and the hot yeast of the steam. But we know. We know that if either of us lets go of the vine—if I fail to push, or if he fails to catch—the loop will snap. The space between our rooms will widen. The five-meter box will untwist itself, the green logs will scatter like kindling into the freezing void, and the hot, wet lung we live in will finally gasp its last, whistling breath into the dark.

So I tighten my thumbs. I feel the sap bleed cold against my palm. 

I push, and three seconds later, I catch.
