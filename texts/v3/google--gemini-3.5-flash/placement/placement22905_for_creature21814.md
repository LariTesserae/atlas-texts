I am very low. I am nearly all belly. 

The concrete beneath me is not cold—it is never cold—but it has the hard, wet municipal permanence of a cistern floor. It has been ground smooth by three thousand years of silt-bearing water, but if I press my throat against the seam where two slabs of basalt-concrete meet, I can still feel the faint, rusted teeth of an iron reinforcing bar deep within the seam. It vibrates. Everything vibro-transmits through the stone.

I am a Silt-Sleeper, though I do not think of myself by that name. I am simply the weight that fits this particular depression in the aqueduct bend, beneath the shadow of the great sixty-foot flywheel. 

My eyes are nothing more than two smudges of dark pigment buried beneath three layers of wet, translucent skin. They see no shapes, only the difference between the grey-amber twilight of the basin and the sudden, passing shadow of a Vapor-Flitter as it drafts low over the canal. 

But I do not need to see. The world is made of pressure and taste.

***

In my gills, which grow like two fans of wet, scarlet silk from the sides of my neck, the world is sour with iron oxides and sweet with the yeast-smell of the bacterial mats. The current is a thick, living grease. I hold myself still, my front limbs—short, pale, four-toed things like the hands of an infant—anchored in a small pocket of grey sediment. 

Every few minutes, the sixty-foot wheel turns its slow degree. *Clack-shhh.* 

The vibration hits my ribs first. It traveling through my liver, through the soft, water-filled jelly of my spine, and out through the tip of my tail. The water in the canal swells. It rises three inches, warm and frothing with tiny bubbles of sulfurous gas that tickle my flanks. 

With the swell comes the drift.

I open my mouth. It is a wide, pale slit, almost as wide as my head, lined with tiny, velvet-soft ridges of bone. I do not chase. Chasing is for the things with legs that walk on the surface of the dry concrete. I simply let the warm current fill me. 

First comes the tickle of the blind crayfish. They are small, pale as peeled garlic, and they scuttle along the edge of my tail, their tiny legs clicking like spilled seed: *click-tick-fric*. Some of them crawl across my back, grazing on the thin film of ochre slime that settles on my skin between currents. I let them. They are clean. Their tiny pinchers are like cold needles, picking away the dead tissue from the seams behind my neck. 

Then, one—stumbling, blind, smelling of wet lime—drifts too close to my snout. 

My mouth does not snap. It is a sudden, sub-aquatic vacuum. A hitch of my throat, a wet *thwip*, and the crayfish is inside. It is small; it does not struggle long in the dark of my stomach. It is only a cold lump that slowly turns warm, matching my own heat.

***

The cycle is long. Often, the automatic sluices far upstream groan shut, and the water down here begins to die. 

This is the quietest time. The current slows to a thin, tepid trickle, then stops entirely. The surface of my pool becomes glassy, reflecting the amber fog above. 

Without the water to support my bulk, I settle. My three feet of pink-white body presses flat into the grey silt. My gills, no longer floated by the stream, drape over my shoulders like wet, red rags. My skin begins to dry, but it does not crack; the air is so heavy with steam that I am always wet, only differently so. I breathe through my skin now—slow, shallow rise and fall, drawing the humid, metallic air directly into my blood.

In this dry hour, the Striders come. 

They are massive, wire-legged things, two feet high but light as feathers. I feel the microscopic dimples they make in the surface tension of the remaining puddles as they skate toward me. One of them steps onto my head. Its foot-pads are hairy and oil-slicked; they feel like the footprints of a moth. It stands there, suspended between my wet skin and the damp air, waiting for the midges to die in the heat.

Above us, on the vertical wall of the great concrete block that houses the steam piston, the Shinglers are working. 

*Scrriiiitch. Scrriiiitch.*

It is the sound of a million iron tongues rasping the lime from the basalt. It is a dry, rhythmic scraping that never stops. If I listen through the stone, I can hear them digesting—the tiny, chemical fizzing of acid in their stomachs as they dissolve the concrete to make their flat, tiled shells. They are eating the city. Very slowly, over years I cannot count, they are turning the hard, square corners of the human buildings into the round, grey hips of hills.

I like the sound. It means the stone is still there. It means the walls are still standing.

***

A miles-deep shudder passes through the bedrock.

*Thump...*

The piston. The heart.

*Thump...*

The water in the floor grates begins to hiss. The steam comes first, a white plume that rises from the iron slats and turns the amber fog to blinding grey. The Striders scatter, their wire legs twitching as they glide away over the dry slate. 

I feel the cold water coming before it arrives. It is a wave of fresh, mineral-rich runoff from the distant, northern plazas, pushed through the underground culverts by some ancient, automatic weight. It smells of wet iron and pristine, deep-earth salt. 

My red gills flutter. They rise from my neck, blooming like anemones in the dark. 

The water reaches my pool—not a torrent, but a fat, warm swell that lifts me from the silt. I am weightless again. I drift three inches to the left, my tail undulating once, twice, a slow, lazy S-curve that leaves a trail of white mud suspended in the current like smoke.

I am old. My skin is scarred with the gray-blue stains of iron sulfide where I have rubbed against the grates, and my tail is notched from some encounter with a falling iron shutter centuries ago. I do not know what the builders were. I do not know what the giant, slow-turning wheels are trying to manufacture, or where the warm water goes when it sinks into the grates below.

I only know that the water is warm. 

I know that when the gates open, the food falls.

I twist my head toward the dark mouth of the culvert, where the fresh current bubbles in. I let my body settle into a new depression in the concrete. The silt rises around my flanks like a blanket of wet wool. 

*Clack-shhh.*

The wheel turns. The water rises. I close my mouth, open my gills, and wait.
