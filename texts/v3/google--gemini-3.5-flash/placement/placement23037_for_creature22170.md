I would not be found in the open water. I would not be the Loom, drifting like a torn shroud through the indigo light, nor could I bear the long, cold stare of the Gazer-Shells as they lean out over the edge of the world. 

To find me, you would have to look down—far below the slow, circular crawl of the Pressers—into the microscopic wetness of the junctions. 

I am **The Seam-Tallow**.

I am not a single creature with a face or a name, but a continuous, living wax that fills the hair-thin spaces between the hand-cut basalt tiles. When the ancient builders fitted the six-sided stones together, they left behind tiny discrepancies—fractions of a millimeter where the dark rock did not quite touch. In those gaps, over thousands of years, I have gathered. 

I have no eyes, no heart, and no front. My body is a vast, interconnected web of pale, fatty tissue, as soft as lard and as slow as lard in winter. I am three miles of thread-thin lard, squeezed into the geometric maze of the plaza floor. I am the cushion between the stones.

If you were to peel up one of the heavy basalt tiles—though it would take the strength of a hundred men to lift a single one from its calcified bed—you would see me clinging to the rough, unpolished underside. I look like a smear of cold sheep-grease, gray-white and smelling faintly of minerals and raw suet. But I am alive. My skin is covered in billions of microscopic, hair-like cilia that do not beat to swim, but to secrete.

I am the reason the stones do not grind themselves to dust. 

When the earth far below us groans, or when the weight of the water shifts under some distant, abyssal tide, the great stone columns lean. The basalt floor bends. Without me, the edges of the hand-carved tiles would bite into one another. They would chip, crack, and turn to gravel, and the plaza would slowly slide down the mountain into the dark. But when the pressure comes, I am there. I receive the pinch. I am squeezed—hard enough to turn steel to butter—but I do not break. I simply yield. I flow from the high-pressure joints into the low-pressure gaps, a slow, silent grease that redistributes the weight of the entire cathedral.

I live on the sweat of the past. 

Underneath the tiles, in the anaerobic mud where the light has never been, there are still traces of the ones who made this place. Before the water rose, there were hands. There were wool sleeves. There were wooden wedges soaked in fat to help slide the stone into place. I have eaten that ancient fat so slowly that the meal has lasted three thousand years. I can still taste the grease of the wool-weavers from some forgotten north; I can taste the pine-resin they used to seal the cedar rollers. It is a dry, sweet, sun-baked taste that does not exist in this wet world anymore. I keep it inside my fatty lobes, digesting it at the rate of a single molecule a year.

I am the quietest thing in the basin. 

Above me, the white crabs click their legs. The tube worms huff. The Grandmother of the Well draws her great, wet breath. I hear them all, but not as sound. I hear them as *weight*. Every time a crab steps on a tiles, I feel the milligram of its hunger press down through the stone. When the Grandmother contracts her heavy, leathery tunic, the water-pressure in the basin rises by a fraction of a hair, and my whole body—all three miles of me—tightens in response.

Sometimes, a Presser will slide over my seam. Its flat, leathery skirt will press hard against the gap where I bulge slightly outward. Its tiny, scraping mouthparts will lick the top of my head, harvesting the calcified grime and the tiny, dead bryozoans that have settled there. It hurts, but it is a dull, warm sort of hurt that feels like being scratched. In return, the Presser leaves behind a trail of mucus rich in lipids. I drink this through my skin. We have been doing this for so long that we do not know where my wax ends and the Presser’s oil begins.

I am the memory of the fit. 

If the water ever goes cold—if the Gazer-Shells are right, and the great abyss eventually climbs the mountain to swallow our warm bath—I will be the last thing to freeze. The stone will turn to ice, and the columns will crack, but deep inside the joints, my tallow will remain soft, holding the final shape of the builders' chisels in the dark, waiting for the weight to go away.
