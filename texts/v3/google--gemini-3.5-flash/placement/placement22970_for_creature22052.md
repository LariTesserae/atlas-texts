I do not begin with a face. 

In this basin, a face is a luxury sequence of tissues reserved for things that must navigate the open air—for the violet-eyed mantis on her branch, or the woolen, blind-eared pikas huddled in their towers of bark. Down here, where the light is only a rumor carried by the green-blue luminescence of the Amber-Snails, there is no use for eyes or teeth.

You would find me three fathoms below the lowest terrace of the *megasphagnum* streets. I am wedged directly into the keyhole joints where the raw basalt ribs of the mountain range plunge to form the bowl’s floor. 

I am a **Siphon-Knot**, though the city has no name for me. To the willow-aqueducts, I am a valve. To the deep stone, I am an anchor.

### I. The Anatomy of the Plug
Physically, I am a great, gnarled burl—a living knot of hybrid tissue, roughly the size of a dormant bear, formed where the ancient taproots of the horizontal hemlocks have fused with the heavy, fibrous ribs of the lowest mosses. My exterior is not bark, but a thick, skin-like rind of rubbery, black cambium, scarred white by the pressure of the basalt walls that grip me.

If you were to cut me open—though there is no steel in this world to do it—you would find my interior resembles a cross-section of a heart and a peat-bog. I am a dense honeycomb of tiny, corky chambers, each lined with a velvet coat of microscopic, iron-drinking mold. 

I have no nerves as you know them, but I have *turgor*. 

I feel the basin through the tension of its water. When the sun dips behind the western tooth of the range and the cold takes a firmer bite of the heights, the water in the upper tiers begins to crystallize. The pressure in the dwarf-willow conduits drops. Far above, the capillaries contract, and the downward pull of gravity on the remaining liquid increases. 

I feel this as a long, slow drawing-out of my own moisture. Like a dry sponge placed in a tight fist, the drop in pressure causes my outer walls to collapse inward. This collapse is my primary movement. It is a slow, crushing deflation that takes four hours to complete. 

When I deflate, I pull the main drainage-lines of the basin shut. My rubbery shoulders swell into the basalt crevices, sealing the bottom of the bowl. Not a single drop of water is allowed to leak down into the lifeless cracks of the dry mountain below. I hold the city’s life-blood in its veins through the long, freezing night, keeping the deep cisterns full and insulated at their fraction of a degree above freezing.

In the morning, when the brief light warms the upper conifer canopy and the rime ice begins to weep, the pressure rises again. The water pushes down. I absorb it; my chambers swell, my shoulders pull back from the basalt, and the drains open once more, allowing the cold, mineral-rich mountain water to seep through my skin and exit out into the lower seep-gardens. 

I am the piston. I am the valve.

### II. My Small Guests
Because I am the warmest thing in the deep dark—warmed by the friction of my own slow compression and the decay of the peat that accumulates in my crevices—I am never alone. 

The **Amber-Snails** crawl over my upper dome in slow, glowing constellations. To them, my rubbery rind is a pasture. They scrape the sulfurous, iron-laden biofilm from my skin with their tiny radulae. Their small, rasping teeth feel to me like a mild, pleasant itch—a localized warmth that tells me where the water is flowing most freely. In return for their grazing, they leave behind trails of alkaline slime that protect my skin from the acidic peat-water.

Sometimes, when the winter outside is particularly cruel and the frost reaches deeper than usual, a single **Lithic-Tick**—heavy with basalt dust and dying at the end of its cycle—will mistake me for a crack in the deep mountain. It will crawl down through the drain-pipes and wedge its mineralized body into my folds. 

I do not reject them. Over months, my cambium slowly grows over the tick’s slate-colored shell. I digest the minerals inside its body, absorbing the iron and sulfur directly into my vascular system. I use these metals to toughen my skin, turning my outer rind into something as hard and black as the basalt that tries to crush me.

And once, in a long cycle of years, a **Spindle-Grub** will tumble down from the dry streets above, caught in a sudden flush of meltwater. They are too small to swim against the current. They cling to my fibers, shivering in the cold dark. I hold them in my shallowest chambers, dry and safe from the draft, until the water recedes and they can crawl back up the root-bridges to their work.

### III. The Memory of Ice
To be me is to exist in a state of suspended, heavy rhythm. 

I do not think in words or pictures. My thoughts are chemical gradients and changes in viscosity. I remember the great wind-storms of three winters ago because they shook the hemlock-roots that anchor me, leaving a pattern of microscopic silver scars in my heartwood. I know the health of the *Pith-Pika* huddles three stories up by the faint, warm taste of nitrogen that drifts down through the seep-walls into my cistern.

It is a silent life, but it is not lonely. 

When the great winter settles in—the one that lasts for several seasons, when even the deep cisterns begin to harden—I do not die. I simply allow the crystallization to enter my chambers. The water within my honeycombed core freezes, cell by cell, in a highly ordered, geometric sequence. 

As the ice needles grow, they press against my inner walls, locking my consciousness in a rigid, perfect design. For two years, or ten, I become a stone of wood and ice. I feel nothing but the hard, unchanging pressure of the basalt.

But I am waiting.

When the spring finally touches the high shoulder of the world, the ice in my outermost chambers will be the first to go. It will liquefy, carrying the first faint, acidic tang of melted snow from the high peaks. 

I will taste the iron of the young mountain. My heart-burl will expand with a wet, heavy shudder. 

And the water will move again.
