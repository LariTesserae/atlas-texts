The first thing you must understand is that the floor does not belong to the tree. 

Sixty yards above, the ceiling is alive; it sweats, it groans, it drops its greasy tears into the void. The three Stems are alive; they thrum with high-pressure sap, their bark shifting under the steam-harnesses like the hide of a chained bull. But down here, under the soles of my feet, the heartwood is dead. It is stone-wood. It is a cold, dark mirror that has finished its growing a million years ago, polished by the sliding bellies of ten billion shield-beetles until it looks like frozen oil.

Up on the scaffolds, they live on the vertical. They have the long, prehensile toes for gripping the hemp ropes, yes, but their minds are always looking up or down, measuring their lives by how many yards of brass pipe separate them from the boiling-vat or the drop.

I do not look up. If I did, my neck would set like old tallow.

My feet are different from the Mid-Stemmers’ feet. They are wide, flat spade-palms, the skin on the soles so thick and cured by the heartwood-slick that I can walk through a drift of bronze beetles without feeling their legs. To me, the swarm is not a carpet; it is a fluid. It is like wading through a river of cold, dry lentils. They click and slide against my shins, their bronze shields leaving grey grease-streaks on my hairless legs, but they do not bite. They know the smell of my tallow. I smell like the bottom of things. I smell of old starch, cold grease, and the vinegar-sharp rot of the floor-seep.

I do not wear the latex suits of the Coopers. They are too loud. They click and groan like wet leather, and when you are down here in the dark, you must hear what is coming before it touches you. I wear only a breech-clout of smoked hide, held with a strip of copper wire, and a thick smear of grey boiler-grease three layers deep over my shoulders. The grease is my skin. It keeps the midge-clouds from my neck and stops the acid-drip of the ceiling from pitting my collarbones.

I am a **Floor-Stalker**. The Low-Sinks call me *The Pry*, because of the iron bar I carry, but to myself, I am simply what remains when you let go of the ropes.

***

My world is five miles wide, though nobody has ever reached the wall-bark to prove it. It consists of the three massive columns—Stem One, Stem Two, the Bleeder—and the long, dark hollows between them. 

The vibration is different down here. If you press your forehead against the heartwood, you do not hear the high, screaming whistle of the fractionators. That is a Mid-Stem sound. Down here, the earth hums in the bone of your jaw. It is a slow, heavy *thrum-thrum-thrum* that sounds like a massive iron heart drowning in lard. It is the root-system drawing the deep water from names we do not have.

I have my work. The Low-Sinks in their wicker baskets are too light, too flighty; they cannot stay on the floor for more than an hour before the beetle-tide makes them dizzy. So they drop their baskets from thirty yards, and they call to me in the Clack.

*Tock. Tock-tock.* 
"Tool-drop," they click, striking their brass thimbles against the guide-chains. "Copper elbow. Three-inch. North of Stem Two."

I do not clack back with fingers. I use my pry-bar. It is five feet of heavy, cold-rolled iron with a chisel-tip, salvaged from an old steam-pump before my grandfather’s graft was green. I strike the heartwood. 

*Clong.*
"I have it," the floor tells them.

I find the copper elbow half-buried under a three-foot ridge of live beetles. I slide my spade-toes under the metal, hoist it with my instep, and catch it with one hand. It is warm, coated in the sweet,sticky yellow latex that leaks from the gaskets above. I carry it back to the guide-chain, hook it on, and give the wire three sharp tugs. The basket rises into the yeast-mist, disappearing toward the yellow flare of the oil-lamps like a spider retreating into a drafty chimney.

But my true work is not the iron. It is the **Spit**.

***

Every few cycles, the Stems reject a Graft.

The families do not like to talk about it. They carve the Fold in the bark, they lay their dead uncle inside with the yeast-paste, and they smear the latex-seal over the seam. They expect the tree to drink him. Most of the time, it does. The bark rolls over, the grain swells, and he becomes steam-pressure and sap-pumps.

But sometimes, the sap in that particular vein is too sour. The yeast goes black and vinegary. The Fold does not heal; it becomes a wet, frothy boil. The latex-seal blisters, turns the color of a dead bladder, and splits. 

Then comes the Spit.

It happens in the dark, usually between the Breaths. There is no steam-whistle to warn you. Just a wet, heavy *thud* that shakes the heartwood—a sound like a sack of damp grain falling from the clouds. 

When I hear that sound, I go. 

I find them in the amber-channels of the floor—the shallow, ancient depressions where the fossilized wood has slumped. The beetles are already there, a boiling mound of bronze shells, trying to get to the yeast-paste. I use my pry-bar to clear them, swinging it in wide, low sweeps that sound like gravel being shoveled in a cellar. 

The things the tree spits back are not meat, and they are not timber. They are something between. 

Last cycle, I found a girl from Stem Three. The wood had taken her legs and her left ribs before it sickened; they were gone, replaced by thick, fibrous cords of yellow heartwood that had began to take the grain of the column. Her face was still wax-white, her eyes sealed with cold resin, but her skull had grown three long, hollow flutes like the wood of a steam-pipe. 

I did not carry her back to the baskets. The Low-Sinks would have screamed and dropped her. Once the tree has touched you like that, you do not go back to the shanties. You do not belong to the Clack.

I picked her up. She was heavy—half-timber now, her wet wood-legs dragging behind her like the roots of a pulled weed. I carried her out into the Dark Mile, three miles east of Stem Two, where the light of the fractionators is nothing but a smear of yellow grease on the air.

Here, where the floor curves up to meet the outer wall of Node 12, I have my **Orchard**.

It is not an orchard of fruit. It is an orchard of standers.

I have set forty of them upright in the deep crevices where the fossilized heartwood has cracked from the weight of the mountain above. I pack them in with the black-grease from the bottom of the vats and the ground-up shells of the tarantula-wasps. 

They do not rot down here. The air is too oily, too thick with sulfur and yeast-spores for regular decay. Instead, they dry. The resin in their bones hardens; the yellow wood-fibers in their chests turn the color of old horn. 

I have built them in a circle around my shelter—which is nothing but an old copper fractionator-saucer, turned upside down and sealed with river-mud. 

When the **Hush-Hour** comes, I do not go into a cedar box with five other sweating Sappers. I do not have a box. 

I lie down under my copper saucer, flat on my belly, with my forehead pressed against the cold heartwood. 

Outside, the whistles scream. The steam-valves roar—a sound that would drill through an ordinary man’s ears, but my ears have been stuffed with yellow beeswax and grease-felt since I was seven. To me, the scream of the vents is only a pressure in my sinuses, a hot, sweet hand pressing down on my temples.

Then comes the mist. The lower twenty feet of the world fills with the white, boiling sugar-fog. It smells of hot rum and wet loam. It enters my shelter through the small breathing-holes I have punched in the copper, settling on my back like a warm, sticky oil.

Then, the **Swarm-Rush**.

Through the metal of my saucer, I hear them. Millions. Billions. The shield-beetles are falling from the columns, raining onto the floor, scrambling toward the sugar-mist. They climb over my copper roof. The sound is a deafening, metallic rattle—*clack-clack-clank-clack*—the scraping of ten thousand brass-bronze legs on the iron plate just three inches from my head. 

I do not move. I do not breathe.

In the dark of my shelter, my guide-moth crawls out from my hair. 

It is a Tear-Moth, bigger than the ones they keep in the shanties. Its wings are the grey of old mold, and its antennae are as long as my fingers. It does not need a thread. It knows me. It crawls down my forehead, its cold, wet feet prickling my skin, and settles near my nose. 

It reaches into my left eye-socket—the one the fungal spores took when I was young, the one that doesn't see but still weeps the thin, salty grease-water—and it drinks. 

The moth’s wings flutter against my cheek. *Flut-flut-flut.* 
Outside, the beetles scream to drink the sugar.
Beneath me, the fossil-wood groans as Stem Two takes its deep, structural breath.
And around me, my forty dry standers—the things the tree did not want—vibrate in their cracks. The steam-pressure hums through their horn-hard ribs, turning them into forty hollow pipes, forty flutes of bone and wood, singing a low, whistling note that no Sapper on the scaffolds will ever hear.

The note says: *We are boiling.*

I close my one good eye. The grease on my skin is warm. The moth is drinking. The world is full of sugar and iron and beetles, and we are all, every one of us, down in the Sump, breathing together in the dark.
