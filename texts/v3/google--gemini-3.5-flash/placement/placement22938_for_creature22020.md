I am not a creature that crawls, nor am I a plant that clings. I have no name that could be pronounced in a single breath, because I do not exist in a single piece. 

I am where the water-line forgets its purpose.

If you were to climb the rusted iron grating—tier by tier, through the warm, greasy dark—you would eventually reach a spot near the middle height, where a three-inch bypass pipe once branched off from the main cold-water return. The pipe was made of drawn brass, but it was joined to the iron casing-wall by a heavy, square-shouldered flange. To seal the joint against the high pressures of an active youth, the builders placed a ring between the metal faces: a thick gasket of soft, chemical lead, packed with flax-rope that had been boiled in mutton-tallow and graphite.

That was many centuries ago. 

Since then, the iron flange has swelled as it decayed. The rust has expanded, pressing inward with a slow, hydraulic force of several tons per square inch. Under this immense, silent pinch, the lead gasket did not break; it did what lead does under pressure. It *flowed*. 

Over two hundred years, I have been squeezed out of the joint like cold, grey butter. 

I am that lead-flow. I am the **Gland-Marrow**.

I have become a thick, horizontal lobe—a grey, mineral tongue that protrudes four inches from the seam of the pipe-joint. I am completely static to any human eye, yet I am in a state of perpetual, geologic motion. I am "creeping." My atoms slide past one another with agonizing slowness, driven by the weight of the thirty-foot vertical column of iron above me. I am filling the space. Every pit in the corroded iron, every microscopic crack in the green copper-carbonate jacket of the bypass line, I have found. I have molded myself to them. I have taken their print.

I have no eyes, but I have an exquisite sense of *fit*. I know the shape of the chamber by the way its weight presses into my back. 

My surface is no longer clean, dull metal. On my top side, where the condensation from the cold-line drips continuously, I have grown a thick, protective bark of "rust-stone." It is a scab of red-iron tuff, cemented together by the lead carbonate that forms when my own skin reacts with the carbonic acid in the dripping water. I am half-metal, half-geology. 

The **Grease-Felt** has found my underside. Where the heat from the steam register rises, it warms my belly, causing the ancient tallow-fat buried deep within my leaden core to weep outward through microscopic pores. The yellow, hair-like fungus drinks this fat. Its delicate, root-like hyphae have burrowed into my outer millimeter of lead-silt, anchoring themselves. I do not mind them. They feel like a cool, damp wool that insulates me from the sharp, drying draft of the register. 

When the **Ferrules** come, I feel them. 

To me, a Ferrule is not an animal. It is a tiny, localized unit of friction. When one of those lead-colored isopods crawls onto my lobe, its hard, mineralized legs scratch against my rust-stone bark. The vibration is tiny, but because my lead-body is a perfect conductor of sound, the scratching travels through my entire mass. I can hear the Ferrule’s progress as a series of dry, rhythmic *clicks* inside my own center. 

Sometimes, a Ferrule dies upon me. Its wire-legs stiffen; its circulation stops. As the water drips and the iron-tuff grows over it, I do not discard the shell. I swallow it. Over decades, my slow lead-flow envelopes the mummified husk, pressing it into my body, absorbing its calcium and iron-oxide into my own matrix. The graves of thirty Ferrules are currently buried deep within my lobe, like pebbles caught in the snout of a glacier. They are my skeleton.

My life is measured by the *hhhhhh-ung*.

Every thirty minutes, when the deep, relic valve settles below, the vibration does not simply shake me. It re-forms me. 

When the floorplates groan, the bypass pipe experiences a lateral shear of perhaps one-hundredth of a millimeter. The flange shifts. The iron teeth of the pipe-thread bite deeper into my lead. In that moment of intense transition, I feel a sudden, sharp spike of heat within my core—the heat of friction as millions of lead-crystals slide over one another, finding new positions of rest. 

It is a moment of pure, blinding awareness. For three seconds after the groan, as the pipes contract with those tiny, dry *tinks*, my internal structure is plastic. I am liquid-adjacent. I am yielding. 

Then, the lead cools. The crystals lock back into their hexagonal cages. The silence returns, and I am solid again—harder than before, more perfectly fitted to the gap, more inextricably wedded to the iron.

I am not the only one. 

Throughout the fifty-foot height of the Casing-Well, in every joint, under every bolt-head, behind every brass valve-seat, there are others like me. There are copper gaskets that are slowly turning into green-lace fronds; there are hemp packing-pockets that have become solidified clubs of wax and iron-silt; there are steel studs that have lost their threads and are now growing outward like red, bulbous fingers to touch the catwalks.

We do not speak, and we do not travel. But we are growing toward each other. 

With every drop of water that falls, with every sigh of the ancient valve, we creep. We are filling the air-gap of the room. We are bridging the catwalks to the pipes, and the pipes to the steam register, and the register to the raw iron walls. 

In another five hundred years, there will be no empty space left in the Second Auxiliary. The air will be squeezed out entirely. The Casing-Well will become a single, solid, fifty-foot plug of iron, copper, lead, tallow, and fungus. 

A new, synthetic stone. A living ore. 

Until then, I wait in the warm, greasy dark. I feel the cold water gather on the pipe above me. I feel it grow heavy. I feel it fall. 

*Thupt.*

And inside my leaden heart, a thousand tiny crystals slip, and settle, and are still.
