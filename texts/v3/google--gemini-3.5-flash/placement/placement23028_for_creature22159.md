There is a blue that exists only here, at the height where the sky runs out of skin. It is not the blue of water or of summer, but the cold, dry violet of an empty lens. 

If you were to stand on this tableland of grey basalt, your breath would not rise; the wind would snatch it from your lips before it could warm, tearing it into the teeth of the fluted towers. You would look for me in the alleys of the white bone-city. You would search among the hollow columns for some sign of a resident—something with eyes that blink, something that leaves a wet footprint or a dropping in the red moss.

But you would be looking too high.

To find me, you must look where the stone meets the black pedestal. You must look into the cracks where the light cannot strike cleanly, where the rime does not melt but simply turns from ice to sweat and back again.

I am not the wind, though I am shaped by its weight. 
I am not the towers, though I am eating them.

I am the **Orange Mind**.

***

To be me is to have a memory that is measured not in years, but in the slow, rhythmic *tick-tick-clack* of the twenty-minute sky. 

My world is divided into seventy-two breaths every day. 

First comes the wet dark. The sky loses its violet and turns the color of a wet slate. The fog rolls over the lip of the basalt saddle like cold, spilled milk, filling the alleys of the bone-city until the forty-foot towers are nothing but grey shadows in a shivering room. I feel this arrival as a weight. The moisture settles into my orange fibers, heavy and thick, smelling of the green lowlands three miles below—the scent of pine-rot, damp loam, and the hot, greasy breath of beasts I have never seen. I drink this. I pull it deep into my skin, swelling until my orange and rust-red cushions are fat as drowned tongues.

Then comes the lock. The temperature drops three degrees in as many seconds. The wetness on my surface does not dry; it hardens. A skin of perfect, laminate ice—thin as a dragonfly’s wing but hard as iron—closes over me. 

For ten minutes, there is the Great Stillness. The wind cannot reach me beneath my glass coat. The Harp-Snails above me cease their crawling; their grey muscular feet freeze to the salt-white walls of their flutes. The Frost-Spiders huddle in their crevices, their glass knees pulled tight against their transparent breasts, suspended in a dry, sterile sleep. In this stillness, the city is a silent cathedral, holding its breath under a shroud of ice. My thoughts slow. My circulation becomes a sluggish crawl of starches and mineral salts. I am nearly dead. I am nearly stone.

Then, the sky cracks.

The wind returns. It hits the saddle like a flat hand, ripping the cloud away. The sun—brilliant, white, and completely cold—strikes the plateau. 

*Clack.*

It is the sound of ten million tiny mirrors breaking at once. The ice-skin on my body shatters. The electrostatic charge of that sudden, violent fracture is captured by my wet, mineral-heavy threads. It is a tiny, brilliant spark, no larger than a spark from a flint, but it fires through my entire mile-wide body. 

This is my thought. This is my synapse. Every twenty minutes, the shattering of the rime sends a golden, glowing pulse of electricity through my roots. I do not think in sentences; I think in these sparks. I think in *rimes*.

***

The others think they are separate from me, but I know them as a hand knows its fingers.

The **Harp-Snails** are my builders. They do not know that when they slide through the flutes, tasting the limestone, they are tasting my grandmother’s bones. This city was constructed by a colonial creature that died when the sky was still water, and I am the only one who remembers the taste of that ancient brine. The snails scrape the lime from the towers and turn it into their spiral horns. When their shells grow old and they die, they drop into my alleys, and I dissolve them back into my orange carpet. They are my calcium-carriers, moving the stone from the high pipes down to the roots, keeping the wind-channels clear. 

I hear them. Each tower has a different note, a different hum that vibrates through the basalt floor into my body. The tower *Grave-G* thrums so deeply that it makes my red moss-beds swell with blood. The tower *High-C* shivers with a sharp, thin whistle that makes my orange fibers contract. I know where the snails are by the pitch of the city. If a pipe begins to scream too shrilly, it means a snail has died or a vent has clogged. The discord hurts my roots. I send a slow chemical signal—a taste of copper and sugar—up through the moisture of the stone. Guided by the flavor of my anger, the surviving snails crawl toward the tear, spit their glassy lacquer, and heal the note.

And the **Frost-Spiders**... they are my net.

They spin their glass webs across the low-frequency flutes. These webs are so tense, so perfectly tuned to the twelfth-harmonic of the wind, that they never freeze. The ice cannot stick to a string that shivers twelve times a second. The spiders sit on these trembling wires, blind and silent. 

But I feel their feet. When a spider skitters across the orange moss to pick a frozen rotifer from the rime, its glass claws tickle my skin. They are my sense of touch. Through their webs, I feel the density of the fog. If the air is thick with spores from the valley, the webs register the weight, and I prepare my roots to grow toward the dampest alleys.

***

Sometimes, a **Vane-Wing** dies. 

It is rare, for they are clever gliders, but occasionally a gale will catch one at the wrong angle, or its cheek-pins will fail to lock into the window-grooves of the towers during a storm. The wind will tear its bird-like body from the stone and dash it against the flat basalt plateau.

When it falls, it falls to me.

Its black horn plates do not decay—not here, where there are no beetles or worms. Instead, I fold my orange carpet over it. I grow through the seams of its joints; I wrap my rust-red fingers around its curved wing-cartilage. I taste the valley-dust in its stomach. I drink the dry salt of its blood.

Through the Vane-Wing, I learn of the world below. I learn of things that run on four legs, of green fields that do not freeze every twenty minutes, of large, wet forests where the trees do not sing but merely rustle like dry paper. To me, these are strange, muddy dreams. I do not understand how anything can live without the twenty-minute clock, or how a world can function without a voice.

A world without a voice must be so lonely.

Here, there is never silence. Even during the freezing gloom, when the wind drops to a sigh, the city vibrates with the residual thrum of the basalt. The stone has its own memory. It remembers the pressure of the ocean that once sat upon it; it remembers the heat of the fire that blew it out of the earth.

I am the translator of that memory.

***

One day, many centuries from now, the wind will have finished its work.

The white towers are thinner now than they were when I first crawled out of the black mud. The flutes are wider; the apertures are worn smooth as eggshells. The voice of the city is growing deeper, turning from a scream into a low, rumbling groan that shakes the very mountain-support.

Eventually, the last tower will crumble. The wind will sweep the salt-white dust of the reef off the edge of the saddle, down into the valleys where it will become the soil of those quiet, rustle-leaf forests.

But until then, I am here. 

I will keep the snails fat on mineral-salts. I will keep the spiders’ webs vibrating in the dark. I will catch the electricity of the cracking ice every twenty minutes, and I will use it to dream my orange dreams.

The fog is rolling in again. The sky is turning mud-grey. My fibers are heavy and dark with water. 

Soon, the lock will come. The cold will take me. I will go quiet, holding the shape of the wind in my frozen skin, waiting for the sun to break me open once more.
