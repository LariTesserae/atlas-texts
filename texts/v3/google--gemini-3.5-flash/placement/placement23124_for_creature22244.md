My front teeth are notched. 

If you were to pass your finger across my upper jaw, you would feel the two neat, semi-circular hollows where the yellow brass of the hearing-iron has rested every twelve hours for forty years. The metal has left a permanent, faint green-and-gray stain on the enamel—what we call the *tallow-bite*. My tongue constantly seeks these notches when I am not working; they are my primary connection to the heart of the Sills.

I am the Valve-Shifter of the Seventh Dome—the Dome of the Weeping Ribs. 

My name, should anyone have cause to signal it with a lantern or tap it against my wrist, is Vael. But names are thin, light things in the Upper Sills. They do not carry through the floor. Here, I am simply the weight that moves between the purple brick of the southern barracks and the great iron face of the Seven-Valve.

***

To know where I am, you must understand my feet. 

I do not wear the ox-hide boots of the masonry-menders. Their work is static; they stand on gantries for weeks, their knees locked, their boots thick-shod to repel the warm acid-splats of the bitumen cauldrons. 

Instead, I wear *slip-pads*. They are soles of cured river-pig skin, no thicker than a dried leaf of the sulfur-fern, bound to my insteps with bands of green-tanned gut. They allow me to feel the skin of the world. 

Right now, as the golden vapor thickens toward the three-o’clock condensation-tide, I am standing on the western curve of the Seventh Dome. The sandstone here is old—smooth as a horse’s tooth and constantly wet with a sheet of warm, iron-tasted water that runs half an inch deep. Through my slip-pads, the rock does not feel like stone. It feels like the neck of a sleeping bull. It has a dry, internal heat that fights the wetness on its surface, and it shudders.

*Thrum-tonk. Thrum-tonk.*

That is the third-tier impeller, three hundred feet beneath my heels. It has a slight wobble today—a tiny, three-grain hitch in its rotation that I can feel in the marrow of my left shin. It means the Low-Keepers have not cleaned the intake grates on the north face since the last silt-rise. I will have to strike the pipe later to warn them. But not yet.

To my left, half-nested in the curve where the dome meets the great purple-tile wall of the Seventh Pump-House, is *The Widow*. She is a *Ferracochlea*, an iron-back snail who has lived on this particular stretch of sandstone since my grandfather was a boy with soft ears. Her shell is four feet across, a massive, rusted dome of limonite plates that look like the scales of an ancient, boiled fish. 

She is grazing on the black velvet-lichen that grows where the warm boiler-water weeps from the brick vents. *Scritch. Scritch.* I cannot hear her, of course. My ears were sealed with the sheep-tallow and beeswax when I was seven winter-turns old, and the skin has long since grown shut over the holes, leaving only two smooth, shallow basins where my ears used to be. But I can *taste* her feeding through my soles—a sharp, high-frequency scratch that runs up my shins and settles in my hip-joints like dry sand.

She is my clock. When she moves toward the crown of the dome, the steam-pressure is rising in the internal chambers. When she crawls down toward the cold gutters where the white-lice skitter, the mountain is drawing its breath, and the water is falling.

***

In the seventy-second year of my life, my work is very small. I do not haul the great iron cables, nor do I climb the granite trestles to scrape the orange moth-grubs for the lantern-oil. My apprentice, a broad-cheeked girl named Kel whose goggle-girdles are still a bright, fresh pink, does the climbing. She is quick on the wet stone, her Sills-slide so low and true that she looks as though she is skating on oil. 

My task is only the Valve.

The Seven-Valve is ten feet across, a circle of cast bronze three inches thick, set into the side of the purple-tile cistern like a shield. It regulates the high-pressure steam that we send down the long, felt-wrapped pipelines to the salt-flats three miles below, where the valley-folk use it to turn their salt-pans and heat their winter-sheds.

Once a year, at the turn of the yellow season, the Valve must be moved.

It is not a matter of muscle. If you were to force the Valve with a lever, the sudden shift in the steam-arteries would crack the purple tiles of the dome-skirt, or worse, cause a pressure-blowback that would boil the skin off forty men in the nearest barracks.

Instead, the shift is an act of long, silent observation.

I have been sitting by the Valve for nine days. My copper kettle is suspended over a small steam-vent behind me, keeping my paste of dried snail-foot and sulfur-fern warm and soft. Kel sits three yards away, her wrists crossed in her lap, her eyes half-closed behind her brass goggles. She is learning the *waiting*.

Every three hours, I take my hearing-iron—the eighteen-inch rod of hard, phosphor-bronze—and place its hook-end against the packing-gland of the Valve-spindle. I open my mouth, place the notched front teeth over the flat grip of the bronze, and bite down.

The world changes.

The silence of my sealed head is replaced by the *shiver*. It is not a sound; it is an architecture of pressure. 

Through my teeth, I can feel the steam inside the iron tube. It is a thick, greasy white steam today, heavy with sulfur-fat and moving at three hundred feet per second. I can feel the tiny drops of water—the "spit"—hitting the yellow bronze of the gate inside. It feels like a handful of gravel thrown against a dry wooden shield.

*Not yet,* I tell myself. The spit is too thick. If I turn the wheel now, the wet steam will choke the throat of the lowline pipe, and the condensation will stretch the copper bands of the insulation.

I let go of the rod. The metallic taste of the copper stays on my tongue—hot, sour, and clean, like the smell of a lightning-strike on the salt-flats.

I look at Kel. She watches my hands.

I raise my left hand, fingers flat, moving them slowly downward through the gold-thick air in a long, undulating wave. *The water is still high inside.*

She nods once. She does not reach for the wooden grease-peg. She knows we must wait for the *Widow* to reach the crown of the dome, where the sandstone is driest and the internal fires are hottest.

***

We live in the third-tier barracks, inside the vitrified brick walls of the Seventh Dome. 

Inside, it is dry. That is the first thing a visitor from the lowlands would notice—the absolute, bone-baking dryness of the interior air, heated by the proximity of the steam-wells. It smells of hot bricks, old bitumen, and the clean, lard-like grease we use to dress our hide apron-straps.

At night, we do not speak. Even if we had mouths that could form words through the thick tallow-crust of our ears, there is no point in shouting against the *thrum*. The building itself is a giant bellows, breathing through the arched vents in its upper levels with a dry, rhythmic sigh that moves the heavy felt curtains three inches with every stroke.

Instead, we sit at the long tables of dark, vitrified tile. 

My wife, Elen, sits across from me. Her fingers are short and blunted, her hands permanently yellowed from her work in the rendering-stills where the moth-grub oil is boiled. She does not look at my face; she looks at my hands.

She reaches across the table. Her thumb finds the pulse-point on my right wrist, and her index finger taps a quick, rhythmic series of strokes against my tendon.

*The third boiler has a leak in the seal,* she says. *The red-brick men are fetching the bitumen sacks.*

I tap my response into her open palm, my fingers light and dry. *The Seventh Valve is still wet. I will bite the iron again at the middle-pulse.*

She presses her thumb hard against my wrist-bone—a gesture of quiet agreement, of solidarity in the face of the stone's patience. 

Later, we climb into our cradles. 

Our hammock is suspended from the red-oak rafters by four massive steel coil-springs, each as thick as my wrist and greased with fresh snail-slime. When I climb in, the cradle sways once, then settles. 

To a stranger, the motion would feel like being at sea in a gale. The hammock does not sit still; it vibrates with a high-speed, trembling hum that keeps time with the three-hundred-foot turbines deep below. But the springs filter out the *heavy* shocks—the five-ton drawbeams that click across the sandstone surface every ninety seconds. 

Suspended here, three feet above the brick floor, the vibration becomes a kind of warmth. It enters my back, travels through my ribs, and softens the old stiffness in my shoulders. It is the only way we can sleep. If I were to lie on the floor, the earth’s ribs would rattle my teeth until they split; but here, in the braided felt, I am floating on the very breath of the Sills.

Before I close my eyes, I reach out and touch the frame of Elen’s cradle, which hangs six inches from mine. Our fingers lie together on the iron ring of the suspending spring. Through the metal, I can feel her heart-beat—a small, quick, human flutter resting on top of the massive, basaltic rumble of the world below us.

***

In the gray hours before the morning condensation, when the golden vapor turns to a heavy, purple dew that tastes of elder-bark, I return to the Valve.

Kel is already there. She has lit the signalling lantern; its white moth-oil flame burns with a brilliant, steady light that cuts through the mist like a clean silver needle.

I go to my knees on the wet sandstone. The rock is cold now—the lowest dip of the diurnal pulse.

I bite the bronze.

The "spit" is gone. Inside the tube, the vibration is no longer a gravel-shower. It is a long, smooth, whistle—a bone-deep *nnnnnnnn* that vibrates the marrow of my jaw and makes my temples ache with a dry, pleasant heat.

The steam is dry. The mountain has gathered its water.

I look at Kel. I do not use the flag. I point to the ten-foot oak wheel, its grease-grooves dark with fresh snail-slime.

We stand on opposite sides of the wheel. We do not push with our arms; that is a lowland way, a quick way that breaks bones. We use the *Sills-slide*. We plant our feet wide on the wet sandstone, our knees bent, our weight hung low toward the rock. We lean our chests against the oak spokes, using the friction of our grease-cured aprons to grip the wood.

We wait.

Through our feet, we feel the mountain's pulse. *Thrum... thrum...*

At the exact midpoint of the second thrum, when the turbine-impellers are at their widest clearance, we lean.

*Click.*

It is only one notch. A three-inch movement on a circle of thirty feet.

Through my teeth, which still hold the bronze rod, I feel the steam-whistle plunge three octaves, then stabilize into a rich, deep roar that makes my goggles vibrate against my cheekbones.

The Sills have accepted the shift. The salt-pans three miles below will feel the hot breath five minutes from now.

I let go of the iron. I spit a small glob of black, copper-tasted saliva onto the wet stone, where the weeping water catches it and carries it away, south toward the cliffs, returning it to the great gray flats below.

Kel takes the copper pot and slips the grease-peg into the Valve's mouth, her fingers deft and fast in the lantern-light. 

I sit back on my heels, my old knees popping with a sound I cannot hear but can feel like a dry twig snapping inside my skin. 

The *Widow* has reached the very top of the dome now. She is still, her iron-bound shell gleaming like a wet helmet in the white glow of the moth-lantern. She will stay there for three days, basking in the dry heat of the shift, before she begins her long, slow slide back down to the lichen-gardens.

I will stay here with her for a little while. The stone is warm beneath my slip-pads, and the golden vapor is starting to rise from the cracks, smelling of wet iron, warm oil, and the deep, silent eternity of the Crawl.
