The world here does not have a horizon; it has a skin. 

If you were to look for me, you would find me forty feet up, suspended between the middle tier of iron dwellings and the damp dark of the ceiling vault, hanging in a sling of braided bast. 

I am a **Suture-Smith**—what the younger Cups, who swing through the high wire-nets with their copper collars, call a *Graft-wright*. My work is the work of the boundary. I do not catch the rain, nor do I boil the leaf-mulch in the vats below. I am the one who bargains with the cambium. When the hamlet needs a new winch-mount, or when an iron catwalk begins to sag and requires a fresh masonry bolt driven six inches into the bruised purple bark, they send for me.

I am forty-eight seasons into this skin. My hair, once the long, free-floating river-weed black of the young, is now bound in six flat platts against my skull, greased with vulcanized latex to keep the copper dust from clogging my pores. My shoulders have the thick, bunched slope of a spade-blade—the shape that comes from decades of holding seventy-pound iron templates against the living wood while the steam-drills bite. 

But my feet are changing.

Lately, when I press my bare soles against the wet hempen webbing of the sling, my third and fourth toes do not curve as easily around the rope as they once did. Last night, during the three-hour hush before the morning sap-tide, I shifted in my hammock and heard it: a dry, hollow *click-click* in the joint of my right ankle. It was identical to the sound of the gasket-beetle currently scraping its bronze belly against my grease-pot. 

The wood is starting to call its liquor back. I have perhaps fifteen seasons before my boots never leave the petrified peat of the basin floor—before I become one of those silent, heavy-headed elders who sit on the benches near the boilers, reading the heartbeat of the pumps through the seat of their trousers.

But today, there is a slit to be closed.

***

Around me, the Basin is in its post-tide quiet. The transpiration pumps have just been turned off; their deep, water-muffled *chuff-thump* has ceased, leaving a silence so absolute that the air itself feels heavy, like wet wool. The emerald daylight filtering through the skylight fronds above is thick and green, as though we are all living at the bottom of a forest pond. 

I am working on a "healing niche" third-level-east, where a heavy copper distillation pipe was torn away during a storm-gust in the outer world last month. The tare is ugly: a twelve-inch gash of pale, sticky pulp that has begun to ferment in the damp air. It smells of sour apple and wet iron. 

My tools are laid out in a shallow copper tray that hangs from my belt:
*   A jar of hot latex-mortar, kept warm by a small charcoal ember in its double-walled base.
*   Two wedge-shaped plates of cold-forged pig iron, pickled in vinegar to make them dark and toothy.
*   My mallet—not iron, which would bruise the cambium's lip, but a heavy, oil-soaked lump of petrified root-heart, bound in brass hoop-shrunk bands.

To my right, resting on the curve of a bark-ridge, is my apprentice, a young girl named Sula. Her skin is still that clear, water-gilded ivory of the young, almost blue in the hollows of her collarbones. She has not yet developed the broad, heavy smith-shoulders; she moves with the spider-like lightness of a Cup, her prehensile toes twitching as she grips the iron bracket of the lower catwalk.

She does not speak. No one speaks here unless they must, and then only in the dry, breathy *Sigh-Talk* that doesn’t carry past the hand. 

Instead, she reaches out with her copper wrench and taps twice on the head of an old gantry bolt near my knee. 

*Tink. Tink.*

*Is the wood cold?* she asks through the iron.

I press my bare palm against the purple cambium beside the wound. The bark is cool, but beneath it, I can feel the slow, deep throb of the internal vascular channels—the heavy, subterranean *shrr-shrr* of the tree's great heart drawing water up from the deep earth.

I strike my heel against the iron rung of my sling. 

*Thump. Slide. Thump.*

*It is ready. Give me the wedge.*

She hands me the iron plate. It is heavy, cold, smelling of the blacksmith's coal. I smear the back of it with the warm latex-mortar—thick, grey, smelling of scorched sugar—and press it into the raw pulp of the gash. 

The tree reacts instantly. It is a slow reaction, but to those of us who live here, it is as obvious as a flinch. A pale, amber bead of latex wells up from the cut edge of the bark, touching the cold iron. The wood *groans*—a low, five-second creak that vibrates through my teeth and into the soles of my feet. 

To a stranger, it would sound like a ship's timber straining in a gale. To me, it is the sound of the cambium recognizing the metal. 

Now comes the "taming." 

I take the mallet and begin to tap. I do not strike hard. I tap a steady, complex rhythm into the head of the iron wedge—the *Solderer’s Lullaby*. It is a sequence of three quick, light strikes followed by a dragging pause, mimicking the natural micro-vibrations of the transpiration pumps when they are running at half-load. 

*Tick-tick-tick... shhh.*
*Tick-tick-tick... shhh.*

Through the iron, I am telling the tree that this metal is not an enemy. I am telling it that this is a rib, a tooth, a part of its own skeleton that has fallen out and is now being returned. 

Sula watches, her dark, river-weed hair falling over her forehead in wet strings. She is holding her breath. If I strike too hard, the bark will split further, and the sap-pressures during the next tide will blow the iron right out of the wall, showering the lower tier with boiling, forty-pound shrapnel. If I tap too softly, the wood will reject the graft, and the iron will rot in its socket within three room-rains.

My right knee clicks again as I shift my weight. A sharp, dry needle of pain runs up to my hip. 

*I am hardening,* I think, but I do not let the rhythm of the mallet waver. *Tick-tick-tick... shhh.*

Slowly, the magic of the Basin happens before our eyes. The grey-purple lips of the bark, stimulated by the rhythm and the heat of the latex-mortar, begin to swell. A smooth, skin-like lip of scar tissue, wet with green moisture, slowly rolls over the top edge of the iron plate, gripping it like a gum gripping a new tooth. 

In ten seasons, this iron plate will be entirely invisible. It will be a dark, silent bulge in the wall, three inches deep beneath the bark, holding the third-tier catwalk steady against the weight of twenty households.

Sula lets out her breath—a long, dry sibilant that sounds like a dry leaf falling onto moss. 

*Good,* her heel taps against the catwalk.

***

Then, the sky above us changes. 

The translucent skylight fronds eighty feet up begin to lose their green-pond hue. The outer sky is turning; the sun has hit the noon-apex, and the light filters down through the gargantuan leaves in a deep, burning amber. It is the color of honey held against a candle.

A cold wind—the first sign of the "room-rain"—stirs the copper mist-nets in the high void. 

"The tide," I whisper in the *Sigh-Talk*, my voice nothing but a puff of wind through my teeth. 

Sula is already moving. She does not need to be told. She swings herself out of the catwalk, her prehensile toes catching the hemp guide-ropes with a soft *shruf*, sliding down thirty feet inside of three seconds, her copper wrenches clinking against her harness like small bells.

I am slower. 

I pack my mallet and my jar of mortar into the copper tray. I look at the iron wedge I have just set. It is already half-swallowed, the wet grey lips of the tree closing around it like an eyelid. I reach out and touch the bark one last time. 

My palm reads the vibration. The transpiration pumps are still dead, but deep within the eighty-foot walls, the pressure is rising. The sap is coming up from the world below—millions of gallons of thick, mineral-sweet oil, rushing through the channels just three inches behind this purple skin. The wood begins to creak. Not the high, sharp creak of a dry branch, but the low, liquid rumble of a wooden ship taking on water.

The first drop of the room-rain hits my shoulder. It is the size of a plum, cold and heavy, smelling of crushed mint. 

I release my safety-sling. My knees click—the left one this time, a dull, metallic *tock* that matches the first drop of rain hitting my copper tray. 

I do not slide down the ropes like Sula. I cannot. Instead, I climb down the carved bark stairs, my old, stiffening toes finding the ridges with slow, deliberate precision. With every step, I feel the giant node swelling around me, its great ribs expanding in the amber light, filling the basin with the immense, heavy breath of a beast that has been sleeping for ten thousand years.

I will make it to my tier before the heavy downpour begins. I will sit in my small, bracketed room, grease my clicking joints with the fresh latex oil we boiled yesterday, and listen to the rain drumming on the copper nets above. And tomorrow, when the green light returns, I will go back to the wall. 

Until the tree finally finishes swallowing me, I still have rungs to set.
