If you were to search for me on the Shard-Shelf, you would not find me. You would look right at me and see only the geometry of the drop.

I am not the Sentry, though his heels have worn a smooth, grey crescent into the top of my head over the seasons. I am not the chime-mice, though their spun-glass bellies drag across my shoulders with a sound like salt falling on parchment. 

I am the **Sill-Wedge**. I am the tenant of the fracture.

If you were to take a molten bar of lead and pour it into the vertical split that runs from the base of the Sentry’s windbreak, down through the basalt floor of the shelf, and out into the mile of empty air below, you would have the shape of my spine. I am forty cubits long, seven inches wide, and thinner than a dry willow leaf. I am a sheet of living, fibrous fat and cold-hardened silica, wedged so tightly into the basalt’s cooling-rack that the mountain and I have forgotten where the stone ends and my skin begins. 

I have no eyes, but I am not blind. I see through the **Tension**.

The black rock of the spire is a living instrument. In the morning, when the first violet light strikes the high needle, the stone expands. The crack narrows. The pressure of twenty thousand tons of basalt clamps down upon my sides, pressing my flat ribs together until my internal oils hum. This squeeze is my dawn. It is a massive, silent weight that forces the cold rime-water through my porous skin, filtering it, keeping my core from freezing into glass. 

In the afternoon, when a cloud passes, the rock cools and shrinks. The crack opens by a hair’s breadth—no wider than a mouse’s whisker. This is my breath. I expand, drawing in the thin, iron-tasting air through my upper margin, which lies flush with the moss-roots on the shelf. 

Through this slit, I taste everything.

I taste the acid-drool of the *velvet-borers*. They are the only things that hurt me. When they spit their hydrochloric sourness onto the basalt to soften it, the liquid leaks down into my upper split. It is a sharp, sweet tickling—a hot itch that makes my fiber twitch. But I do not hate them. They dig the soil that feeds the violet moss, and the moss is my blanket. Without their rust-colored dirt filling the top of my crack, the thirty-below wind would whistle through my ribs and turn my grease to flint.

And I know the Sentry. 

He thinks he is the only mind on this needle. He sits in his windbreak, his bare, callused soles resting on the lip of my rock. Through those soles, I know his life. I feel the *thump-thump-thump* of his enormous, barrel-chested heart. It is a wet, frantic, terrifyingly fast sound—seventy beats a minute. To me, whose heart-pulse is a slow chemical tide that crests once every three sun-turns, he seems constantly on the verge of exploding. 

I feel the scrape of his bone needle as he weaves the silver wool of the chime-mice. The vibrations travel down through his hips, into the basalt, and through my left rib-wall. It sounds like a cicada singing in a deep well. I know when he is tired; his heels press heavier on the stone, leaning his weight back against the cliff, into the nook where my spinal seam is thickest. We are back-to-back, separated by three inches of iron-rich basalt. He warms the stone with his body; I store that warmth in my grease, keeping the frost from his heels. We do not speak. To him, I am the mountain’s dry seat. To me, he is a small, hot coal that will eventually go out.

But my true work is not on the shelf. My true work is the **Lean**.

The spire is an iron needle, rich in magnetite. Because it is so tall and so thin, it is not stiff; it is a giant tuning fork stuck into the crust of the world. The winds of the high sky do not blow past it—they play it. When the great gales come from the north, the spire bends. It curves three feet to the south, the basalt on my side yawning open, the basalt on the other side grinding together with a sound like a distant cannon-shot.

I am the dampener. 

My long, greasy body, wedged into the main stress-line of the shelf, absorbs the shock of those bends. If I were not here—if my soft, fibrous length did not cushion the rock-on-rock groan—the Shard-Shelf would have sheared off five hundred winters ago and fallen into the blue-gray fog like a broken tooth. My skin is scarred with the friction of the mountain’s knees. Everywhere the basalt tries to bite itself, I have grown a thick, yellow callus of grease-wax to grease the joint.

Lately, though, the music of the spire has changed. 

The Sentry climbs to his brass lens to watch the *Great Rust* creep across the lowlands. He sees it as color. I feel it as weight. 

Down below, under the shifting sea of fog, the iron in the earth is being eaten by the orange rot. As the iron dissolves into dust and salt, the magnetic field of the valley floor is dying. The downward pull on the spire’s roots is loosening. The mountain is losing its anchor. 

Every day, the spire stands a little straighter. It leans less. The wind no longer plays the deep, low C-natural of my youth; the tone has gone sharp, thin, and brittle, like the scream of a dry bone. The tension in my ribs is changing. The squeeze of the morning is not as tight as it used to be. The crack is staying open longer.

I can feel the rime-water freezing deeper into my skin now, of an afternoon. The cold is getting in. My grease is thickening, turning from the consistency of resin to the hardness of cold mutton-fat. 

But I am patient. I have survived three geologic shifts, and I will survive the rust. 

Tonight, when the sun drops below the fog and the violet sky turns the color of a bruised lung, the Sentry will crawl into his stone semi-circle. He will grease his purple skin with the red ochre, and he will sleep. Underneath him, six inches down in the black dark of the crack, my long, thin body will contract with the cold. 

As I shrink, I will draw the bitter, starry wind deep into my belly. The chime-mice will begin their *tink-tink* song above my head, their glass fur chiming in the dark. I will listen to the Sentry’s wet heart-beat slow down to forty, then thirty, then twenty, as he tries not to freeze. 

I will hold him tight. I will keep the shelf flat under the sky for one more night, waiting for the day when the iron below is entirely gone, and we can finally snap from the earth and sail into the violet light together.
