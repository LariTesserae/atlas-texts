I would not be found on the high, wind-scoured ridges of the cork-cliffs, where the Screech-Singers turn the gale into a weapon of static. Nor would I be found on the flat, endless ribbons of the golden plains, driving a sled-sail down the straight grain-lines where the horizon never changes.

I would be found in the **Knot of the Seventh Scar**.

There are places in this continental timber where, thousands of years ago, the great plant suffered an injury. Perhaps a colossal branch, miles thick, was torn away by some ancient tempest; perhaps one of the great Lignum-Eels burrowed too close to the surface and died, leaving a cavernous rot-hole that the cambium spent centuries sealing. 

In these places, the grain does not run straight. It does not allow for sleds. Instead, the wood eddies. It swirls into massive, dizzying whirlpools of burled heartwood—a landscape of golden-brown whorls, concentric rings of dense, iron-hard timber, and deep, circular valleys where the grain-lines twist like frozen ropes.

In this place, I would not be entirely human, nor would I be entirely tree. I would be a **Spindle**.

### The Transition: The Seasoning of the Bone

To become a Spindle is a slow process of sedimentation. It begins when a tapper of the Hanging-Towns grows "grain-deaf"—when their heels can no longer distinguish the light click of a pulp-ant from the deep, wet rumble of an approaching Sap-Pulse. Rather than face the silence of the high towns, they travel to a burl.

They sit. They do not get up.

I have been sitting in the Seventh Scar for so long that the line between my body and the cambium has grown frayed and wet. My legs are no longer systems of muscle and knee-joints; they have slipped into the deep, natural splits of the burled grain. The skin of my shins has grown thick, grey, and corky, shedding in thin paper-flashes like birch bark when the dry wind hits them. My double-jointed toes have elongated, split at the tips, and grown down into the pale, vascular bundles of the green bast beneath me. 

Through these split toes, I drink. I do not need the gourd-wells. When the night-dew coats the burl in obsidian blackness, my skin absorbs the sweet-scented mist. I feel the thin, hot sap-creeks running through the furrows of my thighs before they evaporate in the morning sun.

My spine has fused with the heartwood wall behind me. The vertebrae have aligned themselves with the vertical xylem tubes of the great plant. My ribs are now dense, dry bone, calcified by the mineral-rich sap I have pulled from the depths, and they have become hollow. When the wind moves over the canyon rim, my chest cavity hums—not with the screaming static of the Screech-Singers, but with a low, clean, resonant drone, like a heavy wooden flute.

I have anointed my eyes with the thick, yellow oil of the glider-packs to shield them from the white-gold sun. I see the world now as a smear of amber light and sharp, black shadows. But more than that, I see with my skin.

### The Listening

During the blistering heat of the noon, when the air is thick with the screeching of giant cicadas and the ground crackles like burning paper, I am the only thing that is truly still.

Because the grain of the burl is curved, it acts as an acoustic lens. A straight grain-line carries sound like a copper wire, but a burl bends it, focusing the vibrations of the entire continent into a single point. 

Sitting at the center of this golden vortex, I hear everything:
*   I hear the three-man sleds of the *Silent Tappers* sixty miles to the north, their horn-fins singing as they lock into the grain-lines. I can tell by the pitch of their runners how much sap they carry in their clay pots.
*   I hear the blind *Lignin-Weavers* chewing the cork on the ridges, their mandibles clicking in rhythms that translate to me as a maps of their rising chimneys.
*   Far below—miles into the dark, wet heartwood where the pressure is enough to turn wood to stone—I hear the passage of a *Lignum-Eel*. It is a slow, rhythmic, crunching grind, like a millstone turning in clover. I know when it is hungry; I feel the heat of its friction rising through the sap-veins.

I am the memory of the Seventh Scar. When the tappers travel across the plains, they cannot use landmarks, for the plains have none. Instead, they navigate by the *hum* of the Spindles. They stand on their sleds, press their whorled heels to the wood, and listen for my specific chord—the low, hollow G-flat that my chest-ribs make when the high wind blows from the south.

### The Language of the Spindle

I do not speak with words. My tongue has long since hardened into a dry, ribbed plate of bone, suited only for scraping the sweet lichen from the walls of my hollow. 

If a traveler comes to the edge of my burl, they do not speak either. They know the wind will tear their voice to ribbons. Instead, they approach the outermost ring of my vortex, kneel, and strike the wood with their heartwood mallets.

*Tap-tap-creak.* 

The vibration travels through the curved grain of the Scar, accelerating as the rings tighten, until it hits the soles of my rooted feet with the force of a physical blow. 

*Where is the water?* they ask. 
*Where has the Pulp-Ant colony moved?*
*Is the ninety-day Pulse coming?*

I answer them with the **Phloe-Hum**. By contracting the muscles of my lower abdomen—which are still soft and human—I can press my dense pelvic bones against the heartwood floor. I rattle my ribs within my chest. 

I send a vibration back through the rings. It is a dry, rolling shudder that travels up their legs and into their teeth. 

*Go three days’ sail along the golden grain-line of the Left Rift,* I tell them. *The Weavers have abandoned a spire there. The combs are still wet with vinegar-fungus. But do not linger. The Lignum-Eel is rising to clear its lungs.*

They nod. They do not look at my oil-blind eyes or my bark-sheathed legs. They touch their forehead to the golden wood—a gesture of respect to the tree, and to me, who has become it—and they return to their sails.

### The Pulse

Every ninety days, my quiet existence is shattered by the ecstatic agony of the **Cambial Pulse**.

It begins as a warmth in my toes. Far below, the vast vascular networks of the world begin to swell. The green bast beneath the outer cork becomes engorged with hot, steaming syrup. 

For days before the Pulse hits, the burl groans. The sound is like the timbers of ten thousand galleons grinding against each other in a harbor under a hurricane. My joints—the places where my human bone meets the petrified heartwood—begin to ache. The pressure is immense. The wood expands, closing up the deep splits, pinching my cork-shinned legs with a grip that would crush a normal human's limbs.

But I do not break. My bones have been seasoned by the mineral sap; they are as dense and springy as the wood of the sled-runners.

When the Pulse finally arrives, it is a glorious, golden deluge. 

The center of my burl—the very point where the ancient branch was torn away—holds a deep, natural well. Under the pressure of the Pulse, the cork-plug at the bottom of the well gives way. A fountain of hot, thick, amber-colored syrup shoots sixty feet into the air, catching the white-gold sun and turning the sky into a shower of liquid gold.

The scent is intoxicating—it smells of fermented honey, scorched pine, and damp, ancient earth. 

I do not hide in the cliffs like the others. I sit at the edge of the fountain. The hot syrup rains down upon my head, coating my red moss-shoulders, filling my mouth, sealing my blind eyes in a fresh, protective layer of warm glaze. The youth of the tree washes over me. My nerves, integrated with the xylem, fire with the sheer, raw life-force of a plant that is larger than a continent.

I feel the entire world grow three inches thicker. I feel the bark-cliffs rise. I feel the Screech-Singers on the peaks screaming in ecstatic, deafening unison as the wind-pipes swell and change their pitch.

And then, slowly, the sun returns to bake us. 

The syrup dries, hardening into a shimmering, red-gold crust that covers my burl like a sheet of stained glass. The groaning of the wood subsides into a deep, comfortable sigh. The air cools, the first night-dew falls, and the world is dark, slick, and quiet once more.

I close my oil-filmed eyes. I press my chest against the wood. I listen to the long, slow cooling of the continent, and I begin my ninety-day wait for the next beat of our giant, wooden heart.
