### The Nature of the Room

To understand the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts, you must first abandon the concept of "the ground." 

There is no soil beneath you. There is no bedrock. If you were to drop a plumb line down through a hole in the floor, you would not strike granite; you would strike more terracotta, stitched together with lime-mortar that has calcified over centuries into a single, planet-sized, porous sponge. You are stepping into an artificial stomach—a self-regulating, lukewarm, weeping machine of brick and bronze that has forgotten its creators and decided to become an swamp.

It is loud, it is incredibly humid, and it is crowded. To visit as an ordinary human and return without broken bones, infected cuts, or sensory exhaustion, you must prepare with meticulous, practical intent.

---

### 1. Footwear: The Battle with Friction

Your primary enemy in the Room is the floor. The thousands of steps, pavers, and channel lips have been worn down by water and the scurrying of countless crabs until they are as smooth and curved as wet bars of soap. 

*   **Do not wear standard hiking boots.** Hard rubber lug soles, designed for biting into dirt and gravel, will slip off these wet, algae-filmed brick rounds like skates on ice.
*   **Do not wear leather boots.** The constant puddle-stepping, weeping walls, and high humidity will rot the stitching within forty-eight hours, and leather will never dry in this air.
*   **Recommended:** **Canyoneering boots** or **sticky-rubber approach shoes** with felt or specialized high-grip rubber outsoles (similar to those used by fly-fishermen on river rocks). You need a shoe that acts like a suction cup on wet, mineral-slick surfaces. 
*   **The Crab Problem:** The floor is alive with thousands of orange river crabs. You will inevitably step on them. Their shells are tough, and when crushed, they are incredibly greasy and slippery. You need a shoe with a fully enclosed, reinforced toe cap to protect your toes from the pinches of startled crabs, and you must expect your soles to become coated in a paste of crushed orange chitin and wet lime. Bring a stiff wire brush to clean your soles during rest periods.

---

### 2. Clothing: Managing the Warm Wet

The Room sits above a geothermal vein. The air temperature is a steady, suffocating $82^\circ\text{F}$ ($28^\circ\text{C}$) with $100\%$ humidity. It feels like the inside of a laundry room where someone is boiling turnip greens.

*   **The Outer Layer:** You will want a light, highly durable, water-resistant but breathable shell. A thin, ripstop nylon utility jacket is ideal. The walls are narrow and everything is within arm's reach; you *will* brush against rough mortar, corroded bronze pegs, and rotting cedar walkways. Your clothes must be tough enough to resist snagging on rusted iron pipe hoops.
*   **The Underlayer:** Synthetics or merino wool. Under no circumstances wear cotton. Cotton will absorb the weeping condensation, the tallow soot, and the crab-muck, holding it against your skin until you develop severe chafing and fungal rot. 
*   **Headwear:** An **oilskin or waxed-canvas wide-brimmed hat** is non-negotiable. 
    *   *Why?* The ceiling is a forest of hanging, greasy roots, dripping lime-water (which can burn your eyes), and roosting bats. A brimmed hat keeps these dripping fluids, bat droppings, and the occasional falling slug from landing in your hair or running down your neck.

---

### 3. Sensory Mitigation: The Wall of Sound and Smell

The Room is an acoustic nightmare. The *ping-ping* of leaks on bronze, the *slap* of water, the clicking of crab legs, and the high-pitched, liquid screeching of thousands of hairless bats will wear your mind down within hours.

*   **Ear Protection:** Bring **high-fidelity acoustic earplugs** (such as those worn by musicians or motorcyclists). These will muffle the overwhelming, clattering din—which can approach 85 decibels in the narrow vaults—without completely robbing you of your situational awareness. You still need to hear if a rotten cedar joist is cracking beneath you.
*   **Olfactory Shielding:** The smell is a heavy, greasy cocktail of wet clay, old mutton-tallow, yeast-like decay, and bat guano. It is thick enough to taste. 
    *   Apply a small smear of **mentholated ointment (like Vicks VapoRub) or eucalyptus oil** just beneath your nostrils. This will mask the greasy, organic stench of the room's slow, wet metabolism.
    *   Bring a few lightweight neck gaiters to pull up over your mouth and nose to block drifting spores from the white velvet mold and liver-colored lichens.

---

### 4. Illumination: Navigating the Steamy Gloom

The light-wells provide a beautiful, dusty gray daylight, but only directly beneath the shafts. The rest of the room is a world of deep, moving shadows cast by the massive brick pillars and the guttering orange glare of the tallow cups.

*   **Avoid High-Lumen, Blue-White LEDs:** The air is thick with mist, steam, and drifting spores. A powerful, cold-white flashlight will simply bounce off the vapor, creating a blinding white wall in front of your eyes (like high beams in a heavy fog).
*   **Use Warm, Diffused Light:** A headlamp with a **warm-neutral LED (3000K or lower)** or a yellow filter is far superior. It cuts through the steam without scattering, rendering the red brick and orange crabs in natural tones and helping you judge depth on the slippery steps.
*   **Red Light Mode:** Keep your headlamp on a red light or ultra-low setting when looking up into the ceiling vaults. Direct, bright white light will panic the hairless bats, causing thousands of them to drop from their roosts, chittering and blind, directly into your face.

---

### 5. Movement and Pathfinding

*   **Test the Cedar Walkways:** The cedar catwalks are lashed together with hemp ropes that have been rotting in the moist heat for decades. 
    *   *Never* step onto a wooden plank without testing it with your weight first.
    *   Keep your hands free. Do not carry anything in your hands if you can avoid it.
    *   If you must use a walking aid, use a **trekking pole with a soft, sticky rubber tip**. A metal spike will skitter off the tiles and cause you to lose your balance.
*   **Avoid the Sluice Gates:** The copper sluice gates are under immense hydraulic pressure from the secret workings of the room. Do not touch the bronze pegs or lean on the iron-banded ceramic pipes. A single leaky joint can spray a jet of scalding, mineral-heavy water with enough force to pierce skin.

---

### 6. Medical and Hygiene Considerations

In this warm, wet, organic incubator, any cut or scratch will infect with terrifying speed.

*   **The First-Aid Kit:** You must carry a waterproof dry-bag containing:
    *   **Isopropyl alcohol wipes** or chlorhexidine.
    *   **Liquid bandage (skin glue).** Standard adhesive bandages will peel off within minutes due to the humidity and sweat.
    *   **Broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment.**
    *   Any scratch from a copper edge or a pinch from a crayfish must be immediately washed, disinfected, and sealed with liquid bandage.
*   **Water and Rations:** Do not drink the water pooling in the brick pans, no matter how clear it looks. It is packed with lime carbonates, bat waste, and crab runoff. Bring your own water in hydration bladder systems that are completely sealed. Pack salty, dry rations; anything soft or bready will turn to mush in your pockets within an hour.

### What to Expect: The Experience

When you step into the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts, the world will shrink to thirty paces in any direction. The air will feel like a warm, wet wool blanket pressed against your face. Your ears will immediately ring with a dry, mechanical *scritch-scratch*—the sound of ten thousand tiny crab feet marching over endless, wet, red clay. 

The light-wells will look like solid pillars of gray-green glass cutting through the orange soot-light of the lamps. You will feel remarkably small, not because the room is large, but because it is so packed with ancient, indifferent life. Everything here has a job, from the mold on the beams to the blind crayfish in the pans, and none of them care that you have arrived.

Keep your head down, watch your feet, don't shine your light at the ceiling, and tread with the slow, deliberate care of someone walking on a roof of wet tile.
