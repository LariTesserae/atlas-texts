To visit this world is to step inside a planetary engine that has been flooded to the brim. It is a place of sterile majesty, staggering scale, and utter indifference to terrestrial life. Because there is no dry land, no air, and no warmth, an unprepared human will die here in under three minutes—not from monsters or magic, but from the simple, physics-based hostility of cold, deep water. 

To survive and appreciate this illuminated iron cathedral, an ordinary human traveler must prepare with the precision of a deep-sea saturation diver and the spatial awareness of an astronaut.

Here is your comprehensive preparation guide for the Flooded Cage.

---

### 1. Life Support: The Pressure and the Chill  

You cannot visit this world in a standard wetsuit or even a standard recreational scuba kit. The water is a constant **40°F (4.4°C)**. At this temperature, water saps body heat 25 times faster than air of the same temperature. Within fifteen minutes in a standard suit, your fingers would lose all dexterity; within an hour, hypothermia would stop your heart. Furthermore, because this world is miles deep, the hydrostatic pressure increases by 1 atmosphere (about 14.7 psi) for every 33 feet you descend. 

**The Recommended Vessel: An Atmospheric Diving Suit (ADS) or Micro-Submersible**
*   **The Atmospheric Suit:** Do not use soft drysuits unless you plan to hover exclusively within the top 100 feet of the ceiling. Instead, procure an **Atmospheric Diving Suit (such as a hard-shelled Nuytco Exosuit or a modified WASP suit)**. These are essentially one-man, human-shaped spaceships built of cast aluminum. They maintain an internal pressure of 1 atmosphere, meaning you will not have to worry about decompression sickness (the bends), nitrogen narcosis, or the crushing weight of the deep water.
*   **Thermal Protection:** Equip your suit’s internal cabin with an active, battery-powered heated undergarment system. Do not rely on passive insulation like wool; the cold steel of your suit will act as a massive heat sink, drawing warmth out of your air supply.
*   **Gas Mixtures:** If you *must* use a flexible drysuit for maneuverability, you will need a closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) running **Trimix** (helium, nitrogen, and oxygen) to avoid oxygen toxicity and gas narcosis at depth. You will also need to plan for days, perhaps weeks, of decompression before returning to your home portal. The ADS is vastly superior.

### 2. Mobility: Traversing the Great Voids

The distance between the vertical columns is roughly **fifteen miles**. A human swimming at average speed would take over seven hours of constant, exhausting effort to cross a single "square" of the grid. If your thrusters fail in the center of a grid square, you will be suspended in a featureless blue void, miles from any handhold.

**Propulsion and Tethering Gear:**
*   **Industrial Diver Propulsion Vehicles (DPVs):** Do not rely on your legs. You need a high-speed, dual-propeller underwater scooter mounted to your suit or held in your hands. Ensure it has a high-capacity lithium-ion battery rated for low-temperature environments.
*   **Electromagnetic Clamps and Anchors:** The world is made of steel. Carry heavy-duty, switchable electromagnets (mag-boots or mag-clamps) on your suit’s arms and utility belt. If you get caught in a sudden draft, these magnets will allow you to instantly anchor yourself to a structural girder, a floor plate, or a ceramic tile seam. 
*   **Acoustic Positioning System (APS):** GPS does not work underwater. You must bring a transceiver that can ping the steel columns and calculate your exact three-dimensional coordinates based on the time it takes for the sound to bounce back.

### 3. Acoustic Defense: Surviving the Planet-Machine

The quiet of this world is an illusion of the ears. Water is an incredibly efficient conductor of sound, transmitting acoustic waves four times faster than air. The mechanical thrumming, the high-pressure valve releases, and the monstrous *clack* of hydraulic pistons are not just loud—they represent massive physical energy.

*   **Vibro-Acoustic Protection:** The low-frequency hum of the 50-mile-distant turbines (*thrum-shudder*) will literally vibrate your lungs, liver, and bladder inside your body. Over several hours, this can cause severe nausea, disorientation, internal tissue bruising, and anxiety. Your diving suit must be lined with **viscoelastic acoustic dampening tiles** (similar to those used on the interiors of military submarines) to absorb these low-frequency rumbles before they reach your bones.
*   **Bone-Conduction Communications & Active Noise Suppression:** Standard earplugs are useless because sound will bypass your outer ear and vibrate the bones of your skull directly. You must wear integrated cranial dampeners that counteract the planetary vibrations, alongside a bone-conduction communication system so you can hear your own instruments.

### 4. Vision: Navigating the Sodium Glare

The illumination here is brutal, clinical, and sodium-yellow. Massive arrays of high-intensity floodlights are mounted directly to the steel pillars, casting a hard, unyielding glare across miles of clear water. 

*   **Anti-Glare polarized Visors:** The water behaves like liquid crystal under these lights. To prevent snow-blindness (or "water-blindness"), your suit’s viewport must be treated with a high-grade polarizing filter. Bring a visor with a transition tint that can shift rapidly from dark amber (when near a floodlight array) to clear (when exploring the darker shadow-zones beneath structural plates).
*   **High-Power Green Lasers:** Due to the extreme clarity of the water, a high-wattage green laser pointer will cast a visible beam for hundreds of yards. This is your best tool for pointing out structural details to companions or marking your path back to a portal.

### 5. Tactical Hazards: The Drafts and the Wildlife

While there are no monsters here, the physical forces of this world can kill you by accident, and the local wildlife—though normal—presents unique challenges.

*   **Recognizing "Drafts":** The oceans of this world are moved by giant, automated intake pumps. If you find yourself in a current that suddenly increases in speed, **do not fight it.** Assess the direction. If the bladderwrack and hair-algae on the floor plates are bent flat and shivering, you are in a draft. Immediately descend to the floor plates or move to a vertical truss and engage your electromagnetic anchors. If you are sucked into an intake grate, the pressure differential will instantly crush your suit.
*   **Wildlife Protocol:** 
    *   **The Cod and Herring:** Enormous schools of these fish, numbering in the millions, can block out the sodium lights entirely. If a school begins to turn rapidly, the collective displacement of their bodies can create a localized current that can spin a diver out of control. Stay clear of the center of these migrations.
    *   **The Sharks and Squid:** The dogfish and sleek sharks here are wild, curious, and hungry. They are not accustomed to humans or metal suits. They may mistake the soft hum of your suit's electronics or thrusters for the bio-electric field of an injured fish. Keep a passive **shark shield** (an electromagnetic field generator that irritates sharks’ sensitive ampullae of Lorenzini) active on your rig at all times. If a curious common squid clings to your viewport, do not panic; simply activate your suit's exterior LED flash to startle its sensitive eyes and force it to release its grip.

### Psychological Survival: The Agoraphobia of the Grid

Perhaps the hardest part of visiting the Flooded Cage is the psychological toll. 

There is an eerie, cosmic loneliness to a place that is so meticulously built, yet totally devoid of human purpose. The world is a cage, but it is too big to feel like one. When you are floating in the center of a fifteen-mile empty cell, with a mile of clear water below you, three miles above you, and nothing but yellow light and distant, humming steel on the horizon, you will experience a profound sense of insignificant isolation. 

To combat this, **bring a tethered companion.** Do not explore this world alone. Keep a constant, rhythmic monologue going over your comms. Look at the yellow serial numbers stenciled on the columns—remind yourself that someone, or something, built this place with a blueprint. You are not lost in the infinite void of space; you are simply walking through the pipes of an incredibly grand house.
