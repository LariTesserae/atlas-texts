To visit the Phloem Basin is to step inside a slow, wet lung. It is a world that requires a shift in your physical rhythm—a dampening of your stride, a lowering of your voice, and a deep tolerance for constant, cool humidity.

An ordinary human traveler should prepare not for danger, but for a profound, cloistered absorption. Here is how you should prepare, pack, and adjust your body for a stay in the Basin.

---

### 1. Wardrobe: The Rule of "Wet-Cold" and Quiet Soles
The atmosphere in the Basin is "cellar-cool"—hovering around 52°F (11°C) with nearly 100% humidity. Standard modern outdoor gear (like crinkly nylon windbreakers or squeaky Gore-Tex) is highly discouraged. The acoustic dampening of the wood makes the rustle of synthetic fabrics sound like a chainsaw.

*   **Footwear:** Leave hiking boots with heavy vibram lugs at home. They will chew up the ancient, delicate peat floor and slip dangerously on the copper-and-iron catwalks. You need soft-soled boots—either high-quality oiled leather with crepe-rubber soles or traditional vulcanized deck shoes. They must be quiet, water-resistant, and flexible enough to grip bouncing, wet iron slats.
*   **The Base Layer:** Merino wool is your best friend. It retains heat even when saturated with moisture, and it resists the mild, yeasty smell of decomposing leaf-mulch that permeates the lower tiers. 
*   **The Outer Layer:** Oilskin or waxed cotton. A long duster or a heavy, hip-length jacket in dark colors (to hide grease and latex stains) is perfect. The wax coating will shrug off the five-minute "room-rain" and keep the dripping moisture from the ceiling fronds from soaking through to your woolens.
*   **The "Rain Hat":** A wide-brimmed, stiff leather or oilskin hat is mandatory. The droplets falling from the ceiling are fat and heavy; they fall eighty feet and hit with a physical thwack. A hood ruins your peripheral hearing (which you need to listen for the creaks of the catwalks), but a wide-brimmed hat keeps the water off your neck while keeping your ears clear.

### 2. Gear: Fighting the Moisture
In the Basin, nothing stays dry on its own. 

*   **Dry Bags:** Bring heavy-duty, submersible roll-top bags for your electronics, spare dry clothes, and papers. If you leave a notebook open for an hour, the pages will swell, turn soft as felt, and run your ink.
*   **Writing Instruments:** Bring pressurized ballpoint pens (like space pens) or soft 4B pencils. Fountain pens and standard gel pens will bleed into the damp paper instantly.
*   **Lamps:** The daylight is a dim, submarine green, shifting to amber. It is beautiful but lacks the blue-spectrum light humans need to keep their circadian rhythms balanced. Bring a high-intensity, warm-spectrum yellow lantern. Do not bring cold blue LEDs; they are jarring to the residents and make the purple-grey wood walls look deathly pale.
*   **The "Grease Rag":** Carry a small, lint-free cloth tucked into your belt. The handrails of the catwalks are often slick with condensation or a thin film of refined latex oil from the pumps. You will find yourself wanting to wipe your hands constantly.

### 3. Physical Conditioning and Sensory Adjustment
The physical reality of the Basin will affect your body in specific ways.

*   **The "Catwalk Sway":** The tiers of homes and workshops are suspended on brackets driven into the cambium. When you walk on the catwalks, they bounce. If two people are walking toward each other, a rhythmic, springy wobble develops. You must learn to walk with slightly bent knees, absorbing the movement of the iron with your joints rather than fighting it. Imagine walking on a ship’s deck in light swells.
*   **The Acoustic Trap:** The lack of echo in the Basin is psychologically disorienting. Humans use micro-echoes to map space unconsciously. In the Basin, sound dies three feet from your mouth. The sudden silence can cause a mild form of sensory-deprivation anxiety or a feeling of "stuffy ears." Spend your first afternoon sitting quietly on a peat bench to let your brain adjust to the dead air.
*   **The Deep Vibration:** When the transpiration pumps kick on in the evening, you will not just hear the *chuff-thump*; you will feel it in your teeth and your shins. The vibration travels through the peat floor and up your skeletal structure. For some, this is soothing, like a giant heartbeat; for others, it causes mild nausea or headache. Bring ginger lozenges or motion-sickness bands if you are sensitive to low-frequency vibrations.

### 4. Navigating the Rhythms of the Tree
The inhabitants do not watch clocks; they watch the walls. You must learn to read the environment.

*   **The Sap-Tide (Twice Daily):**
    *   **The Warning:** You will hear a series of deep, resonant groans—like a wooden galleon turning in a storm—as the giant cambium swells. The copper pressure gauges on the walls will begin to whistle softly as steam and latex build up.
    *   **What to do:** *Stop whatever you are doing.* Do not attempt to cross high catwalks during the height of the tide; the cambium shifts slightly, drawing the iron cables taut and making the bridges stiff and unstable. Find a bench, sit down, and mimic the locals: pull your coat around you, close your eyes, and let the tree "breathe."
*   **The Room-Rain:**
    *   **The Warning:** Immediately after the sap-tide peaks, a cool fog will roll down from the ceiling fronds. The air will smell intensely of sliced cucumber and damp stone.
    *   **What to do:** Cover your gear. Find a doorway or stand close to the curved cambium walls under one of the carved niches. Within a minute, a heavy, vertical drizzle will begin. It lasts exactly five minutes, clearing the air of coal-smoke and steam, before the floor drinks it all down.

### 5. Social Etiquette in the Hollow
With only thirty households, everyone knows everyone, and resources are highly conserved.

*   **Speak in the "Basin Register":** Never shout. If you need to get someone’s attention fifty feet away, do not yell their name—the sound will muffled and distorted anyway, sounding like a bark. Instead, use a sharp, rhythmic whistle or wait until you are closer. Speak in a quiet, conversational tone; because the wood absorbs background clutter, people can hear soft speech remarkably well if they are close.
*   **Respect the Gasket-Beetles:** These flat, bronze-shelled creatures are the unsung janitors of the Basin. If you see one slow-crawling across an iron gear or a copper pipe, do not disturb it. If one is on a handrail where you need to grip, gently nudge its rear with a finger; it will slowly, blindly crawl down to the underside of the pipe out of your way. Stepping on one is considered a major social offense—akin to breaking a neighbor's window.
*   **Gifts to Bring:** The residents have plenty of water, latex, simple starches (from the edible fungi), and wood. What they lack are dry, pungent sensory luxuries. Bring tins of dry, black tea, whole black peppercorns, dried chili peppers, or small tins of salt (which is hard to come by in a purely vegetable ecosystem). High-quality sewing needles and beeswax (which doesn't rancidify in the damp) make excellent currency.
