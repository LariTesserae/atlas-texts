To step onto the Plateau of Olaus is to voluntarily enter a monument of deep geological time. It is a place that does not actively hostile to human life, but rather utterly indifferent to it. The scale is so massive and the environment so quiet that an unprepared traveler can easily be crushed by the silence, lost in the wet corridors, or succumb to the slow, steady draw of hypothermia. 

To visit Olaus, an ordinary human must prepare not for a hike or a climb, but for a slow, damp immersion into a labyrinth of stone and water. Here is how you must prepare.

---

### 1. Physiological Preparation: The Thin, Cold Air
Because the plateau sits at a massive elevation where the air is bright and thin, your body will face immediate oxygen deprivation.

*   **Altitude Acclimatization:** If you have the luxury of time, spend at least a week at high altitude (above 3,000 meters) before transitioning to Olaus. If you are stepping directly through a portal, you must take a regime of **acetazolamide (Diamox)** starting 48 hours prior to arrival to assist your kidneys in compensating for the thin air.
*   **Supplemental Oxygen:** Carry a compact, lightweight oxygen concentrator or a few canisters of compressed O₂. You will find that navigating the slot canyons—where you must constantly shimmy, press your body against stone, and hoist yourself over weirs—is exhausting. In the thin air, lactic acid will build up rapidly.
*   **UV Protection:** The sun at this altitude is brutal, and its radiation is doubled by the reflection off the wet, polished basalt and the standing pools of water. Bring high-altitude glaciology sunglasses (with side shields) to prevent snow-blindness (or in this case, "wet-stone blindness") and apply a mineral-based, water-resistant block to any exposed skin.

---

### 2. Clothing and Armor: The "Weeping" Gear
Olaus is a world of constant condensation. You will not get rained on from above, but you will be constantly kissed, brushed, and dripped upon by the stone itself. Normal hiking gear will fail here; you need the gear of a professional sport-caver or a canyoner.

*   **The Outer Layer (The Drysuit):** Do not wear breathable rain jackets like standard Gore-Tex; the constant pressure of leaning against wet basalt walls will force water through the membrane. Instead, wear a light, abrasion-resistant **canyoning drysuit** with latex seals at the neck and wrists. The basalt is unyielding and rough; the suit must have reinforced Cordura or Kevlar panels on the elbows, knees, and seat.
*   **The Insulation:** Beneath the drysuit, wear a high-loft synthetic fleece or wool one-piece undergarment. Avoid down entirely; if it gets wet from a tear in your outer shell, it will clump and lose all insulating value, and in the cool plateau air, hypothermia will set in within hours.
*   **Footwear for the Slick-Stone:** The flat mats of liver-colored moss and crustose lichen are incredibly slick when wet. Standard hiking boots with hard-rubber soles will cause you to slip constantly. You need boots designed for wet-rock scrambling—specifically, **canyoning boots** with sticky, soft-compound rubber soles (like Vibram Megagrip or Stealth rubber) that can grip wet, polished basalt. They should have drainage ports, as you will spend hours wading through ankle-deep silt and pools.

---

### 3. Mobility and Packing: Squeezing Through the Labyrinth
The "streets" of Olaus are often narrow slot canyons, barely wider than a human shoulder. A traditional backpack is your enemy; it will wedge you between two towers of basalt, leaving you stuck.

*   **The Drag-Bag:** Do not wear a backpack. Use a cylindrical, waterproof **caving tackle bag** (a "drag-bag") made of heavy-duty PVC. It should have a single, low-profile shoulder strap and a haul loop at the top. When the canyon narrows, you slip the bag off and drag it behind you or push it ahead of you.
*   **Hands-Free Lighting:** By mid-afternoon, the rising mists form a thick ceiling on the tower tops, turning the slot canyons into dark, eerie tunnels. You must have a powerful, high-IPX-rated (waterproof) headlamp mounted to a lightweight climbing helmet. The helmet is crucial; the slot canyons have low-hanging, water-rounded ledges, and one slip on the moss could send your head against solid basalt.

---

### 4. Sustenance: Purifying the Basin Water
There are no plants to forage, and no animals to hunt. You are entirely dependent on what you bring.

*   **The Water Problem:** The water of Olaus is beautiful—derived from pure condensation—but it sits in ancient, silt-bottomed fens. Walking through it kicks up incredibly fine silt that will quickly ruin standard pump filters.
    *   Bring a **gravity-style water filter** with a hollow-fiber membrane, but pair it with a pre-filter (like a coffee filter or a fine mesh) to catch the silt first.
    *   Be aware that since this is an ancient geological landscape with no active soil biome, the water may contain high concentrations of dissolved minerals or heavy metals washed from the basalt over epochs. Bring carbon-filter inserts to strip out mineral tastes and potential toxins.
*   **Caloric Efficiency:** Because your body will be fighting both the cold, damp climate and the altitude, you will burn calories at twice your normal rate. Pack high-density, high-fat foods that do not require cooking (which is difficult in a wet, windless environment with no wood). Pemmican, nut butters, and dense energy bars are ideal.

---

### 5. Navigation: Solving the Continental Grid
Because the plateau of Olaus is a gargantuan, repetitive grid of geometric towers, it is incredibly easy to lose your sense of direction. Everything looks like a street; everything looks like a temple; everything looks the same.

*   **The Magnetic Failure:** Basalt is highly magnetic/ferruginous. A traditional magnetic compass will spin erratically or point falsely toward the nearest tower. 
*   **Inertial and Visual Navigation:** Use an **inertial navigations system (INS)**—a device that tracks your steps and turns relative to your starting point without relying on satellites or magnetic fields.
*   **Leave No Trace, but Leave a Pathway:** Because the landscape is so pristine, do not write on the stone or use permanent spray paint. Instead, carry **highly reflective, non-adhesive vinyl trail markers** (weighted with small lead shot) that you can drop at intersections and retrieve on your way back. If you must leave a breadcrumb, use a UV-reactive liquid spray that will wash away in a few weeks under the weeping walls but will glow brightly under your headlamp's UV setting.

---

### 6. Psychological Preparation: Embracing the Lithic Silence
The silence of Olaus is not a peaceful quiet; it is a heavy, pressurized silence that presses against the eardrums. The only sounds are the *tink-plip* of water and the occasional squelch of gas from the silt. For a human brain wired for constant sensory input, this can induce auditory hallucinations, paranoia, and severe vertigo.

*   **The Sound of Your Own Body:** Within hours, the silence will make you hyper-aware of your own heartbeat, the rushing of blood in your ears, and the squeak of your joints. Prepare for this. Some travelers bring bone-conduction headphones to play soft, low-frequency pink noise or familiar ambient sounds to keep their brains from fabricating voices in the dripping water.
*   **The Melancholy of the Absent Builders:** The scale of the city—resembling a metropolis but made entirely of geological-scale wet stone—will evoke a profound sense of loneliness. There are no ghosts here; there is not even the memory of a tool. You must go in with a meditative, stoic mindset. Accept that you are a temporary, fleshy spark of warmth in a cold, eternal cathedral of water and stone.

---

### A Recommended Landing Protocol:
When you first step onto the edge of the plateau of Olaus, do not immediately descent into the canyons. 

Sit on the rim. Let your lungs adjust to the thin, cold air. Watch the sun hit the dark basalt. Listen to the micro-acoustic chorus of the dripping walls. Let the vast ness of the continental grid settle into your mind. Only when your heart rate has slowed and your boots have found their grip on the wet, yellow lichen should you slip into the narrow shadows of the towers, descending into a city that was old before your ancestors learned to shape clay.
