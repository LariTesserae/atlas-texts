The Attention did not fall upon the plateau like a falling star, nor did it break through the charcoal clouds with a crown of unwanted noon. To bring light to this place would be an act of violence. To blind the eyeless, to dry the paraffin pools with solar wind, to halt the great, rhythmic grinding of the basalt—these are the impulses of a power that can only love its own image. 

The Power that looked down was old enough to know that a world must be met in its own tongue. And the tongue of this world was gravity, heat, and the long, low song of steel.

So, the Attention became a weightiness. It descended into the damp, warm gloom, settling over the high tables of weathered basalt like a secondary atmosphere, cool and infinitely patient. It listened. 

For three hundred years of the plateau's slow time, the Power did nothing but learn the rhythm. It felt the three-hundred-ton piston-strokes every twenty minutes; it tracked the migration of the slide-grubs along the slag-lines; it memorized the dry, clicking language of the Echo-Children’s iron-wood staffs. It saw that this was not a place of suffering, but of a terrible, beautiful economy. Every scrap of grease was reused; every death was a continuation of the road.

Yet, there was friction. And where there is friction, there is eventual silence.

The Power saw that the *Great Striders* were dying. It was a death of centuries, but it was inevitable. The green-black grease in their joints was thick with crushed stone; their boilers were pitted with acid-burns from the water-grease; their iron skin was thinning where the basalt ground them down. When the last giant seized and grew cold, the Echo-Children would follow them into the dark. The wire-reeds would go silent. The hush-cutters would starve in the windless plains.

The first change occurred deep within the basalt itself. 

The Power did not reach down with hands to rebuild the machines. Instead, it reached into the roots of the world, five miles beneath the flat tables of stone. There, in the slow, boiling chambers of the earth's mantle, it altered the chemistry of the basalt. It sowed the rock with microscopic veins of self-lubricating graphite and soft, silvering talc. 

When the Striders grazed, their massive crushing-plates no longer met only the jagged, screaming resistance of pure iron-ore. They found the rock slightly softer, yielding its minerals with a smoother, more fluid grace. The dust they left behind was no longer sharp and dry like iron filings; it was soft as soot, mixed with microscopic flakes of silver-lead that polished the Striders' heavy feet even as they trod upon it.

Slowly, over decades, the *groan like a winter gale* changed. 

The Echo-Children were the first to feel it through the soles of their slate-grey feet. The deep grinding of their bonded giant—that familiar, harsh fifth—had lost its ragged edge. It was rounder now. The vibration that traveled up their legs and into the discs of their faces was no longer an ache; it was a steady, resonant thrum that sat in the bone like a warm drink.

Then came the change to the water.

The stagnant, paraffin-rimmed pools in the basalt depressions did not turn to sweet, blue spring water—for such water would have rotted the leather-wings’ skin and killed the soot-moss. Instead, the water-grease became purer. The natural paraffin that skimmed the surface began to form a clean, white wax that smelt of pine resin and cold salt, rather than scorched tallow. 

When the Echo-Children drank, they found their throats cleared of the constant, abrasive ash. Their skin, rubbed with this new, clean tallow, became supple, resisting the wind-burn of the chasm-gales without cracking into painful, red fissures.

But the greatest gift of the Attention was reserved for the path.

In the second century of the quiet watch, an old Echo-Child, whose face-disc had grown stiff and silent with age, laid himself down in the path of their Strider. His band stood upon the high, grease-slicked scaffolding, their hands pressed flat to the cold-iron flank of the machine. They waited for the shudder. They waited for the crunch of bone that would merge their brother with the road.

The colossal iron foot rose. It lingered for a moment in the warm, heavy air—not in hesitation, for the machine was blind and simple—but because the steam-valve had released its pressure with an unusual, sighing softness. 

The foot came down.

But there was no shudder. No hard, wet crack. 

The Power had touched the moment of contact. As the iron sole pressed the body of the old man into the basalt, a sudden, cold-blue luminescence sparked beneath the foot—a silent heatless fire that flared for three seconds in the everlasting dark. The body did not grind into raw dust; it *transmuted*. The minerals of his bones and the grease of his skin bonded instantly with the silver-lead of the basalt, hardening into a smooth, shining vein of blue-grey stone that lay perfectly flat across the rough tableland.

And up on the scaffolding, the band felt a change in the machine's heartbeat.

The twenty-minute stroke came. The three-hundred-ton piston dropped. But as it hit the bottom of its stroke, the metal did not emit its flat, iron *clink*. 

It sang.

It was a low, hollow bell-note—a sound like a great bronze bowl struck underwater. The frequency matched precisely the unique clicking rhythm the old man had used with his iron-wood staff when he was young and could guide the band through the ash-storms. 

The Echo-Children did not have eyes to cry, but their face-discs flared, their cilia trembling in the warm exhaust. They pressed their flat palms harder against the iron skin. 

Beneath their fingers, the metal was not just vibrating; it was remembering. Every time the Strider took a step upon the path, the blue-grey vein of the old man's resting place hummed in response, returning a fraction of his lifeforce to the machine's joints as a low, friction-reducing frequency.

The dead were no longer just road. They were the oil. They were the lubrication that kept the giants from dying.

The Power did not stay to be worshiped. It did not leave a book, or a statue, or a name. It simply faded back into the high sky, leaving the plateau to its dark, iron-hearted peace.

But now, the plateau is different. 

The air is still heavy with peat and iron, and the sky is still the color of cold charcoal. The *Hush-Cutter* still glides silently through the shadows of the slide-grubs, and the *Iron-Weaver* still spins its glass-reed nets in the high vents. 

But when the great machines walk, they do not groan. They sing in three-part harmony across the five-thousand-mile tables. Each giant carries the voices of a thousand buried generations in its gears, its pistons rising and falling not with the heavy, dying effort of old iron, but with the light, swinging rhythm of a dance that will never have to end.

And in the warm, soot-choked spaces beneath the steam-manifolds, the Echo-Children sleep. They are still thin, still blind, still covered in grease and dust. But as they rest their heads against the iron beams, they hear the names of their grandfathers whispered in the click of the valves, and they know that the world they walk upon is not empty, and that the road beneath them is listening.
