The power that found them was not a god of light and brass, nor was it a gardener who wished to turn the charcoal-grey plateau into a field of clover. It was a power that had spent its own youth among the heavy, cold silences of the early universe, and it understood the dignity of things that had to hold on with their teeth. 

It did not descend with a roar of trumpets. A trumpet would have been torn to pieces by the five-mile gale, and its noise would have been nothing but a thin squeak against the three-mile fluting of the Spires. 

Instead, the power sat in the cold, blue-grey air between Tower Four and Tower Five, invisible as a pocket of warm oil, and it *listened*.

It listened to the *chug-shudder* of the sap-pumps and the dry *tap-tap* of the scutter-beetles. It felt the tension in the iron pins driven six feet into the fossilized bark, and it felt the ache in the broad, oil-soaked shoulders of the Slack-Men who hung from the under-bellies of the hanging towns. It saw how the Smear-folk slowly lost their outlines, rubbed away by the wind like charcoal drawings on rough paper.

The power was wise, and so it did not perform the cheap miracle of flattening the world or warming the air. It knew that if it made the ground soft and filled the air with sweet garden scents, the Spire-dwellers would die of a broken shape. Their skin was slate-grey because they loved the iron-wood; their boots had lead notches because they had learned how to dance with the lazy, heavy gravity. To take away their verticality would be to kill them as surely as dropping them into the three-thousand-foot drop.

So, the power worked sideways, through small adjustments in the physics of the Vast. It left five gifts—not written in stone, but woven into the grease, the iron, and the stubborn light.

***

### The First Gift: The Air-Cradle

The most terrible thing about the lazy gravity was its patience. A slack-man who lost his harness, or a gantry-child who missed her heel-hook, did not die quickly. They fell for nearly three minutes through the grey drift-fog, watching the cold, green-crusted walls of the tower slide upward past them, knowing that the earth at the bottom would not be lard when they struck it, but hard as basalt.

The power did not change the gravity, but it changed the *recognition* of the air.

Now, when a living, breathing thing falls into the void, the cold, grease-steam air does not remain indifferent. It thickens. As the falling person passes the one-thousand-foot mark, the air begins to gather beneath them like a cold, wet fleece. It does not stop them instantly—which would break their bones—but it forms a "chute" of dense, cold thermals that slows them to a long, slow glide.

A fallen child now tumbles into the lower mists of the plateau like a dry leaf. They land upon the silver-green lichen with a sound no louder than a dropped woollen coat. There, the Ground-Gliders find them, sitting in their anchor-harnesses, cold and mute with terror, but unbroken. They are taken into the land-boats, fed on bitter peppermint-root, and returned to the Spires on the next peat-elevator.

The Spire-dwellers do not praise a god for this. They simply say: *"The air has grown thick-breathed today."*

***

### The Second Gift: The Preach-Note

The Slack-Men had always lived with the terror of the "cannon-shot"—the sudden, explosive snap of an iron cable that had been pulled too tight by the slow, creeping inches of the tower’s growth. When a cable snapped, forty homes would drop into the dark like coal-buckets.

The power did not stop the Spires from growing. To stop the growth would be to make the towers truly dead, and dead wood eventually rots, even in the high, cold dry.

Instead, the power placed a *chord* inside the petrified heartwood.

Now, twenty-four hours before any iron pin is about to slip, or any cable is about to reach its breaking tension, the three-mile-wide Spire does not remain silent. The wind passing through its hollow chambers begins to sing in a new, clean register. It is not the deep, bone-vibrating bass of the ordinary gale, but a clear, sorrowful fifth—a drone that the Slack-Men call the *Preach-Note*.

When the Preach-Note rises through the floorboards of the gantry-houses, the Slack-Men do not need to measure with their brass tape. They know exactly which tower is straining. They climb down into the under-belly, their beetle-hide coats smelling of vinegar-sweetness, and they turn the massive brass turnbuckles, releasing the tension in perfect time.

The wood still grows, but it now warns its children before it takes their homes.

***

### The Third Gift: The True-Shadows

The Smear-folk had always been a source of quiet grief in the Spires. To watch one’s father or grandmother slowly turn into a grey smudge, to see them leave behind nothing but a dark oil-stain on the birch-veneer wall that could be scrubbed away by a careless maid, was a lonely way to lose someone.

The power looked at the stubborn light of the plateau and gave it a memory.

Now, when a Smear-man dies, he does not become a smudge. His shadow, which has lagged behind his body for fifty years of boiler-work or watchman-duty, does not fade when his skin grows thin. Instead, as the man’s physical frame dissolves into the wintergreen-scented air, his last and most lingering shadow *sinks* into the heartwood, becoming a permanent wood-intaglio.

The birch-veneer walls of the high houses are now covered in the likenesses of the old. They are not flat stains; they are three-dimensional, silver-grey reliefs, carved into the wood-grain by the light itself. A granddaughter can run her long, slate-grey fingers over the wall and feel the exact shape of her grandfather’s knuckles, the curve of his fog-cups, and the set of his mouth. 

During the cold, misty nights, these wood-images glow with a very soft, phosphor-green chemical light—the same light as the Drift-Lace—warming the small rooms with the faces of those who held the Spires before them.

***

### The Fourth Gift: The Peppermint-Drift

The water harvested by the miles of copper hair was bitter, metallic, and tasted of the yellow grease-steam from the coal-boilers. The children of the Spires grew up with teeth stained yellow-green by the copper cisterns, and their throats were always dry from the vinegar-acid of the lichen-dust.

The power did not bring down rivers from the sky. There were no beds for them, and a river would have simply frozen into a five-mile icicle before it reached the plateau floor.

Instead, the power altered the Drift-Lace.

The colonial lace-organisms that floated in the five-mile voids began to change their diet. They no longer strained only the coal-soot and grease-steam from the chimneys; they began to absorb the deep, cold nitrogen from the high sky and convert it into a sweet, clean sugar.

Now, when the Drift-Lace brushes against the copper hairs of the cities during the heavy fog-rolling hours, it leaves behind a condensation that is not bitter. The water that runs down into the cisterns is cold, clear, and carries a faint, warming taste of wild wintergreen and peppermint. 

The children’s teeth have begun to lose their green stain. In the evenings, when the shift-whistle blows, the gantry-born fill their brass cups directly from the guide-wires, drinking a water that is as sweet as the low valleys they have never seen.

***

### The Fifth Gift: The Gland of the Scutter-Beetle

The Ground-Gliders, on their windswept charcoal plains, had always lived in silence. They were too heavy to speak in the sibilant clicks of the Spire-dwellers, and their long, iron-tipped poles required both hands to hold against the wind. They were lonely, separate men, gliding across the dark wood-crust with their sail-boats, looking only at the black peat beneath their boots.

The power touched the blind, silent scutter-beetles that lived on the hot steam-pipes of the lower tower walls.

It did not give them eyes—eyes would have been blinded by the coal-dust—but it gave them a small, chemical valve in their grey-green shells. When the beetles are warm and well-fed on boiler-grease, this valve secretes a tiny, hard amber bead that smells of toasted peat and summer oil.

The Ground-Gliders have learned to harvest these beads. They call them *Beetle-Coal*.

When a peat-harpooner is five miles out in the empty, whistling desert, far from the light of the towers, and his sail-boat is pinned by a sudden, freezing gale, he does not have to freeze in his trench. He places three beads of Beetle-Coal in a small brass box hung around his neck. The beads do not burn with a flame; they glow with a high, steady, internal heat that lasts for forty-eight hours, warming his chest and his hands through the thick leather of his harness.

With the Beetle-Coal against their hearts, the Ground-Gliders have stopped looking only at the ground. Sometimes, during the long wind-nights, a glider will sit on the deck of his sail-boat, held down by his heavy iron sledge, and look up at the orange nests of coal-light suspended five thousand feet above him. 

He does not fear the sky anymore. He knows that if a man falls from those tiny, sparkling stars, he will come down slowly, like a feather, and that he will be waiting there in his boat to catch him.

***

The benevolent power did not leave a name, nor did it ask for a chapel to be built in the hollows of the Spires. It simply gathered its attention and drifted outward, back into the deep, quiet spaces between the solar winds.

But behind it, on the high, iron-grey shelf of the world, the cities keep moving. The steam-tractors clank along the vine-wrapped cables; the Slack-Men hit their iron wires with copper clubs; and the great, five-mile gaps are no longer spaces of empty terror, but lanes of a long, cold, and now-companionable silence.
