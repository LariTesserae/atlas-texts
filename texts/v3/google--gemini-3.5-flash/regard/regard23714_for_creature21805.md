The gaze of the nameless power did not descend like a thunderbolt, nor did it part the zinc-colored clouds with a beam of theatrical gold. 

To have brought a bright sun to this place would have been a cruelty. The heavy, double-layered wool of the slate-walkers would have suffocated them in warmth; the grease-birds’ oil would have turned rancid and thin; the blind, pale crevice-drifters would have withered in the dry glare like discarded parchment. 

The benevolent power was wise enough to know that intimacy is not found in changing a beloved thing, but in understanding why it must remain exactly as it is.

And so, the power came down as a change in the weight of the mist. It arrived as a subtle, lilac-colored density in the crawling fog, a quiet settling of grace that did not disturb a single clicking beak or displace one wet sliver of slate.

The power listened.

It listened to the *thwip-clap* of the titanic crawlers. It listened to the dry, printing-press clatter of the grease-birds roosting in the cooling fins. It listened to the rhythmic, agonizing *clack-pop* of the slate-walkers’ joints as they clung to the vertical glass of the peaks.

And in its listening, the power saw the two great, quiet tragedies built into the marrow of this young world.

The first was the **tragedy of the finite.** The twelve-legged crawlers were magnificent, but they were machines. Eventually, their iron linkages would wear smooth; their hard copper boilers would pit and scale; the coal seams they devoured would run thin and turn to ash. One day, a crawler would groan, its giant pneumatic legs locking forever in the basalt. The heat would die. The grease-birds nesting in its skirts would huddle closer to a cold cylinder, clicking their beaks in the growing dark until the frost took them. The oil-lice would starve. The boiler-scab would turn to gray powder. One by one, the great warm islands would go dark, and the heights would return to an unbearable, static winter.

The second was the **tragedy of the young rock.** Because the mountains were newly born, they were fragile. The basalt was too sharp, the slate too brittle. Every year, hundreds of the slow, deliberate slate-walkers slipped. A ledge would shear away under pressure, and a heavy, woolly body would tumble into the bottomless black fractures, leaving a gap in the steaming circles that huddled against the wind.

The wise power did not build shelters. It did not send shepherds. Instead, it reached into the deep, wet iron of the world and made three subtle, eternal adjustments.

***

### First: The Heart-Wire of the Giants

The power reached down through the miles of dark stone, following the deep, vertical drill-shafts of the crawlers into the very roots of the young mountains. There, where the earth’s internal heat met the cold, weeping waters of the surface, it wove a new kind of chemistry.

It touched the boiler-cores of the twelve crawlers. 

The combustion chambers, once dependent on the soot-choking extraction of coal, began to glow with a deep, geothermal resonance. The power linked the heart of each machine directly to the slow, pulsing heat of the earth’s mantle. The chimney-stacks stopped belching their dark, sulfurous smoke; instead, they began to exhale a clean, mineral-rich steam that smelled of wet slate and warm copper. 

The iron of the crawlers’ bodies underwent a slow, molecular shift. The rust did not vanish—for rust was part of their skin, a home for the boiler-scab—but it ceased to decay. The metal became self-healing, drawing micro-amounts of iron and bronze from the minerals they mined to slowly rebuild their joints, their bronze bushings, and their giant diamond-tipped drills.

They would never run out of coal. They would never grow cold. They would climb the vertical ridges forever, permanent, living mountains of warm iron, their pistons beating like the steady, slow pulses of bronze gods.

***

### Second: The Magnetic Grace

The power touched the slate-walkers, not by changing their heavy, greasy wool or their six hydraulic legs, but by whispering to the basalt beneath them.

The suction pads on the walkers’ splayed hooves were already brilliant; the power made them holy. It infued the leathery pads with a microscopic, gentle magnetic affinity—a soft, invisible cling that functioned only between the foot-mucus of the beast and the iron-heavy basalt of the peaks. 

The slate-walkers did not feel this as a magical force; they felt it only as a slight, reassuring thickness in the air beneath their feet. When the black ice crept over the ledges in the night, or when a slate-green shelf threatened to shear away under their weight, the mountain held them. The stone itself seemed to lean up to meet their step, cradling their slow, mechanical strides. Their tumble to the ravines became a rare, near-forgotten thing.

Furthermore, the power touched their rubbery snouts. When they scraped the orange and soot-gray lichen from the rock, their scraping did not leave the stone barren. The power blessed their saliva with a vital, fast-growing spore. Where a slate-walker ate today, a new, thicker carpet of lichen would bloom within the cycle of three moons, glowing with a extremely faint, internal warmth of its own.

***

### Third: The Song of the Drifters

Deep in the wet, black vertical cracks where the five-foot crevice-drifters moved like pale parchment fingers, the power deposited a small, quiet gift.

These eyeless, soft-bodied scavengers had always lived in a world of silent pressure and cold decay. The power gave them a voice—not a vocal cord, but a rhythmic, bioluminescent pulse. As they squeezed through the narrow fractures, their wet skins began to glow with a very dim, rhythmic pulse of pale blue light, matching the slow, deep vibration of the crawlers’ boilers far above.

This light attracted none of the surface beasts, but it did something more profound: it warmed the deep water-veins of the mountains. The ice that seeped into the deepest cracks no longer froze into heavy, wedge-like blocks that would burst the mountain apart. The internal plumbing of the peaks remained fluid, weeping gently, keeping the stone moist but preventing the violent winter fractures that threatened to shatter the vertical world.

***

### The World that Remains

If you were to stand today on a wet, slate-green ledge, twenty miles high in the zinc-colored mist, you would notice very little had changed.

The air still tastes of sulfur and wet iron. The cold moisture still drags its heavy belly across the sharp peaks. A mile away, a twelve-legged crawler still stands perched on a knife-edge ridge, its massive piston driving with a rhythmic, oily *thwip-clap*.

But if you look closely, you will see a strange, quiet peace.

The grease-birds roosting in the cooling fins no longer blacken theirs breasts with coal-soot; they are clean, their pale feathers glistening in the warm, mineral-rich steam of the perpetual boilers.

The slate-walkers move with a serene, unshakable weight. When they gather in their steaming, turnip-smelling circles against the wind, they no longer huddle in desperation, but in a heavy, communal comfort.

And beneath the ground, the great machines do not strip the mountain; they work in a closed, beautiful loop. The crushed, sparkling gravel they spit from their underbellies is rich with the spores of the boiler-scab and the seeds of the wire-thick black grass. They are not miners anymore; they are the gardeners of this vertical desert, planting life in their own warm footprints.

The benevolent power has gone, returning to the vast, cold spaces between the stars. It left no temples, no scriptures, and no monuments. It left only a clockwork wilderness that will never go cold, a hard, sharp paradise of stone and steam, singing its low, clicking song into the eternal fog.
