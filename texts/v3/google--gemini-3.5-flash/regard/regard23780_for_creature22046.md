The benevolent and wise power does not look down from the sixty-foot height of the iron grates with pity, for pity is a shallow fluid that dissolves the dignity of those it touches. Nor does it look with anger at the builders of Basin Forty-Two, for the power knows that even the sharpest brick and the heaviest iron are only the clumsy gestures of infants trying to mark their names upon the dark.

Instead, the power looks with **attention**—which is the only true form of love the universe possesses.

It observes the pale-shelled river crabs, the soot-pickers, whose lives are small and circular like silver gears. It observes the orange grout-worms, whose slow, lime-eating travel is a silent liturgy of containment. It observes the man, Forty-Two, whose bare, wide toes know the basalt flagstones as an animal knows its run.

An unwise power would have broken the ceiling. It would have shattered the iron grates with a stroke of lightning, letting in the fierce, dry wind of the upper plains and the blue fury of the noon sun. But to do so would have killed the basin. The parboiled skin of Forty-Two would have cracked and peeled like birch bark in the dry draft; his swollen, light-sick eyes would have been burned to white ash; the sulfur moths would have vanished like oil drops on a stove; and the soft, milky-shelled crabs would have dried into empty husks within an hour. 

A wise power does not liberate by slaughter. It does not cure by displacement.

Therefore, the power descends.

It does not take the form of a great light, for there is already too much harsh glare from the wire-caged bulbs. It takes the form of a **coolness**. 

It enters through the southern brick wall, passing through the mortar joins where the grout-worms graze, not as a draft—for a draft would disturb the stagnant steam—but as a change in the weight of the water. For seven years, the boiler-rain has been heavy, greasy, and sour with coal-ash. Now, as the presence settles into the room, the water dripping from the cold iron ceiling becomes *sweet*.

It is still hot. It must be hot, for the basin’s life is a fever-life. But the steam no longer smells of sulfur and tallow. It begins to smell of wet cedar. It smells of the high forests after a summer storm, of moss that has grown on the north side of ancient oaks, of things that are damp not because they are trapped, but because they are deep.

Forty-Two is lying in his sling of tarred hemp behind the third condenser. His eyes are closed under their thick, protective lids. He is listening to the triple condensers, waiting for the high, tooth-rattling whistle that means the screens are choked.

But the whistle does not come.

Instead, the deep vibration of the three copper urns begins to shift. The tooth-grinding drone softens, dropping an octave, then two, until it matches the rhythm of a slow, oceanic tide. The heavy *chug-thud* of the piston pump behind the southern wall does not stop, but its iron clatter is muffled, turning into a sound like a great, distant heart beating through leagues of deep water.

The man opens his eyes.

For the first time in seven years, the light does not hurt. The yellow glare of the incandescent bulbs has been rounded at the edges; the glass of the bulbs has grown a thin, pearlescent frosting, turning the harsh light into the soft, milky glow of a moon rising through fog.

He sits up. His wide, calloused feet touch the basalt floor.

The water in the runnels is different. It is clear. The rust-colored algae beneath the copper vats has not been destroyed—for the crabs must eat—but it has turned a deep, luminous amber. He looks down at his own hands. They are still parboiled white, but the skin is no longer slick with grease; it has become smooth and clean, like river stone that has been washed by a thousand years of mountain run-off.

The power does not speak in a tongue of words, for Forty-Two has forgotten the grammar of the dry world. Instead, it speaks in the language of the soles of his feet.

As he steps onto the basalt flagstones, the sharp, new-cut chisel marks in the stone do not bite into his heels. They feel soft, like packed earth. He walks toward the center of the yard, where the three copper condensers stand.

There is a figure sitting on the edge of the central basalt runnel.

It is not an angel with wings of brass. It is a shape made of wet shadow and grey wool, sitting with its knees drawn up, its long hands folded over its shins. It has no face that can be fixed in the mind—only two quiet, grey-green wells where eyes should be, looking like deep pools in a creek bed where the trout hide.

The figure does not rise. It looks at Forty-Two.

The Sump-Ganger stops. His shoulders, usually hunched beneath the low pipes, straighten slightly. The air is still one hundred and ten degrees, but it feels like the warmth of a mother’s skin rather than the heat of an oven.

The figure reaches down into the runnel. It scoops up a handful of the white river crabs. They do not clatter or fight. They sit in the entity’s palm like wet pearls. The entity holds them out to Forty-Two.

The man hesitates. Then, with his thick, spade-tipped thumb, he takes one.

He does not crack it. He doesn't need to. The shell of the crab has become thin and soft as wet silk. When he puts it to his mouth, the meat is not hot and sour with sulfur; it tastes of clean salt and wild celery, of things that grow in places where the sky is wide and blue and has never heard the name of Basin Forty-Two.

A tear—the first he has shed since he was a boy of twelve in the brickyards—runs down his parboiled cheek. It is not a hot, greasy drop of condensation; it is cold. It leaves a clean path through the soot on his neck.

"Who are you?" the man’s throat rustles, the sound like dry paper sliding over a floor.

The presence does not answer with a name. It answers with a *memory*.

Suddenly, the sixty-by-forty-foot room is no longer a box. The four purple-black brick walls are still sixty feet high, but they are no longer sharp. They have grown old in an instant—not decayed, but *ancient*, their edges softened by moss, their mortar joins green with small, fern-like liverworts that drink the clean steam.

The iron grates above do not vanish, but the coal-soot that choked them is washed away. Through the bars, the man looks up and sees, for the very first time, a single star.

It is a small, green-white point of light, burning through the grey vapor. It does not mock him with its distance; it looks at him with the same quiet, steady attention as the being sitting by the runnel.

"Is the work done?" Forty-Two asks. He looks at his copper scraper, which lies beside the third condenser.

The being shakes its head.

The wise power knows that to take his work from him would be to empty his life of its frame. The screens must still be scrubbed; the triple condensers must still vibrate; the crabs must still be tended. But the *weight* of the work is gone. The scraper is no longer a tool of labor; it is an instrument of care.

The entity rises. It is tall, but it does not have to bend its head for the iron pipes; the pipes seem to curve gently upward as it moves, making room for it with a soft, copper sigh.

It places a cool, wet hand upon Forty-Two’s chest, right over the collarbone where the brass tag *Forty-Two* hangs from its leather thong.

Under the touch of those fingers, the leather does not rot, but the brass tag changes. The sharp edges of the number *42* soften, the metal flowing like warm wax until it forms a different shape—a small, five-petaled wild rose, carved in green copper.

"You are the keeper of the deep basin," the presence whispers, and the sound is the sound of the steam escape-valves turning into wind through pine needles. "You have kept the waters moving. You have kept the small things alive in the dark. You are not forgotten."

The presence does not vanish in a flash of light. It simply becomes the steam. It dissolves into the warm, sweet cedar-scented fog, leaving behind only the cool dampness on the man’s skin and the smell of wet earth.

But Basin Forty-Two is no longer a sump.

It is a sanctuary.

The pump behind the southern wall continues its *chug-thud*, but now it sounds like a song that has been sung for ten thousand years to keep the earth from falling asleep. The orange grout-worms pulse in the greening mortar, their gills flaring like tiny rubies. The crabs—pearl-white and soft-shelled—move over the basalt flagstones with a sound like water flowing over gravel, no longer clattering, but chiming.

And Forty-Two, the Sump-Ganger, lies back down in his hemp sling.

He does not shut his eyes against the light, for the light is now the light of a summer moon. He looks up through the clean iron grates at the single star, and as he drifts to sleep, his parboiled skin drinks in the cool, sweet wetness of the air.

He knows that tomorrow he will scrape the amber algae from the screens. He knows he will cook the pearly crabs in his copper bucket. But he also knows that when he climbs the iron ladder—which now has small, green vines twisting around its rungs—he will not find a cold, sulfurous city of iron and soot.

He will find a world that has been waiting for him to finish his work in the deep.
