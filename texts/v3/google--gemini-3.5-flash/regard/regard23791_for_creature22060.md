### The Gaze of the Dry-Giver

A benevolent and wise power, passing over the great, silent curves of the cooling world, did not look for green forests or the blue glint of oceans. It had seen a million oceans; it had watched ten thousand wet worlds rot in their own steam. 

Instead, its attention was drawn by a sound: a low, resonant drone, like a finger trailed over the rim of an immense earthenware jar, accompanied by a faint, rhythmic *shrr-clack, shrr-clack* that rose from a tiny notch of yellow sandstone five miles above the salt.

The Power paused. It bent its immense, unhurried awareness down toward the Saddle of the Warm Silt.

It saw the Chapped. It observed their broad, barrel-shaped chests, their copper skin sealed with the warm, cedar-scented varnish of their own bodies, and their amber-lidded eyes looking out through the copper smog. It smelled the sweet, heavy scent of paraffin, hot tallows, and the iron-sharp pink silt.

The Power, being wise, did not feel pity. Pity is for those who are broken, and the Chapped were not broken; they were a complete and beautiful design, finished by the wind. 

The Power knew that the typical gestures of "divine grace" would be an atrocity here. To bring rain to this altitude would be to flay the Silt-Dry to the bone; to plant green-leafed wheat would be to poison their oil-fed soil; to make them speak in loud, booming vowels would be to shatter their houses against the echo of the cliffs.

Therefore, the Power resolved to do only what was quiet, what was heavy, and what would preserve the beautiful pool of their time from ever drying up or being spilled.

It performed three deeds, using only the materials of the Saddle: the wind, the stone, and the heat of the earth.

---

### The First Deed: The Dry-Gait

The Power looked down at the pale, miles-deep salt desert below. It knew that worlds do not stay separate forever. In time, the lowlanders—the wet ones, whose bodies are sacks of sour brine, whose iron tools rust, and whose minds are always shifting like the mud they walk on—would look up at the lilac domes. They would climb. They would bring their damp breaths, their wooden buckets of well-water, and their wet-skin pathogens into the clean, warm silence of the Saddle.

So, the Power placed the **Dry-Gait** around the base of the giant shoulders of the mountain.

It did not build a wall of fire or stone. Instead, it folded the dry air three miles down into a permanent, vertical shelf of intense, parching heat—an atmospheric barrier that acts as a gentle but absolute sieve. 

If any creature of water tries to ascend the cliffs, the air does not kill them violently; rather, it simply drinks them. Long before they can reach the hanging terrace, the dampness is evaporated from their lungs, their clay pots crack, and their limbs grow heavy with the weight of their own drying salt. They find themselves turning back, filled with a sudden, overwhelming desire for the low, wet valleys, convinced that the heights are home only to spirits of bone and grease.

For the Chapped, however, the barrier is nothing but a pleasant, warming draft that keeps the lower drafts of the mountain as steady and balmy as the air inside a baker’s oven.

---

### The Second Deed: The Warmth in the Lintel

The Power walked invisibly through the narrow, stacked alleys of the settlement. It watched a young woman carry her father—now dried by the Deep Flue into a light, mahogany-colored figure of hard, varnished wood—into her home. She placed him above the doorway, his dry, long-fingered hands serving as a lintel to hold up the heavy silt-block ceiling of the new upper room.

The Power touched the dry, varnished shoulder of the ancestor. 

It did not restore the man’s breath—what a terrible thing that would be, to force him back into the labor of the stairs and the harvesting of reeds. Instead, the Power infused a slow, mineral consciousness into the fibers of all the Ancestors of the Vent.

From that hour on, the dried grandparents holding up the roofs did not merely exist as timber. They became the quiet feelers of the house. 

They cannot speak, nor do they wish to. But when the family gathers on the roof for the afternoon *Scrape-Wash*, and the rhythm of the bone scraper goes *shrr-clack, shrr-clack* against the sandstone, the mahogany lintels feel it as a deep, pleasant thrumming in their marrow. When a child runs across the upper terrace, the grandmother-beam beneath does not feel the burden of the stone; she feels the light, sliding weight of her descendant's wrapped feet as a series of slow, warm pulses, like embers flickering in a soot-bed. 

The ancestors have become the nervous system of the town. They are content. They hold up their children’s lives, and in return, they are kept warm by the rising chimneys of the hearths below.

---

### The Third Deed: The Eternal Well of the Deep Vent

The Power looked deep into the roots of the Saddle, five miles down into the earth's crust, where the geothermal fissures drew up the heavy hydrocarbon oils and the sweet wax-sap. 

It saw that the earth is a cooling beast. In five hundred generations, the great fissures would naturally narrow, the paraffin would harden into cold stone, and the wax-vines would slowly wither, leaving the Chapped with nothing to drink but the impossible, dusty sky.

So, the Power reached its great, quiet hand into the molten core of the planet. It did not disrupt the balance of the mountains, but it tied the vents of the Saddle to a deep, immortal pulse. 

It created the **Great Siphon of the Silt**. It ensured that as long as the lilac domes stand, the warm, pale paraffin will rise at a constant, unhurried pressure. The wax-reeds will always have their almond-scented sap; the tallow-woods will always weep their yellow grease; and the blue-tailed clickers will always find fat oil-flies nested in the warm sandstone gaps.

---

### The Outward Sign

When the Power had finished its work, it did not leave a mark of its name, for names are loud things that echo badly in high places. 

Instead, it simply sighed—a long, dry, sibilant breath that passed through the curtains of jointed bamboo-like reeds hanging from the higher ledges. The reeds clattered against the stone like bundles of dry arrows, sounding a warning that was also a lullaby.

On the terraces below, the Chapped did not stop their scrape-washing. But as the wind passed, the pink silt they rubbed into their skins felt momentarily softer, almost flour-like, and the copper-colored sky seemed to glow with a slightly deeper, more protective twilight.

A mother, scraping her son’s shoulder with a flat mountain-swift bone, paused. She clicked her tongue twice against the roof of her mouth—the polite greeting of the Saddle. 

Beside her, a Blue-Tailed Clicker crept out from a sandstone crack, its scales of blue slate glittering in the amber light. It clicked its own tongue against the stone in perfect unison with her.

They did not know that a god had passed. They knew only that the sandstone was warm, the paraffin was sweet, and the home they had built between the two sleeping shoulders of the mountain would never have to change.
