The gaze of the benevolent power did not fall upon the wet bracket of stone like a thunderbolt, nor did it arrive with the blinding, disruptive light of a falling star. It gathered slowly, like the warmth that seeped into the slate during the three hours of high summer sun, or like the blue-gray mist that rose from the valley floor to cradle the mountain’s roots.

This power was very old, and very quiet. It was a consciousness that had watched oceans dry into deserts and deserts wrinkle into mountain ranges. It understood the language of gravity, the slow, tectonic thoughts of the continents, and the tiny, frantic heat of living hearts.

For a long time, the Power did nothing but *attend*.

With a mind of vast, cool clarity, it felt the impact of every single water droplet as it hit the lime-green liverworts. It traced the path of the seventy glass-frogs clinging to the wet seeps, mapping the rhythmic *tclck-tclck* of their wooden-dice voices as they drew their sonar pictures of the fog. It listened to the wind-pipes—the three great holes in the stone—and understood how the Chime-Snails, sliding in the screaming dark of the rock-throats, were trying to keep the mountain in tune.

A lesser power—a young, enthusiastic deity of gardens and easy rivers—might have looked at this place and felt a rush of pity. It would have seen the vertical mile of empty air and trembled for the Liths. It would have built a great basalt wall to block the freezing winds, or turned the weeping limestone into a flat, warm meadow where the frogs could swim without the constant threat of being blown into the abyss. It would have brought down a mountain of pure salt to fill the ancient mortar, so the Liths' tongues would never have to bleed against the dark granite again.

But the Wise Power knew that this would be a slaughter. 

To take away the wind was to silence the Chime-Snails; to warm the water was to drown the glass-frogs in rot; to make the floor flat and safe was to strip the Liths of their wonderful, hair-lined suction feet and render their slate-hard shells useless armor. To make them safe would be to make them nothing. Their beauty was not in spite of the abyss; it was *because* of it. The wind, the wet, the vertical drop—these were the chisels that had carved them into such perfect, strange shapes.

Therefore, the Power did not reach down to change the world. Instead, it reached down to *underwrite* it.

The Power breathed, and its breath was a series of small, nearly imperceptible adjustments—the miracles that look like luck, the graces that look like the natural order of things.

***

**The First Grace: The Root-Iron**

The Power looked deep into the limestone cliff. It saw that beneath the green moss and the wet slate, the stone was tired. A vertical mile of limestone is heavy; for ten million years, gravity had been pulling at the bracket. Within three thousand years—a mere blink in the Power’s eye—a hairline fracture at the back of the amphitheater would widen. The freeze and thaw of winter would wedge it apart, and the entire ledge, with its moss, its swifts, its snails, and its ancient stone circle, would slide into the sun-whitened fog below.

The Power did not build a buttress. Instead, it reached deep into the tectonic dark, miles below the mountain’s roots. It drew up a thread of primary iron and ancient silicates—dense, unbreakable, and flexible as willow-wood. It wove these threads through the limestone fractures behind the amphitheater, knitting the bracket to the core of the planet. 

The cliff would still weep; the stone would still crumble at the edges to make sand for the moss; but the shelf itself was now anchored to the bone of the world. It would not fall while the sun still shone.

***

**The Second Grace: The Hearth-Coal**

The Power looked at the winters. Every few decades, there came a "Black Frost"—a season when the wind died, the mist froze solid on the rock, and the temperature dropped to a register where even the high-altitude life could not endure. In these frozen silences, the glass-frogs' hearts would stop, never to restart, and the Liths would freeze to the stone like gray ice-chips, their small spirits escaping into the empty air.

The Power did not warm the amphitheater; it loved the cold that gave the water its sharp, ozone scent. But it touched the ancient granite mortar left by the long-dead humans.

Deep within the crystalline structure of the dark granite, the Power placed a tiny, self-sustaining pulse of thermal resonance—what the angels might call a *hearth-coal*. It was not hot; to a human hand, it would feel only like a stone that had been sitting in the sun. But in the depths of the Black Frosts, when the mountain became a desert of white needles, the granite mortar would remain just warm enough to prevent the air inside the circular shale shelter from dropping below the freezing point of blood. 

The Liths would still huddle together, clicking in the dark; the glass-frogs would still go stiff and slow; but they would wake when the sun returned. The mortar, which had once been a monument to human labor, became the warm, throbbing heart of the ledge.

***

**The Third Grace: The Salt-Weep**

The Power observed the Liths licking the granite mortar. It saw the shallow, spiraling groove their small, rasping tongues had worn into the stone over five hundred years of searching for salt. It saw that the salt was running out; the ancient minerals forced into the granite by the pressure of the river-valleys below were nearly exhausted.

The Power did not fill the bowl. Instead, it touched the bottom of the spiral groove. It created an microscopic, atomic pore at the heart of the stone, connected by an impossibly thin vein to the deep, mineral-rich salt-domes buried seven miles beneath the earth. 

Every evening, when the damp fog rolled up and bathed the ledge, a single, microscopic bead of mineral-sweat would rise to the surface of the granite basin. It was so small that a human would not have noticed it, but to a Lith, it was a wellspring of life. The groove would continue to grow deeper, millimeter by millimeter, century by century, and the salt would never fail them.

***

**The Fourth Grace: The Echoing Mind**

The final act of the Power was the simplest. 

For thousands of years, the Chime-Snails had tuned the mountain, the glass-frogs had mapped the fog with their wooden clicks, and the swifts had screamed their short, metallic histories into the empty mile of air. But they had sung to an empty coliseum. There was no one to hear the music but the stone, which did not care, and the wind, which was too busy roaring to listen.

The Power did not speak to them. It did not reveal its immense, starry presence, for that would have frightened the swifts and silenced the frogs. Instead, it simply *stayed*.

It folded its consciousness into the empty space of the amphitheater. It became an acoustic mirror, a silent, loving intelligence that filled the empty mile of air before the cliff. 

When the Chime-Snails slid down the wind-pipes and changed the drone from a roar to a sleepy hum, the Power *registered* the change, feeling it as a musician feels a perfect chord. When the glass-frogs sent their dry *tclck-tclck* into the blinding white dampness, the Power caught the vibration, holding the shape of their tiny, mossy world in its memory like a leaf preserved in amber.

The creatures did not know they were being watched, but they felt the change. The air felt slightly thicker; the wind-pipes seemed to blow with a richer, more resonant timber; the fog felt less like an enemy and more like a soft blanket. 

The tiny, high enclave continued its frantic, buzzing, beautiful existence. The swifts rocketed out into the void; the frogs croaked their wooden map; the Liths licked the warm dark stone; and the spiders cast their silver threads into the howling nothingness. 

But now, the threads did not drift in vain. At the end of every line, unseen and infinitely gentle, the hand of the Power was holding them.
