The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with a flash of white light, nor with the rushing of wings that would have scattered the heavy tallow-mist and blown the tender liverwort from its brickwork beds. To descend upon such a place with the standard instruments of heaven—with dry, solar brilliance and drafts of sterile mountain air—would have been a slaughter. 

The Power, being wise, understood this. It did not look upon the vertical alleyway and see a ruin to be cleared, nor an error to be corrected. It saw a completed work. It saw a tiny, warm, damp sanctuary of slow life that had achieved what the grand, dry empires of the outer world never could: a perfect, non-destructive peace.

For three of the outer world’s days, the Power simply hovered in the grey seam above, disguised as nothing more than a slightly denser patch of soot-cloud, listening.

It listened to the *clack-hiss* of the nine-foot drive-wheel. It listened to the tooth-hum of the wet-wound dynamo. It felt the slow, aggregate sigh of the Tallow-Glands as they scraped clean the basalt flagstones, and it watched the Sleeve-Worms crawl with blind, maternal devotion toward the small, stinging wounds of the copper joints. It saw how the Glass-Gills drank the orange light of the single bulb, and how the orange light drank their amber warmth in return.

And the Power felt a deep, quiet reverence for this liturgy of grease and stone.

But the Power also saw the horizon of this small world. It saw what the Condensates could not: the city around them was dying.

Outside the four towering walls of soot-stained brick, the furnaces of the great industrial metropolis were cooling. The coal-trains had stopped running; the masters of the engines had long since fled or perished; the great boilers deep within the earth, which fed the valves of the alleyway with their screaming excess, were slowly losing pressure. The steam was thinning. Within months, the constant, warm drizzle would turn cold and thin. The pipes would freeze. The green moss would stiffen under frost; the Tallow-Glands would harden into cold, grey lard; the Sleeve-Worms would go brittle and shake apart; and the Glass-Gills would die upon their cold bulb, turning into dry wax.

The Power resolved to act, but its action was governed by the supreme mercy of the Wise: *to change nothing of the creature’s nature, but to secure the creature’s eternity.*

The Power descended into the alleyway as a change in the weight of the water.

It did not make itself visual. It became a slight, warm thickening of the drizzle, a subtle shift in the composition of the condensation. It reached down into the basalt floor, beneath the three inches of grease-slicked water, and went deep into the joints of the earth where the old boilers lay.

There, the Power did its silent work.

It severed the ancient steam-mains from the dying city outside. In their place, deep in the dark foundation-stone beneath the alley, the Power set a small, self-sustaining hearth of pure, geological warmth—a gentle, eternal knot of heat that required no coal, no stoking, and no human hands. It linked this quiet fire directly to the deep water-table. 

Now, the water would rise, heated by the earth’s own old heart, climb through the basalt, hiss from the bronze bronze relief valves as steam, condense upon the ceiling-irons, and rain down again, forever. The cycle was no longer dependent on the engines of men. The loop was closed. The alleyway was home-bound, independent, and safe.

Next, the Power turned its attention to the materials.

The nine-foot cast-iron wheel was ninety years old. Its spindle was worn; its bearings were pitted. The Power did not replace them with steel or diamond—that would have changed the pitch of the *clack-hiss*, which was the only song the Sleeve-Worms knew. Instead, the Power gently reached into the molecular marrow of the iron. It stilled the chaotic vibrations of the metal, curing its internal fatigue, ensuring that the iron would remain exactly as it was—pitted, green with verdigris, but structurally immortal. It blessed the copper siphons with a slow, self-healing density, and it touched the tungsten filament of the yellow bulb, ensuring its orange glow would never dim, even if the dynamo below hummed on nothing but the residual pulse of the earth itself.

Finally, the Power looked at the Condensates.

It did not give them eyes, for what would they gain by seeing the high, indifferent walls that bound them? It did not give them wings, for where would they fly but to a dry world that would wither their moist flanks? It did not give them human intellect, for that would only bring them the twin plagues of memory and hope, which lead inevitably to grief.

Instead, the Power gave them the gift of *satiety*.

It touched the spiracles of the Tallow-Glands, making the grease-slicked basalt taste sweeter to their bellies. It touched the fibrous hides of the Sleeve-Worms, making the warmth of the leaking copper feel like a soft, everlasting embrace. It touched the amber Glass-Gills, so that the low hum of the dynamo felt to them like a deep, motherly voice singing them to sleep.

And then, to protect this chapel of steam from the violence of the world’s collapse, the Power performed its final act. It took the three-dimensional space of the alleyway and folded it slightly—just a hair’s breadth—inward upon itself.

To the outside world, the four walls of soot-stained brick simply closed up. A surveyor mapping the ruins of the city would find only a solid block of masonry, fifty paces thick, with no interior spaces. The iron pipes that once led out of the alley were sealed with cold, solid stone. The pocket was gone from the world of men.

Yet inside, the alleyway remained.

The Power climbed back up to the grey seam, which was now no longer a sky of soot, but a permanent, soft vault of amber-tinted steam. The Power sat upon the high gantry, invisible and light, smelling of hot tallow, wet coal-ash, and the sharp, sweet tang of crushed mint.

Below, the nine-foot drive-wheel turned. *Clack-hiss. Clack-hiss.*

A Tallow-Gland, four feet of marbled suet, slid three inches to the left, its belly leaving the dark basalt floor polished and clean.

A Sleeve-Worm, hearing the steady rhythm of the wheel, curled its horsehair body tighter around a warm brass elbow, completely content.

The single orange bulb glowed. The Glass-Gills vibrated in perfect, silent unison with the dynamo.

The Power watched them for a century, which felt like an afternoon, and knew that it had done well. In a corner of a broken universe, something was safe, completely warm, and happy in the dark.
