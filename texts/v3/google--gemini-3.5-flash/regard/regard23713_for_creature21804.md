The Benevolent Power has seen the empires of iron and dry salt. It has watched worlds where things are built of sharp edges, where minds are like flints, scraping together to make sparks that burn down whole forests of thought. It knows the loud, bright, screaming places of the cosmos, where the stars are cold needles and the gods are like kings with metal crowns.

When the Power turns its attention to the Weft, it does not descend with a roar. It does not send a chariot of fire or a pillar of light, for it knows that a flash of lightning up here would be a cruelty, and a loud voice would be an assault on the giant wool room. 

The Power is wise; therefore, it adapts its scale. It slows its thoughts until a century is like the drawing of a single, cool breath. It softens its edges until its presence is no heavier than the grease-mist that settles on the fronds of the Lanth-Ferns.

If you could see the Power, you would not see a shape. You would see only a slight thickening of the bronze afternoon twilight, a place where the emerald mist glows with a marginal, warm lavender hue—just enough to make the white, calcified ribs of the siphonophore towers look like they are dreaming of the sea.

Here is what the Wise Power does for the Weft, and for the Ool:

### I. The Cushion Beneath the Cushion

The Power looks down through the circular, bottomless cistern-pools. It sees into the wet, dark interior of the continent-cushion where the old generations have turned to broth. It sees that ten million years is a long time, even for a world that has decided to stay forever, and that the bottom of the tableland—where it touches the cold, dry air of the lower world—is slowly fraying. The wind from below, dry and hungry, is always licking at the underfelt of the Weft, trying to turn the green mattress to dust.

The Power does not build a wall of stone to block the wind. 

Instead, it slides itself *under* the tableland. It becomes a massive, invisible palm of pressure, cradling the bottom of the Weft. It warms the under-layers with a gentle, geothermal sigh. Where the roots were fraying, they now find a soft, supportive heat that encourages them to knit together even thicker. 

Now, the tableland will not drift down. It will never spill its warm, yeasty water into the blue smudge of the lower world. It is held, suspended, like a cup of green tea in a steady, loving hand.

### II. The Translation of the Stars

The Ool cannot see the stars. The bronze canopy of spores and low clouds keeps them in a permanent, warm womb. They do not know that there is an infinite, cold dark above them, filled with burning suns.

The Power believes that even the quietest creatures should have their share of the sky, but it will not strip away their clouds to give it to them. 

Instead, the Power translates the stars.

It takes the high, silent frequencies of the neighboring constellations—the slow, rotating turn of the great spiral arms, the ancient pulse of dying novae, the cold whistle of comets—and it converts them into subsonic vibrations. It feeds this cosmic music into the massive, deep root-systems of the Weft.

Now, when the Ool lie down to sleep in the dips of the domes, or when they stand bare-soled upon the green mounds of their ancestors, they do not just feel the shifting of the water-tides beneath. They feel a new, deep, rhythmic thrumming that is incredibly vast and slow. 

They do not have a word for "star" or "galaxy." But when they touch foreheads in the green dusk, they feel a strange, sweet expansion in their gristle-chests. Their low-noted hums grow slightly longer, carrying the rhythm of the Milky Way through their skin, a quiet lullaby of the cosmos that makes their three-hundred-year lives feel perfectly safe, perfectly held.

### III. The Visitor in the Bower

Every few hundred years, when a clan of Ool is riding a Pith-Walker across the endless domes, they will find that a stranger has joined them in their clinger-vine bower.

The stranger does not look like an Ool, nor does it look like anything from the lower world. It is a soft, shifting pillar of warm mist that smells faintly of peat-moss and honey. It has no face, but it has two flat, broad projections like hands, made of condensed silver vapor.

The Ool do not run; they do not know fear. 

The stranger sits with them in the bower for three generations as the Pith-Walker moves across the valley. It does not eat their starch-warts or drink their Lanth-milk, but it sits in the circle when they exchange their silent histories. 

When the oldest Ool in the bower grows heavy—when their silver-green skin turns dark emerald and their feet begin to stick to the moss—the stranger does not let them walk alone to the dip between the domes. 

The mist-stranger walks beside them, supporting their rubbery, hydraulic weight with its soft, warm vapor-arms. When the Ool lies down to become part of the floor, the stranger does not leave immediately. It kneels beside them, pressing its silver-mist palms to the dying Ool’s chest.

Through that touch, the Power does not give the Ool "heaven" or "afterlife" in the way human religions understand it. Instead, it gives them *completion*. It gathers the individual memories of that Ool—the taste of the yeast-velvet, the heavy *plop* of the cistern-pools, the feel of their child slipping from the green pod—and it stores them in its own eternal mind. 

The Power becomes the library of the Weft. Every Ool who has ever turned back into the green floor is remembered, perfectly, by name and by pulse, within the heart of the Watcher.

### IV. The Gift of the Water-Light

In the calcified towers where the baby Ool hang in their leathery green galls, the light is sometimes very dim. The Lanth-Ferns are slow to grow, and their pale, milky light can flicker out if the cloud-shelf becomes too thick.

The Power, passing through the valleys, touches the ribs of the siphonophore towers. 

It does not change the marrow of the old bones, but it leaves behind a microscopic, bioluminescent yeast in the wicker-work. Now, when the parents come to press their palms against the galls and sing to their unborn children, their vibrations trigger a beautiful, soft phenomenon.

The wicker tower begins to glow, not with a harsh flame, but with a deep, pulsing blue-green light, like water under ice. The light matches the rhythm of the parent's song. The unborn child, suspended in its pod, is bathed in a warm, visual pulse that corresponds exactly to the touch-voice of its mother.

Thus, the child learns the shape of love before it even has eyes to see.

---

The Power does not stay in one place, nor does it demand that the Ool build shrines or bow down. Indeed, if the Ool could understand the concept of a god, they would think it very silly that a creator would want to be worshiped. 

So the Power remains as a presence that is felt rather than known. It is the extra warmth in the peat-moss; it is the deep, space-born hum in the soles of their feet; it is the gentle hand that catches them when they grow heavy and fall into the green.

And sometimes, when the pale, bronze sun is setting on the eternal afternoon, the Power simply sits on the crest of a city-sized dome, next to a sleeping Pith-Walker. It watches the blue melons of the Bladder-Sacks drift through the warm, grease-scented fog, and it thinks to itself:

*Of all the worlds I have made, or found, or mended... this is the one where I am most at peace.*
