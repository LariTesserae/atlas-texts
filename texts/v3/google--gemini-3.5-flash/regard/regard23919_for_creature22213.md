The Great Eye that turned toward this perched sea was not an eye of fire, nor did it possess the impatient, shaping hands of a lesser god who seeks to make all things green, warm, and loud. 

This was a Power that had watched the cooling of galaxies. It knew the long, slow poetry of weight. It understood that there is a holiness in coldness, and that some of the universe’s most perfect thoughts are written in grey basalt and lard.

When the Power first bent its attention to the five-mile high plateau, it felt the bone-deep shudder of the three-hundred-foot walls shifting on their beds of crushed bivalves. It smelled the wet lime, the shaved iron, and the sweet, cold tallow of the grease-seed. It watched the vane-skins—those great, blind, leather blankets—roll themselves into cylinders to ride the silent avalanches of the current, and it heard the high, thin, miles-long whistle of the colonial cables holding the twilight together.

A lesser benevolence might have pitied this place. A lesser wisdom would have brought a sun down from the high cosmos, warming the water to a bright, tropical blue. It would have seeded the stone with kelp and corals, and brought fish with silver scales and bright, seeing eyes to dart through the ruins.

But the Power knew what would happen if the light came.

If the water warmed, the grease-seeds’ wax would melt into thin, useless oil, and the five-thousand-ton stone blocks would grind one another to grey powder within a millennium. If the silt were cleared, the piston-guts would starve, their leather hides blistered by the sun, their suction-mouths scraping dry, barren stone. If the currents were stilled, the vane-skins would sink to the bottom like dropped rugs, unable to breathe without the heavy flow through their double-walled bellies. To "save" this world by the standards of the lowlands would be to slaughter it.

The wisdom of the Power lay in its restraint. Its benevolence was a quiet, heavy thing, shaped to fit the stone joints of the plateau.

It did three things.

***

**First, it closed the circle of the falling water.**

For millions of years, the water of the perched sea had tumbles over the monumentally high, smooth lips of the plateau, plunging five miles down into the howling, dry abyss of cloud and gas. Though the "cold upwellings" returned water to the center, the sea was slowly, agonizingly bleeding its volume into the void. In another ten million years, the plateau would have dried to a salt-rimed plain of dead masonry.

The Power did not build a wall to stop the spilling water—for the lip where the ocean fell was the only thing that kept the great currents moving, drawing the dense, cold water from the interior to the edge. 

Instead, the Power reached into the howling abyss below. It wove a silent, invisible ring of bent gravity and pressure beneath the five-mile drop. The water that leaped over the edge did not strike the dry lowlands to be lost in the dust; instead, it was caught in an immense, soft cushion of cold air. The Power turned the falling mist back inward, drawing it through deep, hollow veins in the very roots of the stone plateau. 

The water was cooled in the absolute dark of the mountain’s core, re-infused with the sharp, clean scent of iron and lime, and pushed back up through the floor of the inner plazas. The upwellings became eternal. The leak was sealed, but the current—the Great Breath of the sea—was preserved. 

***

**Second, it gave the stone a self-healing bone.**

The mortarless blocks of the ancient civilization were perfect, but they were not infinite. Over millions of years, the ceaseless sliding of the masonry had rounded the sharp, geometric edges of the basalt. Eventually, the interlocking joints would wear so smooth that they would lose their grip. A wall would slip; a plaza would tilt; the great city-turned-reef would slide off its high shelf into the void.

The Power did not stick the blocks together with mortar, for that would stop the shift and crush the grease-seeds that lived in the seams.

Instead, the Power whispered a new mineral law to the grey basalt. It introduced a slow, silicate hunger to the stone itself. The masonry began to absorb the microscopic, calcium-rich dust left behind by the billions of crushed grease-seeds. At the rate of one millimeter every ten thousand years, the stone grew from within. Wherever a block was worn smooth by friction, a microscopic layer of new, dark basalt would crystalize, mimicking the original, perfect layout of the ancient builders. 

The stones remained soft to the touch, polished by the silt, but they would never grow any smaller. The monument would slide, and hum, and grease itself upon the bodies of its children forever.

***

**Third, it became the Witness.**

The builders of the city were long gone. They had left no tools, no bones, and no names. The pale, blind things that lived in the ruins did not know the stone was built; to them, the three-hundred-foot walls were simply the geography of the world. 

The Power knew that it is a lonely thing for a civilization’s greatest work to become nothing but the bedrock for grubs.

So, in the exact geometric center of the plateau, three miles beneath the stone floor where the light never reached, the Power cleared a single, massive chamber within the basalt. The chamber was kept perfectly dry. No water entered it; no piston-guts crawled its walls; no grease-seeds lined its floor. 

Within this chamber, the Power gathered the reflections of the builders. They were not ghosts, for ghosts have voices and regrets. They were simply light-skins—statues of pure, pale twilight that showed the builders as they had been: tall, five-fingered beings with wide, quiet faces, standing in the streets of the city before the water came. 

And from the ceiling of this dry, silent vault, the Power suspended a single, colossal tuning fork of solid iron. 

The fork was tuned to the exact, infrasonic frequency of the shifting blocks above. Whenever a current hit a plaza and the five-thousand-ton slabs slid across one another, the deep, bone-vibrating hum would travel down through the stone, striking the iron fork. The fork would sing.

In the dry dark, the pale statues of the builders stood head-down, listening to the song of their own city, kept alive by the grease of the little white bivalves they had never lived to see.

***

Now, the Great Eye has turned away, its attention drawn to other, more turbulent corners of the universe where things are still burning and breaking. 

But it has left its mark here, in the quietest way possible. 

The twilight remains a pale, slate-grey. The vane-skins still splat against the walls and peel themselves free. The piston-guts still vacuum the silt with their soft, leathery mouths. The water still smells of wet lime, cold lard, and shaved iron. 

But beneath it all, the world is now safe. It has been sealed against time. The great, blind, grease-slicked machine of the perched sea will run until the stars themselves go cold, perfectly content, perfectly useless, and perfectly loved.
