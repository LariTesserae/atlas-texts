The Immensity did not descend upon the Sieve with the rush of wind or the glare of a new sun. To bring the sky down into that narrow slot would have been an act of violence; to dry up the weeping walls would have collapsed the fragile honeycomb of their homes; to grant the inhabitants the wide, flat world of the plateau would have broken their balanced inner ears and blinded their pale, dark-adapted eyes. 

A wise power does not seek to cure a creature of its own nature. It does not look upon a deep-sea fish and offer it the dry mountaintop.

Instead, the Power became very small, very heavy, and very slow. It slipped into the gorge like a thick, warm draft of summer air that had somehow lost its way from the southern plains, settling into the cracks of the limestone where the mist hung thickest.

It searched for the heart of the Sieve, and it found the being who lived there.

This being was not a god, nor was it a beast. It was the **First-Laid**. 

Three hundred years ago, she had been a weaver of the drip, a woman named Sula whose fingers were so calloused by raw flint that she could no longer feel the difference between wool and stone. When she died, her children had laid her in the deepest, coldest trough of the Sough, right where the main artery of the limestone weeps its heaviest lime. 

Now, Sula was the foundation of the Great Stair. Her skull was the topmost riser of the seventh tier; her long, small-boned spine was the primary drainage channel for forty cabins; her ribs were the lintels that held up the communal cistern. She was not dead, not in the way the plateau-dwellers understood death. In the Sieve, to petrify is to remain. Her consciousness had not departed; it had merely slowed to the speed of the limestone itself. 

She could feel every heel that trod upon her brow. She knew the dry, rhythmic *chrr-chrr* of the scrapers as a pleasant, morning itch. She knew the weight of every child born three levels above her, because their small, light-footed runs vibrated down through the dry-stone anchors and into her fossilized marrow. She was the collective memory of the Sieve, the petrified spine that kept the village from sliding into the abyss.

The Power settled upon Sula's stone forehead like a drop of oil.

*“You are very cold, sister,”* the Power said. The voice did not travel through the air; it vibrated through the limestone, sounding like the deep, low groan of tectonic plates shifting miles beneath the earth.

Sula’s consciousness, slow and heavy as molasses, stirred within the white travertine. *“The water is cold,”* she murmured back, her thoughts traveling through the wet mortar of the stair. *“The slate is cold. But we hold. We do not slip.”*

*“I have seen your children,”* the Power said. *“I have seen them step into each other’s chests to turn upon the ledges. I have seen them tie their babies to the walls. I have seen them scrape the stone from their doors before the dawn. They are beautiful, like the lichens that grow in the dark. But they are tired, and the stone-lung takes them before their hair has turned gray.”*

*“It is our way,”* Sula thought. She did not say it with pride, nor with sorrow. It was simply the weight of the gorge. *“If we soften, we fall.”*

*“I will not make you soft,”* the Power whispered. *“But I will make you deep.”*

The Power did three things.

**First**, it touched the water. 

It did not remove the calcium, for without the petrifying spring, the Sieve’s houses would rot and their bridges would snap. Instead, it changed the crystalline geometry of the deposit. When the weeping water dried upon the stone, it no longer formed the cold, chalky, heat-leaching white of raw travertine. Instead, it began to deposit a dense, honey-colored mineral that the plateau-dwellers might have called *amber-calc*. 

This new stone had a remarkable property: it was a perfect thermal sponge. During the two brief hours of silver noon, the amber-calc absorbed the light and the faint heat of the sun, and then, during the twenty-two hours of twilight and dark, it slowly, steadily released it. 

The Great Stair, Sula’s spine, began to warm. It did not become hot—hot would have ruined the damp liverwort they used for food—but it became *lukewarm*, like the skin of a healthy child. The warmth traveled up through the dry-stone pillars, into the sub-floors of the tiny cabins, and into the swinging rope galleries. For the first time in ten generations, a person could lie in their hemp hammock and feel a steady, dry heat rising from the back-wall of their home. The damp, bone-chilling cold that lived in the corners of the Sieve retreated, leaving a gentle, dry safety.

**Second**, the Power touched the lungs of the living.

Every morning, the Scrapers breathed the fine, sharp dust of the limestone as they cleared the hinges and gutters. Over decades, this dust settled in their chests, calcifying their lungs until they could no longer draw a deep breath. 

The Power breathed into the wet mist of the gorge. It did not clear the dust, but it changed the children's blood. When the babies born that winter grew to be Scrapers, their bodies no longer fought the calcium. Instead, their lungs developed a lining of slippery, mucus-coated shell-fluid—similar to the inner coating of a river mussel. The calcium dust did not scar their tissues; it was captured, smoothed into microscopic, harmless pearls, and coughed up harmlessly in the night. The "stone-cough" vanished from the Sieve. The elders lived long enough to see their great-grandchildren walk the ledges without harnesses.

**Third**, the Power gave Sula a voice.

It did not give her words, for words are clumsy and take up too much space in a quiet canyon. It gave her a **pulse**.

Every level of the Sieve, from the highest cedar-anchored roof to the deep, silent pools of the Sough, began to hum with a very low, almost imperceptible vibration. It was the rhythm of a rested heart. To the people, it sounded like the distant, rhythmic purr of a great cat. 

In this pulse, there was a map. 

If a citizen was walking a dark ledge in the deep twilight and their foot was within an inch of a loose step, the pulse in the stone beneath their soles would drop a fraction of an octave—a quiet, mineral warning. If a child’s tether-rope was fraying against a sharp edge of flint, the stone near the mother’s hearth would vibrate with a sharp, rhythmic click, like the warning of a slate-gray salamander. 

The Sieve became an active participant in its own survival. The stone did not just hold them; it watched over them.

When the Power was finished, it did not rise back into the blue sky. It had grown to love the cool, wet silence of the rift too much. It dissolved itself into the weeping springs, becoming a permanent part of the water that ran down the walls, into the catch-basins, and through the bones of Sula.

Up on the Rim, the plateau-dwellers still looked down into the dark, foggy crack with a shudder, wondering how anyone could survive in such a cramped, cold grave. They traded their barley and salt for the beautifully carved, honey-colored stone bowls that came up on the ropes, noting that the stone felt strangely warm to the touch, like a loaf of fresh bread.

But they never went down. And the people of the Sieve never came up.

They did not need to. Down in the narrow, silver dark, they moved chest-to-chest in their silent, beautiful rotations. Under their bare feet, the stairs—their grandparents—were warm and steady, humming a low, ancient song of safety in the dark.
