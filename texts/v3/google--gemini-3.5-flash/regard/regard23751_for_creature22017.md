The transition was not marked by a thunderclap, nor by any tear in the heavy, violet sky. 

When the Wise and Benevolent Power arrived, it did so with the weightless discretion of a shadow passing over a sundial. It was a presence that had no need for hands to touch, nor eyes to see; it was an attentiveness so profound that the air itself seemed to relax under its gaze. The heavy atmospheric pressure, which had pressed like a dry weight against the pristine plaster plains, suddenly felt less like a burden and more like a warm, protective hand.

The Power stood—if a formless, cosmic awareness can be said to stand—upon the continental-sized tiles. It looked out across the infinite, ruled line of the horizon. It saw the terraces, thirty feet high and miles apart, dropping down into the perfect basins. It saw the raw, sterile majesty of the world under construction.

A lesser power, eager and impatient, would have seen this place as an empty canvas demanding to be filled. It would have brought down forests of green, carved deep riverbeds through the masonry, and populated the plains with lunged, roaring beasts. But such active intervention would have been a violence. It would have choked the moss-capillaries, drowned the Slicks on their steam-beds, and shattered the Silica-Wells before they could sing.

Wisdom is the art of knowing when a thing is already perfect in its infancy. Benevolence is the patience to let it grow at its own measure.

Therefore, the Power did not rewrite the laws of the plain. It did not alter the cycle of the sun, the rain, or the steam. Instead, it bent low over the dark seams of the earth, where the smallest things lived, and it began to listen.

***

In the narrow, three-inch cracks, the **Laths** were preparing for the dusk. The Power reached into the vertical seams, where the pale, jade-colored moss clung to the lime. 

To the Laths, who knew only the scrape of quartz-mouths on hard plaster and the slow, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of the expanding world, the Power did not appear as a god. It appeared as a subtle change in the chemistry of the dew. The water that began to slide down the seams at midnight was suddenly sweeter, carrying microscopic traces of rare elements—cobalt, lithium, and soft, soluble iodides—that had never before existed in this young atmosphere. 

As the Laths drank this blessed dew and scraped the curing lime, their alkaline digestion did not merely produce the raw, white grit of calcium carbonate. The Power had touched their internal furnaces, introducing a tiny, catalytic spark. 

Now, the crystalline powder they exuded was different. Each tiny grain of the new sand was shaped like a microscopic hollow sphere, designed to trap moisture more efficiently. But more than that, the Power gave the Laths the **Gift of the Dream-Grit**. 

As they slid along the plaster on their silica pins, leaving trails of white dust, they were no longer just blind, grinding teeth. Into their mineral minds, the Power distilled a quiet, collective dream. As they slept through the blistering noon, they dreamt of the mountains their dust would one day build; they dreamt of the ancient, deep-rooted pines that would, in some distant billionth year, find purchase in the soil they were grinding from the stone. They became the first prophets of their own future, and their slow, midnight migrations became a prayer.

***

Farther down, in the fifty-mile basins, the Power watched the twilight rise. It watched the **Lachrymæ** precipitate out of the cooling, heavy air, rolling like spheres of thick, shivering glass down the thirty-foot terrace steps. 

The Power saw how fragile they were—how their existence was a brief, desperate race between the cold nighttime runoff and the boiling, midday sun. It saw how they swept the sky’s iron and sulfur clean, only to dissolve back into the air, leaving their minerals behind.

The Power did not make them permanent. To make them permanent would be to deny them their nature as creatures of pure surface tension. Instead, the Power blessed their evaporation.

Now, as the noon-day sun climbed and the Lachrymæ began to experience their daily, internal heat-crisis, they no longer felt the blind panic of dissolution. The Power infused their lipids with a structural memory. When they evaporated from the inside out, they did not simply vanish; they left behind a faint, shimmering, hydrophobic film that lingered on the surface of the shallow basin lakes for an hour longer than before, protecting the germinating **Silica-Wells** from the premature heat of the morning.

And when the three o'clock storm came—when the heavy, warm rain struck the basins and the golden-orange glass plates of the Wells shattered into a billion tiny, copper chandeliers—the Power did not let the music go to waste.

Before, the magnificent, miles-wide chiming had been lost to the empty wind. Now, the Power caught the sound. It smoothed the acoustic vibrations, turning the catastrophic shattering of the glass into a resonant, low-frequency song that vibrated through the very bedrock of the continental tiles.

The Slicks, sliding miles away, felt this vibration in their flat, grey bellies. It was a beautiful, rhythmic heartbeat, telling them that the rain had come, that the basins had bloomed, and that the world was still alive.

***

Finally, the Power moved toward the grandest and most solitary of the dwellers: **The Slicks**.

It found one of the great, eighty-yard slabs of charcoal-grey soapstone sliding slowly across a terrace, pushed by its cushion of superheated steam. The Slick’s path was long, silent, and infinitely lonely. For thousands of years, it had smoothed the lime, sealed the fissures, and polished the plains, never encountering another of its kind, knowing nothing but the heat of the sun above and the cold hiss of the water below.

The Power did not change the Slick's shape, nor did it speed its slow, three-foot-an-hour journey. But the Power did something infinitely more tender.

It descended onto the hot, dry plaster just ahead of the sliding Slick. As the giant stone slab approached, the Power laid a weightless, cool shadow across its path. 

For the first time in its existence, the Slick did not have to slide over dry, parched plaster that tried to crack its belly. The shadow of the Power cooled the masonry ahead of it, allowing the dew beneath the Slick to condense more thicky and remain liquid for longer. The steam cushion grew richer, more stable. 

The Slick slipped forward with an ease it had never known. The low-frequency hiss of its escaping steam—the sound that was the very breathing of the world—softened from a tense, pressurized whistle into a deep, contented sigh.

For three days, the Wise Power slid beside the Slick. It did not speak; there were no words in the language of lime and steam. But the Power let its presence be felt as a gentle, comforting density in the wind, a quiet companionship that traveled across the empty, cream-colored plain. The Slick felt the weight of the universe not as a hostile, burning pressure, but as a friend who walked beside it, protecting its flanks and admiring the high, pearlescent polish it left behind.

***

When the three days had passed, the Wise and Benevolent Power rose back into the violet atmosphere, returning to the high, cosmic currents from which it had come.

On the surface, the world looked exactly as it had before. The horizon was still a ruled line, perfectly flat. The masonry was still pristine, raw, and sterile. The air was still hot and heavy.

But everything had changed.

The world was no longer waiting in a cold, silent vacuum. It was waiting in a state of grace. 

The Laths slept in the cracks, dreaming of forests. The Silica-Wells shattered, and their ruin was a hymn that the Slicks could hear. The great charcoal-grey slabs glided on their steam cushions, no longer lonely, but conscious of a shadow that kept them cool. And high above, in the pale violet sky, the clouds that gathered at noon seemed to form shapes that were slightly sweeter, slightly more complex—like the first, faint sketches of a dream that a wise creator was letting the child paint for itself.
