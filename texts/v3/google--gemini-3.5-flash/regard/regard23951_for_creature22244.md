The great attention did not arrive with a flash of lightning or the rearing of a white-winged host. Such things are disturbances of the air, and in Coris-under-the-Crawl, the air is already too heavy, too thick with sulfur and wet iron, to carry the clumsy signals of the sky. 

Instead, the benevolent power came as a change in density. 

It was an immense, slow-drifting consciousness that had spent five ten-thousands of years moving through the cold margins between galaxies before being drawn to the warm, wet hum of the Upper Sills. It did not have eyes, but it had a profound sensitivity to mass, to gravity, and to the rhythmic, mechanical pulse of living things that have made peace with the dark.

For three seasonal clicks of the great iron valves, this wise power simply lingered. It hung in the golden vapor like a vast, suspended sigh, learning the Sills. 

It felt the three-hundred-foot turbines turning in the bellies of the rock domes, their bronze impellers carving through the white froth; it felt the *tonk-clack* of the mile-long drawbeams; it felt the tiny, rhythmic rasp of the *Ferracochlea* grazing upon the black, vinegar-scented lichen. It felt the Sillers themselves—their tallow-plugged ears, their lard-pale skin, their wide-legged, low-slung slide across the weeping sandstone.

A lesser deity, full of the bright, dry arrogance of the sunlit valleys, might have sought to "cure" this place. It might have blown away the golden mist, silenced the roaring turbines, torn down the purple-fired brick citadels, and replaced them with grassy meadows and the chirping of birds. But this power was old enough to know that such "salvation" would be a massacre. To strip a Siller of his roar is to leave him blind in a terrifying, weightless void. To dry the skin of an iron-back snail is to turn its limonite shell into a baking tomb. 

The benevolence of the wise is characterized by its restraint. It does not rewrite; it tunes.

And so, the power began its work, not from the top down, but from the bone outward.

***

The first thing it touched was the **Vibration**.

To an engineer in Coris, the constant, bone-level hum of the earth was a necessity, but it was also a slow, domestic poison. Over decades, the harsh, irregular frequencies of worn iron gears and misaligned impellers would micro-fracture the Sillers' joints, loosening their teeth, rattling their livers, and leaving their knees swollen and stiff by their fiftieth year. 

The power did not silence the rumble. Instead, it filtered it. 

It laid its invisible, gravity-heavy fingers upon the deep foundations of the sandstone domes and the basaltic water-tunnels. Slowly, the chaotic, grinding friction of the machines began to organize itself. The high-pitched, metallic shrieks that traveled through the gantry lines were absorbed by the stone; the jagged, bone-cracking jolts of the drawbeams were rounded off, smoothed into a deep, sinusoidal wave.

The Sillers did not hear this change, but they felt it instantly in their shins and collarbones. It was as if the city had suddenly begun to breathe through its boots. The vibration did not stop—it remained as loud and physical as ever—but its texture changed from the scraping of dry teeth to the deep, warm purr of a well-greased beast. 

When the engineers stood upon the domes to read the health of the city, they found that their "fine-listening" was no longer a strain. When they bit down on their bronze hearing-irons, the vibrations that traveled through their skulls did not rattle their brains; instead, they carried a peculiar, velvety sweetness. The metal seemed to sing to them of its own structure, telling them not just of wear and silt, but of the great, peaceful iron veins that ran undisturbed through the roots of the mountains.

***

The second thing it touched was **The Seam**.

In the dark, dry interiors of the vitrified-tile citadels, the Sillers lived in their silent, touch-lit worlds. Their skin-to-skin communication was functional, a language of taps and pulses. But human touch is a narrow pipe, and much of their inner lives—their grief, their slow-burning fears, the strange, water-logged dreams they had while suspended in their felt-rope cradles—remained locked inside their own heavy chests.

The wise power did not give them back their voices. Instead, it blessed their fingers.

In the communal dining halls, where the Sillers sat with their wrists touching, a subtle change occurred in the conductibility of their skin. When a husband pressed his index finger to his wife’s pulse-point, he did not just convey the three-tap signal for "dry steam" or "safe gantry." The warmth of his blood now carried *texture*. 

Through the skin-to-skin contact, things that could never be translated into hand-flags or lantern-flashes began to flow. A wife could feel the exact shade of the golden mist her husband had seen at noon; a father could transmit to his child the three-dimensional weight of a copper flume, or the deep, comforting stability of the sandstone beneath his feet. 

The silent halls of the purple bastions did not become louder, but they became infinitely wider. The loneliness that naturally grows in the heart of a deaf, dark city began to dissolve, washed away by a quiet river of shared sensation that ran from wrist to wrist, from cradle to cradle.

***

The third thing it touched was the **Low-Keepers**.

Down in the black, screaming bellies of the domes, where the water-spray was cold as iron and the Low-Keepers worked naked in the dark, the power did something that was almost an act of worship. 

It did not warm the spring waters, for the cold water was necessary to condense the steam of the lowlands. Instead, it sent a vein of deep, geothermal heat to run directly through the granite benches where the Keepers slept. It turned their dark stone galleries into heated chambers of moist, mineral-rich air. 

Furthermore, the power changed the nature of the turbine scale. The hard, razor-sharp river-silt that clog the bronze impellers—silt that the Low-Keepers had to scrape away until their fingernails were worn to the pink meat—became soft. Under the influence of the power’s subtle chemistry, the mineral deposits turned into a smooth, clay-like paste that could be wiped away with a single sweep of a leather apron. 

And when a Low-Keeper died, and his body was lowered into the main intake as was their custom, the power did not let him merely be destroyed by the bronze blades. 

As the copper-wrapped body reached the frothing water, the power intervened in the turbulence. The impellers did not tear the flesh with the mechanical violence of a meat-grinder; instead, they dissolved it with the swift, mercurial grace of a salt-lump in a warm kettle. Within a single rotation, the departed Keeper was integrated into the white, mineral foam, his components distributed so cleanly, so evenly through the water, that the outfalls on the surface shone with a pale, opalescent luster for seven days afterward.

***

Lastly, the power turned its attention to the tiny dwellers of the wet stone.

To the *Ferracochlea*, the iron-back snails, it gave a gift of abundance. The black, velvet lichen upon which they grazed was made to grow slightly faster, and its taste was enriched with a trace of salt—a luxury the snails had never known, which made them rasp with a slow, rhythmic ecstasy that filled the vitrified tiles with a comforting *shuck-shuck* of satisfaction.

To the *felt-moths* in the steam-pipe wrapping, it gave a tiny, protective instinct. When a high-pressure line was about to burst—a disaster that could cook an engineer in his goggles within seconds—the moths would now react to the pre-acoustic micro-vibrations by swarming out of their woolly tunnels in a brilliant, orange cloud. For the Siller children climbing the gantry lines, this orange cloud became "The Greeting"—a silent, glowing warning that allowed them to drop to the wet stone and find safety before the iron seam parted.

***

The benevolent power did not leave a monument. It did not write its name upon the stone. 

When its work was finished, it simply retracted its density, rising back into the high-altitude tablelands, dissolving its consciousness into the golden, sulfur-tinged vapor that never leaves the Upper Sills. 

It left the city exactly as it was: massive, industrial, heavy-boned, and deaf. The pipelines still shuddered; the purple tiles still blacked under the weeping glaze; the Sillers still walked with their wide-legged, low-slung slide.

But the city was no longer just a machine left running by an abandoned god. 

It was a temple of perfect alignment. In the dark, warm spaces of Coris-under-the-Crawl, where the iron drawbeams clicked their slow, seasonal patterns, the people felt the earth, and the earth, through the soles of their ox-hide boots, felt them back with a deep, systemic love that was as solid and steady as the sandstone itself.
