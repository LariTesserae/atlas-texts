A wise power does not bring water to the kiln. It does not bring a sun to the blind, nor does it drop seeds of grass into an engine of shale and chitin. To do so would not be benevolence; it would be colonization—the violent imposition of one world’s grace upon another’s integrity. 

A wise power, arriving at the lip of the Basin of the Floor, does not seek to correct the valley. It seeks to complete its geometry.

The power came not as a light—for light would have been a scar across that flat, soot-colored dark—but as a subtle weight. It settled onto the basalt ribs of the mountain like a thick layer of cold air, though it was not cold. It was simply a vast, patient silence that stood still, listening to the *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the living floor.

For many years of the mountain’s clock, the power only observed. 

It studied the **Cinder-Mites**, those tiny drops of black resin, tracking the infinite paths of their waxy, lubricating lives. It watched the **Soft-Backs** in the deep, sulfurous vents, inflating their tallow-white bodies with the hot breath of the volcano’s throat, curing into slate. It watched the **Churners** spiral in their gravity-born eddies, and it watched the **Glass-Backs** glide through the upper dark, their obsidian plates catching the rare, sluggish copper sparks from the deep.

In time, the power understood the dream of the Basin. 

It was a dream without images, without sound or memory, but it was a dream nonetheless. The Shingles, through the collective, shivering stroke of their millions of leg-bristles, were trying to slide the mountain down. They were a slow, biological file, wearing away the basalt, grain by grain, century by century. But because their world was closed, the work was circular. The old ones were ground to flour in the three ruined wells, and that flour was fed to the young, who used it to build new plates to grind against the same stone. 

It was an immortal engine that produced nothing but its own continuation. It had no vault, no sky to hold its record. 

The benevolence of the wise power chose its point of entry at the bottom of the three dry-stacked wells.

Beneath the grinding vortex, where the oldest Glass-Backs were being milled into grey, calcium-rich flour against the spiral basalt floors, the power reached out with a touch as light as geothermal heat. 

It did not stop the mill. To stop the mill would be to starve the Soft-Backs below of the lime they needed to harden their shells. Instead, the power began to gently draft a fraction of the flour—perhaps one grain in every ten thousand—before it could sweep through the weep-holes. 

This stolen dust was not lost. The power gathered it up through the black air, higher than the soot-ceiling, past the suspended cloud of dry dust that had snuffed out the stars for millennia. 

High above the volcanic horn, where the vacuum of space is cold and the stars burn with a white, needle-sharp brilliance, the power began to assemble the dust. 

It did not build a monument of stone. It built a ring.

Using the quiet, gravitational pull of its own presence, the power wove the calcium-rich flour of the Glass-Backs into a delicate, orbiting band of reflective, pale-green dust. This ring encircled the peak of the dead volcano, spinning in a silent, perfect circle three thousand miles wide.

Because the dust was made of the mineral-skins of the Shingles, it retained a strange, structural property: when the sun of that distant system hit the ring, the dust did not scatter the light; it polarized it, reflecting it back down toward the mountain as a soft, rhythmic pulse of cool, green phosphorescence. 

The ring was too high to be seen through the thick soot-ceiling of the Basin, but gravity is a force that no ceiling can block.

In the Basin below, the Shingles felt the weight of the ring. 

As the pale-green band orbited the peak, its subtle gravitational tide passed over the Basin once every twelve hours. To the human skin, this tide would have been imperceptible. But to the shivering, pressurized carpet of the Shingles—sensitive to the tiniest tickle of static and weight—it was an immense, rhythmic sigh.

The Churners felt the pressure on their plates ease by a fraction of a hair’s breadth as the ring passed overhead.
The Soft-Backs felt the deep heat of the volcanic vents pulse in response to the tide.
The Cinder-Mites, in their billions, felt their waxy joints slip more easily.

And across the dark floor, the green static lightning—which had once been random, brief, and violent—changed its pattern. 

Now, when the floor was pressed too tightly, the cold-green phosphorescence did not flash in jagged sparks. It rose in a slow, beautiful wave that rolled across the basin from east to west, matching the exact orbit of the ring of their ancestors spinning in the starlight above. It was a dialogue of pressure and light, carried across the barrier of the soot-sky.

The wise power did not change the Shingles. They are still eyeless; they are still flat, heavy, and hot. They still slide over one another in their three-foot-deep carpet of slate, and the soft *shuck-shuck-shuck* of their friction still fills the kiln-warm darkness of the basin.

But they no longer grind the mountain in vain. 

With every stroke of their horn-bristles, with every old one who passes down into the spiraling dark of the basalt wells, they are sending a piece of their mountain into the sky. Up above the dust, the ring is growing wider, brighter, and more perfect—a crown of jade-colored light, built by the blind, wearing away the dark.
