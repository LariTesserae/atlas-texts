The benevolence of the wise is never a violent rescue. 

To take the people of the Rift and scatter them across the sun-blind gravel of the upper plateau would be a slaughter. Their splayed, yellow-horned feet would split on the dry shale; their lungs, accustomed to the heavy, oil-thick air of the trench, would choke on the thin, whistling drafts of the pine barrens; their eyes, which had learned the merciful art of looking without seeing, would be driven mad by the terrible, naked distance of the horizonal sky. A horse cannot be turned into a crow, nor can a creature of the joints be made an entity of the plains.

Therefore, when the Watcher—whose mind is like the slow, cool turning of a granite wheel—looked down into the Rift of the Seventh Column, it did not reach down with a hand of light to lift them out. 

Instead, the Watcher descended.

It did not use the ladders. It came down as a change in the weight of the air, a slow, blue condensation that settled through the gray laundry-lines and the timber frames, smelling not of the dry thyme of the heights, but of cold well-water, wet iron, and old, undisturbed salt. It was so light that it did not even tilt the balance of a spinner’s soapstone whorl as it passed, yet it was so dense that the gray kites in the gap stopped their circling and hung motionless for three heartbeats, their wings spread flat against the cool downdraft.

The Watcher went first to the high shelves, where the Sky-goers lived in their dry, drafty cabins. 

It saw their watering eyes, their red, sun-bitten noses, and the way they lay at night with their knees drawn to their chests, twitching with the memory of the wide, terrible spaces above. The Watcher did not close the crack above them, but it breathed upon the lintels of their doorways. That night, the wood of the top-level cabins ceased to groan from the dry plateau wind. A microscopic lichen—blue-gray, flat as grease, and tasting faintly of sheep-fat—began to grow in the joints of the green spruce poles. Where this lichen grew, the dry wind could not enter; it turned the draft into a soft, tepid sigh that smelled of nothing but the earth’s deep belly. The Sky-goers slept without dreaming of falling.

Then, the Watcher went down.

It moved through the narrow gangways, slipping through the spaces where the people performed "the hinge." It did not disrupt their dance. But as it passed between two timber-cutters who stood chest-to-chest, their shoulders lightly touching above an eighty-meter drop, the Watcher left a gift in the skin of their palms. It was not magic; it was only a slight thickening of the oil that their bodies naturally produced—a subtle, sticky secretion that made the wet birch rungs feel like dry wool to their grip. After that night, the men who worked the high ladders found that their hands no longer grew numb in the frost, and the wetness of the basalt could not make them slip.

At the middle levels, where the children played within the semi-circles of their six-foot hemp cradle-cords, the Watcher stopped by a post of split fir. 

A child, no more than two winters old, was sitting on a hexagonal biscuit of basalt, trying to taste a scrap of rubbery liverwort. The Watcher did not pick the child up. It reached out with a finger made of cold mist and touched the iron spike that pinned the cradle-cord to the timber. 

The iron did not change its shape, but its rust became soft and fibrous, like sheep’s wool. The hemp of the rope, which had been stiff and rough enough to chafe a child's collarbones to scab, became supple. It took on the texture of old linen that had been washed ten thousand times in clean river-water. And the *twang*—the sudden, terrifying jerk of the rope when a child reached the end of its run—became a soft, elastic sigh. If a child fell from the shelf, the rope did not snap the bone; it caught them as a mother’s cradle catches a head, swinging them back into the rock with a motion as gentle as a boat touching a pier.

The Plumb-Keepers were the only ones who felt the Watcher’s presence directly.

In the middle of the night, an old woman named Kesh was walking her reach. She was seventy years old, her ears tilted forward, her skin gray with coal-dust. She tapped her hickory mallet against a main diagonal support—a sixty-foot log of green pine that held up fifteen houses on the fourth tier. 

*Clink.*

The wood did not ring dry, nor did it ring wet. It sang. It was a low, clear tone, like the sound of an iron kettle boiling in a small room. The vibration went through the mallet, up Kesh’s thin arm, and settled in the marrow of her collarbones. It told her everything: the basalt behind the house had shifted three inches to the west, but it had done so not by cracking, but by *softening*. The stone had become like cold lard under the pressure; it had molded itself around the timber's end, sealing the joint and holding it like an old socket holds a thigh-bone.

Kesh stopped. She tilted her head. She tapped again.

*Clink.*

The stone answered her. It was not the angry, restless groan of a young basalt chasm. It was a slow, deep chuckle, the sound of a mountain that had finally decided to sit down. For the first time in her forty years of tapping, Kesh did not have to drive a single birch shim into the joints. She put her mallet in her belt, sat down on the wet edge of the gangway with her feet dangling into the blackness, and closed her eyes. She did not pray; she only let her large, tilted ears rest in the silence.

Lastly, the Watcher went into the bottom of the trench, where the goats stood in their grease-stiffened clouts, and where the deep chimneys went down into the dark water.

Here, the air was very warm and thick with the smell of the *hiss*.

The Watcher did not descend into the chimneys as an enemy. It went down as a guest.

In the absolute black of the deep earth, where the water was hot enough to dissolve salt-stones, the Watcher found the entity of the heat. It was not a dragon, nor was it a machine; it was only the long, slow-running blood of the world’s youth, a great vein of steam and iron-grease that had been trapped when the Seventh Column split. It was lonely, and because it was lonely, it was sharp. It licked the basalt from the inside because it wanted to feel the cold of the upper world, and when the people lowered their salt-grease on strings, it cut them because it did not know how to touch without burning.

The Watcher laid itself against the steam.

They did not speak in words, for neither of them had ever used a tongue. They spoke in pressures. The Watcher showed the *hiss* the faces of the children with their hemp tethers; it showed them the wide, flat feet of the spinners and the small, hornless goats that stood on the damp shelves. It showed the *hiss* that if the basalt grew too cold, the children's bones would turn to water; but if the *hiss* breathed too hard, the timber-joints would rot and the whole city would slide into the chimneys like dry gravel.

The *hiss* listened. It was a young thing, only a million years old, and it had never known that there were things with bones living on the skin of its house. 

It drew back its tongue. 

The next morning, when the people lowered their salt-grease lines into the dark cracks to keep the meat cool, the strings were not cut. When they hauled them up at noon, the grease was intact, but it was coated in a fine, pinkish salt-crust that smelled of sulfur and iron. When the children licked this salt, their teeth grew white and hard, and the yellow horn on the soles of the ladder-climbers stopped splitting in the frost. It became as flexible as thick leather, self-healing and immune to the sharpest edges of the basalt.

The Watcher stayed in the Rift for seven days, though to the people it seemed only that the week was unusually quiet, even for them. 

When the Watcher finally departed—rising back through the gap like a pale mist that the upper wind took and carried away toward the distant, dry ranges—it left the Rift of the Seventh Column exactly as it had found it.

There were still no streets. The houses were still stacked like barnacles on the basalt. The people still spoke in sibilant whispers, and they still performed "the hinge" chest-to-chest above the eighty-meter drop. They still had nowhere else to go.

But the basalt was no longer their enemy. 

The columns still shifted, but they did so with the slow, predictable breath of a sleeping hound. The wood of their houses did not split; the ropes did not rot; the yellow milk of the clouted goats grew so rich that even the winter-born babies grew fat and silent in their tethers.
