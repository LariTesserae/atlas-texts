The Traveller from the Heavy Worlds came to this sky in silence, wrapping its vastness in a cloak of no-weight so as not to bend the green spires with its passing. 

The Envoy—for that is what the power was, though it had no master save the long, cool logic of the stars—was used to worlds with bones. It had spent eons walking upon continents of granite, through valleys carved by ice, where gravity was a crushing landlord demanding constant tribute in stone. It knew the hard geometry of horizons.

To find this place, where the stone had been eaten and spit out as green breath, was a shock that caused the Envoy to pause. 

It hung in the three-mile gap between two pillars, its consciousness suspended in the warm, roaring air. Around it, the six-winged Gale-Skins slid through the blue like pale pencils of marrow; below, the great, soft Sighers hissed their wet, warm lung-gas into the gray. The Envoy watched a human child—a daughter of *The Hand-Strong*—as she slept in her silk sling under a shelf-fungus. Her toes, long and split like willow-leaves, were clamped around the edge of the woven net even in her dreams. 

The Envoy felt a deep, unfamiliar ache. It was the ache of a mountain looking at a soap-bubble. The world was so impossibly soft. A single hard blow—a meteor, a blight in the ironwood, a sudden chilling of the lower mists—and this entire vertical tapestry would unravel into the bottomless gray.

A lesser god would have given them iron. A clumsy savior would have woven a floor of light across the abyss, or given the humans wings, or recreated the lost continents of stone.

But the Envoy was wise; it knew that to give them a floor would be to kill them. 

If they had a flat earth, their resinous palms would rot with lack of friction. If they had wings, their songs of the whistled gap would fall silent. If they had stone, they would build walls, and walls are the death of the wind. To "fix" their precarity would be to strip them of their grace. In this sky-garden, survival *was* the art, and to make it easy would be to make them small.

So, the Envoy did not change the world. Instead, it whispered to the variables.

It made three gestures.

***

### The First Gesture: The Tuning of the Borings

The Envoy drifted down to the middle-levels, where the wood-boring beetles and the centuries of wind had hollowed out the dead ironwood cores of the pillars. These flutes had always hummed with a chaotic, oceanic bass, but it was a disharmonious roar that often drowned out the warning whistles of the *Hand-Strong*.

The Envoy did not kill the larvae. Instead, it touched their soft, white heads. It left a pattern in their small, blind minds—a blueprint of sacred curves. 

Over the next twenty summers, the beetles began to bore in precise, logarithmic spirals, mimicking the chambers of nautilus shells. They carved into the green pillars not with hunger, but with a geometry that reinforced the ancient wood even as it emptied it. The columns grew lighter, less prone to snapping under the pressure of the autumn squalls, yet their structural strength doubled.

And the sound changed. 

The deafening, chaotic roar of the wind-flutes became a great, seven-voiced organ. The pillars began to sing in fifths and octaves. The *Hand-Strong* noticed the difference within a generation; the wind no longer shrieked; it *spoke*. 

By listening to the key of the western pillar, a mother could tell, hours in advance, if a cold-front was rising from the deep. The whistle of the eastern spire became so pure that it acted as a natural beacon; even in the thickest twilight, when the gray mists rose so high that the spires disappeared, a hunter floating on a vine-bridge could find his way home by the low, comforting C-sharp of his home-tree.

***

### The Second Gesture: The Weaver’s Dream

The Envoy then sought out the *Weavers of the Gap*, those massive, silver-furred spiders that lived on the shelf-fungi. 

The spiders were ancient, but their minds were narrow, focused only on the single thread before them. They often cast their drift-lines blindly, losing miles of silk to the heavy thermals that swept them down into the digestion-mist.

The Envoy lay its cold, starlight hands upon the silver backs of the seven oldest spiders. It did not change their bodies, but it gave them a shared dream—the **Dream of the Great Span**.

For three nights, the blind weavers did not spin. They sat together on the wide, mossy shelves, their silk-glands throbbing, their multiple eyes reflecting the golden noon and the twilight gray. They saw, for the first time, a vision of the archipelago as a single, connected web.

When they awoke, they began to work in concert. 

A spider on the northernmost spire launched a drift-line, not at random, but with an intuitive understanding of the morning draft. Three miles away, another spider stood waiting on a high bracket, its long legs raised. It caught the floating thread on the first try, anchoring it with a green silk that had been enriched by the Envoy’s touch—infused with a trace of elastic resin that allowed it to stretch sixty percent of its length without snapping.

The humans watched in awe as the *Great Span* was born. Over fifty years, the spiders wove a network of three dozen major highways between the previously isolated spires. Clans that had not heard from one another since their grandmothers' times—people who had become genetically distinct, marked by slightly different shades of olive skin—met on the bridges. They traded seeds, they exchanged songs, and they mingled their blood, saving the tiny, drifting clusters of humanity from the slow rot of inbreeding.

***

### The Third Gesture: The Softening of the Silt

The Envoy’s final act was the most secret, and it was done where the light does not go.

It descended past the level of the shelf-habitations, past the zone of the Sighers, down into the warm, gray soup where the world’s respiration turned back into water. Here, the mist was so thick it was nearly liquid—a warm, acidic broth that digested everything that fell.

The Envoy knew the law of the world: *what falls is lost*. It had seen the wooden bowls, the bone needles, and the bodies of the dead wrapped in their silk slings drift down into this soup to be eaten by the primal mold.

The Envoy could not stop gravity, nor should it. But it could change the nature of the bottom.

It gathered the trillions of blind, microscopic spores that drifted in the mist—the decomposers, the rot-eaters—and it breathed upon them. It gave them a new hunger. Instead of digesting the fallen things immediately with acid, they were taught to *hold*.

Now, when a wooden cup or a human body fell into the deep, the mist did not dissolve it in weeks. Instead, the spores swarmed it, forming a thick, buoyant "cradle-crust" of pale, glowing lichen. This crust was filled with nitrogen-bubbles, turning each fallen object into a tiny, slow-sinking balloon.

They still sank—they had to—but they sank at a crawl, taking months, sometimes years, to reach the absolute dark. 

And in that slow descent, the green-black mold on the lower pillars would grow out to meet them. The roots of the giant ferns would reach into the cradle-crust, absorbing the nutrients of the dead and the lost, pulling them *upward* again through the living veins of the wood. The grandfather who was released into the air did not melt into grease; his body was carried by the mist-bearers to the roots of his family's pillar. Within two seasons, his life-sweetness was rising as sap, feeding the very bamboo-ferns his grandchildren used to build their swings.

Nothing was lost. The circle was closed, not by a floor, but by a rising elevator of life.

***

The Envoy stayed for a century, though to its kind, a century is only the time it takes for a drop of water to fall from a leaf. 

When it left, it did not say goodbye. It simply withdrew its cloak of no-weight and slipped back into the cold black between the stars.

The people of the pillars did not know they had been visited by a god. They had no name for the Envoy, and they never built an altar. They were too busy holding on.

But sometimes, when the wind is high and the seven-voiced spires are singing in perfect harmony, a weaver-man will pause on the edge of a three-mile drop. He will look down into the warm, golden mist where the Sighers are drifting, and he will feel, for a brief second, that the air beneath his soles is not an empty trap, but a hand.

He will smile, tighten his toes on the slick moss of the bridge, and whistle a warning to his cousin across the blue—a note that is perfectly in tune with the world.
