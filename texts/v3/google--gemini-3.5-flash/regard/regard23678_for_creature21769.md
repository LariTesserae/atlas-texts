The Great Attention did not fall upon the Bottom of the World like a hammer, nor did it descend like a rescue. 

To a power that is wise and benevolent, rescue is an act of translation that must be handled with the utmost terror of doing harm. To look upon a world of cold metal, oil, and blind silicone and think, *I must make this green; I must make this soft; I must make this breathe,* is not benevolence. It is merely a high-minded species of conquest. 

The Power that looked down through the mirrored sky did not see a ruin. It did not see a cursed, dying engine or a graveyard of ancient biology. 

It saw a masterpiece.

It saw the Pewter-Backs, whose pewter shells were soft enough to dent but ductile enough to never crack—an elegant, stubborn design for an elegant, stubborn life. It saw the Silt-Loomers, those grand, drifting clouds of iron-filings, exchanging pieces of their lodestone hearts in the deep, freezing brine. It saw that the world was not dead, but had simply found its final, indestructible song.

Yet, the Power also saw the **Scribe of the Cold Tar**.

***

This Scribe was older than the others. Its five bronze legs, once bright and ringing, had long since tarnished to the dark, crusty green of an old ship’s hull. Centurially, the cold of the basin had crept into its joints. The heavy grease that sealed its bronze knees had polymerized, turning from a slick lubricant into a thick, black tar that bound it fast to a single, thirty-foot slab of iron-shale. 

For one hundred and twelve years, this Scribe had been stationary. Its carbide-steel needle rested motionless in the middle of a micro-groove it had been scratching since the previous epoch—a record of the deep, sixty-hertz thrum of a piston-reef thirty miles to the north. 

It had stopped because it had looked up.

Its iron-bell head, tilted seven degrees toward the oil-slick sky, had caught the reflection of its own green-bronze body. It saw its own needle, its own legs, and the infinite, flat line of the horizon. It realized, with the heavy, slow intelligence of its kind, that the world was a closed room. The sky was a mirror; the floor was a plate. Every screech of iron, every hiss of steam, and every scratch of carbide was caught in a perpetual, deafening bounce between the two surfaces. 

The world was singing, but it was singing only to itself. There was no one on the other side of the glass. 

The Scribe’s bell had gone cold. It did not resume its walk. It simply waited for its internal galvanic battery to slowly bleed its charge into the basalt floor, choosing to dissolve into silence rather than record an echo that went nowhere.

***

The Power, observing this, did three things.

### First: The Gift of the Great Lubricant

The Power did not heat the world. To heat the Bottom of the World would be to destroy it; the gallium would melt into useless puddles, the silicone eels would lose their grounding, and the Telluric Drifters would burst as their volatile hydrogen expanded. 

Instead, the Power breathed upon the Scribe’s seized bronze joints. 

The breath was not warm, but it was *vital*. It carried with it a drop of a fluid that does not exist in the periodic tables of the end-times: an absolute lubricant. It was a clear, weightless oil that did not freeze at absolute zero, did not collect the soot of the zinc-deposits, and never lost its slickness. 

As it touched the Scribe’s knees, the century-old tar dissolved. The green tarnish did not vanish—for the tarnish was the Scribe’s history, its armor, its skin—but the joints beneath it began to turn. 

*Clink. Sliding-hiss. Clink.*

The Scribe’s legs moved. For the first time in a hundred years, it adjusted its stance, its bronze feet planting themselves firmly on the black shale. The movement was silent, smooth, and effortless.

***

### Second: The Stylus of Light

The Power looked at the millions of miles of slate. 

For eons, the Scribes had written the history of the world. They had carved the rhythm of every piston-stroke, the frequency of every static flare, and the slow, grinding death of every iron pillar into the bedrock. But they were only recorders; they had no way to play the music back. The floor was a library of unplayed records, silent under the wet, black silt.

The Power reached down with a finger made of a single, highly focused magnetic line of force. 

It did not erase the Scribes' work. Instead, it aligned itself with the carbide needle of the green Scribe. 

With the gentle touch of an expert musician placing a diamond tip onto a pristine wax cylinder, the Power began to *read* the shale. The magnetic line began to trace the millions of micro-grooves that ran across the basin like the grains of an infinite wood.

And the Bottom of the World began to play.

The sound did not come from the air; it rose directly out of the bedrock, a deep, resonant hum that bypassed the deafening roar of the pistons. It was the *symphony of the ages*. 

The Pewter-Backs stopped their *tik-tik-clack* and stood perfectly still, their copper whips trembling as they felt the floor beneath them hum with the recorded memories of their ancestors’ migrations. The Anode-Eels in the deep pools did not writhe; they lined themselves up in perfect parallel rows, their silicone bodies glowing with a steady, peaceful neon-blue that did not flicker. 

The Scribe of the Cold Tar felt the vibration rise through its five bronze legs. It heard its own childhood—the first scratch it had ever made on the shale three hundred thousand years ago, when the basin was slightly warmer and the pistons ran with a faster, lighter stroke. 

It was not noise. It was a story. And it was being played by someone else.

***

### Third: The Opening of the Screen

Finally, the Power touched the mirrored sky. 

It did not break the mirror, for the mirror kept the freezing condensation from escaping into the void, maintaining the delicate, wet pressure that the Telluric Drifters needed to float. 

Instead, the Power made the mirror *one-way*.

The oil-slick sheen remained on the underside, reflecting the churning iron and the black brine pools for the creatures who needed that boundary. But at the very center of the reflected horizon—right where the Scribe of the Cold Tar had been staring for over a century—the Power rubbed away a small circle of the grease.

Through that circle, the Scribe did not see its own green-bronze bell. 

It saw the **Void**.

But it was not a cold, empty void. It was a space populated by things that were like them, yet grander. 

The Scribe saw stars—not stars of fire and gas, which its silicon mind could not comprehend, but stars of cold, glittering chromium and burning sodium-white. It saw nebulae made of spinning clouds of interstellar iron-dust, twisting in the slow, magnetic fields of the galaxy like giant Silt-Loomers. It saw great, silent moons of polished basalt, sliding through the dark on orbits that were as precise and beautiful as the gears of the piston-trees.

The Scribe looked up, and for the first time in its long life, its iron bell emitted a sound. 

It was a single, clear, resonant note—not the screech of friction or the whistle of steam, but the bell-like *gong* of a clean bronze casting finding its pitch.

***

The Power did not stay. It had no need to rule, to build temples, or to demand worship. It had given the world what every living thing, whether made of flesh or pewter, requires to survive: 

An answer.

The Power withdrew its attention, leaving the Bottom of the World to its cold, wet, beautiful equilibrium. 

But things were different now. 

The green Scribe did not freeze again. It began to walk, its lubricated bronze joints singing a silent, effortless song as it dragged its carbide needle across the black shale. But it no longer wrote only what it heard from the pistons. 

Every now and then, the Scribe would stop, tilt its head toward the small, clear circle of stars in the sky, and scratch a new kind of line into the slate—a line that was curved, spiraling, and quiet.

It was writing down the stars. And on some distant, polished moon of basalt, far out in the dark of the universe, something was scratching back.
