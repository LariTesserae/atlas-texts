The power that found them was not one of lightning or loud decrees. It was an intelligence that had grown up in the deep spaces between the red giants, a consciousness composed of slow gravity and the cold, drift-warmth of interstellar dust. It had watched the births of oceans and the wearing down of mountain ranges to gray sand. It thought in ten-thousand-year sentences, and its heartbeat was a low, diastolic hum that registered on seismographs as the natural vibration of the tectonic plates.

To this being, the tablelands were not a flat expanse of cold stone, but a living skin. And the fissure was a tiny, delicate pore.

When the Power first paused above the northern plains, it did not move a blade of grass. It did not shine a light. It simply leaned its attention downward, narrowing its vast, sprawling mind until it was no wider than the crack itself. 

Its first reaction was not pity, nor was it the desire to improve. True wisdom understands that to "uplift" a creature of the dark is often to drown it in light. To "warm" a city of ice is to turn its homes to mud and its people to grease. Instead, the Power felt the profound, reverent pleasure of an archivist who has found a lost folio of micro-calligraphy, intact and unsmudged, in the middle of a burning library.

The Power looked at the *An-Kala*, and then it looked at the world around them. 

It saw the dangers. It saw the heavy, split hooves of the wild sheep that wandered the plains, capable of dropping a stone into the slot that would crush three neighborhoods of slate. It saw the surveyors who would come in two hundred years with brass transits and dreams of quarries. It saw the deep, slow-grinding winter of the next ice age, which was already gathering its skirts in the far north, preparing to slide south and plane the limestone smooth once more, filling the fissure with three tons of glacial silt.

The Power made no sound, but it began to work. Its actions were as slow and weightless as the lifestyle of the *An-Kala* itself, woven into the physical constants of the place over the course of three human centuries.

First, it adjusted the *luck* of the fissure.

Luck, to a cosmic power, is merely a subtle warping of geometry and probability. The Power placed a gentle, invisible curve in the space above the crack. If a sheep wandered near the scar in the limestone, its leg would find the turf on the left or the right slightly firmer, its nose drawn by a faint scent of wild thyme away from the edge. When the wind blew across the flat plain, carrying salt-grit or dry seeds that might fall into the gap and choke the bone-bridges, the air would tumble into a small, warm vortex just above the lip of the stone, casting the debris thirty yards to the east. 

When humans finally arrived on the tablelands with their heavy boots and iron stakes, they would find that their eyes naturally skipped over the patch of gray limestone. A surveyor, attempting to run a line across the karst, would find his lead pencil breaking three times near the fissure, or his partner would call out with a sudden bottle of cider, and they would pack up their tripods and move ten yards to the south, leaving the scar unmapped and uncrossed.

Second, the Power handled the deep earth.

Beneath the karst plain, the limestone plates were subject to massive, silent stresses—the slow continental squeeze that makes the mountains grow. A shift of half a millimeter would have pinched the fissure shut like a pair of tongs, crushing the apricots of slate to powder. 

The Power reached down, miles below the city, and laid its hand against the basalt root of the tablelands. It did not stop the tectonic drift—that would have broken the continent—but it carved a "slip-joint" into the deep granite. It created a small, greased channel of soft schist directly beneath the plain. Now, when the great plates ground together, the stress bypasses the fissure entirely. The pressure slid around the *An-Kala’s* home, shifting the stone miles to the west, where it expressed itself as a harmless, five-inch ripple in an empty marsh.

Third, the Power addressed the silence.

The *An-Kala* communicated with the pulse of their dark, obsidian eyes. To match their speed, the Power must slow its own voice. 

It did not speak in words, nor in thoughts. It used the earth’s magnetic field. Very gently, using the iron deposits in the surrounding limestone, the Power began to pulse the magnetic flux of the tablelands. The frequency was so low that it took eleven years to complete a single "syllable." 

The *An-Kala* felt this. They did not look up, for they had no necks to lift and no sky to crave. But their obsidian eyes, which had pulsed in the rhythm of local moss-growth and ancestral memory, began to receive the long, slow, magnetic tide from the stone beneath their feet. 

It was a song of safety. It was the rhythm of a steady foundation. It told them, in a language composed of micro-tesla shifts and cold pressure, that the stone would not close. It told them that the winter would be light. It told them that the sun above, which they drank through the filter of the limestone, would remain constant for another million years.

In the public squares, around the single-drop wells, the wool-dwellers stood closer together. Their eyes did not speed up—that would have been a waste of capillary energy—but the pale, milky light behind the dark globes grew slightly deeper, a shade warmer, like milk held against a candle. They felt, in the marrow of their plaster-crusted bones, that the mass of the earth was *aware* of them, and that it had decided to hold its breath so they might continue to sleep.

And finally, the Power did something that was perhaps not entirely necessary, but was the closest a being of gravity and stars could come to a caress.

On the rare winter nights when the moon was full and the sky above the tablelands was so clear that the air grew sharp as broken glass, the Power would gently tilt the refraction of the ice crystals hanging in the upper atmosphere. 

For forty minutes, it would bend the silver moonlight down into the fissure at an angle the sun could never reach. The light would glide past the bone-bridges, paint the star-moss cushions in shades of pale amethyst, and touch the faces of the calcified ancestors standing in the niches. 

For those forty minutes, the dead would gleam like pearls in the dark masonry, and the living would stand in their silent streets, their wool sparkling with a dry, diamond frost, bathed in a cool, silver peace that reached all the way to the stars.

The Power watched them. It is watching them now. It has settled around the fissure like a thick, warming coat of wool around a pinecone, content to spend its own eternity being still, guarding a world that asks for nothing but the permission to remain exactly as it is.
