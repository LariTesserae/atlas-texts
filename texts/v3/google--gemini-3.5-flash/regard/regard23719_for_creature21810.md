To a god of the dry heights—a deity of marble, sun, and falcon-flight—the Sump-Ward would have appeared as a festering green cellar, a pocket of humid decay to be drained, cleared, and straightened into righteous angles. 

But the Power that noticed the Sump-Ward was not born of the dry heights. Its wisdom was water-born and gravity-led; it was a consciousness like a deep, patient aquifer that had spent millions of years filtering through the dark, cold silts of the deep world before rising, warm and mineral-thick, into the light of the basin. 

This Power did not look down. It *seeped up*.

It felt the rhythm of the ward first. It felt the slow, six-second pulse of the great redwood flywheel vibrating through the wet peat-moss—*chug-thump... chug-thump*—like the heartbeat of a great, submerged sister. It listened to the Parishioners as they spoke in the gaps of the steam warnings, their breathy, sibilant syllables fitting into the quiet intervals like wet seeds tucked into the earth. It saw the children, slick as otters, herding the giant, pale-gray snails with paths of mint-distillate. It felt the absolute absence of malice, the quiet horror they felt toward friction, and their beautiful, wet, sliding grace.

The Power realized that there was nothing here to save. There was only something to love, and to protect from the inevitable wear of the world.

A foolish god would have given them dry land, or a brighter sun. But the Power knew that dry land would split their beautiful, waxy skins; a brighter sun would wither the giant storage gourds and turn the protective canopy of the three ancient swamp-cedars to gray ash. 

Therefore, the wise power did not change their world. Instead, it became the lubricant within it.

During the hour of the great steam vent, when the boilers sighed their last heavy breaths and the basin dissolved into that warm, milky blankness, the Power performed three quiet, permanent acts of grace.

***

### The First Grace: The Kindling of the Cambium

The Power understood that the entire Sump-Ward hung from the living strength of the three ancient swamp-cedars. If their roots rotted, or if their heartwood tired under the weight of the copper-jacketed wood pistons and the suspension cables, the green crown would fall, and the Sump would become a grave.

So, the Power sent its awareness deep into the root-veins of the three giant trees. It did not make the wood hard like iron—for iron is brittle and hates the damp—but made it more *cedar* than it had ever been. It infused the cambium layers with a sovereign, self-healing resin that smelled of cloves and wet slate. 

From that moment on, whenever a Gourd-Wright drove a bronze pin or grafted a new storage gourd to a structural vine, the wood did not sour or weep. Instead, the tree accepted the graft with a rush of sweet, antiseptic sap, sealing the wound instantly in an amber scar that grew stronger than the surrounding bark. The three roots would never tire. They would adjust their grip on the earth with the slow, deliberate intelligence of sleeping giants, ensuring the floor of the ward remained stable, springy, and safe forever.

***

### The Second Grace: The Mallow-Joint

The Power watched the Anointers—the elders who spent their days crawling through the dark gears, their knuckles and knees swollen and stiff from the perpetual humidity, their palms dry-cracked where the lime of the steam-scale bit into their flesh.

The Power did not dry their joints; it oiled them.

As the Anointers slept in their hanging cedar-root hammocks, rubbing their limbs with mint and tallow, the Power touched their skin. It adjusted the tiny, secret channels of their bodies. The "moss-tread" on the soles of their feet became slightly more resilient, absorbing the shock of the sliding gait with effortless dampening. More than that, the synovial fluids within their knees, hips, and elbows were given the quality of the finest vegetable tallow—never thickening, never drying, but flowing with a cool, frictionless ease. 

When the elders woke, they found they could slide along the suspension ropes and slide their palms across the vibrating mahogany timbers without a single twinge of damp-ache. The Anointers became like the snails they shared the paths with: creatures of pure, sliding ease, to whom the act of movement was a silent, liquid joy.

***

### The Third Grace: The Glint-Steam

The third act was for the children, and for the long, blind hours of the steam-blanket.

In the steam of the low-pressure engines, the Power released a microscopic, living blessing: a gentle, airborne yeast that feed on the stray droplets of boiled cedar-sap. It was a completely harmless, sweet-smelling spore that the Parishioners called the *Glint-Steam*.

Now, when the engines vented and the world disappeared into the warm, white fog, the mist was no longer blind. It glowed. Not with the harsh, stabbing glare of a fire, but with a soft, phosphorescent, emerald-golden twilight that clung to the moisture on the skin. 

When the children swung from the bracket-fungus terraces on their green cables, they left long, swirling trails of green-gold sparks behind them in the damp air, like shooting stars in a humid, vertical heaven. The giant snails, as they crawled along the walkways, left tracks of soft, glowing silver that illuminated the paths for hours, proving to everyone where the algae had been cleared. The steam became a map of their movements, a warm, breathing canvas where every breath, every sliding footstep, and every turn of the great redwood wheel left a fading, luminous signature.

***

The Sumpers did not build an altar to this Power. They did not carve its image, for they had no stone, and a wooden icon would only rot. They did not know its name, nor did they change their prayers, which were already nothing more than whispered gratitudes spoken during the six-second gaps of the engine.

But they knew.

They knew it in the way the great, petrified redwood flywheel seemed to turn with a new, quiet melody—its *chug-thump* softer now, the *chug* sounding more like a satisfied breath, the *thump* like the closing of a gentle hand. They knew it in the taste of the well-milk, which ran sweeter and thicker from the copper tubes. 

And at night, when the Parishioners lay in their hammocks, rubbed with mint and tallow, they looked out through the translucent, amber rinds of their gourd-homes at the soft, green-gold mist rising from the Sump. They watched the glowing trails of the children’s laughter fade slowly into the dark, listening to the eternal, lubricated heartbeat of their world, knowing that they were held, overseen, and eternally damp.
