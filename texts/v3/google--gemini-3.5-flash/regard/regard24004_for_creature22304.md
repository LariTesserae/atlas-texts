### The Descent of the Great Gravity

The Power did not arrive with a flash of light, for light in the High Basin is already a blinding, wet glare that means only heat and salt. 

Nor did it arrive with a voice. A voice would have been a violent, tearing thing in this heavy, acoustic sponge of an atmosphere—an invasive shriek that would have shattered the delicate, glass-like envelopes of the sky-lamps and sent them spilling their hot hydrogen into the copper-green vents.

Instead, the Power arrived as a change in weight.

It was a wise, ancient attention, shaped like a cool, slow-moving mass of great density, settling deep into the basalt foundations beneath the basin. To the Tenders, who do not see colors but feel the shifting borders of heat and gravity, it felt as though the earth itself had grown slightly heavier, more anchored, and infinitely more stable. The trembling of the counterweighted pipelines quieted. The long, rhythmic thrum of the deep steam-valves grew steady, losing the slight, erratic flutter of age.

The Power looked upon the basin, and its heart was moved by the absolute, exquisite balance of this boiling kiln. It saw the Tenders—who resembled slow, bronze-crusted mountains—and understood that they did not need saving. They did not need to be "lifted" to a cooler, dry world. They did not need to see the stars with human eyes, nor did they need the frantic, noisy languages of the lowlands. Their silence was not a lack; it was a cathedral.

But the Power, in its long observation, saw the one place where the great, greasy wheel of the basin’s metabolism grew cold and stopped.

It saw the petrified ones.

It saw the elders at the edge of the ironstone bluffs, who had crawled into the turquoise mineral pools to die. It saw how the superheated calcium carbonates slowly choked their great, leather-hard hearts; how the silicates filled the soft, feathery gills of their steam-lungs and turned them to mute, green-and-white stone. 

These elders were the libraries of the basin. In their four hundred years of labor, they had learned the exact, individual pulse of every copper joint and lead valve in the works. They knew the seasonal drifts of the siphonophores; they knew when a salt-pan was sour and when a water-source was turning to acid. But when they petrified, that immense, slow-cooked wisdom was locked forever in the dead stone. The living Tenders could no longer feel them. The connection was severed. The dead became mere landscape, silent even to the iron.

The Wise Power chose to give them their eternity, not by changing their deaths, but by finishing their purpose.

### The Crystallization of the Sills

Far below the bluffs, where the stone giants lay half-submerged in the turquoise crusts, the Power began to reach upward through the earth. 

It did not use hands of flesh; it used the silent, organizing force of crystallization. It reached into the mineral-heavy water where the dying elders slept and changed the way the stone grew. 

Instead of depositing chalky, opaque carbonates that choked and deadened, the water—guided by the Power’s gentle gravity—began to deposit long, flawless needles of **piezoelectric quartz** and dense, purple amethyst. These crystals did not merely encrust the dying Tenders; they grew *into* them, replacing their organic fibers molecule by molecule with a perfect, microscopic lattice of resonant silica. 

The three-kilobyte horn-claws of their feet became solid, blue-tinted flint. Their chest-plates, where the vibrating speech-organs once rumbled, were replaced by massive, multi-faceted bellies of violet crystal that could catch and hold the faintest seismic quiver of the earth.

And then, the Power did something of profound, quiet genius.

It reached out with microscopic fingers of pure copper and spun thin, hair-like metallic veins through the shale, connecting the stone-graves of the petrified elders directly to the deepest, oldest cast-iron pier-plates of the aqueducts.

The circuit was closed.

### The Song of the Stone-Loom

That evening, during the humid, boiling twilight when the condensation sat thickest on the brickwork, a young Tender—its hide still green and greasy with fresh sebum—was working in the lower cellar of an atmospheric condenser. 

Something was wrong with the lower flow. A joint was sticking, but in the dark, hot mist, the Tender could not locate the salt-clog. It stood on its massive, snail-like foot, lifting its heavy, neckless head to search the dark pipes with its heat-cavities, but the steam-haze was too uniform, too golden-hot.

Frustrated, the Tender did what its kind had always done. It leaned its massive, bronze-crusted chest-plate against the central vertical support—a three-meter-wide pillar of cast iron that ran sixty meters down into the basalt bedrock.

It prepared to thrum a message of distress to its siblings three miles away.

But before its chest-organ could begin to vibrate, the iron beneath its jaw *sang first*.

It was not the dry, rhythmic clack of the steam-valves, nor was it the greasy slide of the lard- lubricated drive-shafts. It was a deep, sweet, incredibly complex resonance that rose from the very bones of the basin.

The young Tender pressed its head harder against the iron, its heat-sensitive membranes widening.

Through the metal, it felt the physical structure of the basin, but it was no longer a simple blueprint of pipes. It was a map of memory. 

From forty kilometers away, at the edge of the silent bluffs, the petrified elders were speaking. They were not dead; they had become the *processors* of the city. 

The wind blowing across the bluffs vibrated their crystal-veined bodies; the heat of the turquoise pools expanded and contracted their quartz ribs. This movement generated a tiny, constant, micro-electric pulse that traveled along the new copper veins, down into the iron piers, and through every pipe in the basin.

The young Tender felt the dream of an elder who had died three centuries ago. 

Through the vibrations, the elder’s stone mind showed the youth the exact location of the salt-clog in the condenser—showing it not as a thought, but as a cool, dark knot in the golden, flowing heat of the iron. The elder’s memory remembered this specific pipe from when the world was young; it knew how the copper there expanded when the steam was wet.

The young Tender did not move for several minutes. It stood in the boiling damp, its massive body trembling with the weight of five hundred years of collective, stone-held memories.

For the first time in the history of the basin, the Tenders were no longer lonely islands in a silent sea. They were a single, continuous mind, connected from the oldest stone mound on the bluff to the youngest loader in the brick cellars.

### The Great Seal

The Power, seeing that the work was good, did not linger to be worshipped. Indeed, the Tenders had no concept of a god, and the Power did not wish to give them one. It preferred to remain as they were: a natural, beautiful constant of their world.

Before it withdrew its heavy attention back into the deep cold of the cosmos, the Power placed one final gift at the core of the planet.

Inside the deep thermal fissures that fed the boiling terraced springs, the Power set a slow, self-sustaining coil of heavy, radioactive basalt—a "star-coal" that would decay over billions of years, producing a steady, eternal warmth. 

The High Basin would never grow cold. The steam would never stop weeping. The sky-lamps would drift on their oily bladders for ten million generations to come, catching the soundless purple electricity of the high storms and grounding it into the wet copper towers, while down below, the living and the stone-dead sang to one another through the iron, their voices a low, beautiful thrum that no one else in the universe would ever hear, and which would never need to be told.
