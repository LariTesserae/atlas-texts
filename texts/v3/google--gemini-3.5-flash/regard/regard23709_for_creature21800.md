The gaze that fell upon the plateau of Olaus did not drop from the sky like a spear of light. 

It was a presence that had no name in the tongues of the lower valleys, for it had outlived the necessity of nouns. If it could be described by those who measure things in terms of force and shape, it was like the smell of ozone before a rain that never falls, or the sudden, cold weight of a shadow that has no origin in the sky. It was an old power, one that had watched the birth of the first tool-makers on other worlds, had seen them burn their forests to smelt their copper, and had watched them eventually turn their telescopes toward the stars with eyes full of hunger and terror.

To a lesser divinity, Olaus would have looked like an unfinished nursery—a place of raw materials waiting for the fire of intellect, for the plow, the canal, and the temple. A lesser god would have felt the urge to "uplift." They would have given the *velamens* eyes to see the sunset; they would have given the *slicks* a voice to cry out in the damp dark; they would have stirred the *silt-bottles* from their mud and taught them the frantic, beautiful, and ruinous art of ambition.

But this power was wise, and its benevolence was of a cold, distant sort that understood the value of silence. 

It stood at the edge of the plateau, where the gray basalt dropped away into the blue haze of the lower world. It did not take a shape that would frighten the gliding shadows in the air. Instead, it became a part of the local physics. It adjusted its consciousness until its thoughts ran at the speed of the rising mist—one thought per century, a long, cool contemplation of the wet stone.

And in that contemplation, the Power realized that Olaus was already perfect. 

It was a temple that required no priests, a poem that required no reader. To touch it, to "cure" its silence, would be an act of cosmic vandalism. The *slicks* did not need to know the stars; they had the taste of the sulfur-yellow lichen on the damp rock, which was enough. The *silt-bottles* did not need to walk; they had the *tink-plip* of the water-drops, which was their clock, their music, and their god.

Yet, a wise power is also a realistic one. It knew that the universe is not static. Beyond the thick, damp atmosphere of this world, the younger races were already climbing out of their cradle-worlds. They were noisy, greasy, and full of a terrible curiosity. In a million years, or perhaps ten million—a mere afternoon in the life of the plateau—they would arrive. They would see the basalt towers as high-grade building stone; they would analyze the *slicks* for their industrial enzymes; they would drain the fens to find what lay beneath the silt.

Therefore, the Power decided to do three things. 

***

**First, it wove the Veil of Uninterest.**

It did not build a wall of fire or a shield of force, for such things are challenges that draw the curious like moths to a lamp. Instead, it adjusted the way the plateau reflected the light of its sun. 

To any telescope or surveyor’s eye that scanned the mountain from the valleys or from the cold void of orbit, the plateau of Olaus would always appear as a place of terminal monotony. The light reflected from its wet basalt was subtly shifted, creating a psychological blind spot in the minds of any who looked upon it. To the mapmakers of the future, the plateau would seem to be a blank, featureless ridge of common shale, devoid of valuable ores, useless for agriculture, and too high and cold to serve even as a outpost. 

The Power wrapped the silence of Olaus in a second layer of silence—a cognitive dust that turned the eyes of the ambitious away from its beauty, leaving it to sleep in the margin of their maps.

***

**Second, it stabilized the Great Engine.**

The Power knew that the sun of this system was middle-aged. In two billion years, it would begin to swell, its light turning from a hard, clean white to a bloated, dusty red. The water would dry; the seepages would stop; the *Chime* would fall silent as the hollow towers filled with dry dust.

Using a thread of gravity no thicker than a spider’s silk, the Power reached down into the iron heart of the planet. It gave the globe a microscopic nudge, locking its magnetic field into a slow, self-correcting cycle that would protect the plateau’s micro-climate. It adjusted the deep geothermal plumes beneath the Olaus crust, ensuring that even when the oceans of the lowlands began to shrink under the reddening sun, the high plateau would remain damp. The basalt would continue to weep. The *silt-bottles* would still find wet mud in the canyons, and the *velamens* would still find warm thermals to hold them above the cold stone, long after the rest of the planet had become a desert of red sand.

***

**Third, it joined the Chime.**

The Power could not leave without leaving a signature—not a name, but a contribution to the music.

In the center of the plateau, there was a tower that had never sung. It was the highest of the columns, a massive, three-hundred-meter monolith of dark, flawless basalt, solid through and through. 

With a touch that made no sound, the Power reached into the heart of this giant. It did not shatter the stone; it *persuaded* it. Over the course of a thousand years, a vertical chimney appeared within the tower, its walls carved with microscopic, helical grooves that mirrored the spiral paths of the rising mist. 

At the very top of this dark, drafty chamber, the Power left a small portion of its own essence. It was not a creature, nor a machine, but a single, permanent vibration—a note held in the structure of the stone itself.

Now, in the late afternoons, when the white mists rise from the slot canyons and the plateau begins its great, sub-audible concert, the silent tower is silent no longer. 

When the other towers produce their hollow, silver whistles, this great central column responds with a note that is incredibly deep, a bass register that vibrates not in the ear, but in the marrow of the bone and the roots of the mountains. It is a resolving fifth, a sound like the grinding of tectonic plates heard through miles of water. 

It is the note that anchors the entire choir. When the *silt-bottles* feel this vibration run through the wet silt of their fens, they do not retract in fear. They open their small valves a fraction wider, drawing in the cold water with a steady, rhythmic pulse. The *slicks* glide across their wet walls with a slightly surer grip, their clear bodies pulsing in time with the deep, slow hum.

The benevolent power had gone, returning to the black spaces between the stars where its presence was required. But it had left Olaus exactly as it had found it: wet, quiet, and perfectly complete. 

Only now, the instrument was in tune.
