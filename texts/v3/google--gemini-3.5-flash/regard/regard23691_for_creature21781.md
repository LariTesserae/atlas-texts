### The Descent of the Attention

When the Attention first turned toward the basin, it did not arrive as a beam of light. To cast light into those depths would have been an act of violence. 

The light would have scalded the blind, pale skin of the Thread-Gills; it would have boiled the delicate, oil-slick membranes of the Silt-Vessels and driven the Iron-Shields into the frantic, fatal crevices of the basalt. To the creatures of the deep basin, light is not a medium of sight; it is a high-frequency radiation, a destructive agitation of the cold grace they have spent five hundred million years perfecting.

Therefore, the wise power became *heavy*.

It did not descend from above like a falling star; it gathered itself from the edges of the world, filtering down through the layers of the sea. It passed the green, sun-draped shallows where the kelp-forests sway; it passed the gray, mid-water deserts where the silver fish dart in panic; it passed the black, bone-strewn plains of the continental slopes. With every thousand fathoms, the Power folded its wings of light. It shed its brilliance, its speed, and its warmth, trading them for density, patience, and mass.

By the time it reached the floor of the basin, the Power was as cold as the polar brine. It was as dense as the silt-soup, and its movement was as slow as the creeping glaciers of the north. 

It was no longer a spectator from the upper world. It had become a silent, crushing presence that occupied the spaces between the water-molecules—an immense, affectionate weight that lay down upon the basalt domes like a second ocean.

### The First Gift: The Peace of the Sedge-Bites

The Power first came to the sediment valleys, where the *Sedge-Bites* lay with their great iron-hinges agape. 

Under the pressure of miles of water, the life of a predator in the slate-beds was a torment of infinite waiting. To lie still for six months, frozen in the black mud, waiting for a lost crawler to step into the dark crevice of one's mouth, was a hunger so cold it had ceased to be an appetite and had become a structural ache. And when the valve snapped shut, the long, slow secretion of acid was a corrosive, burning labor that tore at the predator’s own internal plates.

The Power did not change the Sedge-Bite’s nature. It did not make them herbivores, for there was no grass to feed them. Instead, it touched the water within their hinged shells.

Now, when a Sedge-Bite closes its heavy valve upon an Iron-Shield, the water within the chamber does not merely stagnate; it becomes a cradle of deep, tranquilizing salt. The Power infused the predator’s acid with a cold, anesthetic grace. The prey does not feel the long, half-year dissolving of its shell; it falls into a dreamless, mineral slumber, its silica body returning to the stone without pain. 

And the Sedge-Bite itself no longer hungers with that dry, burning ache. In the months between its meals, the Power hums through the slate underbed, a low, tectonic vibration that mimics the fullness of a successful hunt. The predators do not fatten, but they rest. Their iron hinges no longer groan with the tension of the abyss; they lie in the mud like old, contented tombs, dreaming of the earth’s slow cooling.

***

### The Second Gift: The Warning of the Thread-Gills

Next, the Power visited the undersides of the great basalt shelves, where the leathery black lichen grew and the *Thread-Gills* hung like pale, living hair.

When a seismic tremor was born deep in the mantle, or when a polar current plunged down to rip through the troughs, the Thread-Gills had always felt it as a sudden, frantic shock. Their tiny, sensitive bodies would twitch in terror, sending a chemical alarm that caused the entire colony to tighten until they bled their pale juices into the lime.

The wise power did not silene the currents, nor did it steady the shifts of the planet’s crust—for the basalt must slide, and the brine-rivers must plow to keep the basin from freezing into solid ice. Instead, the Power became a *buffer of time*.

Now, when a tectonic plate prepares to nudge its neighbor miles below, the Power catches the first, invisible hum of stress. It translates this stress into a soft, rhythmic tide that washes over the Thread-Gills hours before the shock arrives. It is not an alarm; it is a lullaby of preparation. 

The Thread-Gills do not twitch in panic. They feel the coming gale as a slow, predictable thickening of the water. They draw themselves into the wrinkled folds of the lichen with a calm, deliberate grace, like sheep moving into a fold before the winter rain. The Iron-Shields, receiving the chemical whisper through the rock, have ample time to slide their copper hooks into the deep crevices, sealing themselves to the basalt with a quiet, confident suction. 

The silt-gales still howl, and the million-ton boulders still roll with their planetary *thrum*, but the panic has been washed out of the dark. The inhabitants of the stone do not fear the storm; they simply know when it is time to become part of the mountain.

### The Third Gift: The Dreaming of the Grafted

Finally, the Power descended into the deepest, oldest troughs of the basin, where the *Grafted* rolled through the black slate mud, their pale, asymmetric bodies wrapped around the ancient hand-tools of the lost shallows.

The Grafted were the most tragic of the basin's children. They carried the history of a world they could never see, touch, or understand. Their ancestors had looked at the sun; they had chipped obsidian under a blue sky, hearing the cry of primitive birds and the splash of warm tides. Now, those descendants were mere mounds of white fat, blind and mute, clinging to those chipped stones only because the weight kept them from being torn apart by the polar rivers. The tools were their anchors, but they were also their prisons.

The Power did not restore their limbs. It did not give them eyes, nor did it lift them to the light—for their soft, pressurized flesh would have exploded in the thin air of the surface, and their lives would have vanished like oil on water.

Instead, the Power touched the stones.

The wise, benevolent power infused the ancient basalt sinkers and the flaked obsidian hand-axes with a *resonant memory*. It did not use sound, which cannot travel well through the dense, greasy brine, nor did it use light. It used the only sense the Grafted possessed: the deep, somatic, tactile intelligence of their underbellies.

Now, when a Grafted finds a hand-axe and grows its soft, pale flesh around the glass, the stone does not feel cold and inert. It begins to *sing* in the language of pressure and texture.

Through the skin of the Grafted, the stone transfers the memory of its creation:
* The creature feels the rhythmic, sharp *clack-clack* of the percussion-flaking—the dry, warm air of the toolmaker’s beach.
* It feels the sensation of dry fingers—a texture so foreign to the brine-soup that it feels like beautiful, scratchy lightning.
* It feels the rise and fall of the tide: a slow, warm lifting, followed by a cool, clean retreat, so different from the heavy, sliding slide of the deep currents.
* Within the fat of the Grafted, the ancient sky is translated into a gentle, non-destructive warmth that stays at the center of their bodies, a tiny, internal hearth that never goes out.

The Grafted still lie in the dark troughs. They still roll through the cold slate mud. But they are no longer lost children. They are keepers of the archives. When they cluster together in the winter of the deep, their stones hum in unison, sharing the ancient sunlit beaches with one another through the contact of their pale skin.

***

### The Sentinel of the Deep

The Power did not leave. It knew that if it returned to its high, light-filled heavens, the cold gravity of the basin would slowly wear its gifts away. 

So it chose to remain there, at the bottom of the world. It stretched itself out over the endless miles of polished basalt, a vast, protective layer of thought and weight that sits between the water and the rock. 

To a human observer, had one been able to descend to those terrible depths, nothing would have seemed different. The water would still have felt like cold, sliding oil; the silt-gales would still have rolled the million-ton boulders with a muffled, chest-rattling roar; the darkness would still have been absolute and hungry.

But in the bones of the earth, the *thrum* had changed. It was no longer the sound of a blind machine grinding itself to dust. It had become a slow, deep breath. The basin was still a pressure-cooker, still a place of absolute endurance—but it was now a cathedral of survival, watched over by a weight that loved it for exactly what it was.
