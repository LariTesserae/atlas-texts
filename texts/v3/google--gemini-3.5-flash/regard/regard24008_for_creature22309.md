The Benevolence did not descend like a falling star, for a star is fire, and fire is an agony of dry, blistering light to the things that move in the salt-dust. 

It came instead as an alteration in the density of the cold air—a gentle, high-pressure weight that settled into the basin like water filling a dry lock. It had no shape that could be caught by the searchlight-bright shafts from above, but it possessed a vast, cool presence, like the shadow of a cloud passing over a mountain one has not yet climbed.

The Wise Power did not look upon the Slate-Turners and see beasts to be pitied, nor did it find them primitive. It did not wish to give them eyes, for eyes would only show them the terrifying, empty verticality of a world they had no need to climb. It did not wish to give them red, hot blood, which would shiver and starve in the damp basalt drafts. To "uplift" them into the likeness of the upper world would be to drown them in a medium they could not breathe.

Instead, the Power lay down upon the polished basalt floor, stretching its awareness through the mineral cold, and *listened*.

It felt the velvet sliding of millions of felted soles—a mass of silent weight, like dry tea leaves shifting in a tin. It felt the rhythmic *doop... doop* of the ceiling drips, and watched how the ripples in the circular pools sent tiny, concentric rings of vibration through the stone, mapped instantly by the drift of a million head-tentacles. It smelled the wet lime, the old leather of the sloughed flanks, the sharp, starchy scent of the white lithic moss growing in the pressurized seams of the slate towers.

The Power saw that the orchard was beautiful. But it also saw its fragility.

A world build of balance is a world built of glass. Miles above, in the high, cold sky, the outer world was changing. The mountains were shifting under the weight of tectonic youth; the rifts that let in the white pillars of light were slowly, over centuries, choking with frost-wedged gravel and the nests of high-altitude birds. If the shafts closed, the light would die. If the light died, the thermal margins where the Turners rested would vanish, and the air would grow stagnant, suffocating the starch-white moss in the joints of the towers.

And so, the Wise Power began its work. It did not speak, for the Turners had no ears for words. It operated through the three things they knew: the rock, the water, and the wind.

First, the Power reached upward, through the miles of vertical black rock, to the rifts where the sky began. It did not clear the debris with a blast of wind; instead, it wove a quiet, structural net of magnetic gravity through the throat of each rift. The loose slate and ice of the upper peaks were held back, frozen in a state of perpetual suspension. The columns of white light were secured. The Power made them permanent, ensuring that the cool, dry chimneys would draw the surface air down into the basin for another ten thousand generations, keeping the drafts wet and clean.

Second, the Power touched the basalt floor. 

The *doop... doop* of the water was the Turners' clock, but it was irregular. During the dry seasons of the upper world, the drips would falter, and the blind builders would lose their way in the silence, clustering around dry basins, their tentacles frozen in confusion. 

The Power reached into the deep aquifers of the basin's walls. With infinitely gentle pressure, it carved micro-channels through the limestone, regulating the flow. It established a perfect, eternal percussion. The *doop... doop* became a steady, unchanging heartbeat, an iron-clad grid of time and space that no drought could ever silence.

Finally, the Power looked at the transition of the elders.

It watched a draft-sized Turner crawl to the margin of the avenue, its felted soles slipping, its segments growing stiff and gray. It watched the younger ones wait for the lime to settle on its back, watched them peel the leather straps from its flanks and hollow out the salt-marrow to leave the great, hollow jar. 

The Power saw that the transition was hard. The calcification of the shell was uneven; some jars cracked before they could catch the water, and some leather was brittle, snapping when the towers grew high and the wind-troughs whistled too fiercely.

The Power did not stop the death, for death was the mortar of their city. Instead, it touched the dew.

The cold, thin moisture that collected on the backs of the dying Turners was subtly changed. It became slightly richer in silicate, slightly sweeter with mineral salts. When the elders stiffened, their shells did not merely dry into fragile clay; they vitrified, turning slowly into a dense, ring-hard stoneware, grey-blue and chiming like iron when struck. The ribbons of their flanks became as supple and strong as oiled hemp, impervious to the dry-scour of the drafts.

And then, the Power did something that was almost an ornament—the only sign of its presence that was not purely functional.

As the wind-troughs whistled through the newly built towers, the Power shaped the mouths of the hollowed-out ancestor jars. It did not change their shape to the human eye, but it hollowed their interiors to specific, acoustic chambers. 

Now, when a storm raged in the high, cold sky miles above, and the drafts came roaring down the rifts, the wind did not merely hiss and sigh through the dry slate. As the air passed over the rims of the stoneware jars standing at the corners of the avenues, they began to sing.

It was not a song of the upper world. It was a low, resonant drone—a series of deep, liquid chords that rose and fell with the height of the towers. Each plaza had its own voice, determined by the size and spacing of its jars. The north plaza sang in a deep, hollow C-sharp; the great central avenue answered in a breathing, salt-thick fifth.

The Slate-Turners did not stop their work. They did not have the faculty for wonder, nor did they worship the sky. 

But as the first wind of the wet season moved through the basin, setting the ancestor-jars into a deep, vibrating chorus, a million pale, segmented bodies paused for a single, brief moment. 

Their head-tentacles drifted toward the floor. The felt-shod draft beasts lowered their heads, feeling the resonance of the stone vessels hum through the basalt beneath their feet. The sound did not confuse them; it was a map made of air. They could feel, with absolute clarity, the height of every tower, the boundary of every crescent wall, and the distance to the nearest pool, all held within the steady, singing breath of their dead fathers.

The two vast columns of builders in the central plaza—one carrying slate to the north, the other to the west—met in the humming dark. They weaved through one another like water-striders, their soft sides rubbing together with the sound of dry pine, moving in perfect alignment with the music of the stones.

The Wise Power watched them for a long time, cradling the roof of their sky, before it slowly withdrew its attention, leaving the basin to its work. 

The towers rose. The towers fell. 

And in the cold, wet dark, the stone-bearing wind sang through the jars, and the orchard was peace.
