The Long Attention had drifted across the emptiness between stars for several turnings of the young mountain range, a vast and quiet awareness that did not possess a face, nor hands, but had the weight of a slow-moving sun and the tenderness of dry earth after frost.

When It looked down upon the three summits, It did not see a tragedy to be corrected, nor a primitive error to be swept away. A lesser, more foolish god would have pitied the cold. Such a god would have cracked the peaks to let up the boiling magma from the deep earth to warm the stones, or brought down rain to soften the sharp slate into mud where gardens might grow. 

But the Long Attention was wise. It knew that to melt the glaciers would be to drown the valleys; to soften the peaks would be to slide the great barnacle-city into the abyss; and to warm the air would be to burst the great, bellows-like lungs of the people, whose blood was thick as grease and meant only for the biting, violet-blue heights. To change their world would be to erase them.

So, the Long Attention did not change the mountain. Instead, It settled over the range like a massive, invisible pressure—a warm inversion layer of absolute care that did not melt the frost, but made the air slightly more substantial, a fraction more generous to those who had to wring their life from it.

And then, with the patient delicacy of a mason fitting two blocks of dark diorite, the Power began to make Its adjustments.

***

First, It went down into the dark, wet *Roots* of the city, where the condensation ran like sweat and the elderly sat in the warm, musky straw of the ox-vaults. 

The Power saw the old ones, their fingers cracked and gray with grease-tallow, scraping the fat from bird-skins and spinning the coarse hair of goats into the *Syllable-Knots*. It saw how their memories of the high, cold wind were fading into the grey "slate-cough," their lungs filling with the very rock they had spent their youth cutting.

The Power did not take away their labor, for in this city, to be useless was to be dead. Instead, It touched the *Syllable-Knots*. 

Through the greasy, grey twine of goat-hair, a subtle change ran. Now, when an old man or woman ran their calloused thumbs over a knot representing a famine fifty winters past, they did not feel only the cold pinch of remembered hunger. They felt a sudden, deep, golden warmth in their chest. The knot gave back to them the smell of the first blue-shrub berry they had tasted as a child; it gave back the physical memory of a young spouse’s shoulder in the *Heep*; it gave back the magnificent, soaring weightlessness of their twelve-year-old climb. 

At the same time, the Power touched the grey-green lichens that grew in the crevices where the stone-cutters worked. A new, pale yellow spore began to bloom among the scabs of saxifrage. It had no taste, and it did not change the color of the stone, but when the stone-cutters inhaled the dust of their chiseling, this new herb did not turn to stone in their breasts. Instead, it dissolved the sharp silica, converting the grey slate-dust into a soft, cooling oil that the lungs could easily pass. The old ones in the Roots still coughed, but their breath remained clear enough to tell the old tales, and their knees, though stiff, did not turn to rock before their time.

***

Next, the Power hovered over the vertical chimneys and the sheer, slate drops where the children played within their nine-pace tethers. 

It looked upon the children—small, fat, grease-coated creatures sliding bone-toys across the stone, linked to their hearths by ropes of braided hair. It saw the terror under the skin of the mothers who watched the lines grow taut over the edge of the abyss. It saw the silent ledger of those whose ropes had frayed, or whose feet had slipped during the *Climb of the Five Crevices*.

The Power could not banish gravity, for gravity was the law that kept the peaks from flying into the void. But It could reason with the wind.

In the narrow, vertical crevices where the air usually howled like a flayed beast, the Long Attention established a permanent, invisible cushion. It was a column of dense, upward-curving thermal pressure, born from the pooled heat of the great ox-vaults below and the dry midday sun above. 

A child who slipped from the wet slate of an upper terrace did not fall like a stone. Instead, the "Up-Draft"—which the people came to call the *Sleeve of the Sky*—would catch them. To a watcher, it looked as though the child had been seized by an invisible, woolly hand. They would descend through the grey mist not with a skull-cracking rush, but with the slow, drifting grace of a falling condor-feather, settling onto a lower roof or into a heap of dried moss, startled and cold, but whole. 

The *Climb of the Five Crevices* remained hard; the youth still had to prove their thighs and their balance to the community. But the mountain no longer kept its silent, bloody ledger. The debts were paid in bruises, not in lives.

***

Finally, the Power looked upon the *Bone-Gatherers*—the great black condors with their grease-shining wings—and the *Sky-Steward* who cut the dead into segments on the High Spire. 

The Power saw how the people loved their ancestors, how they kept their skulls for lamps and their thigh-bones for looms, refusing to let them be lost to the earth. It saw the beauty of this bone-woven city, where the dead literally held up the roofs of the living.

In the high, thin air above the summits, the Power breathed a small, lasting sweetness into the bodies of the dying. When the Sky-Steward performed his solemn work on the High Spire, the air did not smell of iron and rot. It smelled of the dry, ancient pine-woods that had long since disappeared from the peaks—a resinous, clean scent that filled the nostrils of the family waiting on the lower terraces. 

And when the condors leaped from the diorite needles, carrying the flesh of the grandmothers and grandfathers into the violet sky, they did not fly away into the blank distance. The Power touched the minds of the birds, binding them closer to the human hive. At dusk, as the flight of the Bone-Gatherers returned to the city, they would sweep low over the low, flat-roofed dwellings, their great wings beating in rhythm with the *Sentry-Warmer* dogs’ purring. 

***

At the end of Its long observation, the Power did one last thing. 

It waited for the evening hour, when the sun sank behind the western glacier, leaving the three peaks in a deep, electric-blue shadow. It watched as the stone-masons across the vertical labyrinth took up their hammer-stones to play the *Rock-Chime*.

The first mason struck his slate-flange: *ting*.
A second answered from the middle summit: *clink*.
Then a third, far down the serrated ridge: *tong*.

As the polyphonic stone-song began to drift through the thin, biting air, the Long Attention did not silence the wind to let the sound travel. Instead, It joined the song.

Deep within the roots of the world, three miles beneath the basalt needles and the slate cliffs, the Power nudged the young, hostile earth. It did not trigger a slide, nor open a fissure. It simply set the great granite bones of the continent to vibrating at the exact, resonant frequency of the masons' hammer-stones.

The mountains answered the people.

When the mason struck the slate, the very ledge beneath his knotty feet did not just repeat the sound; it *hummed* with a deep, organ-like drone that vibrated through the marrow of the stone, through the dry-stone walls, through the sheepskin-covered floors, and into the soles of the families huddled in the *Heep*. 

It was a sound that had no words in their click-tongue, though they would later invent one for it: *Soh-Hum*, the tongue-resting-against-the-teeth sound of the earth agreeing to hold them.

The people did not look up into the violet-blue sky to find the source of the drone, for they were a folk who looked with their torsos and kept their eyes on the path. They did not need to see a face. They knew only that the young rock beneath them had finally stopped its hostile shifting and had settled into a long, slow weight.

The wind still howled, the water was still cold enough to crack teeth, and the grease-tallow still smelled of old fat. But as the families snuggled into their nests of hair and fur, their dogs purring against their legs and the bones of their ancestors keeping out the dry, drifting snow, they felt the mountain singing back to them in the dark. 

They were clinging to the edge of the sky, and they would never fall.
