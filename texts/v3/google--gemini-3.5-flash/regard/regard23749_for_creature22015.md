The entity that found them had no name that could be spoken with a throat, but if its presence could be translated into the physical laws of the *Phloem Basin*, it would have felt like the moment a long-held breath is finally, safely released. 

It was an awareness as vast as a mountain range, yet its attention was as fine and targeted as the tip of a copper needle. It had drifted through the cold, non-spatial voids of the outer cosmos until it felt a vibration—not a sound, but a rhythmic, microscopic shudder traveling through the great wooden skeleton of the world. It was the pattern of sixty boots heel-tapping a message across eighty yards of petrified peat: *“The tide is high. Keep the valves clear.”*

The Power did not break the wood to look inside. To peel back the cambium would be to kill the lung. Instead, it did what any wise gardener does when encountering a delicate, perfect thing growing in a dark corner: it sat down beside it, became still, and began to listen.

It listened for a century of the outer world’s years—which, to the Power, was a single afternoon, and to the Basin-dwellers was perhaps two generations of stiffening joints and new iron bolts. It learned the sibilants of the *Sigh-Talk*; it memorized the weight of every copper collar; it grew to love the slow, rhythmic *chuff-thump* of the transpiration pumps, which sounded to the Power like the heartbeat of a very small, very brave beetle.

And because the Power was benevolent, and because it understood that the greatest cruelty one can inflict on a closed, perfect ecosystem is to "liberate" it into a world it was never made to survive, it decided not to change the Basin. 

Instead, it chose to *cushion* it. It began to act through the very medium the inhabitants trusted most: the damp, the wood, and the vibration.

Here is what the Power did.

***

### I. The Deepening of the Wood-Telegraph
The first change was noted by an elder named Kael, who sat on the lowest peat floor with his palms pressed flat against the damp root-system. For seventy years, the wood-telegraph had been a dry, functional thing—codes for pressure, for assistance, for the arrival of the room-rain. 

But on a morning when the green daylight was particularly soft, Kael felt something else traveling through the heartwood. It was not a tap. It was a *reverberation*, broad and deep as the sound of a bronze bell struck underwater. 

When he pressed his ear to the wood, he did not hear the current workers. He heard the *dead*. 

The Power had touched the "healing niches" where the ancestors lay sealed under the grey lips of scar tissue. It had not raised them—for their flesh was now the tree’s flesh—but it had preserved their memories in the vascular rings of the wood. The copper collars and iron hooks protruding from the bark were no longer just grave-markers; they had become tuning forks. 

Now, when a young Hydrologist swung sixty feet in the air and felt her fingers grow numb with the chill, she could press her forehead against the cambium. Through the wood, the quiet, sibilant comfort of her great-grandmother would vibrationally rise: *“Secure the knot, little sprout. The copper is cold, but the rope is true.”* 

The past was no longer lost to the great vascular flow; it was held in a warm, acoustic library, whispering beneath their feet and hands.

***

### II. The Gift of the Dream-Rain
Once every twelve cycles, when the "room-rain" fell from the skylight fronds, the water did not smell of mint and wet iron. The Power, reaching through the external roots that drank from the sky-rivers of the outer world, filtered a new kind of moisture into the Basin. 

The inhabitants called it the *Amber Mist*. 

During these rains, the fog that rolled down from the ceiling fronds carried microscopic, luminescent spores that did not rot the bast clothing or slick the catwalks. When breathed in, they did not make the people sick; they made them *dream*.

These were not individual dreams of anxiety or hunger, but a shared, collective vision. For five minutes, as the room-rain pattered against their copper nets, the thirty households of the Basin would sit on their balconies, eyes closed, and see the *Great Without*. They saw oceans of salt water that stretched until the sky curved; they saw mountains made of dry, red stone that did not grow or breathe; they saw stars that did not shine through leaves, but hung like cold diamonds in an endless, velvet throat.

It was not a goad to leave—for they knew their ivory skin would burn and their prehensile toes would find no grip on the flat, dry earth—but it was a gift of scale. It gave them a horizon. It meant that when they worked their clicking looms, they knew they were not the only tiny things spinning in the dark.

***

### III. The Softening of the Stiffening
The most merciful act of the Power was directed at the elders. 

The transition from a life of active climbing to the dry, clicking stiffness of the end had always been a time of quiet, sibilant sorrow. The elders would sit on the benches, their joints popping like dry twigs, watching the young ones swing through the rigging with a grace they had lost.

The Power could not stop the aging of their bodies, for that was the law of the node. But it chose to sweeten the sap.

The latex that the transpiration pumps drew up from the deep roots became slightly different. It lost its bitter, oily sting and took on a faint sweetness, like parched maple. When the elders drank the tea brewed from the root-condensates, their joints did not stop clicking, but the pain went out of them. A strange, warm lightness took its place. 

When the time came for an elder to be carried to a healing niche, they did not go with the dry, rattling fear of suffocation. They went as if falling into a deep, warm bath of moss. As the family applied the paste of charcoal and latex, and as the grey bark of the great tree slowly began its years-long roll to claim them, the elder felt no cold. They felt the great, slow pulse of the tree’s own life-blood wrapping around them like a wool coat. They did not die *out* of the Basin; they were gently pulled *in*, their last conscious sensation being the wonderful, steady *chuff-thump* of the pumps, which now sounded like their own chest.

***

### IV. The Shield of the Outer Pores
Outside the Phloem Basin, the world was vast, chaotic, and sometimes violent. There were blights that rotted forests; there were fires that ran through the canopy of the titanic vegetable giants; there were dry seasons that turned heartwood to dust.

The Power did not interfere with the weather of the outer world, but it cast its own vast, invisible shadow over the node that housed the Basin. 

It wrapped its own non-corporeal roots around the outer bark of the node, a silent, protective hand. When the wild sky-fires passed over the forest, the node did not burn; the heat was deflected by an invisible moisture-barrier that the Power sustained. When the timber-boring beetles of the outer wild—creatures the size of riverboats—crawled near the Basin’s outer pores, they found themselves gently turned away by a low, systemic vibration that made their antennae itch with unease.

The Basin remained safe, a warm, green pocket of perfect, delicate clockwork, suspended in a dangerous universe, preserved by a sentinel who asked for nothing in return.

***

### The Quiet Watch
The people of the Basin never built altars to the Power. They did not know its shape, and they had no words in the *Sigh-Talk* for "god" or "providence." They were too practical for theology; their days were too full of copper wire, steam valves, and the maintenance of the mist-nets.

But they knew things had changed. 

Sometimes, a child sitting on a high catwalk, polishing the bronze back of a gasket-beetle, would hear the beetle let out a low, sweet purr—a sound these blind insects had never made before. 

Sometimes, a Hydrologist swinging in the green void would lose his footing, only to feel a sudden, unaccountable density in the cool air—a moment where the atmospheric draft seemed to thicken and hold him like a hammock for the half-second it took to catch his rope.

And sometimes, when the entire hamlet was asleep, and the only sound was the lazy *tink-tink-drip* of the cisterns, a single, deep vibration would run through the petrified peat floor. It was not the *chuff-thump* of the engines, nor was it the warning groan of the cambium. 

It was a slow, rolling tap-tap. It was the Power, using their own *Tread-Talk*, sending a message through eighty feet of living wood to the hundred sleeping souls inside:

*“Sleep well. The wood is dry. The water is sweet. I am listening.”*
