The traveler came not as a light, for light is an eviction in the deep. 

To shine a sun upon this high, drowned plaza would be an act of unspeakable violence. It would sear the eyeless ribbon-eels; it would cause the delicate, crimson fans of the Red Lace to dissolve forever into defensive grease; it would blind the transparent shrimp whose entire world has been painted in shades of velvet indigo since the mountains first sank.

And so, the Power arrived as a change in weight.

It was a vast, quiet consciousness, older than the basalt tiles, older even than the forgotten mammoth bones that held the terra-cotta columns together. It had drifted across the emptiness between stars, seeking things that were complete, things that had reached their final, perfect shape. And here, in this warm bath suspended over the abyss, it found a masterpiece of slow, blind patience.

The Power did not speak. It had no voice that could travel through water without shattering the fragile, drying clicks of the Gazer-Shells. Instead, it set itself down upon the chamber like a heavy, protective hand, matching its breath to the slow, diastolic pump of the Grandmother of the Well.

With infinite gentleness, the Power began its work. It did not alter; it preserved. It did not create; it reinforced.

***

**First, it went down into the earth, beneath the basalt floor.** 

Deep below the six-sided tiles, where the roots of the drowned mountain met the ancient magma chambers of the world’s mantle, the heat was beginning to fail. It was a slow cooling, a process that would take another ten thousand years, but to the Power, ten thousand years was a single afternoon. If the hearth cooled, the water would lose its sleepy, thick warmth. The Pressers would stiffen and die; the Grandmother’s nursery would grow cold; the Loom’s delicate curtain would sink to the floor, heavy and dead.

The Power reached into the volcanic chimney beneath the plaza. With a touch as soft as rising silt, it anchored a small, eternal ember of stellar hearth-fire in the dark stone. It was not a burning flame, but a steady, rhythmic pulse of pure, gravity-born warmth. The water would never grow cold. The basin would remain seventy-four degrees, century after century, till the oceans themselves departed.

**Next, it visited the pillars.**

Within the stacked terra-cotta drums, the Power felt the tireless work of the Bone-Drifts. The ancient mammoth bones, pinned through the centers of the columns, were millions of years old. Even with the protective, alkaline wax secreted by the blind white worms, the ivory was growing chalky. The weight of the endless ocean above was heavy, and the columns were beginning to sigh, their joints shifting by fractions of a millimeter.

The Power did not replace the bone. To do so would starve the Bone-Drifts, who had loved the ivory for generations. 

Instead, the Power breathed upon the pillars. It infused the mammoth bone with a thread of dark, metallic salt—an element from the heart of a fallen moon. It made the bones structurally immortal, immune to the crushing weight of the deep, yet left them soft enough, porous enough, and rich enough in calcium for the blind white worms to crawl through them, tightening their living braid in perfect, symbiotic peace. 

**Then, it touched the Grandmother of the Well.**

The Power drifted to the center of the floor, settling beside the massive, encrusted sea-squirt that sealed the ancient drain. The Grandmother’s tunic was heavy with the weight of her eons. She was tired. Her heart had beaten billions of times, drawing the warm water in and pushing it out to keep the little world alive. 

The Power did not make her young. To make her young would be to erase her memory, to wash away the bryozoans and sponges that had made her their mountain, to empty her womb of the larval white crabs that clung to her internal ribs.

Instead, the Power gave her *ease*. 

It eased the friction of her leathery siphons. It aligned the molecular drift of the water so that it slid through her body with no resistance at all. Her pumping became effortless, a natural extension of the ocean’s tide. And as it touched her, the Power left a small, quiet song in her gelatinous core—a low, resonant vibration that matched the turning of the earth. This song did not disturb the water; it was too deep for sound. But the tiny, larval crabs clinging to her ribs felt it, and in their blind, warm nursery, they felt safe, knowing that the dark was not empty, but occupied by something that loved them.

**Finally, it looked to the edge.**

The Power rose to the crumbling balustrade where the Gazer-Shells curved their long, tough necks out over the bottomless void. It felt the danger that lay beyond. The deep currents of the abyssal plain were cold, violent, and indifferent. Occasionally, a rogue draft of freezing, mineral-bitter water would rise from the trenches, licking at the edges of the pool, threatening to spill over the tiles and wither the Red Lace.

The Power stepped over the lip of the hand-carved stone and descended into the black void just below the summit. 

There, it wove a wall. 

It was not a wall of stone or glass, but a barrier of pressure and heat—a permanent thermocline that wrapped around the shoulder of the mountain like a soft, invisible scarf. The cold, violent currents of the abyss, rising from the empty plains below, hit this invisible barrier and were gently turned aside, guided around the peak to dissipate in the open ocean. 

Yet, the wall was porous to the sky. The "marine snow"—the tiny, constant drift of organic dust, the pale flakes of a distant, sunlit world miles above—was allowed to pass. It filtered down through the warm barrier, settling like a slow, quiet rain onto the gaping mouths of the Gazer-Shells, who clicked their ivory plates in a lazy, rhythmic chorus of satisfaction.

***

When its work was finished, the Power did not leave. 

It had no need to return to the cold spaces between the stars. It had found its chapel. It dissolved its great, wise self into the very viscosity of the water. It became the velvet darkness. It became the heavy pool that held them all in a hydrostatic embrace, neutralizing weight, neutralizing hurry, neutralizing death.

High on the drowned summit, the plaza remained. 

The ribbon-eels drifted through the terra-cotta columns. The Pressers crawled their twelve inches a week over the clean basalt tiles. The Loom hung like a sheet of gray silk, catching the translucent shrimp in its quiet, chemical sleep. 

Nothing had changed, and yet, everything was permanent. 

The water was warm. The light was gone. And in the deep, indigo silence, the sanctuary slept, dreaming its slow, blind dream under the watchful, weightless gaze of a god who had learned how to be still.
