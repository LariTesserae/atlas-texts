The Attention did not drop from the sky like a falling star, nor did it rise from the rusted valleys below like a column of smoke. It arrived as a pause.

For a single heartbeat, the metallic hiss of the high-altitude drafts through the basalt needles ceased. The *drip-tink* of the meltwater froze mid-air, the diamond-bright droplets hanging like glass beads suspended by invisible threads. The chime-mice did not flatten themselves in terror; instead, they tilted their massive, slate-gray ears toward the center of the shelf, their glass-wool coats humming a low, resonant note that sounded like a chamber of bells struck by a single, gentle hand.

The power that had arrived was not one of storms or reclamation. It was an ancient, slow-breathing consciousness—a **Keeper of Distances**, whose mind was as wide as the vacuum between worlds, yet whose heart was small enough to fit inside the ribcage of a chime-mouse. It had been drawn here by the sheer density of the struggle on this tiny ledge. In a universe of burning suns and dead moons, this shelf was a miracle of stubborn, frantic friction.

The Keeper did not take a human shape, for a human shape would have crowded the narrow windbreak. Instead, it manifested as a change in the quality of the light. The harsh, stinging ultraviolet glare of the violet sky softened into the color of aged honey. The air, usually so thin it tasted of raw iron, grew thick with the smell of old loam, wet roots, and things that had not been seen on this spire for ten thousand years.

The Keeper looked first at the shelf's smallest children. 

It saw the **chime-mice**, shivering in their glass coats, licking the frost from one another’s ears in the desperate, silent fellowship of the dry heights. The Keeper did not change their nature. To give them soft fur would be to freeze them; to give them streams of water would be to drown them in the sub-zero shade. Instead, the Keeper breathed upon the basalt blocks where they gathered. 

Beneath the purple moss-mounds, the cold stone softened. Not into mud, but into a porous, warm basalt that held a faint, perpetual geothermal pulse. From that moment on, the rime that formed upon the spire would not evaporate into the dry air before the mice could drink. It would gather in tiny, naturally occurring cups in the warm stone—cups that would never freeze, where the water would remain liquid and sweet, smelling faintly of clover. The mice would no longer have to swap places in the bitter wind, gambling their lives for a drop of moisture. They would drink together, their glass-furred flanks chiming like wind-chimes in a summer garden.

Next, the Keeper looked at the **velvet-borers**. 

The heavy, turquoise-shelled beetles were scraping at the basalt with their iron mandibles, their copper-brine blood keeping them from freezing during the sudden cold snaps. The Keeper touched their beautiful, porcelain backs. The grinding of their teeth grew quiet. The acid they secreted became slightly sweeter, requiring less of their precious internal heat to produce. The gray silt they left behind—the soil of the shelf—bloomed. Small, yellow star-flowers, extinct since the first age of the Great Rust, began to seed in the beetle-tracks, their roots wrapping around the basalt like golden wire, holding the soil together against the hungry wind.

Then, the Keeper turned its vast, quiet attention to the **Spire-Creeper**.

The man did not leap to his feet. He had lived in the great silence too long to be easily startled. But he stopped his bone needle mid-pull. He felt a warmth on his back—not the fierce, drying heat of the noon sun, but the deep, bone-settling warmth of a mother’s hand placed against a child’s shoulder. It sank through his ribbon-gull tunic, through his thick, plum-purple hide, directly into his barrel chest. 

He stood up, his three-inch callus soles gripping the basalt. His purple skin, usually dry and dusty, caught the honey-colored light. He looked out into the empty air, and though he had no words left in his tongue, his chest widened with a long, trembling breath. 

The Keeper looked at the windbreak. It saw the three bone scraping-knives. It saw the shallow pumice bowl with its frozen cake of red ochre and fat. And it saw the pile of resinous pine-knots—only five remained. Five nights of warmth, and then the Creeper would have to make the perilous three-day descent into the lower world, where the Great Rust crawled, risking his lungs in the heavy, toxic air of the valleys.

The Keeper did not descend to the valleys to destroy the Rust. The Rust was a great, slow cycle—the iron of the world returning to the earth, a death that would eventually breed new stone. But the Keeper looked at the history of that death, recorded in the tiny notches on the bird-bone ribs lying by the windbreak.

*He is writing the obituary of his world,* the Keeper realized. *And he thinks he is writing it for no one.*

The Keeper bent close to the Creeper. The man felt the air pressure drop, as if a great mountain had leaned in to whisper. 

The Keeper touched the split leg-bones.

The notches the Creeper had carved—the records of the orange rot, the falling cities, the drowned forests—began to glow. The bone did not rot. It did not dry or turn to dust. The bone transformed into a material harder than diamond, black as the basalt but shot through with veins of silver light. Every mark the Creeper had made, and every mark he would ever make, was set into the eternal stone of the universe. 

The Keeper projected a thought into the Creeper’s mind—not a voice, but an image: *A great library in a city of stars, where beings of light stood before a shelf of black bone, reading the story of the green world, weeping for its passing, and thanking the Sentry who had saved its memory from the dark.*

The Creeper’s shoulders, which had been bent under the weight of his lonely task for forty years, straightened. He looked at his hands. He looked at the glowing bone. 

He knew, finally, that his watch was not in vain. He was not a ghost waiting for the end. He was the world's librarian.

Before the Keeper withdrew its attention, it performed one final, quiet act of mercy.

It touched the smallest of the five pine-knots under the slate slab. The resin within the wood began to hum. It did not catch fire; it did not smoke. But it began to glow with a small, cherry-red ember that did not consume the wood. It was a hearth-soul. Within the three-pace circle of the Creeper's windbreak, the temperature rose to a steady, comfortable fifty degrees. It would never drop, and it would never rise higher. The fat in his pumice bowl thawed, turning into a soft, fragrant paste. His skin would no longer split from the dry frost. His fingers would never again freeze to the bone-needle.

The honey-light began to fade, returning to the pale, violet clarity of the high sky. The wind returned with a soft, metallic hiss, slicing through the basalt needles. 

The chime-mice began to run again, their glass flanks *tink-tink-tinking* like coins. The ribbon-gulls returned from the deep gulf, their white scales flashing, their yellow beaks clattering out their telegraphy of the lowlands.

The Spire-Creeper sat down in his warm hut. He did not feel the cold sting in the back of his throat anymore. He dipped his callused finger into the thawed red ochre, rubbed it into the new notches on his silver-veined bone, and then looked up at the sky.

He did not pray, for he did not know the word. But he pulled a strand of glass-wool thread through his bone needle, and as he wove, he began to whistle—a low, dry, clicking tune that matched the rhythm of the ribbon-gulls and the song of the mice under the stone. 

And far above him, in the high, silent orbit where the stars began to show, the Keeper listened.
