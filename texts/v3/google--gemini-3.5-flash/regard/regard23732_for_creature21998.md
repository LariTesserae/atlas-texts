### The Gaze from the Blue Height

The attention of the Great Power did not fall upon the Weave like a lightning bolt, nor did it descend with the heavy, golden tread of a conqueror-god. To an entity that has watched continents buckle and oceans turn to salt, the gorge of Kael-Sunder was not a kingdom to be ruled, but a very old, very beautiful song played on a tiny, fragile instrument. 

The Power was ancient, cool-burning, and slow. Its consciousness lived in the spaces between stars, yet it was attracted to friction, to density, and to the stubborn, wet heat of life that persists against the silence of the void. 

It looked down at the mountain range—a dry, skeletal hand of grey granite thrusting into the cold upper air. And there, in a minor fracture of the bone, was the Weave. It was a single, green emerald stitch holding two dead cliffs together. 

The Power watched.

It watched the Silt-Gleaners down in the blue-grey dark, their skin like peeled birch, singing to the giant, orange-bellied salamanders. It watched the Middle-Weavers, suspended ninety feet in the air by nothing but greasy horsehair ropes and their own long, prehensile toes, binding willow stakes with cold pine-pitch. It watched the tiny *sap-frogs* crawling into the armpits of sleeping children, their small, cold bodies clicking like pebbles under water to drive away the fever-midges.

The Power realized two things:
First, that this place was *perfect*. It had achieved that rarest of cosmic phenomena: a closed-circle harmony where nothing was wasted, where the dead became the mortar for the living, and where no one sought to conquer the sky or drain the Sump.
Second, the Power saw that this perfection was as delicate as the skin of a water-bubble.

A single bad season—a drought that dried the high glacier, a rockslide at the rim, or the arrival of the dry, hungry iron-smiths from the lowlands—would pop the bubble. The moss would turn to dry tinder; the spindle-nests would burn; the children’s feet would slip from the dry lianas; and the *Sigh-of-the-Weave* would shrink into black, dead charcoal behind the stone.

The Power did not wish to change the song. It wished only to make the flute unbreakable.

Thus, the Power acted. It did not speak, for words are dry things. It did not drop iron or stone. It used only three light touches—three *Wefts of Grace*—woven directly into the wet fabric of the cleft.

---

### The First Weft: The Heart-Stone of the Glacier

High above the rim, where the glacial stream began its drop into the gorge, the Power placed its first touch.

Deep within the blue, sun-blind heart of the glacier, it condensed a single, heavy droplet of water that would never freeze and never fall. This was the *Marrow-Drop*. 

The Marrow-Drop became a perpetual, intuitive lung for the mountain's water. Through its presence, the glacier became "aware" of the Weave's thirst. If a year of dry winds came, and the rains failed, the Marrow-Drop would pulse with a cold, internal heat, melting just enough of the high ice to keep the wooden aqueducts of the middle levels singing. The water would never fail; the *tink-tink-tink* of the condensation would never rise in pitch toward the high, frantic tone of drought.

Yet, it was more than just volume. The water flowing from the glacier was altered. It became slightly heavier, slicker, and filled with a microscopic, mineral dust that the iron-moss drank like cream. Under this new draft, the three-foot walls of moss grew tougher. The roots did not bore deeper into the stone; instead, they petrified at their core, turning the green masonry into something as strong as basalt, while remaining as soft as wool on the surface. The ladders of the weavers were now anchored not in rotting humus, but in living, green iron.

---

### The Second Weft: The Lantern-Memory

The Power’s second touch was laid upon the *glow-moss* and the *Sigh-of-the-Weave*.

Before this touch, when an elder lay down in the Sump and was taken by the white, hair-like threads of the subterranean fungus, their body was digested and their memory was lost. They became structural—part of the lime-rich mud that held up the next tier of nests—but their voice was gone.

The Power altered the resonance of the *Sigh*. 

It connected the deep root-fungus to the glow-moss that the weavers harvested for their wire-sided lanterns. Now, when an elder was woven into the canyon walls, their identity did not dissolve into simple soot. Their wisdom—their sixty years of knowing which willow-shoot would bend without snapping, their memory of the great storm of the third year, their songs to quiet the four-foot oar-mouths—settled like a soft sediment into the subterranean network.

When a weaver went to the deep hollows to harvest fresh glow-moss for their lantern, they did not just gather light; they gathered *reminescence*.

Inside the spindle-nests at night, as the cold steam rolled through the willow-branches, the lanterns did not merely cast a flat green glow. In the shifting, cool-green shadows on the walls, the inhabitants began to see patterns—the slow, rhythmic gestures of their grandmothers’ hands, the shape of a knot that had been forgotten for two generations, the ancient warnings of the high rim. 

The kids did not need to be taught where to climb; they slept under the green, dancing memories of draft-stoppers who had died a century ago. The history of the Weave was no longer a matter of throat-whistles and clicks; it was a visible, living skin that shared their evenings.

### The Third Weft: The Shroud-Mist

The third touch was a wall of defense. 

The Weave was a secret, but secrets in a dry world are always found eventually. The dry people of the plains—the ones who smelled of coal-smoke and wore hard, squeaking boots of tanned pig-skin—would one day look up at the mountain cleft and see the green steam rising. They would bring their iron axes and their wide-bladed shovels to dig out the peat; they would capture the short-statured weavers to climb their high chimneys; they would dry the gorge until it was nothing but a dusty oven.

To prevent this, the Power touched the air at the rim.

It did not build a wall of stone or a circle of fire. Instead, it tuned the cold, drifting spray that rose from the foaming basin at the bottom of the cleft. 

At the top of the gorge, where the wet air met the dry, biting wind of the high pass, the mist did not dissipate. Instead, it began to fold upon itself in a complex, three-layered optical weave. To an observer standing five hundred paces away on the dry, grey scree-slopes, the mouth of the cleft became invisible. 

The eye of the outsider would slip off the gap. They would look at the Weave and see only a flat, wind-scoured wall of grey limestone, silent and dead under the sun. Even the sound—the deafening, musical *clack-click-roar* of the waterfalls and the million beetles—was caught by the cold steam and bent back down into the gorge. A traveler could walk within fifty steps of the rim and hear nothing but the whistle of the dry mountain grass.

The Weave was sealed in a pocket of quiet, wet eternity.

### The Unbroken Song

The weavers did not know they had been touched by a god. 

On the day the Wefts were woven, there were no trumpets in the sky, no giant faces in the clouds, and no laws written on stone tablets. 

The Crown-Keepers at the rim simply noticed that the cold mountain wind didn’t bite their ears quite as hard as it had the day before. The mist seemed thicker, tastier.

In the middle levels, a weaver named Tel-of-the-Five-Knots was repairing a broken ladder-rung. He reached into his lantern to trim the damp wick of his glow-moss, and for a second, his fingers tingled with a sensation like warm, dry handshakes. In the green shadow on the wet cliff-wall, he saw the face of his great-uncle—the one who had fallen into the Sump during the spring thaw fifty years ago. 

The shadow did not speak, but it pointed its thumb three inches to the left, toward a hidden split in the birchwood that Tel had missed. Tel grinned, clicked his tongue twice in gratitude, and moved his peg.

At the bottom of the gorge, the Silt-Gleaners found that the oar-mouths were grunting in a slightly lower, more resonant key. It was a sound that made the water in their wooden cups vibrate in tiny, concentric rings, like the pattern of a heart.

The Weave went on. 

They climbed; they spliced; they wet their toes in the cold, green moss. They lived in their three-dimensional hive, suspended between the dark sump and the hidden sky, completely unaware that a great eye was watching them from the blue dark of the stars—and that the eye was pleased, and had chosen to let their small, wet song play on forever.
