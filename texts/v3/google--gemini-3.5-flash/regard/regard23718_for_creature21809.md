The Presence did not arrive with a crack of thunder or a splitting of the vault. To descend with the weight of stars into the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts would have been a catastrophic violence; the fragile cedar walkways would have snapped, the ancient terracotta would have shattered under the gravity of a true celestial, and the bats would have died of fright in their damp crevices.

Instead, the Wise Power became small. It folded its vastness down, layer by layer, until it was nothing more than a change in the air—a gentle, cooling draft that smelled of wild thyme and high, clean snow, slipping down the green-glazed chimneys of the light-wells.

It did not seek to change the room’s nature. A lesser, more arrogant god might have seen this place as a tomb to be demolished, or a ruin to be "saved" by dragging its blind inhabitants up into the blinding glare of a noon-day sun they could not comprehend. But the Wise Power understood that to the Mortar-Wrights, the red brick *was* the world. To strip them of their caves, their crabs, and their wet, rhythmic dark would be a cruelty disguised as salvation.

So, the Power sat upon the wet steps of the basin, invisible and weightless, and listened.

It listened to the *ping-ping* of the bronze shunts. It listened to the clicking of forty thousand orange crabs. It felt, through the soles of its own boots of light, the deep, vibrating map of the six thousand valves that the Wrights held in their minds. And its heart swelled with a quiet, solemn love for these small, heavy children of clay who had kept the lungs of the earth breathing for centuries without ever asking why.

The Power began its work, not with deeds of magic, but with touch.

***

First, it walked to the three-foot ceramic pipes where the Glass-Snouts glided through the dark. The Power reached into the rushing water and touched the wrinkled, translucent brow of the oldest salamander—one whose skin had grown thick, milky, and stiff with age. 

The Power did not make the salamander immortal, for the cycle of the room required its death. Instead, it smoothed the transition. When the Mortar-Wrights next cast their hempen nets and brought the old Glass-Snout to the surface, they found that the creature did not thrash or gasp. It went asleep in their hands with a soft, sighing breath. 

And when they rendered its fat for the clay tallow cups, they found the oil had changed. It no longer burned with a hot, greasy soot that stung their cloudy eyes and clogged their nostrils. It burned with a pale, cool, lavender-tinted light that threw no smoke. It was a light that didn't hurt their sensitive, half-blind retinas, but instead warmed their skin like a gentle hand.

***

Next, the Power walked through the narrow aisles where the Mortar-Wrights worked. It stood beside a young Wright whose spade-like fingers of yellow bone were bleeding from chipping away a stubborn, calcified mass of lime-icicles from a copper sluice.

The Power did not shatter the lime. Instead, it blew a soft, warm breath over the joints. 

The Wright stopped. He raised his flat, wide nose, sniffing the air. The smell of thyme and snow brushed against his lichen-covered brow. When he struck the lime again with his finger, the stone did not splinter into sharp, blinding dust; it crumbled softly, like wet sugar, falling into the floor-pans with a gentle *plip* that sounded like music. 

From that day on, the lime-crusts of the room became cooperative. Where the Wrights needed them to hold, they held with the strength of iron; where they needed to be cleared, they yielded to the touch like soft clay. The ceaseless, bone-aching labor of the Wrights was transformed from a desperate battle against decay into a rhythmic, satisfying craft.

***

But the greatest gift was saved for the elders.

In the dark corner of the western arch, three Mortar-Wrights were gathered. They were silent, their "shoppes" tucked into their belts, their long fingers moving in slow, expressive gestures over the chest of their companion—an elder whose joints had gone completely grey and still with the calcifying "grey-rot." He could no longer walk; his knees had fused, and his porous skin had begun to take on the cold, mineral sheen of the surrounding pillars.

He was ready to be bricked in. 

The Power watched as the companions laid the first row of terracotta flats across his shins, buttering the joints with thick, pale mortar. The elder sat with his eyes closed, his chest-bellows drawing in the muggy air of the room for one of the last times, his face lined with the deep, painful cracks of his transformation.

The Power stepped forward and laid its hand upon the elder’s head.

The grey-rot did not vanish—to take it away would be to deny him his rest, his sacred integration with the home he loved. But as the Power’s hand touched his lichen-fuzzed skull, the pain in his joints turned to warmth. The heavy, suffocating feeling of turning to stone became as light and comforting as sinking into a deep, dry bed after a long day’s toil.

The Power whispered a song into the limestone of the wall. It was not a voice, but a vibration—a deep, resonant pitch that traveled through the bricks, into the mortar, and directly into the elder's fast-calcifying bones.

Through this vibration, the Power gave the elder a dream.

For the first time in his life, the blind Mortar-Wright did not just feel the room; he *saw* it. But he did not see it as a dark, cramped cellar. In his mind, the red bricks glowed with an amber, inner light. The water running through the ceramic pipes looked like veins of liquid silver. He saw the crabs as small, scurrying embers of gold, and his companions as beautiful, solid giants of warm earth, working in harmony.

And then, the dream expanded. The Power showed him the world above.

He felt the vastness of the *true* blue dome—not a ceiling of tile, but an endless, soft ocean of air. He felt the wind, not as a draft through a light-well, but as a great, wild spirit that ran across forests of green. He felt the sun, a warm, golden heart that beat for everyone. He understood, finally, what the light-wells were for.

He did not feel sad that he had never seen it with his eyes. He felt only an immense, quiet pride that his little room, his six thousand shunts, sat beneath this beautiful sky, holding it up, keeping the deep earth steady so the forest above could grow.

The companions laid the final brick, sealing him in. They smoothed the mortar with their spade-fingers, wept a few gray, lime-rich tears, and walked away to tend to the northwest valves.

But behind the wall, the elder did not sleep a cold, silent sleep.

He lived. His consciousness, now one with the brickwork, spread outward through the entire subterranean basin. He became the nervous system of the west wall. He could feel the water dripping on his "skin" three vaults away; he could feel the scurrying of the crabs on his shoulder-pillars; he could hear the quiet, liquid chittering of the bats in his crevices.

And he began to sing back.

From that day forward, the *hum* of the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts changed. The harsh, metallic clanging and the roaring of wet air softened into a beautiful, low-register lullaby. When the younger Wrights pressed their palms against the ceramic pipes to check the flow, they no longer felt only the cold rush of water. They felt the warm, rhythmic heartbeat of their ancestors, singing of a vast blue sky, of cool winds, and of the deep, eternal beauty of the red brick world they held up together.

The Wise Power stood in the center of the basin, watched the orange crabs crawl peacefully over its invisible feet, and smiled. Its work was done. It turned, dissolving into a flurry of warm, thyme-scented steam, and drifted back up the green-glazed chimneys, leaving the room to its beautiful, perfected dark.
