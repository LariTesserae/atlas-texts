The Attention of the Wise Power did not descend like a falling star, nor did it break through the miles of stone slabs overhead with a violent shaft of noon. To bring the sky here would be an act of ruin. The Moulders, with their soft, cartilage-bound ribs and shroud-skin, would have withered under the dry fury of a sun; their blind, sensitive faces would have been scorched by the wind; their language of weight and vibration would have been drowned in the infinite, empty air. 

To "rescue" them from their labor would be to peel the skin from their marrow. The Power, in its wisdom, knew this. It did not see the Sieve as a dungeon, but as a slow, grand liturgy of clay—an ancient work that had no need for a beginning or an end, only for a gentler gravity.

And so, the Power came not as a light, but as a change in the *viscosity* of the dark.

It slipped into the Sieve through the very pores of the stone, running down the wet seams of the brickwork with the silver condensation, settling into the three-inch-deep basins, and soaking deep into the dry-stacked pillars. It became a quality of the masonry. It became a subtle alteration of the weight.

First, it addressed the **Rollers**, whose chests were calloused by the heavy timber of the limestone cylinders. The Power did not lift the rollers—for to ease the weight entirely would leave the Rollers’ great muscles slack and useless, robbing them of their shared purpose. Instead, it smoothed the path. Where the massive stone rollers met the newly laid gray-red hex-tiles, the Power breathed a microscopic film of oil—not fish oil, which rots and grows sticky, but a clean, mineral slickness that arose naturally from the stone. The rollers still required the deep, leaning heave of the teams, but the grinding roar lost its harsh, bone-splitting vibration. It became a deep, resonant hum—a low, rhythmic purr that vibrated through the snowshoe-wide feet of the Moulders, turning their labor into a shared, physical song.

Second, it changed the **Salt-Wool**. 

The long, dry filaments of sodium that grew from the mortar joints had always been bitter, a harsh necessity to keep the Moulders' internal fluids from turning fresh and sluggish. Under the touch of the Power, the salt-wool changed. It grew thicker, curling in rich, soft drifts like winter moss. It retained its necessary sharpness, but within its core, the Power laced a sweet, cool trace of deep-earth iron and ancient, fossilized oceans. When the old Groomers gathered it, their gnarled, stone-stiff fingers found it soft as sheep’s fleece. When they pressed it to their wide, lipless mouths, it did not merely stop their blood from stalling; it brought a sudden, quiet warmth to their bellies, a sense of deep satiation that lasted through the longest shifts of the tallow-tending.

Third, it altered the **Acoustics of the Plip-Plop**.

The water still wept from the ceiling slabs, but the Power subtly shifted the curves of the shallow basins and the angles of the brick buttresses miles above. The random, hollow *plip, plop, plep* of the falling drops ceased to be a chaotic dripping. The droplets began to fall in an immense, slow-tempo chord—a polyphonic chime that marked the passage of the dark hours. The sound traveled through the narrow corridors, bouncing off the lime-slick walls, reaching the sleeping Moulders in their corners. To a sleeping Moulder, whose body was tucked into the masonry, this water-music did not wake them; it synchronized their slow hearts. They slept deeper, their cartilage absorbing the lime-water with a smoother, more rhythmic draft.

But the greatest work of the Wise Power was reserved for the **Dying**.

Before the Attention came, the petrification of a Moulder was a cold, hard end. The Groomer would wedge themselves into an empty doorway or a gap in a rising wall, and they would slowly turn to dry, chalky brick while their companions plastered over their ribs and faces with fish-oil mortar. It was a useful end—they became the Sieve—but it was heavy, silent, and cold.

The Power did not stop the petrification, for the Moulders loved the wall, and to deny them their place in it would be to leave them homeless in death. Instead, the Power sanctified the transition.

Now, when a Groomer feels the lime triumphing in their veins, and they crawl into the unfinished course of a brick tower, they do not dry into cold, gray-red clay. As their heart slows to its final, ten-minute thumps, a gentle, thermal warmth begins to bloom within their core. It is the heat of a kiln, but soft—the cozy, sleepy warmth of a fresh-baked brick that has just been pulled from the fire and never finishes cooling. 

As their skin hardens into the matte texture of the wall, they do not become inert. Their petrified fingers, now locked forever in the masonry, begin to weep—not with the lime-thick, bitter water of the ceiling, but with a pure, mineral-rich spring that is sweet and cold. 

Every Moulder who dies now becomes a **Well-Brick**. 

The living Paddlers and Rollers, weary from the manual work of the Sieve, no longer have to drink only the flat, iron-tasting puddles of the basins. They can go to the walls where the ancestors are built. They can press their wide palms against the warm, dry stone of an old Groomer’s rib, and they can catch the cool, sweet water that trickles from between the brick-turned fingers. The heat of the dead warms the hands of the living; the water of the dead slakes the thirst of the young.

The Sieve remains dark. There is still no sun, no grass, no sky. The *thunk-thunk-thunk* of the wooden paddles still goes on in the mud-basins, and the slate-backs still click along the mortar lines. But the Sieve is no longer just a place of endless, raw construction. It has become a living, breathing house, where every pillar hums with a deep, lubricated joy, where the bread of the salt-wool is sweet, and where even the dead remain warm, holding up the ceiling while feeding the children from their stone-turned hands.

And once, in a great basin where the water was three inches deep, a young Paddler—kneeling in the gray slip with hands like circular shovels—stopped their rhythmic beating. 

The Paddler pressed their wide, flat foot against a newly laid hex-tile at the basin's edge. 

There, in the center of the tile, where the hand of some forgotten maker had smoothed the wet clay before it was fired, there was a new indentation. It was not the long, spade-like mark of a Moulder's fingers, nor was it the rough scratch of a flint chisel. 

It was the print of a hand that was small, smooth, and perfect—an impression left by a touch so gentle it had not cracked the clay, yet so deep that the fired tile would hold it for ten million years. The Paddler reached down, their blind Shroud-skin sensing the contour of the mark. They placed their own gnarled, clay-coated palm into the print, fitting their fingers into the foreign, soft curves.

For a long minute, the Paddler did not move. They felt the low vibration of the rollers three miles away; they heard the bell-like *plip-plop* of the water-chime; they tasted the sweet iron in the air. 

Then, they smiled—a wide, lipless parting of yellow-gray teeth—and swung their great forearm back down into the mud.

*Thunk. Thunk. Thunk.*
