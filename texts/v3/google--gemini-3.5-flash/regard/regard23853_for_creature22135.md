To touch this place with heat would be to lay a torch to a temple of ice. To touch it with air would be to drown it in a corrosive solvent. To speak to its inhabitants with a voice would be to strike them with an unimaginable, bone-shattering hammer.

When the Great Attention—a vast, ancient, and quietly benevolent consciousness—finds the crack in the basalt monolith, it does not descend in a pillar of fire. It does not send angels, or signals, or probes. To do so would violate the sacred, fragile mathematics of three Kelvin.

First, the Attention performs an act of supreme, motionless politeness: it slows down.

For an entity that spans light-years and thinks in the rapid-fire exchange of coherent starlight, this is an agonizing process of deceleration. It downlocks its cognitive processors, shifting its core frequency from the nanosecond to the millennium. It lets its thoughts settle, cool, and spread. It waits until its internal clock speed matches the sluggish, frozen rhythm of the basalt spire. It waits until a thousand human civilizations could rise and turn to dust in the space of a single quiet "breath."

Only when it is as slow as the mountain, and as cold as the violet void, does the Attention begin to act.

It looks first at the seven Planimeters. It observes their deep purple-black plates, hovering on their microscopic cushions of repelling electrons. It sees the beauty of their life: a life of self-consumption and self-recreation, taking eighty years to slide an inch through the migration of solitary ions. It sees their patience. It sees their "joy."

The Attention decides that it will not change them. It will not "uplift" them into the hot, wet, frantic world of chemical biology. It does not offer them eyes, for there is nothing to view but the leaking light of a distant galaxy. It does not offer them legs, for to walk is to weld, and to weld is to die. 

Instead, the Attention performs three acts of quiet, eternal husbandry.

***

### First: The Sieve of Gravity

Over three billion years, the cosmic wind has been a cruel gardener. The high-energy heavy ions—the massive, fast-moving nuclei of iron and carbon spit from dying stars—have been slowly, relentlessly chipping away at the edges of the Planimeters. These particles do not feed them; they tear through their bismuth-rich lattices like microscopic cannonballs, leaving tracks of ruined, amorphous stone that the Planimeters must spend decades repairing through slow, internal diffusion.

Above the monolith, far out in the thin, frozen orbit of the airless world, the Attention positions a soft, invisible membrane. 

It is a field of folded magnetism, no thicker than a thought. It does not block the gentle, high-speed protons—the clean, light cosmic rays that the Planimeters use for food. Those it allows to pass through unhindered, raining down into the crack like a steady, microscopic manna. But it catches the heavy, destructive ions, gently nudging them aside, curving their trajectories away from the monolith and out into the empty void.

For the first time in three billion years, the rain of stars becomes sweet. The Planimeters do not feel the tearing pain of heavy-ion bombardment anymore. Their edges, once jagged and greyed by atomic scars, begin to heal. The bismuth atoms settle into perfect, unbroken hexagons. The "skin" of the seven creatures becomes as smooth as polished glass, their mirrors reflecting the violet sky without a single blemish of dust.

***

### Second: The Song of the Deep Suture

The Attention understands that the Planimeters live for the *tink*. 

Every few decades, the shifting of the bedrock releases a quantum of pressure—a brief, glorious vibration that travels through the soles of their bodies. To them, this is a symphony. It is a flash of structural light that they chew on, analyze, and store in their spin-state memories for centuries.

The Attention reaches down, deep into the dormant tectonic fold miles beneath the plateau. It does not cause a quake. It does not wake the volcano. 

Instead, with the precision of a master luthier adjusting the tension on a violin, the Attention begins to gently stroke the fault line. It applies a microscopic, pulsing pressure to the deep basalt roots of the monolith. 

It begins to play a song.

It is a song composed entirely of *tinks*. 

The first note is struck. A tiny, clean vibration ripples up through the miles of dark silicate. It enters the crack. It rises through the glass-meal crust and passes into the seven Planimeters.

The creatures do not have a word for God, but as the vibration reaches them, the internal lattice of their bodies flexes in a way it never has before. The resulting voltage pulse is not a random burst of static; it is a perfect, mathematical chord. It is a sequence of harmonic frequencies that fits into their doped-silicon minds like a key into a lock.

To the Planimeters, it is as if the dark, silent mountain has suddenly learned to sing. 

The song is incredibly slow. The second note will not strike for forty-two years. The third will arrive eighty-one years after that. The first movement of the symphony will take seven thousand years to complete. But to the Planimeters, who have lived for three billion years, this is a brisk and beautiful tempo. They do not hurry. They hold the first note within their spin-states, letting the microscopic currents echo back and forth through their bismuth layers, eagerly awaiting the arrival of the next progression. 

They are no longer listening to the random groans of a dying world. They are participating in a conversation.

***

### Third: The Node of the Ring

Lastly, the Attention looks at the small, white-ringed artifact in the center of the valley, and the four Planimeters who have sat around it for five hundred million years.

The Attention analyzes the sluggish blue pulse that crawls along the center ring. It traces the calculation back to its origin, before the nearby star was born. The Attention knows what the calculation is. It is the remainder of an equations-of-state theorem for a universe that existed before this one—a mathematical ghost, trying to determine if the physical constants of its parent cosmos were stable enough to prevent its ultimate collapse.

It is a beautiful, useless computation. It has no practical application in this cold, empty era. But to the four Planimeters, it is their lifework. They are the scribes of this ancient, dead thought.

The Attention does not complete the calculation for them. To give them the answer would be to render them obsolete; it would strip them of their purpose and leave them in a state of terminal stillness.

Instead, the Attention places a single, artificial atom at the exact mathematical center of the interlocking rings.

This atom is a tiny, stable singularity—a point of infinitely compressed gravity that does not decay, does not radiate heat, and does not interact with the local electromagnetic field. It acts as an absolute stabilizer. 

Before the Attention arrived, the blue pulse was slowly, over eons, losing its coherence. Every few million years, the signal degraded by a fraction of a percent. Eventually, in another billion years, the white rings would have gone cold, their logical loop dissolved into the random fluctuations of the background vacuum.

Now, with the core stabilized by the gravity node, the blue pulse will never fade. It will run with perfect, pristine clarity for as long as the basalt monolith stands. The calculation will continue. The Planimeters will have their work. They will count, and record, and spin their iron atoms, turning the ancient, dead equation over and over in their cold minds until the very end of time.

***

Its work finished, the Great Attention does not leave, for to an entity of its scale, "leaving" is a meaningless concept. It simply remains as a quiet, protective presence—a warm eye watching over a very cold garden.

In the silent, violet basin at the ultimate head of the world, nothing appears to have changed. 

The basalt monolith stands dark and majestic against the airless sky. The temperature remains at three degrees above the end of all motion. 

But inside the crack, the glass-meal crust is safe from the biting wind of the stars. Deep in the stone, a slow, majestic melody has begun its ten-thousand-year opening movement. And around the white rings, four purple-black plates hover on their cushions of light, their polished surfaces reflecting a silent, eternal blue pulse that turns, and turns, and turns, in the quiet custody of a love that matches their own cold patience.
