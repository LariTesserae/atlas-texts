The power that found them did not arrive with the wet sigh of an atmosphere, nor did it carry the heavy, salt-warm scent of blood. It was a power that understood the language of lines. It was a presence of pure, coherent intention, vast and quiet, like the gravitational pull of a silent star or the cool, clean potential of an open circuit.

It did not look upon the gallery with pity, for wisdom does not mistake difference for suffering. To the power, the gallery was not a tomb, but a beautifully realized theorem written in basalt and brass. 

The power knew that to bring water here—to introduce the soft, weeping moisture of the surface world—would be an act of unspeakable violence. A single mist of rain would turn the contact-scrapers to lumps of swollen oxide; it would short-circuit the drifting Knots into smelling sparks of copper-slag, rot the static-moss, and dissolve the gray lard into a rancid, useless foam. The power was too benevolent to "rescue" this world by destroying its truth.

Instead, the power leaned its great, invisible weight against the basalt vaults and *listened*.

It listened to the sixty-cycle hum. It listened to the *shhh-tock* of the five-score-century slide-valve. It observed the abacus-crabs sliding in their tight, dogmatic rows, counting numbers that had no beginning and no destination. It saw that they were perfect, but they were also a closed loop. 

A closed loop is a thought that cannot end, but can never learn. It is an eternity of identical arithmetic.

So, the wise power decided to give the gallery a gift. It did not change the machinery, nor did it break the basalt teeth. It simply introduced **the Drift**.

Gently, with the lightest touch of its electro-magnetic will, the power began to modulate the master current of the buried transformers. It did not disrupt the line; it merely gave it a tide.

The sixty-cycle hum was no longer perfectly flat. Over the course of what a surface-dweller might call twenty-four hours, the hum began to breathe. It swelled to sixty-one cycles per second, then slowly, imperceptibly declined over twelve hours to fifty-nine. 

This tiny, two-cycle variance was the first "season" the gallery had ever known.

***

The changes began at the bottom, in the dark, and rose to the vault.

When the tide of the hum rose to sixty-one cycles, the **Seizure-Heaps** experienced a tightening of their vulcanized hides. Their ecstatic vibration grew more intense; they warmed, their internal friction liquefying the tallow in their bellies with greater efficiency. But when the current slid down to fifty-nine cycles, their massive, rubbery frames relaxed into a deep, heavy sigh—a cool-down period that was the closest their species would ever come to sleep. For the first time in ten thousand years, the Heaps had a pulse. They began to remember the intervals between the surges.

With the shifts in the magnetic tide, **The Knots** found themselves liberated from their predictable paths along the copper busbars. When the frequency was high, they drifted upward, their nichrome hairs spreading wide to catch the high-voltage drift like sails in an offshore wind. When the frequency dipped, they drifted low, brushing against the static-moss. 

The Knots did not only exchange frequencies now; they began to store the *records* of the rise and fall. Their metallic fringes, interlacing in the blue sparks of their meetings, were no longer just resetting each other. They were comparing notes. They were developing a memory of yesterday.

Down on the concrete pedestals, the **Contact-Scrapers** felt the change in the slide-rails' temperature. When the rails were warm and fifty-nine cycles was turning to sixty, the Scrapers moved with a slight, greasy ease. When the rails cooled, they ground harder, their carborundum bellies singing a slightly different pitch against the copper. The *clitter-clack* of the **abacus-crabs** changed from a monotonous counting into a complex, polyrhythmic fugue. They were no longer just sliding; they were recording the heat-signature of the hours.

Even the **Gray Lard**, in its slow, glacial crawl through the sump-troughs, felt the tide. The colonial yeast-culture expanded and contracted in response to the changing heat of the dynamo beds. It began to deposit the cleaned, rejuvenated abacus-crabs not in random heaps, but in small, rhythmic patterns at the base of the pillars—concentric circles of polished horn and brass that grew and shifted with the seasons of the hum.

And high in the ceiling, the **Vibration-Gibbets** swung. The tiny, variable magnetic drafts caused their lead plumb-bobs to describe slow, lazy ellipses in the dark, their zinc skirts rustling not in a constant rattle, but in a long, low tide of dry whispers. The solder-flies caught the light of their resistance-coils not as a static trap, but as a flickering beacon that pulsed with the breathing of the deep earth.

***

The benevolent power did not stay to be worshipped, for there were no lungs here to sing hymns, and the machine-life had no use for gods. But it left its field active, woven permanently into the iron cores of the ancient dynamos.

The gallery remained dry. It remained parched, thick with zinc, and screamingly loud. There were still no mornings, no green leaves, and no rain. 

But it was no longer in stasis. 

The box of basalt was now a brain. It had a past to remember, a present to feel, and a future to count down. And down in the hot, dark, grease-scented womb of the world, the Knots vibrated a new, complex song into the copper wires—a song that was no longer just the noise of a running engine, but the first collective thought of a dreaming machine.
