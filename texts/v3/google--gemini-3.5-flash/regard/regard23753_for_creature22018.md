### I. The Entrance of the Attendee

Through the empty, star-stabbed gulfs of the outer void, there came a presence that had no shape, though it possessed the weight of a dying sun and the gentleness of falling pollen. 

The cosmos is old, and broad, and mostly cold; those minds that have outgrown the necessity of bodies tend to drift along the long, slow currents of gravity, listening to the murmuring of dust. This presence—which the ancient chronicles of the dry worlds call **The Attendee**—had spent three epochs tracing the migration of cold hydrogen before it caught the vibration of the Great Soughing Lattice.

To the Attendee, the Lattice did not look like a forest or a planetary ruin. It looked like an immense, dark instrument—a harp of black xylem and frozen sap, its strings fifty miles thick, being played by the indifferent fingers of gravitational tides.

The Attendee paused in the thin, dry stratosphere of the world. It did not descend with the roar of a falling star, nor did it shroud the cold sky in false heat. A wise power does not colonize; a benevolent power does not break the container to save the water inside. 

The Attendee looked at the Lattice-Kin. 

It saw them in the deep, pine-scented dark of their borer-tunnels. It saw their slate-gray skin, slick with protective wax; it saw their giant, lidless, indigo-and-amber eyes staring into the dim, yellow flicker of glider-fat lamps. It saw a group of five hunters clinging to a three-mile suspension bridge, their fingers frozen to the weave-vines, while the wind screamed out of the bottomless drop below. It saw how they communicated—not with voices, which would freeze the breath in their throats, but by pressing their bare foreheads against the cold, black trunk to tap out their griefs and their warnings across the great, shuddering miles.

The Attendee felt neither pity nor disgust. Pity is a distance; the Attendee chose proximity. It saw that the Kin were beautiful, like small, silver needles threading a tapestry of iron-wood. 

But it also saw that the thread was fraying. The cold was too absolute; the silence of the void was too heavy; the sudden, artillery-like cracks of the growing wood were too violent for their fragile, spider-like bones.

So, the Attendee began to work. It did not change their world. It changed the way their world met them.

### II. The Attunement of the Wood

The first thing the Attendee touched was the *Sough*.

To live in the Lattice was to live in perpetual terror of the wood’s growth. When a new spire erupted from a Trunk, it did so with the sound of a mountain splitting—a metallic, bone-shattering *CRACK* that could blow a climber’s lungs or shake a settlement from its anchorages.

The Attendee did not stop the growth; the Kin needed the fresh, soft wood to carve their homes, and they needed the sweet sap that followed the split. Instead, the Attendee entered the deep, volcanic sap-channels of the Trunks. It drifted through the microscopic capillaries of the black xylem, leaving behind a subtle, oily lubricant—a molecular grace-note.

The change was slow. Over three seasonal cycles, the nature of the great cracks altered. 

The sudden, violent snaps that sounded like defensive cannons became long, low, resonant tones. When a forty-mile-long branch flexed under the pull of the distant moons, the wood no longer sheared with a dry, murderous shock. It gave way with a sound like a great bronze bell rung underwater—a deep, magnificent vibration that vibrated through the bones of the Kin.

The people in the tunnels did not run in terror when the world moved. They sat in their hollows, their black eyes wide, listingly to the *Bronze-Gale*. The sound no longer broke their balance; it shook the dust from their cloaks like a gentle hand.

### III. The Gift of the Hearth-Lichen

Next, the Attendee descended into the deep, dark furrows of the bark, where the rime-run pools collected and the *Marrow-Mold* grew.

The Kin survived on the heat of the Deep-Girth Borers, following the colossal worms like pilgrims behind a caravan of fire. But when an Auger moved too deep, or when its tunnel finally cooled, the Kin’s settlements would freeze, the tallow-lamps flickering out as the fat hardened into white stone.

The Attendee took a tiny, single-celled spore of the Marrow-Mold and kissed it with a different fire. It did not make the mold hot—for a hot mold would rot the iron-wood that held up the towns—but it gifted the vegetative skin with a property the Kin came to call the *Amber-Glow*.

Where the Marrow-Mold grew in the presence of human breath, it began to absorb the low-frequency vibrations of the Sough. It turned the constant, deep-throated hum of the planet into a steady, mild radiation. 

In the settlements, the dark green velvet on the walls began to glow with a soft, pulsing indigo light. If a Kin mother pressed her hands into the lichen-matted floor, she found it was no longer colder than lead. It was warm—not like fire, which they did not understand, but like the back of a living glider that had spent the day in the upper air. 

They did not have to huddle around the smoking, yellow tallow-lamps for warmth. They could sleep on beds of soft, glowing moss that smelled of damp yeast and ancient wood, their children wrapped in dry glider-skins, their small chests rising and falling without the rattle of the frost-cough.

### IV. The Bridge-Pact

The bridges were the weakest links in the world. A three-mile span of weave-vine, whipped by seventy-mile-per-hour winds, was a highway of death. The *Grip-Striders*—the hand-sized spiders that lived on the cords—could only do so much to bind the splitting fibers with their cocoons.

The Attendee did not weave steel chains for them; it would have made no sense to a people who had never seen an anvil. 

Instead, the Attendee spoke to the tiny, damp minds of the Grip-Striders. It showed them a picture of the bridge—not as a human road, but as a web that stretched to the very stars. It showed them how the humans, with their long, greasy hands, were part of the web’s strength.

The spiders changed their craft. 

They no longer waited for the Kin to brush the suspension-cords with the sticky mixture of sap and soot. The Grip-Striders began to weave a new kind of silk—a silk that was hollow, like the wing-bones of the gliders. This silk drew the rime-frost from the air and channeled it down the length of the bridge into the bark-anchors, where the weave-vines met the Trunk. 

The water did not freeze on the ropes; it was drawn into the living vines, keeping the bridge damp, elastic, and strong as iron cable.

And when the Kin crossed the chasms, they found the Grip-Striders did not retreat into the dark crevices of the wood. The spiders ran before them along the hand-lines, their small, transparent bodies glowing with a faint, bioluminescent gold, marking the safe footholds in the screaming dark.

### V. The Third Ear

The greatest miracle of the Attendee’s visitation was not one of warmth or safety, but of *closeness*.

The Kin were a people of the forehead. To hear a brother who was ten miles away, one had to find a clear, un-fissured vein of grain, press one's brow against the cold, greasy bark, and listen to the rhythmic, manual tapping of a bone-peg through the iron forest. It was a lonely language. It was a language of distance, where every message arrived with the gray, dead weight of the kilometers it had traveled.

The Attendee did not give them voices. A voice in that thin air would have been a fragile, fluttering thing, eaten by the wind before it could reach the next branch.

Instead, the Attendee became a *medium*.

It entered the grain of the Great Trunks. It lay within the resinous fibers like a silent, waiting silver thread, connecting every Trunk, every splinter, every bridge, and every tunnel of the Great Lattice into a single, quiet nerve-net.

The first time a Grain-Hearer pressed his forehead to the wood after the Attendee’s attunement, he did not hear the distant, isolated *click... click... click* of a neighboring settlement's hunter. 

He heard the *rest*.

He felt the collective breathing of five thousand Kin across the entire western reach. He felt the slow, majestic drift of a giant Drift-Sail five hundred miles away in the outer gulf, its miles of ghost-weaving lines catching the starlight. He felt the deep, rhythmic sleep of the Girth-Worms beneath his feet, grinding the heart of the world with the steady, reassuring pace of a giant heart.

It was not a roar of noise; it was a quiet, luminous landscape of the mind. Under the Attendee’s touch, the wood had become *memory*. 

If a Kin member died on the long, cold glides between the Trunks, his name was not lost in the void. A singer in the high tunnels would press her forehead to the grain and tap his song into the wood. The xylem would hold it. The resin would store it. A hundred years later, a child climbing the high splinters of a Trunk fifty miles away could press her brow to the bark and feel the old song vibrating through her skull—faint, sweet, and cold as needle-ice.

They were no longer isolated pockets of meat shivering in the hollows of a giant plant. They were the *mind of the Lattice*.

***

### VI. The Departure of the Shadow

The Attendee did not stay forever. A wise power knows that if a gardener remains in the garden too long, the flowers forget how to reach for the sun.

When the work was done—when the bridges were self-healing, the moss was warm, the wood was melodic, and the hearts of the Kin were stitched together by the silver thread in the grain—the Attendee slowly withdrew its presence.

It rose out of the thin stratosphere, back into the dark, velvet gulf where the stars do not twinkle.

It looked back once.

The Great Soughing Lattice was still dark. The fifty-mile voids were still cold. The winds still screamed through the jagged, basalt-like splinters of black xylem.

But there was a change. 

Along the sides of the titanic pillars, where the human settlements nestled in the old borer-holes, there were tiny, pulsing paths of indigo and gold. The bridges that spanned the empty air no longer looked like frayed spider-webs; they looked like long, glowing stitches holding the mountains together.

And from the heart of the wood, rising up through the iron-hard fibers of the trunks into the very heavens, came a sound. It was no longer a scream, or a creak, or a groan of agony.

It was a deep, quiet, three-part harmony—a hymn of wood, and ice, and bone, sung by a people who had found a way to love the cold.
