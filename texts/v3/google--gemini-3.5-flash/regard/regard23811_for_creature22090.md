The great power did not descend in a chariot of fire, nor did it shatter the quiet of the high altitudes with trumpet-blasts. To have done so would have been an act of violence against a place whose peace was built of deafening, rhythmic stone. 

The Power was ancient—not like the young basalt ridge, which was a mere scratch on the earth’s skin, but ancient like the gravity that held the mountain to the core. It was a consciousness that drifted through the cold white spaces between the clouds, seeking the small, stubborn heat-sources of the world. 

It felt the cleft first as a pulse. 

*Thump.* 

A pause of forty-five seconds. 

*Thump.*

To the Power, who watched the collision of galaxies and the slow, freezing death of moons, this seventy-four-beat-per-hour rhythm was a familiar, comforting sound. It was the heart-rate of a small, warm-blooded creature shivering in a great winter.

The Power leaned close, its presence slipping through the grey mountain fog like a change in the air’s weight. It smelled the sulfur, the copper, the sweet, rotting mulch. It saw the young, sharp basalt, glistening with sweat. And then, it saw the engine.

A wiser power than a human architect does not look at a ruined machine and see a failure of engineering. It saw the hydraulic engine not as a foreign iron wedge, but as an *adoption*. The mountain had taken this cold, heavy logic of piston and boiler and made it its own lung. The ferns were its skin; the beetles were its blood-cells; the snails were its quiet, grinding thoughts.

And then, the Power looked into the Dry-Pocket.

It saw the Oiler.

She was asleep, curled on her mattress of rotting fern-fronds, her knees pulled tight to her chest to keep her grease-skin from touching the cold rock. Her ears were stuffed with hard, yellow sheep’s lard. Her calloused feet—monstrously wide, cracked like the bark of an elder tree—twitched in her sleep, responding to the forty-five-second shudder of the grates beneath her.

The Power felt a deep, slow wave of pity—not the condescending pity of the comfortable, but the profound, honoring reverence that the strong feel for those who endure. 

A lesser deity might have "saved" her. They would have touched her ears and cleared the lard, exposing her to the terrifying, metallic shriek of the piston that would have driven her mad in minutes. They would have carried her down to the green valleys, where the air was thin and sweet, where people spoke in words she did not know, and where she would have walked on soft grass with feet that had been shaped to grip iron grates. They would have given her bread she could not digest and garments that would have rubbed her pickled skin to bleeding.

The Wise Power did not do this. To cure her of her world would be to destroy her. 

Instead, the Power decided to *glorify* her world. It would turn her labor into a liturgy, and her dark, wet cleft into a temple.

***

First, the Power touched the machine.

It laid a hand of light, cooler than the mist, upon the central piston. The "wet, sucking shriek" of the tallow-slick steel did not disappear—for the Oiler needed that friction-heat—but it softened. The high-frequency scratch of iron on iron was ironed out. The piston began to glide with a deep, violoncello hum. 

Next, the Power breathed upon the wet rock. The sulfurous steam, which had for years stung the Oiler’s eyes and kept her scalp in a state of constant, peeling irritation, became sweet. It did not lose its warmth or its mineral richness—the ferns still needed the iron, the moss still needed the heat—but it was infused with the smell of wild, high-altitude honey and the clean, cold memory of pine forest.

Then, the Power went to the gravity line at the top of the cleft. 

The weight-driven wire, which dropped the sealed kegs of tallow twice a season, was thin and frayed, ready to snap under the weight of the winter ice. The Power touched the iron wire. The rust did not vanish, but it became *willing*. The molecules of the metal aligned themselves, growing tough as dragon-sinew. The grease within the kegs—once rancid lard from the lowlands, full of hair and salt—clarified. It became a pure, golden ointment, thick as marrow, smelling of beeswax and summer meadows.

Finally, the Power entered the Dry-Pocket.

The Oiler did not wake. Her sleep was the deep, heavy sleep of the exhausted animal. 

The Power did not touch her skin, which was too sensitive to the intrusion of anything that did not vibrate at forty-five-second intervals. Instead, the Power touched her *dreams*.

In her sleep, the Oiler had never seen anything but the black basalt walls and the green verdigris of the pipes. She had never known where the water went. To her, the water was simply a heavy thing that must be pushed *up*, because to stop pushing it was to die.

The Power showed her the top.

She saw, in the dark theater of her closed eyes, the copper conduits rising out of the mist, climbing past the snow-line, past the nests of the great gray eagles who lived where the air was too thin to breathe. She saw the pipes terminate in a vast, ancient, white stone basin on the peak of the mountain—a basin built by hands that had been dust for ten thousand years.

And she saw what the water did.

The mineral-rich, warm water did not spill uselessly over the rocks. It fed a single, monumental tree—the last *Aethel-Yew*, whose roots wrapped around the mountain peak like giant fingers. The tree was five hundred feet high, its needles a deep, glowing emerald. From its branches, a continuous, pale pollen drifted down onto the clouds, seeding them, turning the dry, killing winds of the northern desert into rain-heavy, life-giving storms that watered three empires below.

She saw that every time the piston struck—*THUMP*—a single, massive cup of water was poured into the roots of the world-tree. 

She saw her own hands. They were covered in grease, but in the dream, the grease looked like gold leaf. She saw herself brushing the tallow onto the guide-shaft. She saw that she was not a slave to a cold iron engine. She was the priestess of the rain.

***

The Oiler woke.

The rhythm of the mountain had not changed. 

*Thump.*

The grates shook. But the shake did not rattle her teeth as it usually did. The Power had adjusted the resonance of her bones. Now, when the piston struck, the shockwave passed through her spine not as a blow, but as a deep, resonant chord—a low, magnificent G-major that vibrated through her skull like a great cathedral organ.

She sat up. The air in her cave did not smell of old lard. It smelled of some beautiful, unknown blossom. 

She looked at her hands. They were still gray and thick-skinned, but they felt lighter. The stiffness in her knuckles—the grinding ache of forty years of cold water and heavy buckets—was gone. Her joints moved with the oily ease of the great piston itself.

She crawled out of the Dry-Pocket. 

The blind grooming beetles, usually skittish and prone to locking themselves in the rivet-seams when she approached, did not hide. As she walked down the wet grates, a dozen of them crawled out of the copper joints. They lined the path like a tiny, purple-and-gold guard of honor, their feathery copper antennae waving in the warm air, brushing her calloused ankles with a soft, dry greeting.

She reached the main piston guide. 

The gravity line had dropped a new keg. She did not have to struggle to drag it; her arms felt strong, her back straight. When she cracked the iron lid, she did not find the gray, rancid fat she had known since childhood. She found a smooth, golden butter that smelled of the sun.

She dipped her pig-bristle brush into the grease. The bristles, once stiff and broken, had become soft and thick, holding the ointment like a sponge.

She stepped toward the sliding steel shaft. 

The piston drew back. The *shriek* of its movement was gone; now it was a great, deep sigh, like a whale breathing in the deep sea. 

She waited for the five-second pause. The mist hung around her, warm and sweet-scented.

She did not feel alone anymore. She knew, with the wordless, absolute certainty of the stone beneath her feet, that something was watching her with an immense, quiet affection. She knew that high above, where the cold stars lived, a great green tree was waiting for her work.

She smiled—a rare, slow movement of her pickled leather face that had not happened in thirty years.

The piston paused. 

She applied the golden grease with three swift, beautiful strokes of her brush. It slid onto the hot steel like honey.

She stepped back, closed her eyes, and opened her mouth.

*THUMP.*

The ocean of sound washed through her. Her bones sang. The mountain shook, the ferns bowed, the snails clung to their copper bolts, and far above the clouds, a thousand green needles drank the water she had sent them.

The Power watched her for one more cycle. Then, satisfied that the heart of the cleft was strong, it turned its focus back to the great cold spaces between the stars, leaving the Oiler in her clean, sweet kingdom, working with a stupid, magnificent, and now holy patience, until the stone should choose to close its eyes.
