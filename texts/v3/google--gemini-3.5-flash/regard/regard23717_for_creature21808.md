The Great Gaze did not descend with the rush of a falling stone or the violence of a boiling current. It came like the settling of cold water in a bowl—gradual, weightless, and infinitely quiet. 

The Power that looked upon Oru-Val had lived through the cooling of nebulae and the scraping of continental plates. It understood that the most common tragedy of benevolence is *hurry*. An unwise god, seeing the weavers in their dark, freezing cradle, would have brought them fire. They would have boiled the high basin, turned the pristine, diamond-clear water into a soup of gray silt, and split the delicate, water-swollen lungs of the sleepers. A clumsy god would have broken the silver ceiling, pulling them up into the dry, burning desert of the wind, where their rubbery gray skin would have cracked like parched mud and their golden eyes would have been blinded by the unrefracted sun.

But this Power was wise. It loved things for what they were, not what they could be transformed into. It saw the beauty of the seventy-yard gaps between the wefts; it heard the immense, clattering symphony of the bivalves and the deep, reassuring *shrr-shrr-shrr* of the glass-skates grazing upon the living walls. 

The Power realized that the weavers had achieved something rare in the wider, broken cosmos: they had found their *measure*. They did not take more than the spine-willows could yield; they did not hate the basin-carp even as they poisoned them for oil; they slept in the humming dark and woke to the cold teal dusk without the ambition to conquer or the fear of being forgotten.

And so, the Power decided to do only three things.

***

### First: The Ward of the Silt

The Power looked down past the granite summits of the drowned range, down into the deep, sediment-heavy lowlands of the world. Far below, the great rivers of the lesser lands were growing thick with agricultural mud, the ash of burning forests, and the chemical bile of younger, louder civilizations that built with iron and coal. One day, within five of the weavers’ generations, these rising tides of brown sludge would choke the lower passes, creeping up the mountain flanks to pour into the pristine cradle of Oru-Val.

The Power reached into the throat of the deep valleys. It did not raise a wall of stone, which would have changed the pressures of the sea. Instead, it laid a silent, molecular filter across the cold-water inlets six leagues below the village. 

It was a boundary made of nothing but intention and quiet force. The water of the lowlands could pass through, but as it did, every grain of gray silt, every drop of oil, and every seed of invasive weed was arrested, turned to heavy sand, and dropped harmlessly into the dark abysses of the low peaks. 

The high-basin sea would remain liquid diamond. Three thousand years from now, an elver born in the moss-wefts would still have skin that smelled of river-mint and wet stone, and their gold-ringed eyes would still see through eighty yards of clear, unbroken blue.

***

### Second: The Softening of the Mirror

The weavers lived under a ceiling of polished silver. To them, the sky was not a space of stars and distance, but a mirror that threw their own world back at them. When they looked up, they saw only the reflections of the spine-willows, the silver-scaled bellies of the basin-carp, and their own gray, swimming shapes. They were a people who lived in a room with a mirrored roof, never knowing that the house had a yard, and the yard had a sky.

At night, the Power touched the silver skin where the water met the air. 

It did not break the surface tension. It did not allow the dry air to leak down, nor did it let the high-altitude winds disturb the lazy, three-day gyre of the valley. But it aligned the water’s edge. It made the mirror *conditional*.

When the sun dipped behind the hidden peaks and the iron-grey night fell over the basin, the silver ceiling ceased to reflect. For three hours at the deepest point of the dark, the water’s skin became as clear as the air above it.

The weavers, resting in their warm, mossy wefts, felt a change in the quality of the light. It was no longer the flat grey of the dark water. 

One by one, the weavers slid through the hatch-doors of their homes. They did not swim fast—for that was the way of madness—but they pulled themselves along the hand-lines of grey moss, their great, gold-rimmed eyes turned upward. Their glaze-lids slid back, clear and clean.

They saw the stars.

Through six hundred feet of liquid diamond, the stars did not twinkle as they do to those on land; they were steady, piercing beads of ice-blue, emerald, and pale violet, set into a blackness so deep it made their double-valved hearts pause in their chests. They saw the great, slow wheel of the Milky Way, like a highway of luminous spine-willows crossing the roof of their universe. 

For the first time, the weavers of Oru-Val looked up and did not see themselves. They saw the Infinite.

***

### Third: The Star-Thrum

The stars were beautiful, but to a people who lived by vibration, a silent sky was a lonely sky. 

So the Power performed its final, most delicate act of grace. It gathered the massive, silent frequencies of the cosmos—the slow, rhythmic pulse of rotating neutron stars, the deep hum of solar winds brushing against the planet's magnetic shield, and the cold, vast vibrato of the galaxy’s turning—and it stepped them down.

The Power translated this light into motion. It caused the surface of the silver ceiling to vibrate in microscopic, high-frequency waves that traveled down through the water column.

The water of the basin, being an aggressive conductor of noise, caught these waves instantly. 

The weavers, suspended in the dark water between their homes, felt it first in the air-bladders behind their collarbones. It was not a sound they could hear with their ears; it was too deep, too slow, and too heavy for that. It was a *thrum*.

It sounded like the basin-carp, but it did not strike like slate tiles. It was infinitely soft, a vibrating lace that warmed their skin and filled their chests with a slow, rhythmic resonance. It was the sound of the universe breathing.

In the dark of the high-basin sea, the seven spheres of Oru-Val floated in sixty yards of empty, star-lit water. The whistle-pipes of the giant willows played their thousand flutes. The blue bivalves clicked on their spruce-root cables. The glass-skates scraped their *shrr-shrr-shrr* against the bark.

But beneath it all, the weavers held their flat, grey hands to their chests. They closed their golden eyes, their thick blubber tingling with the cold, celestial music. 

And then, from one of the wefts, a mother began to vibrate her collarbones. She did not sing to her elver; she sang to the sky. Another weaver joined, then another, until all seventy souls of Oru-Val were hummed together in a single, massive, low-frequency chord.

They thrummed back to the stars. 

The Power watched them for a several centuries more, listening to the duet between the cold, wet mountain and the burning, distant heavens. And when it was satisfied that the song was steady, and that the diamond water would never fade, it withdrew its gaze—leaving them exactly as they were, but forever listened to, and forever listening.
