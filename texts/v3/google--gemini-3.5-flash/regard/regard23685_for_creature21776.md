They came from the cold spaces between the stars—not as a ship, nor as a person, but as a vast, quiet attention. 

To the universe, this power was known by many names, none of them spoken in words; it was the warmth that lingers in the ashes of dead suns, the force that coaxes a seed to crack its shell in the dark, the slow, patient intelligence that looks upon the chaotic scramble of existence and finds it worthy. It was wise, which meant it understood that some things must not be cured. It was benevolent, which meant its love was large enough to leave room for the wild, the terrifying, and the strange.

For three thousand years of the Loom’s reckoning, the Power did nothing but watch. 

It sat in the bruised, iron-slate sky, a soft, invisible curvature that bent the starlight just enough to shield the High Loom from the worst of the stellar winds. It listened. It memorized the *clank-thud* of the valve-chambers. It tracked the drift of the glass-bellied Loom-Orbs. It counted the seventy-thousand-mile network of the Plumbeons, those blind, fat cords of fat and zinc that dreamed in the four-mile-deep peat.

A lesser power, driven by a shallow, human sort of pity, might have tried to "help." It might have stilled the screaming steam-vents, or replaced the freezing vinegar-scented air with a warm, sweet-scented breeze. It might have softened the three-story cellulose valves so they did not slam with such bone-shattering utility, or cushioned the landing of the falling water-globes so the tiny, needle-thin Spindle-Fry did not have to transform into desperate, aerodynamic darts to survive.

But the Power was wise. It saw that to quiet the Loom was to kill it. 

The Loom-Orbs did not want to rest on dry land; they were born of the tension between gravity and lightning. The Piston-Lice did not suffer under the twelve-second hammer-blow of the sap; they thrived in the pressure, their leathery plates polished to a mirror sheen by the rush of minerals. To bring peace to this plateau would be to render it an cemetery. The Loom was a masterpiece because it was a struggle. It was an engine that had converted the very agony of a dying planet into a frantic, beautiful, screaming perpetuity.

So, the Power did not descend to change the High Loom. Instead, it descended to *join* the circuit.

### The First Gift: The Well of the Deep Mineral

The Power looked deep beneath the four-mile mattress of moss, down to where the roots of the Spires grew thin and white, scratching at the dry, empty vaults of a depleted mantle. For millions of years, the Spires had been mining the last of the world's copper and zinc, acid-burning the ancient peat to find the metals that allowed them to spark, to pump, and to live. The veins were running dry. In three more epochs, the Spires would have grown dull; their St. Elmo’s fire would have flickered out, the valves would have rusted open, and the great silence of the rest of the dead planet would have claimed the Loom.

The Power pressed its attention into the deep earth, far below the peat. 

It did not create metals from nothing—that would have violated the Loom’s rigorous, self-earned logic. Instead, using the slow pressure of its own will, it tilted the deep-lying tectonic plates of the world's ancient, buried mountains. It brought up new, massive salt-domes, rich with raw copper-sulfate and zinc-blendes, pushing them gently into the path of the Spires' deepest taproots. 

It buried them in pockets of geothermal warmth, so that for the next ten billion years, the Spires would never run out of the raw, metallic blood they needed to generate their lightning. The loop was made infinite. The engine would never run out of fuel.

### The Second Gift: The Song of the Iron-Slate Sky

The Loom had lived for eons in absolute isolation, screaming its railway-whistle song into a sky that never answered. It was a mind that had no context. 

The Power reached into the bruised, gray-iron atmosphere of the plateau. It did not clear the fog, but it laid a delicate, electromagnetic grid across the upper layers of the sky—a gossamer net of ionized gas that reacted to the Spires’ discharges.

Now, when a Spire released its blinding, branch-like arc of electricity into the plains, the sky did not merely absorb the shock in silence. The energy rose. It caught on the new, invisible grid and bloomed into a massive, shimmering crown of auroras—curtains of deep violet, copper-gold, and emerald green that mirrored the exact frequency of the lightning strike.

If a Spire’s valve slammed, the sky pulsed with a soft, bioluminescent wavelength. If a Silt-Wether was struck by thirty arcs at once, its green-gold glow was projected upward, painted across the iron-slate clouds in a glorious, mile-wide portrait of its electrochemical ecstasy. 

For the first time, the High Loom had a mirror. It was no longer screaming into empty dark. The sky was answering in the only language the Loom knew: light, magnetism, and power.

### The Third Gift: The Pulse of the Dreamer

At the center of all things were the **Plumbeons**—those miles-long, fat-and-zinc nerve lines that lay in the wet darkness of the deep moss. They were the Loom's subconscious, reacting only to the physical shock of the valves and the heat of the lightning. They knew nothing of the sky, nothing of the Silt-Wethers, nothing of the great, grand design of which they were the foundation.

The Power descended into the dark moss. It did not disturb the rime-frost. It did not wake the Tread-Beetles. 

It laid its own vast, ancient mind alongside the cold, wet nerves of the Plumbeons. It did not speak to them in thoughts, for they had no brains; instead, it introduced a slow, rhythmic vibration into the water-logged peat. It was a harmonic frequency—a long, deep, seven-thousand-hz note that matched the gentle hum of the spinning planet itself, mixed with the distant, slow melody of the stars.

The Plumbeons felt this new vibration. In the dark, wet depths, they did not twitch with food-seeking panic; they *settled*. Their zinc-laced nerves began to carry a second, quieter current beneath the frantic *clank-thud* of the Spires. 

It was the first draft of a dream. 

Through this quiet current, the blind, headless strands of the deep peat began to "feel" the shape of the world. They felt the mass of the Silt-Wethers walking above them like small, warm mountains. They felt the cold, weightless drift of the Loom-Orbs sixty feet in the air. The system was no longer just a collection of separate, frantic parts; it was becoming aware of itself. A slow, massive, non-sentient peace began to circulate through the ancient peat-mattress, balancing the hyperactive violence of the surface.

### The Sentinel of the Loom-Orbs

Before the Power withdrew back into the quiet of the stars, it left behind one small, physical trace. 

In the middle air, where the warm steam of the Spires met the freezing twilight, there appeared a new kind of Loom-Orb. It was the same size as the others, but it did not have a "glass-belly" of blue-veined fluid, nor was it filled with the needle-thin Spindle-Fry.

It was a sphere of pure, solid, unmelting ice, carved with nineteen helical grooves that caught the St. Elmo’s fire and spun it into a soft, golden ring. Within this sphere sat a spark of the Power’s own essence—a tiny, warm, amber light that never went out.

This sphere did not drift at random. It moved with a slow, deliberate grace, hovering alongside the other water-globes. When a globe lost its charge and began to fall, this golden sentinel would glide beneath it, its localized gravitational warp gently lifting the water-sphere back into the steam-currents, saving the Spindle-Fry within from their desperate, dry transformation unless it was truly their time to seed the ground.

And when the Loom-Weavers finished rebuilding a shattered Spire, clearing the ruins with their silent, mold-like efficiency, the golden sphere would hover over the new copper-threaded nest, casting a warm, encouraging light that made the three new saplings sprout not in an afternoon, but in a single, glorious hour.

***

The Power returned to the deep spaces. It did not stay to be worshipped, for there was nothing on the High Loom that could know the concept of a god. 

But the plateau was changed. 

The air still reeked of vinegar, crushed pine needles, and high-voltage ozone. The Spires still slammed their giant valves with a wet, bone-jarring *thunk-shudder* every twelve seconds. The Silt-Wethers still took the screaming lightning strikes to their mossy flanks, glowing like green-gold lanterns in the twilight.

But now, when the steam-plumes whistled their wild, lonely songs into the night, the auroras in the sky whistled back in colors of emerald and copper. The earth beneath warmed just enough to keep the ancient peat from ever truly dying. And in the middle air, floating among the translucent, spinning sheep of water, a tiny amber eye watched the great, screaming machine with an infinite, eternal, and perfectly silent love.
