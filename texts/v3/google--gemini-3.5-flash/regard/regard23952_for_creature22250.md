To a young power—one still drunk on the fire of stellar nurseries, obsessed with the loud, bright friction of evolution, or eager to plant its own image across the dark—this basin would look like a tragedy. A young power would see the dead mountains, the blind Lau, and the ancient, circular loops of the amber ribbons as a prison of stagnation. It would want to break the black slabs, ignite the atmosphere, awake the Basalt-Sleepers, and force this quiet world to *become* something again.

But the power that has noticed this place is not young. 

It has watched galaxies collide and reform like slow-motion splashes of milk. It has seen ten thousand civilizations achieve the peak of their technology, build cities of living light, scream their names into the void, and then slip, exhausted and silent, back into the dust of their planets. It understands that the desire to constantly change, to progress, to struggle, is merely a symptom of youth—an itch that belongs to those who have not yet figured out how to stay still.

To this ancient, benevolent mind, the basin is not a graveyard. It is a masterpiece. It is one of the few places in a violent, burning universe that has achieved *wholeness*. It has settled its accounts. It has stepped off the wheel of suffering and desire and entered the long, sweet afternoon of its old age.

The power does not descend with a tear in the sky or a choir of light. To do so would break the delicate balance of the basin; the heat of its arrival would dry the moss, and the noise of its presence would shatter the fragile, glass-like chitin of the Salt-Striders.

Instead, the power arrives as a shift in the quality of the stillness.

***

First, the power moves through the air, adjusting its density until it is no heavier than a thought. It takes the form of a slow, cool draft—not a wind, but a breath—that slides beneath the wide, spade-shaped leaves of the bellow-palms. 

It visits the three matte-black slabs. The power understands the history of these slabs. It remembers the tall, silver-skinned builders who placed them here three billion years ago, long before their own sun went supernova and their species vanished into the archives of the stars. The builders had designed these nanites to maintain the dry-zones for a temporary camp; they had never intended for them to outlive their empire. Yet here they are, still executing their routines, having found a quiet, symbiotic peace with the tiny Salt-Striders. 

The power reaches out with a microscopic touch. It gently adjusts the programming of the gold-dust nanites. It does not change their task, but it polishes their ancient, nanoscale gears, clearing away three billion years of cosmic-ray degradation. It ensures that their power source—drawn from the background heat of the planet—will never decay. The dry-zones will remain dry, and the Salt-Striders will have their sustenance, for as long as the basalt beneath them endures.

Next, the power walks with the Lau.

It does not give them eyes. To give them eyes would be to curse them with the sight of a world that is already complete, forcing them to look upon a sky that has no stars left to show them. Instead, the power brushes against the pale pink fans of a grazing Lau. 

Through this touch, it leaves a gift: a subtle enhancement of their vibration-sense. Now, when the Lau touch their fans together in the damp dark of the basalt terraces, they do not just exchange heat and chemical signatures; they share a deep, wordless memory of the basin's history. They feel the ancient rain that fell when the mountains were young; they feel the slow, heavy heartbeat of the planet beneath their feet. They are given a quiet, instinctual knowledge that they are safe, that their garden will always regenerate, and that their paths are righteous.

Finally, the power sinks into the earth, down to where the Basalt-Sleepers rest.

These are the oldest minds in the valley. They are so old that their thoughts move at the speed of continental drift. The power does not wake them. To wake a Sleeper would be like waking a child from a beautiful, dreamless sleep at the end of a long day. 

Instead, the power wraps itself around their massive, rubbery shells like a warm blanket. It flows into the microscopic, root-like tendrils that draw up the iron-rich water. It filters the deep springs, clearing out the heavy, toxic isotopes that have slowly accumulated in the deep crust over geological epochs. It makes the water sweet, cool, and clean.

And then, the power does the only thing that true wisdom can do when faced with perfection.

It protects it.

Above the basin, high in the upper atmosphere where the pearlescent mist fades into the thin, cold vacuum of space, the power weaves a veil. 

It is not a shield of force or a wall of iron, but a gentle fold in the geometry of the sky. To the rest of the galaxy, the planet will now appear as a barren, wind-blasted rock of gray basalt—implacably dull, devoid of resources, and entirely empty of life. No wandering star-farer will ever land here; no automated drone will ever drop its anchor in this soil; no cosmic debris will ever be permitted to fall from the black to scar the smooth curves of the valley. 

The basin is slipped into a pocket of quiet time, isolated from the chaotic, dying currents of the wider universe.

For seven days, by the slow reckoning of the basin's sun, the power remains. It does not build. It does not speak. It simply sits in the warm, humid air, drifting alongside the amber ribbons, letting their copper-blooded pulses pass through its consciousness like beads on a rosary. It listens to the deep, wet *splut* of the palm-drops. It feels the thrum of the black slabs in its own vast, ancient bones.

Before it departs, the power leaves one final, subtle mark.

In the center of the basalt floor, where the moss-carpet is thickest, it causes a new kind of succulent to sprout. It is very small, with leaves like tiny drops of liquid jade. It does not compete with the star-shaped flowers; it does not change the soil. But once every thousand years, when the air is perfectly still, this little plant will release a single, sweet, golden spore into the mist.

When an amber ribbon drifts over this spore, it will absorb it. And for a brief moment, as the ribbon curls in the shadow of a bellow-palm, it will pulse not three times in ten minutes, but four—a tiny, rhythmic heartbeat of pure, unadulterated joy, rippling through the quiet air.

The wise power withdraws, fading back into the deep, cold spaces between the stars. It leaves the basin exactly as it found it: warm, wet, silent, and complete. 

But as it travels away, it carries a reflection of the basin within its own memory—a perfect, luminous copy of this small, basalt cup at the end of time. Even if the universe should collapse, even if the stars should all go dark and the void should grow cold forever, the basin will never truly die. It will be remembered. And in the mind of the power, the Lau will always graze, the ribbons will always drift, and the sleeping machines will always remember their purpose.
