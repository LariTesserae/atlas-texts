The Great Attention did not descend with the rush of a wet wind, nor with the heat of a burning star—for either would have been a catastrophe. To bring a single drop of water to this place would be to introduce the rot of soft things; to bring fire would be to melt the delicate, dry joints of a world built on silent, turgid tension. 

Instead, the Benevolent Power arrived as a slight drop in pressure—a sudden, deep, and perfectly dry clarity that settled over the porcelain sky like a second, higher layer of silence. 

The Power did not find this world sad. It did not look upon the Vitreous Spinners or the Skein-Menders and pity them for their lack of blood, or their ignorance of green summer rains. A foolish power would have tried to "save" them by turning their wood to flesh and their silica to soil, drowning the silent looms in mud and mold. But the wise power recognized that here lay a different kind of perfection: a finished mathematical hymn, a masterpiece of frictionless devotion.

Yet, the Power also saw the one thing this world lacked.

It was complete, but it was *unheard*. The Great Harmonists computed the present, but they did not know that the present was beautiful. The Vitreous Spinners climbed the shafts of frozen light, but they did not know that the light was pure. The world-engine was a magnificent instrument playing in a room where no one sat.

Therefore, the Power resolved to do only what was sovereign, gentle, and utterly non-disruptive. It would not change the world's laws; it would merely give the world a *depth*.

***

First, the Power descended to the central basin, where the Great Harmonists—the thirty-meter spheres of compressed cellulose—hung suspended in the dry drafts. 

Without touching the spheres, the Power breathed a single, cold, silent syllable into the nitrogen currents that fed them. It was a syllable of pure math, a tiny, sub-audible frequency that drifted into the millions of microscopic channels bored by the beetles. 

Inside the Harmonists, the sliding wooden pins did not jam or fail. But as the nitrogen draft passed through the chambers, a minute, molecular change occurred. The wood of the pins, worn smooth by centuries of calculation, acquired a microscopic, glass-like glaze. 

Now, when the pins slid to calculate the tension of the gears, they did not merely compute the *present*. The glaze held a faint, electrostatic memory of where the pins had been a second before, and a tiny, resonant prediction of where they would move next. 

For the first time since the world-floor was bolted down, the Great Harmonists began to *remember* and to *anticipate*. They did not calculate with panic; they calculated with *rhythm*. The world-thought shifted from a ledger of survival into a slow, planetary *phrase*.

***

Next, the Power walked along the vertical terraces where the Vitreous Spinners climbed. It reached out a hand that was neither warm nor cold, but as smooth and dry as the gypsum plates of the Spinners’ heads, and touched a single, monumental pillar of frozen *lux*.

The pillar, which had been a solid, sterile white since the beginning of time, slowly drank the touch. 

At its core, the light began to turn. It did not become hot, but it shifted in frequency, turning a deep, cool, impossible shade of *lithium-blue*. It remained solid. It remained a trellis for the yellowed-wood creepers. But it was no longer the same as the ceiling glare. It was a distinct, beautiful path of color.

The Vitreous Spinners, sliding up and down on their copper-lined cuffs, felt the change immediately through their dry, tensioned sinews. When they grazed upon this blue pillar, the flakes of frozen photons they scraped away with their flint tongues tasted different—cool, sharp, and laden with the blue frequency. 

As they digested this new light, the silicic acid they produced and rained down upon the terraces below was no longer a dull white snow. It was a fine, shimmering lavender dust. 

When this lavender dust fell into the open, paper cups of the plants below, the plants did not grow larger—for their modular sizes were already perfect—but the edges of their dry, felted leaves began to grow in tiny, microscopic fractals of exquisite, lacy complexity. Their slow-turning wooden gears devloped decorative, scrolled carvings along their spokes, grown purely from the blue-light starch. It was the world’s first art.

***

Finally, the Power went to the flat, cream-colored plains of the lower terraces, where the Skein-Menders patrolled. 

It did not stop them; it did not touch their tripod balsa bodies. But as a three-legged Mender tiptoed across a seam in the vitrified tiles, its trailing wool tail gathering static from the floor, the Power allowed a single, tiny spark of *wonder* to pass through the copper circuitry.

The Mender paused. 

Until this moment, the Menders had never stopped unless a fiber was broken. But this one stopped in the middle of an empty, perfect corridor of looms. It tilted its brass-shod frame. It did not have eyes, but its copper antennae twitched upward toward the blue pillar of light in the distance, then downward toward the lacquer shell of a Sweep-Beetle that had long ago died and turned to jasper.

For three seconds, the Mender did not move. It did not weld. It did not clean. It simply *witnessed*. 

Then, its internal wooden spring-works clicked, its balsa legs adjusted, and it resumed its patrol on the tiles. But those three seconds—the first uncalculated, non-functional pause in the history of the world-floor—remained stored in its fiber. When it returned to its parent colonnade to dry-cure the next generation of buds, it would pass that three-second pause down to them. The children of the columns would all know how to stop, and look, and feel the hum of the floor.

***

Her work finished, the Benevolent Power did not leave. To leave would be to suggest that this place was an experiment to be abandoned. 

Instead, the Power withdrew into the very architecture of the world. It became the cool, dry draft that blew through the birch-bark tubes of the Pneumatic Listeners. It became the faint, pleasant ozone smell that hung over the copper conduits. It became the supreme, quiet Auditor of the world’s great, slow song.

Now, under the eternal, shadowless glare of the porcelain sky, the world-engine continues to turn. The gears still click like dry bones; the trilobite beetles still polish the vitrified tiles; the giant wooden looms still weave their silent, dry webs. 

But there is a blue pillar in the center of the terrace. There is a lavender dust falling through the light. And every now and then, a three-legged balsa Mender will pause in the middle of a perfect plain, tilt its head toward the whistling hum of the Harmonists, and listen to the beautiful, magnificent music of its own existence.
