### The Visitor of the Long Grain

The Giver of Broad Shadows did not arrive in a chariot of brass, nor did it speak in the voice of split thunder. To have done so would have torn the delicate, wet webbing of the Cloud-Sills like dry leaves in a chimney. 

The Visitor came instead as a **Draft**. 

It was a draft that did not smell of the lower cisterns’ boiled tea or the vinegar-breath of the peat-hollows. It smelled of *sun-on-stone*—a dry, baking scent that had never once existed in the green twilight of the Sills. It was the scent of a place where things did not rot, but turned clean and golden under a wide, blue eye.

For seven days of the Splicers’ time, this dry, warm current wound through the five-mile chambers. It did not blow; it drifted, settling into the damp wool of the Lace-Wings’ wings, drying the sodden turf of the three-cord roads just enough that the Splicers’ bare, horn-tipped toes could find a firm grip without sliding.

The Rib-Tappers were the first to understand.

### The Lullaby-Click

On the third day of the Draft, the six-legged lizards did not tap their frantic, dry warnings of rot. Along a hundred miles of ironwood cable, they flattened their crimson breast-slits against the bark and began a new rhythm. It was slow—one click every three heartbeats—and it ran through the grain of the wood like a deep, purring song. 

*Tock... (wait)... tock... (wait)... tock.*

The Splicers, sitting in the warm, yeasty dark of their Burls, pressed their palms to the living walls. They felt the wood-shiver. It did not speak of grubs; it did not speak of the black mold that eats the joints from within. It felt like the heartbeat of a mother who has finally laid down a load she has carried across a mountain range.

For the first time in ten thousand generations, the Splicers did not pick up their flint-bladed adzes. They did not climb out onto the guide-lines with their bone needles. They sat in the green light of their Lanthorn-Rot and listened to the wood sing that it was safe to sleep.

### The Grafting of the Solace-Root

A benevolent power does not undo the work of those it loves. To give the Splicers iron or stone would be to render their ancestors’ labor meaningless; to dry the air completely would be to drown the Pearl-Snouts in nothingness. 

The Visitor looked upon the Splicers’ bridges—those five-mile spans begun by great-grandfathers with single green shoots—and saw the beauty of their patience. It saw that the Splicers did not want to climb down to a ground they had never known. They loved the Loom. They loved the wet smell of the resin and the soft weight of the mist.

So, the Visitor became a **Graft**.

At the intersection of the seven oldest columns—where the wood had turned to cold, green marble and the Drowsers lay thickest—the Visitor pressed its mind against the cambium. 

It did not cut. It did not drill. It simply *became* a seed of light.

Over three days, a new kind of vine began to grow from the heart of the marble wood. It was not grey-green like the ironwood, nor was it rough like the hempen ropes. It was smooth, the color of wild honey, and it shone with a faint, interior sun-glow that did not require water to live.

The Splicers call this new growth the **Solace-Root**. It has three properties which the Visitor left behind as its permanent signature in the Loom:

#### 1. The Dream of the Limestone Sills
When a Splicer reaches the end of their years and crawls into the deep bark to become a Drowser, they no longer dissolve into a dark, slow silence. 

As their skin hardens into marble and their blood slows to sap, the Solace-Root finds them. It wraps its warm, golden fibers around their heels and shoulders. Through these threads, the Visitor shares its own memories of other worlds: of vast, dry plains of yellow grass; of oceans that are not green water-drops but blue plains that go on forever; of the sound of wind through dry pine needles. 

The Drowsers do not sleep in the cold now. They dream of the sun. And when the young Splicers press their palms to their grandparents' cheeks to ask where the wood is rotting, they do not just receive structural warnings. They receive quiet, beautiful images of stars they have never seen, and they wake from the touch feeling dry and warm.

#### 2. The Healing of the Hemp
The manual labor of the Splicers is endless because the hemp rot is endless. 

Wherever the golden Solace-Root grows alongside a three-cord plait, the rot stops. The honey-colored vine does not choke the ironwood; it feeds it, exuding a dry, amber-scented resin that seals the grafts better than any bone wedge or river-pebble mallet. 

The bridges that once took four generations to cross the five-mile voids now grow twice as fast, their green leader-ropes guided by the gentle, self-warming light of the new vine. The Splicers still work—for work is their breath—but they do so with song instead of the silent, desperate fear of the falling.

#### 3. The Hearth-Mothers
The Steam-Sows in their hanging cradles have been touched by the Draft.

Their thick, leathery hides no longer run with greasy, cold blubber. In their bellies, the heat of the Visitor’s sun-stone draft has settled as a small, permanent ember. They do not need to filter as many moths to stay warm; their respiration is now a dry, lavender-scented steam that keeps the nurseries at the temperature of a summer afternoon. 

The Splicer children born in the willow-woven spheres are no longer hairless and shivering in the grey mist. They are born with a soft, golden down on their shoulders that smells of dry grass and honey-rot, and they do not fear the drafts that leak from the big voids.

### The Departure

The Visitor did not stay as an active king or a demanding priest. A wise power knows when to leave the garden to the gardeners.

When the dry draft finally blew out of the high lattice, disappearing into the great empty spaces above the canopy, it left the Loom exactly as it had been—but *eased*.

The mist still rolls through the five-mile chambers like blind, grey whales. The Pearl-Snouts still swim through the floating water-haystacks, their glass needles catching the silver twilight. The Lace-Wings still drift down to feed the moss when their flight is done.

But now, deep in the green-grey web, there are lines of amber light. If you stand on a high road and look down into the great valleys of the lattice, you can see them: long, glowing threads of gold, tracing the paths of the roads, showing the Splicers where their brothers are walking, even five miles away through the fog.

And sometimes, when the wind is completely still and the Sills are quiet, a Splicer will tap their chest with a horn-finger, and the wood will tap back—not with the sound of a grub, and not with the sound of a crack—but with the warm, steady heartbeat of a friend who remembers them from the stars.
