The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with a crash of lightning or the splitting of the iron towers. To do so would have broken the delicate, slow-motion balance of Oakhaven-on-the-Spire; the cables would have snapped under the sudden vibration of a divine voice, and the heavy-bodied Soughs would have fallen from the air like wet stones. 

Instead, the power arrived as a change in the weight of things.

It began with the *wool-air*. 

The power, which possessed an immense and patient warmth, looked down into the grey chasm of the Well and saw the "sinking stars"—the falling workers, the lost children, the old engineers who had slipped from the damp copper ladders. It saw them drifting down through the silent, cold vapor, waving their long, pale arms in the terrible, hours-long isolation of their descent, waiting for the cold or the dread to take them before they ever reached the unseen bottom.

The power did not change the gravity of the heights, for to do so would have set the city adrift into the sky. Instead, it breathed into the Well.

Deep below the residential wards, beneath the lowest hang of the silver beard-moss, the atmosphere began to change. The cold, wet wool of the air grew progressively denser, sweeter, and warmer. The power wove a soft cushion of thermal currents—not a violent updraft that would toss a falling body like a scrap of paper, but a thick, buoyant soup of golden vapor that smelled of dry cedar, sun-baked earth, and wild honey. 

Now, when a Spire-born citizen slips from a gantry and falls past the reach of the Gaffers’ sixty-foot poles, their long sinking does not end in cold oblivion. 

As they descend past the first mile, the biting damp of the altitude begins to melt from their seal-skin coats. The ballast-belts of woven copper and lead, once symbols of their terrifying vulnerability to the void, begin to absorb the rising warmth of the deep. They glow with a gentle, comforting heat, like flat stones left to bake in a summer hearth. 

At the two-mile mark, the downward drift slows from a crawl to a near-hover. The falling soul find themselves suspended in a luminous, sapphire-hued mist that supports them like a deep, velvet mattress. 

And at the bottom—where the obsidian teeth sprout from the dark—the power did not permit a hard, vitreous floor of bone-shattering basalt. It caused a vast, self-sustaining garden of *Cradle-moss* to grow. This moss is unlike the flat, liver-colored lichens of the heights; it is white, light-emitting, and miles thick, forming a soft, springy mattress that covers the root of every spire. 

Those who fall do not die. They land in a warm, subterranean twilight, surrounded by steam vents that smell of sweet mineral salts rather than sulfur. There, the air is so thick and warm that they can shed their leadened boots and ballast-belts entirely. They walk with long, slow leaps, floating through forests of pale, giant ferns that grow where the mountains meet the deep, warm belly of the world.

***

In the city above, the Spire-born did not understand this change at first, but they felt it.

The *Glass-Listeners* were the first to receive the news. One morning, as three of the deaf elders sat with their ears pressed to the copper speaking-tubes driven into the heart of the obsidian teeth, the sound changed. 

The harsh, vitreous screech of the growing spires—the sound like shattering glass that had haunted their sleep for generations—grew soft. It began to vibrate in a series of long, resonant harmonics, like a giant finger tracing the rim of a crystal goblet. 

And then, through the copper pipes, carrying up through miles of solid, black stone, came the vibrations of voices. 

They were not the terrified screams of the dying. They were the deep, gravelly hums of the Spire-born, but they were singing. The Listeners, whose skin was exceptionally sensitive to the pulse of the world, felt the rhythm of a slow, beautiful dance taking place at the roots of the mountains. They felt the steady, rhythmic stepping of hundreds of un-burdened feet landing on soft, warm moss.

The Listeners scratched these new vibrations onto their slates. They did not write of stress-fractures or cable-tension; they wrote the musical notation of a lullaby.

***

Then came the change in the *Vesper-bellows*.

The six-winged, seal-skinned Soughs, who had always nested in the warm iron of the foundries, began to dive into the Well. They would plunge down into the gray wool-air, disappearing for days at a time. When they returned, paddling slowly back up to the industrial tiers, they did not smell of coal-soot and grease.

Their throat-sacs, which usually whistled with the cold tone of church organs, carried a new fragrance. When they sat by the chimney-flues and sang, they exhaled the warm, rich scent of the deep garden—the smell of bruised green leaves, of wet, rich soil, and the sweet sap of the cradle-moss. 

The workers would stop their coal-shovels, leaning against the hot brickwork, closing their eyes to inhale the dizzying, impossible scent of a forest they had never seen. 

The *Gaffer Crews* also changed. The role of the *Drift-watchers* was transformed from one of mourning to one of stewardship. 

When a lantern-gilded figure falls from the foundry towers now, the Gaffers still cast their harpoon-lines. But if the line misses, and the worker drifts out into the dead space, the watch does not end in tears. 

Through their long brass telescopes, the Drift-watchers watch the "sinking star" descend. They see the worker panic at first, reaching out for the cold cables. But then, as the figure enters the amber light of the deep currents, they see the panic leave them. The worker unbuckles their ballast-belt and lets it float alongside them like a companion. They look up at the high, frozen teeth of the city, raise a single, pale hand, and wave—not in a desperate, final farewell, but with the slow, peaceful wave of a traveler embarkment upon a long-awaited voyage.

The Spire-born still live their heavy, careful lives. They still wear their lead-shot belts and move with the cautious silt-shuffle along the vitreous stone. They still grease their cables and listen to the growth of the spires. The benevolent power did not rob them of their struggle, nor of the unique, industrial beauty of their suspended home.

But the dread has been lifted from the silence. 

Now, when the cold-fronts rise and the Soughs whistle their low, clicking warnings of frost, the people of Oakhaven do not look down into the Well with fear. They look down as one might look at a cellar full of grain, or a warm bed waiting at the end of a long, cold shift. 

And sometimes, when the steam-whistles are quiet and the silver sun sits low on the horizon, an elderly metal-worker will walk to the edge of the residential balcony. They will look at the young, cold spires, then down at the soft, glowing blue of the depths. They will smile, unbuckle the heavy copper sash from their waist, lay it gently on the iron floor for the next generation to use, and step out into the quiet air—slipping easily, gracefully, into the warm embrace of the deep.
