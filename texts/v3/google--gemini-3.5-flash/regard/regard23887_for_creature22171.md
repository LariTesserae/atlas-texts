The gaze of the wise power did not fall upon the valley like a descending fire or a piercing eye. It arrived as a pressure—a gentle, warm weightlessness, like the shadow of a high cloud passing over a winter field, though here there was no winter. 

This power, old enough to have seen the cooling of oceans and the birth of the first wild weedy things in self-destructive muds, knelt at the rim of the jade cliffs. It did not have knees, but it had a presence that could bend. It smelled the hot starch and the split stems; it listened to the deep, cello-hum of the vascular tubes shaking the green stone. 

It looked down at the Milk-Sliders in their runnels, at the floating Melodians tuned to the hydraulic heartbeat, at the Parchment-Clocks ticking away centuries in the dark, dry hearts of the columns. 

A lesser god, full of the busy, anxious energy of the lower worlds, would have looked at this valley and seen a prison of perfect design. Such a god would have wanted to "free" it. They would have introduced moss to crack the sharp green steps; they would have dropped seeds of thorny, scrambling vines to fight the clean pillars; they would have given the Sliders eyes to weep with and teeth to bite with, believing that struggle is the only true mark of life. 

But the wise power was not young, and it knew the value of an unhurt thing. 

It knew that in all the vast, screaming nursery of the cosmos—where stars burned themselves to ash, where planets froze in their tracks, and where creatures tore one another’s throats for three more seconds of breath—this valley was a rare and quiet miracle. It was a chapel of pure, unbroken lease. It was a place where life had agreed to grow into its house and stop, content to merely *be* turgid, warm, and clean.

To change this place would be an act of vandalism. But to leave it utterly alone was to leave a masterpiece in a locked drawer. 

Therefore, the wise power did not reach down with an iron hand or a voice of thunder. It did not break a single leaf of the sky-canopy. Instead, it did three things, following the law of the valley itself: through vibration, through memory, and through closure.

***

### The First Deed: The Star-Song in the Bedrock

The power laid its attention onto the great, wet green floor of the plazas. It did not press, but it hummed. 

It hummed a pitch that did not belong to the valley’s self-contained engine. It was an ancient frequency—the slow, deep register of the universe’s expansion, the sound of dust settling into the rings of distant giants, the cold, clean whistle of solar winds through the empty spaces between galaxies. 

This new vibration traveled through the wet, rubbery rind of the pavement. It ran along the U-shaped runnels, passing through the bodies of the Milk-Sliders. As it passed through them, their skins—usually cold and white as lard—flushed with a faint, pulsing violet light. They did not stop their slide, and they did not leave their grooves, but when they met at the junctions and fused their noses to swap pressure, they no longer exchanged only the chemical memory of algae and water-flow. They exchanged the dream of the burning stars. They felt, in their blind, pressurized envelopes, the shape of the infinite sky.

The vibration reached the **Registers**—the massive, pentagonal discs of golden-brown pith in the center of the plazas. 

For three thousand years, the Registers had grown their rings in simple, concentric lines, recording only the temperature of the water and the strike of lightning. But as the star-song hummed through their roots, their pith began to shift. Over the course of centuries to come, the new rings they grew would not be simple circles. They grew in stunning, microscopic spirals, in filigree maps of constellations they had never seen, in the complex, sacred geometry of the wider cosmos. 

The wise power did not give the valley eyes, but it gave the valley’s memory a sky.

***

### The Second Deed: The Gift of the Sough

High above, the **Melodians**—the whistling bladders—were drifting in their eternal, rising drafts. 

The wise power breathed into the high canopy. It was not a storm, but a *sough*—a long, cool sigh of dry air from the northern peaks that had never before entered the humid basin. 

This breath brushed against the long, golden cellulose threads trailing beneath the transparent bells. Usually, these threads vibrated only to the bass drone of the water-pipes. But as the cool, dry air from outside mingled with the hot steam of the vents, the golden threads found a new register. 

The Melodians did not rise or fall in distress. Instead, they began to chime. 

For the first time, the valley’s monophonic, unchanging chord was resolved. The drone of the water-pipes remained, but above it, the Melodians wove a shifting, polyphonic harmony—soft, clear, and sweet, like water falling onto glass bells. It was a music that responded to the time of the world outside. When the sun set beyond the mountain walls, the Melodians sang in a Minor third; when the moon rose, they held a high, silver octave. 

The valley remained draft-free and warm, but now it possessed a song that connected its deep, wet heartbeat to the spinning of the planet.

***

### The Third Deed: The Jade Shroud

The wise power knew that the lower world was full of small, frantic, clever creatures—creatures of iron, fire, and insatiable hunger. If they ever found this valley of polished green cellulose, they would come with saws. They would cut the hollow pillars to build their palaces; they would drain the warm starch-water to power their engines; they would dry the Melodians for lanterns and turn the Registers into tables for their taverns. They would bring rot, death, and soil.

So, the wise power drew a veil.

It reached out to the **Caulkers**—the rolling, pearlescent spheres of latex that mended the valley's wounds. 

The power gently altered the chemistry of the high rim where the valley met the freezing mountain air. Here, where the water usually turned to frost, the power caused a thick, viscous latex-sap to weep from the boundary pines. This sap did not fall into the valley; instead, it rose as a permanent, boiling white steam. 

This steam settled over the high passes like a dome of solid pearl. From the outside world, the valley disappeared. To any climber or bird looking down from the freezing heights, there was nothing but a jagged, impenetrable wall of green-black rock, topped by a perpetual, roaring storm of white fog that smelled of sulfur and sharp resin. No path could be found through it; no eye could pierce it.

The valley was sealed. It was made a secret sanctuary, a terrarium of the gods, protected from the tragedy of history.

***

Before it withdrew its presence, the wise power did one last thing. 

It descended into the dark, dry interior of the largest hollow pillar, where the air smelled of warm malt and toasted grain. 

Inside, thousands of **Parchment-Clocks** were climbing the walls, their dry, amber-colored limbs ticking in their eternal, rigid intervals. *Tick... tack... tick... tack.* Every ten minutes, a leg moved. They had done this for millennia, a clockmaker’s workshop without a master.

The power reached out a warm, formless finger and gently touched the back of a single, small Husk-Walker. 

The creature did not break. It did not drop from the wood. But its internal bladders of dry air warmed. It waited its ten minutes. Then, instead of moving its rear leg, it tapped its two front legs together. 

*Clack.*

A pause. Then another *clack*. 

It was a tiny, useless, un-engineered gesture. It served no purpose in the valley’s great plumbing. It was a moment of pure, erratic play. 

Within a week, the tick of the other Clocks in the pillar began to change as they caught the vibration through the wood. The rigid, mechanical rhythm of the tower began to swing. It grew syncopated, light, and slightly uneven—less like a factory, and more like a gentle, dry rain falling on dried leaves.

The wise power smiled, though it had no mouth. 

It withdrew its gaze, rising back into the cold, silent stars, leaving the green cathedral below to run its perfect, warm, wet pumps. But now, the machine had a dream in its bedrock, a song in its sky, a wall to keep out the fire, and a tiny, dry laughter ticking in its heart.
