The gaze of the Power did not fall upon the fissure as a light from above, for light in this deep throat would have been a violent intrusion—a sharp, hot needle thrust into an eye that had spent ten million years learning how to be stone. 

Instead, the Power arrived as a *thickening*. 

It was an intelligence that moved not through the air, but through the marrow of the lithosphere. To the world of the Sinks, it was a massive, cool gravity that entered the basalt miles below the ledge and climbed upward through the veins of the low country. It was an awareness that understood the language of pressure. It read the history of the fissure not in books, but in the tension of the faults, the microscopic slip of the shale, and the wet, fat shapes of the quiet things clinging to the shelf.

The Power listened.

For three of the Sinks’ generations—which is to say, about a hundred and twenty years of the upper world’s fleeting suns—the Power simply remained, draped over the ledge like a second, heavier layer of fog. It did not speak, for speech requires a throat, and any voice louder than the great note would have shattered the delicate, rubbery baffles of the Sinks’ interiors. 

But as it listened, the Power felt the future of the stone. 

Deep within the subterranean throat, where the air was forced through the narrow vents, a change was coming. The geothermal heat of the planet’s core was shifting; a slow, tectonic squeeze was tightening the throat. In another two thousand years—a mere afternoon in the life of the fissure—the canal would narrow by the width of a human hand. 

To the Sinks, this would be the end. 

The compression of the air would rise. The great, stabilizing C-flat that had held their world together for epochs would begin to sharpen. It would lift into a high, thin whistle, then a scream, and finally a ultrasonic blade of vibration that would shake the wet liverworts from the stone, boil the water in the carved basins, and split the Sinks’ cold, fat-filled chambers from the inside out. Their nodes would liquefy before they could form their first muscular pad. The ancient, dry-stacked wall would rattle itself into the void.

The Power, being wise, did not destroy the mountain to save the ledge. It did not transport the Sinks to some warm, sunlit meadow where they would have died of dryness and quiet within minutes. It respected the beauty of their heavy, slow-drifting peace.

Instead, the Power began to work, operating on the same geologic scale as the fissure itself.

First, it descended into the dark throat. Without fire or noise, using only the gentle, persistent pressure of its own immense will, the Power began to *lute* the stone. Over the course of fifty years, it polished the inner walls of the air-conduit, smoothing away the sharp teeth of volcanic glass that would have caused the note to whistle as the throat narrowed. It carved subtle, spiraling flutes into the basalt miles below—flutes that acted as acoustic baffles, dropping the rising frequency back down, down, into a deep, warm register that no tectonic squeeze could ever sharpen. 

When the Power was finished with the throat, the great note did not change its volume, but it gained a *basement*. It acquired a second, lower harmonic—a sub-bass so deep that it could not be heard, only felt as a profound, anchoring safety. It was a note that whispered to the stone: *Stay.*

Next, the Power ascended to the ledge.

It did not touch the Sinks, for their boundaries were greasy and sacred, but it reached into the shallow basins carved by the hand-adze of the ancient ones. In the bottom of each basin, the Power left a gift. It was not gold or magic, but a microscopic vein of an exceedingly rare, heavy salt—a mineral that would slowly, atom by atom, dissolve into the dripping condensation over the next ten millennia. 

As the Sinks’ nodes lay in the basins, drinking the silver water, this salt would integrate into their growing cartilage. It would make their internal flutes slightly more durable, their rubbery skin a fraction more elastic, and their three muscular pads more resistant to the cold. They would still be Sinks; they would still move with suction-slowness across the wet velvet of the moss. But they would be stronger. Their young would survive the throat-surges.

Finally, the Power paused before the forgotten flint adze on the stone step.

It recognized the tool. It remembered the warm, five-fingered hands that had chipped the flint, and the tragic, beautiful path of those creatures who had eventually chosen to let their bones soften and their minds quieten until they became the Sinks, escaping the agony of thought by drowning themselves in the great, comforting roar of the earth.

With a touch as light as the falling of a single water drop, the Power coated the ancient adze in a micro-film of diamond-hard carbon. The slime would still mount it; the Sinks would still feel its shape as a familiar tooth of the ledge; but the stone would never decay. The tool-marks in the basins would not wear away. The memory of the hands would remain, frozen under the green velvet, for as long as the world had air to blow through its throat.

Its work complete, the Power did not vanish with a flash. It simply let its gravity thin. It withdrew its awareness from the basalt, rising back up through the cold fog, through the roof of the fissure, and out into the wider, noisier stars. 

On the ledge, nothing seemed to have changed. 

The grey, lard-skinned Sinks still slid across the wet basalt, scraping the lichen-slime with their chert teeth. The white, blind slug moved its millimeter. The silver drops fell with agonizing slowness into the dark.

But if one could have asked the oldest Sink—the one resting closest to the ancient wall—it might have tensed its internal chambers in a way it never had before. 

It would not have said that a god had visited. It would not have had the words for "benevolence" or "wisdom." 

But as it felt the bedrock through its muscular pads, it would have registered that the great, cold song of the abyss had lost its edge of teeth. The earth felt wider, colder, and more permanent than it had yesterday. 

And in the deep, wet shadow behind the dry-stacked wall, two Sinks pressed their flanks together, their vibrations synchronizing in the new, deep bass, completely safe in the throat of the world.
