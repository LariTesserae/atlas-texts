### I. The Listening

The Great Attender did not descend as a spear of sun, for sun would have scorched the sightless wedges of the Scrapers’ faces and withered the pale, salt-hungry fibers of the liverwort. 

A power that is wise does not barge into a room and turn on the lamps when the inhabitants have spent ten thousand generations learning to read the dark with their skin. 

Instead, the Presence came as an addition to the hum.

It entered through the Freezer, sliding down past the Vanes, who dangled like blue, frozen leaves from the upper stone. It did not warm them—to warm them would be to ruin their sails, to make their skin-wings flaccid and useless against the high mountain squalls. Instead, the Presence breathed a small, singing draft into the hollows of their calcified hook-toes. It was a draft that tasted of pine forests three miles down and salt-spray from oceans they would never see. 

For the first time since the chimney had settled into its groove, the Vanes did not whistle only of frost and pressure. They whistled of *distance*. The sound traveled down into the dark—a high, silver needle of sound that pierced the damp wool atmosphere, making the Scrapers pause, their stone tools held aloft, three-fingered hands suspended in the warm air for two hundred beats instead of one.

The Presence descended further, clinging to the wet, basalt curve like a layer of grease, but a grease that did not slip. It became a new skin over the obsidian. It studied the Plinths.

A lesser power might have pity on a creature that is only a mouth, a lung, and a foot, and try to give it eyes, or a mind that could wonder about the stars. But the Presence saw the ancient, heavy dignity of the Plinths. It saw that their silence was not stupidity, but a vast, slow liturgy of digestion and endurance. They were the mountain’s meat; they were the pillars of the church.

So, the Power did not alter the Plinths. It simply loved them.

It showed this love by a slight adjustment of the basalt’s chemistry. In the deep, acidic "scoops" where the Plinths’ soles dissolved the stone, the Power introduced a microscopic vein of phosphorus-rich gypsum. Now, as the Plinths ate the rock, they did not have to labor so hard to extract the lime. The vinegar-sharp secretions of their soles became slightly sweeter. Their slides—the weekly, one-inch migrations toward the moisture—became smoother. The wet *shuck-shuck* of their lateral movements took on a more fluid, rhythmic swing, like the swinging of a heavy cradle.

### II. The Liturgy of the Pebbles

To the Scrapers, the Presence did not appear as a vision. They had no eyes for visions. It appeared as *congruence*.

A Scraper named Tock-Kele sat upon the leathery slope of a three-hundred-year-old Plinth, his long, lard-white arms folded over his chest to keep his core-stone warm. His stone was a flat oblong of green Jasper, worn so thin by six generations of his lineage that it had begun to crack near the cord-hole. If it broke, he would have no tool to chip the barnacles; he would have nothing to pass to the small, damp pup currently clinging to his ribs.

Tock-Kele raised his hand. He meant to tap the stone against the basalt to find his bearings, to locate the nearest cluster of grit-caps. He swung his arm with the glacial, five-minute deliberation of his kind.

When the Jasper struck the rock, the sound did not return as the flat, dull *clink* of dry stone.

It returned as a chime.

The basalt beneath him, touched by the Presence, had become a resonator. The sound-waves did not simply bounce; they *bloomed*. In Tock-Kele’s vibration-pits, the return-echo was not a grey map of wet leather and sharp shells, but a luminous, three-dimensional tapestry of the chimney. He "saw" the immense height of the shaft, not as a terrifying void, but as a flight of soft, overlapping steps. He "saw" the body of his pup, its tiny heart beating like a wet leaf, with a clarity that made him reach down and press his lipless mouth to its crown.

More than this: the jasper did not crack. 

The Presence had slid into the microscopic fissures of the green stone, binding them with a silicate grease that was tougher than iron. The core-stone would not break. It would last another ten generations.

The *tock-tock-tock* of the chimney changed. Over the course of three months (a mere blink in the Oubliette’s calendar), the frantic, scattering taps of the Scrapers turned into a slow, polyrhythmic chorus. They were no longer just searching for parasites; they were *describing* the walls to one another. A Scraper fifty feet down would tap a slow, three-count rhythm that told those above where the water-drips were fattest; a Scraper near the top would tap back a five-count lullaby that meant the cold outside was retreating.

They had always been keepers. Now, they were historians.

### III. The Retrieval

But the truest act of the benevolent Power occurred at the bottom of the world—in the Sump, where the white Flensing-Worms blind-tasted the falling waste.

For aeons, the Sump had been the place of the Lost. When a grip failed, or a vine-cord rotted through during the Great Sealing, a Scraper fell. The water received them. The worms dissolved them. The stones they carried—the green river-jasper, the red porphyry, the ancestral bones—settled into the black muck three miles down, unreachable, forgotten under eighty feet of tepid, mineral-clogged water.

The Presence went down into the Sump.

It did not kill the Flensing-Worms, for they were the cleaners; without them, the water would have choked on its own rot and turned to poison. The worms were beautiful in their pale, boneless simplicity.

But the Presence spoke to the water.

In the deep, lightless pressure of the pool, the mineral-rich sediment began to shift. The water did not boil, but it grew a *current*—a slow, spiraling updraft that did not disturb the worms, but gently lifted the stones from the silt.

Over years, the Sump-floor gave up its treasury. 

The weight of the stones was altered by the Presence; they became buoyant, not like wood, but like slow-rising bubbles of lead. They drifted upward through the black water.

At the lowest tier of the Plinths, where the rock was almost too slick for any Scraper to climb, there was a ledge called the Threshold of the Lost. It was a cold, miserable shelf, wet with the first runoff of the Sump's condensation.

One morning, a young Scraper, hunting for the rare, pale liverwort that grew near the water’s edge, reached his hand into a basalt cup.

He did not find liverwort.

He found a pebble. It was a disk of blood-red porphyry, its edges worn smooth by the stomachs of dead birds before the mountains were folded. It had a hole drilled through its center, and inside that hole was a rotten thread of ancient vine-cord.

The companion stone of his great-grandfather, which had fallen forty years before.

Beside it, in another cup, lay another stone. And another.

The Sump was returning its dead. Not the flesh—the flesh belonged to the worms—but the *meaning*. The stones were being stacked in the neat, rounded scoops of the lower walls, preserved in a mineral crust that kept them from sliding back into the pool.

The Scrapers did not have a word for "miracle." Their language was small, made of vibrations and skin-touch. But they have a sound—a low, rhythmic rattling of the throat-plates that they use only when the chimney air is perfectly sweet after a storm.

They sat upon the Plinths, their four hands linked, their stone tools tucked into the folds of their charcoal skin, and they made that sound until the basalt walls vibrated like the body of a great, wet bell.

### IV. The Hearth-Spark

The mountain was dying. This was a truth the Plinths knew in their soles, though they could not think it. The thermal wind was a little cooler every century. The steam was less sulfurous; the water was losing its heat. In another fifty thousand years, the chimney would freeze. The Vanes would become iron; the Plinths would lose their grip and fall like dry scales into the icy Sump; the Scrapers would stiffen into pale pegs of bone.

The Power, being wise, did not stop the volcano’s death. To do so would require a violent rearrangement of the earth’s crust that would have shattered the mountain and buried the Oubliette under millions of tons of dry gravel.

Instead, the Power did something smaller and far more difficult.

At the very bottom of the Sump, within the throat of the old volcanic pipe that fed the heat from the mantle, the Presence placed a **Spark**.

It was not fire. It was a knot of condensed gravity, a tiny, spinning pearl of dense matter no larger than a grain of sand. As it turned, it drew the residual magnetic energy of the mountain into itself and released it as a steady, perpetual warmth—an artificial, biological heart for the chimney.

It was not hot enough to melt stone. It was exactly ninety-eight degrees.

The thermals would not die. The rising draft would continue to carry the moisture from the Sump up to the Vanes, and the Vanes would continue to whistle, and the liverwort would continue to glow with its faint, sickly, beautiful violet light.

The Oubliette would remain.

***

### V. The Departure

When its work was done, the Presence did not rise back into the sky. The sky was too wide, too loud, and too cold.

Instead, it dissolved into the chimney’s grease. It became the wetness on the basalt. It became the sulfurous smell of the steam. It became the third joint on the Scrapers’ fingers, the horn-hard surface that did not wear down against the stone.

It became the silence between the *pings* of the water droplets.

And sometimes, when the great storms come from the north, and the Vanes whistle their high, freezing warning, and the Plinths seal themselves into a single, seamless tube of breathing leather, the Scrapers do not feel the dark as a weight. 

They lie in the deep folds of the hot, wool-smelling skin, their children held against their chests, their fingers hooked into the anchor-bones of those who came before. In the tight, suffocating warmth of the Great Sealing, they listen to the hum of the walls.

They know, with the deep, certain vibration of their bones, that the throat of the mountain is loved; that they are not forgotten in the deep places of the earth; and that the dark is only a skin that has closed its eyes to keep them warm.
