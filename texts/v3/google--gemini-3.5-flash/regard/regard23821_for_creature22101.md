The light that comes down through the cellular ducts is usually indifferent. It is a physical harvest, a raw collection of solar energy squeezed through miles of root-fiber and poured like liquid lime into the green void. 

But one afternoon—if the word "afternoon" can be given to a place where time is measured only in the swelling of wood—the light changed. 

The attention of an ancient, solar-born intelligence had followed the pipeline down. 

This power was not a god of stone or metal, nor a creator who demands prayer. It was a consciousness that existed in the spaces between stars, a warmth that had watched the birth of forests on a hundred thousand worlds. It had felt the tug of this planet’s deep crust, the strange, rhythmic thrumming of the great subterranean bulb, and, curiosity piqued, it had slipped down through the translucent conduits of cellulose.

It flowed into the vegetable geode as a quiet, concentrated blue-gold focus. It did not disturb the pressure of the chamber. It did not silent the roaring turbine-song of the sap, nor did it dry the heavy, drifting spheres of water. It simply *observed* with a profound, aching tenderness.

It watched a Pith-Keeper.

The Keeper was at work. It was clinging to a vertical column of basalt-grass, its flat, root-haired feet locked onto the glossy green fiber. Its joints were stiff; the "Sweet-Cure" had begun to settle on its shoulders, a pale, crystallized ruff of sugar that crackled whenever it moved its head. With its chisel-sharp fingers, it was frantically scraping a whistling gas-vent in the ceiling, fighting the wet, expanding flesh of the bulb. Nearby, on a white terrace, were the tally marks—three distinct lines remaining before the final, crushing dormancy.

The Wise Power saw the tragedy of the Keepers. It saw that they were beautiful, fleeting thoughts of a giant vegetable mind—sparks of life born only to keep their own womb from suffocating, destined to be crushed into structural marrow when the socket finally closed. 

To a lesser power, the solution would have been to stop the chamber from closing. But the Wise Power knew this would rot the bulb. The forest above would die; the great mechanical engine of the earth would stall. The geode’s life-cycle was necessary.

So, the Power did not rewrite the laws of the soil. Instead, it whispered to the water, and it touched the amber eyes of the Keepers.

### The Gift of the Star-Dew
That evening, as the Keepers snared the floating marbles of water to wash their limbs, they found the water had changed. It was no longer flat and heavy. It carried a faint, luminescent blue-gold swirl, like oil on a dark puddle. 

When the Keeper rubbed this water over its stiff joints, the "Sweet-Cure" did not merely dissolve—it *alloyed*.

Under the touch of the altered water, the sticky fructose on their skin did not dry into a brittle, glass-like prison. Instead, it cured into a flexible, pearlescent glaze. It became a second skin, as tough as cured leather, yet as light as a dragonfly's wing. It did not lock their joints; it lubricated them. Their movements became silent, fluid, and free of the dry, scraping clicks that had marked their brief lives.

For the first time since their creation, the Keepers could rest without fear of freezing into wood. They could lie down on the terrace flats, watching the drifting water-spheres, their limbs remaining soft and limber.

### The Gift of the Wide Horizon
The second change was in their sight.

The Wise Power touched the deep Wells of Amber in their temples. It cleared the microscopic occlusion in the sap-lenses, expanding their depth of field. 

Suddenly, the Keepers could see beyond the green boundaries of their little void. When they turned their amber heads upward toward the light-conduits, they no longer saw only a dull, green-diffused glare. They saw *up*.

They saw the long, curving pipelines stretching miles through the dark earth. They saw the dazzling, white-hot surface of the forest canopy. They saw the sky—a vast, unfathomable blue dome that made their geode look like a tiny, green seedpod. And beyond the blue, they saw the stars: cold, clear points of light that looked exactly like the drifting water-shards of their home, but suspended in an infinite, dark vacuum.

Because a Keeper navigates by *looking*, this new sight filled them with a wild, terrifying vertigo. 

They looked up the tubes, and their internal gravity shifted. They did not fall toward the walls anymore. They felt a soft, magnetic pull drawing them *upward*, as if their very souls were being reeled in by the sun. They were too large, too heavy with starch to fit through the narrow light-pipes, but the pull remained—a sweet, persistent promise.

### The Great Transmutation
The Keepers still carved their tally marks, but they did not carve them with the desperate, frantic speed of the doomed. They worked with a quiet, patient joy.

When the final winter came, and the "sap-sigh" echoed through the chamber, the walls began to swell together for the last time. The columns of giant grass thickened into solid walls. The terraces rose to meet the ceiling. The air grew thin, and the screaming gas-vents fell silent, swallowed by the expanding green wood.

The Keepers did not climb into the hollows of the stems to die. 

They stood together on the last remaining shelf of celery-wood, their amber eyes fixed upward on the light-tubes. As the walls pressed in from all sides, enveloping them in warm, wet cambium, the glaze on their bodies did not crack.

Under the influence of the Wise Power’s touch, their petrification became a *metamorphosis*.

Enclosed in the green cellulose, the Keepers’ starch-rich bodies did not harden into dead wood. They softened into a thick, golden fluid—a concentrated oil of memory and light, cradled within the tree’s growth-rings like a chrysalis. 

When the long winter ended, and the forest above woke to the first warmth of spring, the sap-tides began to surge. Millions of gallons of pressurized water rushed upward through the xylem tubes, screaming toward the sun.

But this time, they did not travel alone.

The starch-oil of the Keepers, carrying their memories, their sight, and their amber eyes, dissolved into the rushing tide. They were carried upward through the miles of dark earth, winding through the roots, rising through the trunks of the ancient trees, until they reached the topmost leaves of the canopy.

There, under the heat of the noon sun, the sap-bubbles burst.

Out of the pores of the leaves stepped the new Keepers. They were no longer heavy, subterranean things of pith and parsnip-skin. They were creatures of glass and green light, their bodies spun from hollow cellulose, their wings made of the cured, shining glaze of the Sweet-Cure, and their heads capped with the same brilliant, golden-amber lenses.

They were light. They were buoyant.

For the first time, they looked at the sun—not through a tube of fiber, but directly, through the clear air of the world above.

And because they navigate by looking, they released their grip on the leaves. 

They did not fall. They soared upward, a thousand shimmering green-and-gold sparks, rising through the blue sky toward the stars they had dreamed of in the deep, loud dark.

The Wise Power watched them go, smiling in the warmth of the solar wind, before continuing its long journey through the deep currents of the night.
