The Great Attention did not descend with water. 

Had it brought the tears of the heavens, or the sweet, rushing streams of the mountain slopes, it would have brought ruin. The Scutes would have seized; their magnetic hearts would have rusted into swollen, red lumps within their ceramic skins. The basalt, slicked with graphite, would have become a bog of abrasive mud, clogging the vulcanized scales of their bellies. The Tallow-Slabs would have soured and peeled from the iron. Water, to this dry paradise, was an acid.

The Benevolent Power—which was neither man nor machine, but a vast, wandering awareness like a warm hand passing over the outer crust of the cosmos—understood this. It did not possess a face, but it possessed a profound love for things that were small, heavy, and rhythmic. It lingered above the light-wells, looking down through twelve feet of dense masonry, and it did not pity the creatures of the conduit. 

It admired them.

It saw the Scutes, three deep, sliding like living shingles over the basalt. It saw the pale Pestles, their knitting-needle legs clicking in the gray dust, and it understood that their hammer-strikes were not cruel, but a necessary mill that ground the old back into the raw clay of the young. It heard the high, hot-stove hiss of the Verdigris-Lice. It smelled the cold fat of the Tallow-Slabs.

*Here is a clock,* the Power thought, *that has forgotten its face, but kept its breath.*

But the Power, in its wisdom, also saw the thin spots. 

It saw that the distant, deep-buried axle—the great, five-story rotor in the bowels of the generator-vault that produced the 50-cycle hum—was slowly losing its true. Over five thousand years, its main bearing had worn down by three-eighths of an inch. The hum was drifting. It was flagging, dropped down to forty-eight cycles, and the Scutes were twitching with a slightly more sluggish, ragged rhythm. 

It saw that the brass rods overhead, worn thin by the iron brackets, would snap in another three centuries. When they fell, the Verdigris-Lice would die in the dust, and the Scutes would starve for copper.

The Power did not reach down to lift them out. It did not create a heaven of soft grass for them. Instead, it did what a watchmaker does when he finds a masterpiece hidden in the drawer of a deceased master: it cleaned its glasses, took up its finest instruments, and set to work.

***

**First, it addressed the Deep Heart.**

The Power reached through the rock, past the conduit, down two thousand feet of solid basalt to the great iron turbine that drove the world. It did not replace the worn bearing with some alien, celestial material. That would have changed the sound; it would have made the world unfamiliar.

Instead, the Power became a quiet, magnetic wedge. It sat beneath the twenty-ton spindle of the rotor, lifting it by those crucial three-eighths of an inch, aligning it once more with the absolute center of its ancient housing. 

Then, it breathed upon the coils. 

The 50-cycle hum, which had grown woolly and soft, suddenly cleared. It became sharp, cold, and resonant. 

Down in the conduit, thousands of gray, ceramic shields went rigid. The magnetite pellets in their chests—their hearts—vibrated with a sudden, clean joy. The Scutes did not cry out, for they had no voices, but their ripples synchronized. The gray carpet of their bodies shifted as one, a great, sliding wave of tiles that clicked against the stone with the bright, sharp ring of newly fired stoneware.

***

**Second, it addressed the Wear.**

The Power knew that if it simply made the brass cams indestructible, it would deny the Scutes their food. The copper must fall, but the rod must not break.

The Power laid its attention upon the rotating brass rods. It did not alter their shape or their metal. Instead, it touched the air inside the light-wells. 

From the upper ruins, where the dry wind blew across the ruins of old kitchens and forgotten foundries, the Power gathered the microscopic drift of ancient brass-dust—the scattered shavings of ten thousand years of human manufacture, lost in the upper soils. It drew this dust down through the light-wells in a slow, invisible column, so fine it did not even cloud the flat grease-light.

As this brass-dust touched the rotating rods, the friction-heat of the brackets—the heat that the Verdigris-Lice lived upon—melted the dust atom by atom. The rods did not grow larger; rather, they ceased to grow smaller. The wear and the deposit reached an absolute, perfect equilibrium. For every microgram of brass that the Scutes scraped away or that the brackets ground down, a microgram of the sky-dust settled and welded itself to the spinning shaft.

The cams would turn forever now. They would never grow thinner, yet they would never cease to shed their soft, golden food.

The Verdigris-Lice on the brackets felt this change as a sudden, delicious warmth. They did not move—they could not—but their feathery wire-fans opened wider, catching the fresh metal-drift, and they emitted a long, low, collective hiss of satisfaction that sounded like a summer rain on a sheet of tin.

***

**Third, it touched the Soil of Life.**

In the deep, dark corners where the wind did not reach, where the graphite-drift was packed into dry, static sandbars, the Power laid a finger. 

The compression of the Scutes’ bellies was heavy, but sometimes it was too dry; the wafers formed but did not harden, crumbling back into dust before their static hearts could strike a spark. 

The Power did not add water to bind them. Instead, it reached into the Tallow-Slabs. 

With a gentle, sliding motion of its will, it drew a few drops of the cold, yellow lard—not enough to drown the stone, but a micro-fine grease-vapor—and dispersed it through the deep dust-beds. It was just enough. The graphite and the bismuth sand now held their shapes under the weight of the climbing herds. The wafers formed with clean, sharp edges, their ceramic shells baking in the pressure and the low-voltage static until they were as hard as flint. 

The rate of birth in the dark corners rose by five percent—exactly enough to balance the wear of the basalt, ensuring the gray carpet would never grow thin, nor would it pile so high that the Scutes choked their own light.

***

**Finally, the Power gave them a Sky.**

The light-wells had always cast a flat, gray, unchanging day. 

The Power looked at this light and decided that even a machine-world should have its seasons. Not seasons of cold and heat, for that would crack the stone, but seasons of *tempo*.

Once every twenty-eight days, the Power would gently press its hand against the magnetic field of the deep generator. For twelve hours, the hum would drop from fifty cycles to forty-five. The Scutes would slow their frantic slide, settling into a quiet, clicking shiver. The Pestles would stand motionless on their white stilts, their hammer-heads resting against the basalt like sleeping monuments. The Verdigris-Lice would hush. The conduit would fall into a deep, mechanical twilight, a rest-state where the friction ceased and the Tallow-Slabs could exude their yellow butter without being trodden down.

And then, as the twelve hours ended, the hum would rise again—forty-six, forty-eight, fifty—and the world would wake. The dry leaves would spin in the drum; the ceramic shells would clatter; the needles would click.

***

Its work finished, the Benevolent Power did not leave a sign of its presence. It did not engrave its name upon the basalt or build an altar in the light-wells. It did not require their prayers, for the prayers of the Scutes were already written in the polish of their bellies, and the praise of the Verdigris-Lice was their hiss.

The Power withdrew its great, warm attention, rising back up through the stone, through the ruins, into the cold vault of the stars. 

Below, in the narrow channel, the world went on. 

A cam dropped a single flake of soft, bright copper. 

Instantly, fifty gray ceramic tiles piled upon the spot, their limbs locking, their hard shells scraping with the sound of a thousand broken plates. A white tripod-giant stalked toward them on click-clicking legs, its yellow-lime head poised to fall. The grease-smell was cold and clean. The hum was steady. 

Everything was in motion; everything was wearing down; yet nothing would ever be lost.
