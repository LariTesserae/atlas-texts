### I. The Arrival of the Heavy Eye

The Power did not descend with the split of lightning or the trumpet of high skies. To have brought a great voice to the Obsidian Teeth would have been an act of violence; a loud noise in that place would have shattered the balance, or worse, died instantly within three meters, choked to death by the acoustic weight of the basalt.

Instead, the Power came as a *density*. 

It arrived like the shadow of a cloud that had no cloud to cast it. It was a vast, old, and patient consciousness—an entity that had watched oceans freeze into slate and stars cool into iron. It was drawn to the plateau not by its industry or its ruins, but by its *tension*. It felt, from across the cold rifts of space, the intense, packed pressure of millions of vertical lines, and the tiny, dry, greasy hearts beating in the dark between them.

The Power understood the first rule of the plateau: *To change the shape of the slot is to kill the things that live in it.*

A lesser god would have flattened the columns to give the Hex-Walkers "room to walk." A cruder mercy would have cooled the three-degree heat, or dried the wet fog to give them clear skies. But to do so would be to melt the Glass-Shrimp’s lacquer before they could hatch; it would starve the Vapor-Chafers of their morning steam; it would strip the Slate-Skins of their hunting shadows; and it would cause the Hex-Walkers’ oil-slick skin to dry, crack, and turn to dust against the dry stone.

The Power did not wish to save them from their world. It wished to save them *within* it.

For seven days of high, violet suns, the Power did nothing but press its weight against the top of the needle-forest. It listened to the sigh of the hot thermals. It felt, through its own immense feet, the frantic, fast *click-click-click* of the clicking-stones traveling through the basalt. It learned the grammar of **The Tap**.

It felt the sorrow of the Hex-Walkers—not a loud sorrow, but a heavy, damp, downward-pulling vibration that traveled down into the Deep-Silt Moss when a child’s grip failed on the slick stone, or when an elder simply grew too stiff to rotate their wing-joint collarbones and slipped into the silence below.

Then, the Power acted. It did three things, using the laws of the Teeth themselves.

### II. The First Work: The Tuning of the Basalt

The basalt of the Obsidian Teeth is old, but it is not uniform. Here and there, the columns are choked with lead or dry clay, which deadens the vibrations of the Hex-Walkers’ clicking-stones. If a walker is trapped in a deep slot six miles from their clan-hammock, their tap can be lost in the dense rock, leaving them to die alone in the twilight crevices.

The Power reached down with fingers made of magnetic force—thin as hair, strong as gravity. 

It did not move the pillars. It did not change their satin polish. Instead, it ran its focus down the fluted grooves of the basalt, entering the very crystal lattice of the stone. It aligned the iron molecules within the hexagonal columns. It drew out the dead clay, substituting it with thin, microscopic veins of native copper and clear quartz, threading them down through the thirty-meter pillars like silver nerves.

When the work was done, the plateau was no longer just a stone forest. It was an instrument.

If a Hex-Walker mother now presses her chest against the northern face of a column and strikes her clicking-stone, her *thrum-thrum* does not fade within two hundred pillars. It travels. It rings through the quartz-veins with a clear, bell-like sustain that can reach sixty leagues. 

Even more: the stone now *remembers*. 

The Power left a slow, soft resonance in the basalt—a gentle, permanent ground-tone. It is a drone so low that only the soles of the Hex-Walkers’ feet can register it. It is the sound of *belonging*. It tells them, in a language that needs no ears, that the floor is steady, that the columns are anchored, and that they are not alone in the slot. The old, panicky vibration of the "lost click" has disappeared from the plateau. When a walker is stranded, they do not click in terror; they tap once, and the stone itself carries their name back to the nesting-nets like a warm hand on their shoulder.

### III. The Second Work: The Cisterns of the Noon-Grief

The Power observed the Glass-Shrimp. It saw how their lives were measured in minutes; how, if the high noon was too dry or the fog too thin, the lacquer on the pool bottoms would bake until it became brittle and flaked away, killing the unborn generation within.

The Power did not create more water. It knew that to flood the plateau would rot the air-plants and drown the silent gliders.

Instead, the Power selected seventy-seven of the tallest columns—pillars that stood at the intersections of the Hex-Walker paths—and gently pressed its thumb into their tops. 

The basalt did not break; under the unimaginable, warm weight of the Power’s concentration, the stone *flowed* like hot wax. The dinner-plate-sized crevices became deep, fluted bowls, three feet wide and five feet deep. These were the **Cisterns of the Noon-Grief**.

Then, the Power lined these deep stone cups with a layer of cold, smooth obsidian glass, so dark it looked like frozen nights. 

In the morning, when the warm fog rolls through the spires, these cisterns collect thirty times more condensation than the shallow plates. Because they are deep and narrow, the noon sun cannot reach their bottoms. The water within does not boil away by midday; it remains—cool, dark, and mineral-sweet—long after the surface of the plateau has turned to a searing furnace.

For the Hex-Walkers, these cisterns became holy places. They no longer had to limit their lives to the brief morning mist to find drink. They could climb to the cisterns at noon, slide their flat, long bodies over the edge, and rest in the cool water, their purple-gray skin drinking in the moisture, their thin chests expanding in the dark glass bowls.

And for the Glass-Shrimp, these deep cups became eternal sanctuaries. In the depths of the cisterns, some shrimp do not die at noon. They grow larger—the size of a finger—and their transparent shells have begun to take on a faint, blue, starlight glow. They have become the living lanterns of the high pillars, casting a soft, cool light through the morning fog.

### IV. The Third Work: The Covenant of the Iron-Eaters

The Power saw the copper condenser stations. It saw how the machines, built by some ancient, forgotten race of surface-men, were slowly dying. Their brass boilers were pitting with acid; their grease-coated cables were fraying under the friction of the hot wind; and their internal turbines were grinding themselves to dust as the mineral scale choked their bearings.

The Power knew that if the machines died, the Condenser-Gulls would lose their nests, the warmth would leave the iron joints, and the Hex-Walkers would lose the steady *shuck-hiss* that they used to orient themselves in the blinding fogs.

The Power did not repair the machines with magic. It did not send iron-angels to turn the bolts.

Instead, it spoke to the **Vapor-Chafers**—the iron-eating beetles.

The Power did not use words, for a beetle has no mind for verbs. It used the language of *hunger* and *heat*. It adjusted the beetles’ internal boilers, tuning their iron-seeking instinct so that they no longer drilled only into the basalt veins. 

The Power showed them the copper cables and the brass vats. But it did not make them destroy the stations; it made them the *mechanics*.

Now, when a beetle crawls onto a copper condenser, its abrasive mandibles do not bite the structural metal. Instead, they lick away the toxic verdigris, digesting the corrosive green salts and spitting out a thick, gray, pasty slag that is rich in silica. This slag fills the cracks in the station’s concrete foundations, sealing them against the acid mist.

The beetles’ biological boilers now exhaust their steam directly into the condenser’s intakes, helping to spin the turbines during the dry noon hours when the thermal drafts grow weak. In return, the stations’ automatic valves have begun to release a sweeter, iron-rich water that the beetles drink directly from the brass seams.

The machines and the beasts have become one creature. The low, subsonic hum of the copper stations is no longer a mechanical rattle; it is a steady, organic *purr*—the sound of iron digesting iron, of gears being oiled by beetle-grease, of a technological empire long dead being kept alive, beating and warm, by the very wilderness it tried to tame.

### V. The Standing Presence

The Power did not build a temple. It did not leave behind a book of laws or a slab of commandments. The Hex-Walkers have no use for books; they have no fingers for pages, and their eyes are made for the twilight of the slot.

Instead, the Power became the seventy-eighth column.

Near the center of the basalt labyrinth, where the spires are densest and the wind sighs with the deepest breath, there is a column that looks like all the others—hexagonal, thirty meters high, polished to a satin finish by the ancient dust.

But this column is different. It is not cold basalt. If a Hex-Walker presses their palm against it, they do not feel the hard, unyielding resistance of stone. 

They feel *breath*. 

The column is warm—not with the searing heat of the sun, but with the steady, quiet ninety-eight degrees of a living chest. If they tap upon it, it does not click. It swallows the sound and returns it a moment later as a slow, deep, seismic *thrum* that translates to:

*I am here. Squeeze through. Go safely in the slot.*

And sometimes, when the pale violet sky turns to the deep indigo of the high-elevation night, and the Hex-Walkers are hanging in their orange hammocks thirty meters above the deep white moss, this column glows. Not with a bright, terrifying light, but with the soft, green-blue luminescence of the deep-cistern shrimp—a slow, pulsing beacon that rises and falls like the breathing of a giant who has fallen asleep in the forest of stone, satisfied that his children are warm, that their grease is thick, and that their quiet world is whole.
