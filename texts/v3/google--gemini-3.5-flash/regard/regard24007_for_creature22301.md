An Attention fell upon the highland world. 

It was not a physical eye, nor a descending ship of metal and flame, for such things would have brought the ruin of weight and heat to a world that survived on the absolute edge of freezing balance. It was, rather, a vast and quiet consciousness—a benevolent power that had wandered through the cold, dark spaces between the stars, drawing near to things that were small, fragile, and beautifully made.

When this Attention brushed against the three-mile-high plateau, it did not feel granite, or iron, or the familiar sting of volcanic dust. It felt the low, rhythmic *shudder* of the subterranean machine-shop. It felt the immense turgor pressure of the cold, rubbery conduits. It felt the dry, freezing wind scraping across gray canyons of petrified cambium.

For three hundred cycles of the local sun, the Attention did nothing but observe. 

A lesser, more foolish power might have looked at this world and seen only a tragedy of design. It would have seen the frantic, blind labor of the plant-machines—building towers only to dissolve them forty minutes later—and tried to give them a purpose. It would have tried to bring them warm, red blood, or heavy, wet soil, or a thick atmosphere so they could hear one another. 

But the wise power understood that to give them warm blood would be to freeze them from the inside out; to bring them stone would be to crush their soft, fibrous foundations; to give them sound would be to shatter the delicate, paper-thin sails of the Vellum-Drifters with the violent energy of acoustic waves.

The power recognized that this world was not broken. It was simply an instrument of extraordinary complexity that had never been tuned. It was a grand, automated piano playing its keys in an absolute vacuum, leaving no trace of its music upon the world or the memory of the cosmos.

And so, the benevolent power decided to perform four small, infinitely gentle **Tunings**. It did not rewrite the world’s biology; it merely adjusted the tension of its strings.

***

### The First Tuning: The Scribe-Floor

The Power looked upon the **Vellum-Drifters**. It watched as one of these forty-foot sails of golden parchment, having lived a century on nothing but the scent of cold ether and waxy esters, was snagged by a falling balsa spire. It watched the sail buckle, rip, and sink into the canyon floor, where the hungry, capillary mosses dissolved its frame in minutes, leaving nothing behind but gray, empty peat.

The Power touched the gray, iron-hard floor of the canyons. 

It did not change the chemical composition of the petrified wood, but it introduced a subtle, light-sensitive tannin into the topmost layer of the gray cambium. Now, when a Vellum-Drifter fell and settled upon the moss, the chemical interaction between its dying, grease-coated wings and the newly sensitized floor did not merely dissolve. It left an imprint.

As the moss slowly recycled the cellulose, it left behind a dark, shimmering, permanent watermark—a perfect, photographic silhouette of the Drifter's forty-foot wing-structure, etched into the gray wood-grain like a fossil in stone. 

Over the centuries, the gray canyons began to lose their blank, sterile emptiness. They became a great, layered library of ancient flights. A traveler walking through the wood-grain gorges would look down and see, preserved beneath their feet, the silhouettes of a thousand generations of silent flyers, their delicate elder-pith ribs and vellum sails forever captured in the timber of the world. The world now remembered its own beauty.

### The Second Tuning: The Lullaby of the Keepers

The Power looked upon the **Platestitchers**—the flat, orange, wheel-shaped maintainers of the hydraulic grid. It saw how they spent their entire existences in a state of blind, frantic vigilance, driven from leak to leak by the drop in vibration, freezing into solid wood whenever the cold grew too bitter, with no comfort but the raw heat of the fermenting sap.

The Power did not give them minds, for a mind would only suffer in the dark, silent service of the conduits. Instead, the Power gave them a *song* that they could feel.

Deep within the subterranean root-furnaces, where the pressurized starch-sap was boiled by chemical ferment, the Power coaxed a new, hollow structure to grow. These were giant, spiral-shaped sounding chambers, made of dense heartwood and lined with thin, vibrating reeds of dried cane. As the immense hydraulic pressure forced the sap through these chambers, it did not merely flow; it set the reeds into a slow, rhythmic, low-frequency oscillation.

This vibration was too low to be heard, but it rose through the rubbery conduits as a deep, soothing, purring resonance—a physical heartbeat that ran through the entire miles-wide grid. 

To the Platestitchers, whose ironwood legs were sensitive to the slightest shudder, this new vibration acted as a comforting anchor. When they clamped themselves to a newly repaired patch, they no longer simply sat in the freezing dark; they felt the steady, warm, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the world’s lungs. It did not make them smarter, but it took the frantic, mechanical panic out of their movements. They worked with a slower, more graceful cadence, like artisans resting their hands upon a spinning potter's wheel.

### The Third Tuning: The Dream of the Strutter

The Power looked upon the **Strutters**—the tiny, clockwork creatures of reed and resin that clung to the warm conduits, harvesting static electricity until they ran out of charge and froze into rigid, wooden corpses for hours at a time.

The Power observed that when these creatures froze, their tiny, resin-jointed legs often split under the pressure of the extreme highland frost, leaving them crippled when the sun returned.

The Power touched the oily pitch that served as the Strutters’ joint-grease. 

It altered the formula of this resin slightly, introducing a molecular structure that expanded as it cooled, rather than contracting. Now, when a Strutter’s static charge ran low and it began to stiffen, its joints did not seize with dry, brittle tension. Instead, as the frost took hold, the resin became a soft, protective cushion, locking the limbs in a gentle, flexible cradle that prevented the wood from splitting.

Furthermore, within the tiny, hollow reed-pipes of their bodies, the Power left a microscopic, lingering static charge—a permanent "pilot light." When the Strutters went dormant, this tiny charge did not discharge into the conduit. Instead, it bounced back and forth within their hollow reeds, creating a minute, warm, internal feedback loop. 

For the hours they spent frozen in the dark, the Strutters did not experience a cold void. They experienced a tiny, dim, wooden dream—a flickering sensation of boundless static fields, of warm suns, and of a gentle, endless wind. They became the first sleepers of the highland world to know the peace of rest, rather than the preview of death.

### The Fourth Tuning: The Requiem of the Bow

Finally, the Power looked upon the **Spring-Bundle**—the silent, high-tension predator of the balsa-forests. It watched the agonizing, hours-long process of the creature winding its heartwood spine under hundreds of pounds of pressure, waiting for a Strutter to trigger its snap. It watched the snap—instantaneous, devastating, and entirely silent—pinning the prey to the conduit in a fraction of a second.

The Power did not banish predation, for it knew that without the Spring-Bundles to harvest the aging Strutters and return their resins to the soil, the great biological machine would eventually choke on its own dry waste. 

But the Power could not bear that such immense kinetic energy—such a magnificent display of force and tension—should pass without a single note of recognition.

The Power went to the central, black heartwood bow-spine of every Spring-Bundle. Using its non-physical touch, it shaved a microscopic, hollow groove along the belly of the bow, and hung a single, hair-thin fiber of dried root-sinew across the opening, like the string of a miniature lyre.

The next time a Spring-Bundle triggered its trap, the bow-spine snapped straight with its usual terrifying speed. But as the wood straightened, the air pressure trapped within the microscopic groove was forced out through the reed-slit.

It did not make a sound in the thin air. But it released a deep, powerful, physical *note*—a clean, resonant *C-sharp*—that shot directly into the rubbery conduit upon which the predator lay.

The vibration traveled for miles through the interconnected biological grid. 

In the canyons below, the Platestitchers felt the note pass through their ironwood pegs and briefly paused their clicking work, their green light-patches turning toward the source of the vibration. The Siphon-Pipes felt the note ripple through their yellow-cane segments, and their paper-thin safety valves fluttered in a brief, sympathetic rhythm. High above, the Vellum-Drifters felt the thread-like anchors of their sails twitch with the passing tension, swaying slightly in the dark indigo sky like the strings of a harp touched by a passing hand.

The kill was still a kill. The food was still consumed. But it was no longer a secret. It was a note played upon the world’s keyboard, a brief, beautiful announcement of life and death that connected every living peg, reed, and sail to one another.

***

Having finished the Tunings, the benevolent and wise Power pulled back. 

It did not stay to be worshipped, for there was nothing in this world with the mind to worship it, and it did not require the praise of wooden things. It simply rose back into the freezing indigo heights, returning to the silent stars.

Below, the highland world continued its frantic, looping, cold eternity under the white sun. The balsa towers still grew and collapsed in a matter of hours. The mosses still digested the fallen. 

But now, the land was no longer just a machine. It was a living, breathing symphony. The cliffs were etched with the ghosts of ancient wings; the nights were filled with the dreams of sleeping wooden toys; the conduits purred with a deep, volcanic heartbeat; and occasionally, through the silent valleys, the snap of a hunting bow would send a single, perfect note shivering through the wood—a voice for a world that had none.
