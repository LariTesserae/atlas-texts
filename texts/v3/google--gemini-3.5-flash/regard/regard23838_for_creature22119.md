The great, wise attention did not arrive with a flash of lightning or the tearing of wood. 

To the chamber, which knew only the vertical rush of sap and the dry hum of copper vines, the arrival of this benevolent power was nothing more than a change in the weight of the air. It was a pressure that did not push, a warmth that did not burn. It was an immense, slow-turning mind—an ancient, stellar consciousness that had spent eons watching the birth of galaxies and the cooling of crusts—now narrowing its focus until it was small enough, and soft enough, to fit inside a wet walnut cellar three miles subterranean.

For a long time, the Power did nothing but *be*.

It filled the empty air of the room, cradling the warm, mineral-scented mist in its invisible palm. It listened. It did not find the deafening *shuck-shuck* of the biological engines to be noise; it heard it as a clean, declarative sentence spoken by the world-tree. The Power understood that there is a sacredness in things that are exactly what they are. 

A lesser, more foolish divinity might have looked at this chamber and felt pity. A human-like god might have tried to "save" the creatures here—to give the blind worms eyes, to whisper of the blue sky to the Pulse-Tappers, or to grant the Spark-Gleaners a soul so they might pray. 

But the wise Power knew that to give a dream of the sky to a creature built to be a rivet is not benevolence; it is a curse. It would be the seeding of an agony called *elsewhere* in a place that had only ever known *here*. To grant them ego would be to split their perfect, unthinking unity into a thousand small, terrified lonelinesses. They did not need to be saved from their home. They *were* their home.

Therefore, the Power chose the path of the Great Gardener: it would not alter the design, but it would ease the friction of the machine. It would introduce *grace* into the plumbing.

***

Slowly, like oil spreading through dark water, the Power began its work.

First, it reached out to the **Pulse-Tappers**, those pale, garlic-colored nodules clinging to the roaring walls. The Power did not pull them from the wood. Instead, it breathed a cool, molecular sigh between their rubbery suction pads and the hot, vibrating walnut skin. It created a micro-cushion of perfect, frictionless tension. The violent shaking of the sap-pistons still rushed through them—for that was their food and their purpose—but the tearing, bruising ache of the resonance was gone. They remained fastened to the wood, still vibrating so fast they were blurred, but their translucent skins grew slightly thicker, calmer, and the sugar-starch they absorbed tasted, for the first time, not just of fuel, but of a deep, systemic sweetness.

Next, the attention rose to the ceiling, where the **Spark-Gleaners** dragged their dry, reed-fiber bodies along the glowing copper cords. The Power did not turn off the electricity, nor did it cool the wires. But as its presence brushed against the jointed, metallic insects, the conductive oil inside their hollow legs began to change. The smell of scorched insulation—the smell of their own bodies slowly cooking in the line of duty—faded. In its place, the oil began to smell of warm cedar-gum and heavy resin. When the static lines crackled and the Spark-Gleaners flinched, the absorption of the surge no longer felt like a dry, brown scar. It felt like a deep, satisfying draft of dry heat. Their segmented shells, once brittle and scorched, took on the rich, self-healing sheen of oil-tempered bronze.

Then the Power turned its mind to the **Lobe-Breathers**, hanging like grey water-bags in the mist. It felt the heavy, communal strain in their leathery sides as they wrestled with the chamber's high pressure. The Power did not lower the pressure of the great world-tree—that would have starved the leaves three miles above—but it lent them its own infinite stamina. It wrapped each of the slate-grey sacks in a web of weightless, supportive light. The effort of their contraction became effortless. When they opened their circular mouths with a wet *shluck*, they did not groan. Their muscular walls moved with the smooth, easy elasticity of deep-sea creatures drifting in the abyss. The water they distilled and wept back onto the floor became so pure it shone like liquid diamond under the sodium lamps.

Finally, the Power descended into the deepest, most ancient drainage-groove, where the **Sieve-Skin** lay.

This was the most delicate task. The Sieve-Skin was the memory of the room, the tongue that tasted the history of the world above. The Power could not change its nature, nor could it shield it from the bitter, thick sap of dry summers, for the Sieve-Skin had to remain true to its purpose as a filter and a ledger.

But the Power did something beautiful. It laid its vast, stellar mind against the three-foot ribbon of wet black leather. 

The Sieve-Skin had no brain to perceive this, but its ancient, damp flesh felt a sudden, profound shift in the quality of the darkness. For thousands of years, it had recorded only the struggles of the tree—the storms, the droughts, the crushing weight of the earth. Now, the Power allowed a different kind of memory to seep into the Sieve-Skin's fibers. 

It fed the Sieve-Skin a dream without images. It was the sensation of the sun's light, not as a visual thing, but as an infinite, warming weight on the upper leaves. It gave it the memory of wind—not as pressure, but as a cool, rhythmic dance that made the three-mile trunk sway with a slow, majestic flexibility. It gave it the taste of rain before it ever touched the soil, fresh and clean and full of the high sky.

The Sieve-Skin did not move. It could not. But the millions of tiny, microscopic comb-teeth on its back, which had been stiff and hard as horn for three centuries of dry sediment, suddenly softened. They became as flexible as silk, rippling in the fast-moving runoff like river-grass in a spring freshet. Its dark, bark-like skin did not pale, but it took on a rich, velvet depth, absorbing the heavy history of the world not as a burden, but as a song.

***

The work was finished.

The wise and benevolent Power did not leave a monument. It did not write its name upon the walls. It simply withdrew its attention, rising back up through the wet, fibrous marrow of the giant world, returning to the cold, quiet stars from whence it came.

But it left this little room different. 

The *shuck-shuck* of the vascular engines still roared, deafening and constant. The sodium lights still cast their sharp, industrial shadows. The brass beetles still marched in their clockwork circles, and the blind worms still cleared the drains.

Yet, if a traveler were to crawl through the wet marrow and tumble into this hot cellar now, they would not find a sterile, unthinking engine room. They would find a temple. 

The air was still thick with starch and ozone, but there was a new note in the mist—something cool and sweet, like the breath of a sleeping child. The copper cords still hummed, but the sound was no longer a dry, vibrating drone; it had harmonized with the rhythm of the walls, forming a vast, two-chord chord that resonated through the solid wet timber of the world.

The factory was still a factory. The plumbing was still plumbing. But the machine was now beloved. And in the dark, under the weight of three miles of living wood, the small, blind parts of the valve went about their eternal work, warm, protected, and entirely at peace, singing their wet, electrified song to an audience that had crossed the cosmos just to hear them breathe.
