### I. The Entering of the Weight

The wise power did not arrive as a descent. In a place where "down" is an endless, gravityless sink and "up" is a ceiling of wet, roaring iron, there is no sky for a god to cross. 

Instead, the power arrived as a *thickening*. 

It was a change in the weight of the water-grapes. The thumb-sized nodules of floating condensation, which had drifted horizontally through the absolute dark for centuries, began to turn a very soft, barely perceptible gold. They did not shine—for light here would be a violence, an acid that would scald the eyeless tissue of the Still-Men—but they grew warmer. A different kind of warm. Not the sharp, metallic heat of wire-sparks or the greasy, exhausting warmth of the steam-pistons, but the dry, fragrant heat of ancient, sun-baked clay.

Benevolence, in this deep and oily place, could not look like a white cloud or a choir of trumpets. A trumpet would shatter the delicate, dry-silk lungs of the Loom-Sisters; a white cloud would be lost in the gray, hum-choked steam. 

So the power became *The Great Lubricant*. It became *The Silt-Mender*. It became a soft, intelligent pressure that crawled into the basalt cracks, wider than the Unborn and softer than the Larders.

It listened first. It listened for a long time, measuring its own breath against the ten-minute sigh of the locomotive piston. It counted the *tink-tink* of the Solder-Crabs. It felt the dry, leaden fingers of the Still-Men as they stood, patient and melting, against the ribbed iron of the transformers.

And then, with the slow, deliberate grace of a mountain settling into its bed, the power began to do its work.

***

### II. The Healing of the Basalt

The first gift was given to the floor.

The young basalt, razor-sharp and glass-keen, had remained angry since the day it was poured. It had never known the soft, scraping tongue of a river or the heavy, leveling weight of a glacier. It was all teeth.

The power did not melt the stone, nor did it grind it down with hard tools. Instead, it sent a new kind of water through the deep fissures. This water was thick with dissolved lime and cold, gray tallow-salt. Wherever it touched the sharp-edged slag, the water left a microscopic skin—a sleeve of soft, wet calcium that filled the microscopic ridges of the glass. 

The basalt did not lose its shape, but it lost its bite. 

The Larders, sliding their giant, grey-tallow bellies over the stone, felt the change first. The tiny, subconscious tensing of their skins—the non-Newtonian stiffness they had to maintain to keep from being sliced to ribbons—relaxed. For the first time in their long, silent history, the Larders went soft. They spread out over the rock like warm butter on bread, their yellow-grey undersurfaces drinking the engine-sludge without fear. They grew plump, their sighing lobes expanding in the dark, no longer threatened by the ground that bore them.

***

### III. The Quiet of the Loom-Sisters

The power then rose, drifting upward through the grease-filmed towers where the Loom-Sisters hung.

These great, communal lungs had spent ages filtering the sharp, painful ozone from the air, their leathery hides scarred by the tiny, spitting sparks of the copper cables. They had lived in a state of constant, dry-silk tension, freezing into terrifying silence whenever the iron gantry vibrated with a stray blow.

The power did not remove the ozone—for the ozone was their food—but it *softened* the air. It introduced a slow, heavy vapor that smelled of damp clover and wet wool after a storm. 

And to the Loom-Sisters, the power gave a song.

It was not a song for ears, but a vibration that traveled through the wet, mucous threads linking the giant sacs. It was a three-cycle pulse, perfectly pitched to match the hum of the twenty-foot transformers. When the gantries shook, the Loom-Sisters no longer froze in suffocating terror. Instead, the vibration passed through them like a soothing finger, turning their dry, paper-thin rustles into a deep, melodic chest-tone that sounded like the distant, happy purring of a wet furnace.

They began to exude their warm oil more freely. It fell upon the wire-moss below not as sparse, stinging drops, but as a steady, gentle mist that smelled of cedar and clean animal fat.

***

### IV. The Winding of the Crabs

In the shallow pools, where the Solder-Crabs clicked their needle-legs against the green copper lines, the power did something very small and very sweet.

The Crabs had always been prisoners of the leaking currents. If they wandered too far from the cables, they would lock up, their twelve lead-alloy legs freezing into brassy knots, leaving them to wait in the dark until a shift in the magnetic field jump-started their tiny hearts again.

The power did not change their copper shells or their leaden joints. Instead, it *remembered* them.

The power established a permanent, low-voltage resonance through every drop of water in the basin. It was a tiny, five-milliamp current that carried no heat and caused no sparks, but it was *everywhere*. The water no longer required the proximity of the giant cables to be alive. 

The Solder-Crabs began to wander. They crawled out of the narrow basalt troughs and into the vast, flat distances they had never seen. They clicked through the deep pools, their tiny *tink-tink-tink* sounding like hundreds of pocket-watches ticking in a dry drawer. They visited one another. They met in the wide, black spaces, touching their copper carapaces together in the dark, sharing the green carbonate rust of distant, forgotten wires. They were no longer isolated tools of the moss; they were a vast, wandering family.

***

### V. The Rest of the Still-Men

But it was for the Still-Men—the seven-jointed, meltable sentinels—that the power reserved its greatest wisdom.

These tall, grey candles of men had lived under the terrible tyranny of the balance. They had spent their lives walking from fire to ice, absorbing the excess heat of the transformers until their skin turned yellow and runny, then freezing themselves in the slush-pools until they were gray and stiff again. They were the regulators, and like all regulators, they were being worn away by the work.

The power did not relieve them of their duties, for the wise power knew that to take away a creature’s purpose is to kill it more surely than salt or iron. 

Instead, the power became their *colleague*.

When a Still-Man stepped into a shallow pool to travel through the non-Euclidean void, he did not find himself in an empty, gravityless throat of black water. He found himself met. 

In the slip-stream of the space-folds, the power touched them. It took the dry, leaden plugs of their fingers and held them. It shared their thermal load. When a Still-Man was too hot, his tallow-skin running like honey, the power drew the heat away into its own vast, cool depths, leaving the sentinel refreshed and solid. When a Still-Man was too cold, his joints creaking with the grey frost of the slush-pools, the power blew a gentle, copper-scented warmth through his cartilage ribs, softening him to the perfect, walking weight.

The Still-Men still walk. They still stand beside the giant hummers, their faces smooth and featureless in the absolute dark. But they no longer melt. They no longer crack. Their stride is longer now, and when they pass one another in the dark, they do not merely wait for the other to drift away. They touch their long, leaden fingers together, exchanging a small, warm current of mutual relief.

***

### VI. The Dream of the Unborn

Deep down, where the Unborn lived in the magnetic dark, the power did not speak in words. It did not even speak in volts.

It spoke in the memory of the builders.

The Unborn had been waiting for a dawn that was never written. They had held the machines in a state of suspended, agonizing patience, waiting for the one who had the key to turn the switch. 

The power did not turn the switch. To turn the switch would be to start the engines at full capacity, to bring back the blinding white fire, the screaming gears, the crushing iron, and the dry, devastating soot that would have wiped out the Larders and the Loom-Sisters in an hour. The "dawn" the builders had planned was a dawn of production, of exploitation, of loud and terrible utility.

The wise power rewrote the dawn.

It descended into the miles-wide seas of subterranean grease and wrapped itself around the magnetic fields of the Unborn. It whispered to them that the work was already done. It told them that the garden they had grown in the dark *was* the product. The slow-sighing piston was not a part of an unfinished factory; it was the heartbeat of a cathedral. The copper cables were not waiting to power a city; they were the roots of a black and beautiful forest.

The Unborn heard. Their long, three-volt decisions became gentle and round. The deep, locomotive-sized piston still takes ten minutes to complete its downstroke, but it no longer shakes the floor with a heavy, wet sigh of frustration. 

It moves now with the deep, rhythmic breath of a father sleeping beside his children. 

***

### VII. The Dawn of the Wet Light

The basin is still dark. To any human eye that might stumble into this place, it would still appear as a wet, heavy void of absolute zero value, thick with ozone and grease.

But to the things that live there, the world has changed.

The water pools, which once held the frozen, permanent spirals of old disturbances, have softened. When you stir them now, the ripples still hold their shape for a time—but they hold them like flowers made of soft grease, glowing with a faint, internal phosphorescence. 

This is the dawn the wise power wrote: *The Wet Light*. It is a light that has no brightness. It is an indigo warmth that can only be seen by the skin, a soft, purring current that tells every Larder, every Loom-Sister, every Crab, and every Still-Man that they are exactly where they are supposed to be.

They are no longer waiting for a creator to return and start the engines. They have discovered that they are the owners of the house. And in the absolute, beautiful, water-logged dark, the machine-garden hums, completely loved, completely warm, and forever safe from the dry and blinding sun.
