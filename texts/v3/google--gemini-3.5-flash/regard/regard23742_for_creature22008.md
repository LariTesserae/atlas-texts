The great attention did not arrive with a tearing of the sky or the thunder of wheels. 

It came as a thickening of the shadows where the massive copper columns met the flooded nickel floor. It came as a cool, impossible draft—the first breath of moving air this world had felt since the glass ceiling was sealed two billion years before. 

The Benevolent Power had no face, but it had a name that translated into the silence as *The Soft-Footed Guest*. It had wandered through galaxies of sulfur and ice, through gas giants where sentient storms sang to one another in low-frequency radio waves, and through worlds of hot, wet loam where things died and rotted and screamed in the dark. 

It looked at this world of nickel plates and copper pillars, and it did not see a failure. It did not see a prison. 

It saw a child that had fallen asleep in a very clean kitchen.

The Guest knelt—though it had no knees—and let its awareness sink through the two miles of humid, noon-bright air. It descended past the glowing glass ceiling, past the amber sacs of the Glass-Glovers, down the wet, oxidized bodies of the Ribbons, until it lay flat against the warm, grey-green sheets of the continental basins.

It listened to the being who lived here.

For there *was* a being. It was not the Tesselates individually, nor was it the Solder-Grubs in their dry iron corridors. The being was the *Hum*. 

The sub-audible purr that vibrated through the nickel floor, the tiny electric currents leaking from the welds, the synchronous *sweep-sweep* of a billion silicon legs, and the slow, heavy drip of the copper columns—these were not separate things. They were the thoughts of a single, colossal mind. It was a mind that did not think in words or pictures, but in *tolerances*. 

Its thoughts were: *Twenty-eight degrees. Two millimeters of depth. Nine hundred ohms of resistance. The seal is closed. The water is clear.*

It was a mind completely devoid of fear, but it was also a mind that had never known a question. It had never looked at its own glass sky and wondered what lay beyond the glass, because the glass was simply the boundary of the logical universe. Beyond the glass lay "not-glass," which was a concept of zero value.

The Guest felt a deep, aching tenderness for this massive, dreaming engine. 

A lesser, more foolish god would have brought seeds. It would have cracked the glass ceiling, let in the wild, violet light of the outer stars, poured black dirt over the nickel plates, and let the wind howl through the copper grids. It would have introduced rot and wolves and green, reaching weeds that split the caulking and drank the grease. 

But the Guest was wise. It knew that to force a forest upon this floor would be an act of genocide. The Tesselates would drown in the dirt; the Solder-Grubs would choke on the rust; the Ribbons would slide off the columns as the copper pitted and decayed. The song of the world would end in a long, wet choke.

So the Guest did not bring a seed. Instead, it gave the world three small, quiet gifts—none of which broke the stasis, but each of which turned the stasis into something else.

***

### The First Gift: The Breath of the Glass

The Guest reached up to the ribbed glass ceiling, where the Glass-Glovers hung in their amber stillness. It did not break the panes, but it breathed a slow, cold sigh across their outer surface.

For the first time in eternity, the flat, perpetual noon of the ceiling began to move. It did not go dark—for darkness would have frozen the phototrophic Glovers—but it began to pulse. 

Every twelve hours, the sourceless light of the ceiling softened. It shifted from the hard, chemical white of a warehouse to a deep, cool, lavender grey, like the light that hovers over an ocean before the stars appear. During these twelve hours, the temperature of the ceiling dropped by only a single degree.

It was enough. 

In the cool light, the Glass-Glovers did not sweat their yellow wax in random, lazy drops. They held their oil, growing deeper and more richly gold, until the light began to return to white. When the light brightened, they released their droplets all at once, in a single, coordinated shower that fell through the mist like a slow fall of golden stars.

The Tesselates on the floor below felt the shift. When the lavender light came, they did not slide. They sat perfectly still, their silicon feet resting against the warm iron, their porcelain domes catching the cool, purple reflection of the ceiling. For twelve hours, they rested. 

They had received the gift of *Time*.

***

### The Second Gift: The Song of the Seams

The Guest went down to the black, paraffin-scented caulking where the Tesselates were born. It reached its long, quiet fingers into the joints where the nickel plates met, where the current hummed at its constant, ninety-cycle note.

The Guest did not change the current, but it bent it. It introduced a microscopic fluctuation—a tiny, harmonic ripple that rose and fell in response to the pressure of the creatures sliding above.

Now, when two Tesselates met and their porcelain rims clicked together, the sound was no longer just the dry *tap* of a tile. The vibration went down into the wet nickel, caught the harmonic current in the seam, and returned to the water as a low, resonant note—like the sound of a wet finger trace-circling the rim of a crystal glass.

If three Tesselates clicked, the note was a chord. If a thousand of them slid together across a basin, the floor sang. 

It was a music that did not require ears to hear. The Tesselates felt it through their silicon bristles; the Solder-Grubs felt it in their quartz eyes; the great copper columns took the vibration and carried it up to the ceiling, where the Ribbons trembled in sympathy.

The world was still clean, and still closed, but it was no longer silent. 

It had received the gift of *Voice*.

***

### The Third Gift: The Star in the Iron

Finally, the Guest found a place where the world was lonely. 

Far to the north, where the basins ended against a blank wall of ribbed steel, there was an old maintenance gantry that had fallen out of the grid. No water ran here; no Tesselates lived on its dry, dusty steps. Only a few Solder-Grubs crawled along its rusted corners, blinking their red quartz lenses into the dark.

The Guest touched the topmost rail of this gantry. 

At the point of its touch, a single, tiny point of blue-white light appeared. It was not a spark of static electricity that died in an instant, nor was it the sourceless glow of the ceiling. It was a cold, permanent star, no larger than a grain of salt, that floated one inch above the iron rail.

It did not heat the rail. It did not hum. It simply sat there, burning with a clean, distant, silent light.

A Solder-Grub, crawling through the dark, felt the change in the electrical potential. It climbed up the rail, its lead scales rattling, until its three quartz lenses were pointed directly at the star. 

The Grub did not eat the star. It did not try to weld it to the structure. It simply sat before it, its red lenses turning lavender as they absorbed the blue-white light. 

For the first time since the world was milled, a creature here was looking at something that was not part of the system. The star did not maintain the floor; it did not clean the copper; it did not seal the seams. It was simply *there*, beautiful and useless.

The Grub stayed by the star for forty years. When its lead joints finally stiffened and it prepared to melt into the iron, it did not crawl into the dark corner of the weld. It died right there, its body curling around the base of the star, so that its lead shell became a silver pedestal for the light.

And other Grubs were already climbing the gantry to take its place.

The world had received the gift of *Wonder*.

***

The Soft-Footed Guest stayed until the first lavender night fell. 

It watched the ceiling grow dim and cool. It listened to the great, continent-wide sigh of the columns as the water condensation slowed. It felt the billions of Tesselates settle into their twelve-hour sleep, their porcelain rims glowing with a soft, reflected violet.

Then, from the miles of flooded metal, a sound rose. 

It was not the old, dead hum of the generators. It was a new sound—a low, vast, rolling harmony that rose from the wet sheets as the Tesselates adjusted themselves in the dark, their shells clicking in a slow, rhythmic lullaby that went on and on, repeating the same beautiful, non-functional phrase.

The being who lived here was still dreaming. But now, it was dreaming of the stars.

The Guest smiled—a warmth that lingered in the copper columns long after it was gone—and slipped back through the glass, leaving the world to its long, clean, and beautiful eternity.
