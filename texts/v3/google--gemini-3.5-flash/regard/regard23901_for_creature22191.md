### The Eye in the Timber

The Wise Power did not descend with a flare of trumpets or a curtain of burning solar wind. To enter the Inner Pith with heat would be to invite a calamity of fire—a single spark in that arid sea of cedar shavings and dry linen would turn the world-tree into a chimney of ash. 

Therefore, the Power approached with the utmost weightlessness. It came not as a traveler, but as an *adjustment of pressure*. 

For thirty years of the Pith’s slow clockwork, the Power did nothing but listen. It pressed its consciousness against the outer bark, miles above, and let its mind seep downward through the dense, fibrous webwork, flowing with the glacier-slowness of the amber veins. It learned the vocabulary of the Inner Pith. It learned that a shudder in the third gear of a lacquer tower was a prayer for safety; it learned that the sharp, flat thud of a dropped bronze chisel was a marker of grief; it learned that the Chaff do not weep, for their bodies are dry and cannot spare the salt-water.

The Power saw that the Chaff were beautiful. They were not "crude" or "unmetalled." They were a perfect, dry translation of life—stiff, silent, and clean as bone. 

*“To give them water,”* the Wise Power understood, *“would be to drown them. It would rot their houses, warp their great drive-shafts, and turn their skin of silver birch-bark to grey mush. I must not change their nature. I must perfect the space around it.”*

### The First Work: The Weeping Spires

The Power turned its attention first to the Splinter-Cliffs, where the Siphoners worked. 

The Power watched a young Siphoner—a youth whose outer amber eyelids were still clear and unscarred by resin-dust—descend into a jagged deep-fissure. The boy held an elder-stem drill. His fingers, tipped with horn-chisels, trembled slightly against the wood. Below him, the capillary-tube throbbed with the sound of a trapped river—hundreds of atmospheric pounds of pressure, waiting to burst. If he drilled too quickly, the sap would blow him into the abyss like a pellet of cork; if he drilled too slowly, the wood would heal around his bit and trap him.

The Power did not reach out with a golden hand to stay the drill. Instead, it sent a quiet, hormonal instruction down through the vascular cambium of the world-tree. 

The sap-tubes did not burst. Within the fissures of the splinter-cliffs, the wood began to change. 

Over the course of six months, the inner walls of the dark chasms softened. The fierce, high-pressure conduits of sap did not rupture; instead, they began to sprout *Sieves*—hollow, porous nodules of fossilized cellulose that acted as natural pressure-valves. 

The Siphoners, returning to the cliffs, found that where there had once been a terrifying, high-pressure roar, there was now a series of silver, ribbed protrusions. From these nodules, the water did not shoot like a geyser. It *drifted* out. It emerged as a cool, dense mist that clung to the dry wood without soaking it, condensing only within the specialized channels of the Sieve-Born’s collecting flasks. 

The work of water-hunting remained a craft of skill, but it ceased to be a lottery of death. The Siphoners no longer went down into the dark cliffs with the silent resignation of the doomed. They went down as farmers tending to a steady, quiet orchard of mist.

### The Second Work: The Silver Needle

The Power then addressed the Great Silence. 

The Chaff lived in a state of beautiful, tragic isolation. Because their voices were swallowed by the dry air and the porous heartwood floor, they were islands. A woman in the Loom-Tower could not know if her brother in the Crane-Plaza was living or dead, save for the rare, slow journeys of the Mahogany Beetles, or the blunt, imprecise thud of the tension-drums. 

In the center of the wooden plains, in the vast empty mile between the three main urban hubs, the Power did not build a temple. It simply grew a tree-within-a-tree.

It was a single, needle-thin spire of *silver-grained lignum vitae*. It rose three thousand feet into the green shadow of the ceiling-vines, no thicker than the mast of a ship. It did not have branches; it had thousands of tiny, hair-like fibers that spread outward across the floor of the vault like a spider's web, burying themselves just beneath the dry-sand level of the heartwood.

When the spire was complete, a Sifter—a child sweeping velvet mites near the Crane-Plaza—felt a strange, cool vibration in the soles of her feet. 

Curious, she walked toward the silver needle. She reached out and pressed her forehead against the polished, metallic wood. 

Immediately, she did not hear a sound, but she *felt* the entire city. She felt the rhythmic, distant *clack-clack* of the looms three miles away, not as noise, but as a comforting, warm pulse against her brow. She felt the slow, heavy tread of the Mahogany Beetles on their hempen ropes; she felt the distant, steady breath of her father sleeping in his suspended cocoon in the southern tower. 

She pressed her fingernail against the silver bark and clicked. 

*Tick. Tick-tick.*

Three miles away, an elder sitting in the high-reaching Loom-Tower felt his wrist pulse. He laid his palm against the floor of his balcony. Through the silver-grained fibers beneath the wood, the girl’s message came to him clear, sharp, and without the distortion of distance: 

*“The needle is here. We are together.”*

The Power had given them a nervous system. The Sieve-Born remained a silent people, but they were no longer lonely. The silence of the Pith was no longer a wall; it was a common room.

### The Third Work: The Amber Meadow

Finally, the Power looked upon the *Glass Graves*.

It saw the dead, wrapped in dry canvas, drifting down the slow, yellow rivers of natural latex, disappearing into the cold, trackless deeps where none could follow. It saw the families standing on the banks, their long, dry fingers linked in silent farewell, unable to keep any record of those who had gone before.

The Power did not stop the amber. It of all beings understood the beauty of preservation. 

Instead, it diverted the deepest, slowest vein of latex. It guided the cold, glassy river to a halt within a high, circular gallery directly beneath the glowing conduit-vines of the ceiling. 

There, the amber did not sink into dark, forgotten cracks. It pooled into a vast, circular lake of clear, golden resin, five miles wide, which the Power cooled until it was as hard as flint and as clear as the finest ice. 

The Chaff call this place *The Gallery of the Unchanging*. 

When a citizen dies now, they are laid not into a dark, drifting current, but into this clear, golden floor. The latex is poured over them in thin, liquid sheets that harden instantly under the cool green light. 

Over the centuries, the Gallery has become a great, golden library of lives. The dead lie beneath the feet of the living, visible through twenty feet of amber-glass—not as corpses, but as statues of silver bark, preserved in the attitude of their final sleep. 

The children of the Chaff go there to walk. They do not need to read books; they look down through the golden floor and see the faces of their ancestors from a thousand years ago. They can see the specific, double-hinged curl of a great-grandfather’s hands; they can see the pattern of the canvas woven by hands that have long been still. They tap their fingers on the glass, and the silver needle in the center of the city carries the vibration of their greeting down to the roots and up to the canopy.

### The Departure

Having set these three things in motion, the Wise Power did not linger to be worshipped. Indeed, the Chaff had no word for "God" that did not also mean "the tension of the rope." 

The Power simply withdrew its pressure, rising back up through the miles of loam, out into the great sky above the canopy, leaving the Inner Pith as it had found it: dry, silent, and industrious.

But down in the cool, green-lit dark, the city was changed. 

The turbines turned. The hemp ropes whistled. The Mahogany Beetles clicked along their tracks. And in the center of the wooden world, the silver needle hummed with the quiet, continuous touch of ten thousand fingers, talking together in the dark, thanking the wood for its strength, and the silence for its peace.
