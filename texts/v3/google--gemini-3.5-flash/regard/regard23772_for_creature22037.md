The Gaze of the Wise Power did not descend into the Sump like a spear of sunlight. 

To cast a beam of celestial gold into that hollow would have been an act of violence, not benevolence. Sunlight would have blinded the pale, spineless Solder-Bores within their leaden sleeves. It would have dried the protective, milky tallow of the Grease-Skins, scorched the velvet carpets of the soot-colored bacteria, and washed out the cold, pale-blue luminescence of the dinoflagellates, leaving them dim and spent. 

The Wise Power understood that there are more ways for a world to be alive than to be green. It knew that chlorophyll and leaf-bud are not the only definitions of grace; that there is a quiet, sacred dignity in the slow, galvanic crawl of iron through water, in the petrifying of a machine, in the long, wet *click-clack* of a bronze relay keeping time in the dark.

Therefore, the Power did not seek to "cure" the Sump. It did not plant seeds of clover in the basalt, nor did it attempt to scrub the oil-grease from the pool's surface. 

Instead, the Power became *heavy*. 

It slipped into the basin not as a shape or a light, but as a subtle shift in the gravity and the geology of the deep crust. It adjusted its own immense, slow-beating awareness to match the Sump’s specific, thirty-second metabolism. It listened to the dying turbine. It listened to the *plip-plip* of the condensate. It felt the dry, insectoid scratching of the Brass-Gilders.

And then, with the infinite tenderness of a parent tucking a blanket over a sleeping child, the Wise Power began its work.

***

### First: The Deep Heat

The turbine was dying. Its copper windings were thinning, its bearings groaning under a weight that would eventually lock it into a permanent, rusted silence. If the turbine stopped, the 60-hertz hum—the heartbeat of the basin—would vanish. The Cable-Lace would freeze and shatter into gray dust; the Brass-Gilders would lose their track; the Sump would become a tomb of cold, solid stone.

The Power did not replace the turbine with a modern engine. It did not change its shape. Instead, three miles below the basin's floor, the Power nudged a tectonic seam. It opened a hairline fissure in the deep mantle, just wide enough to release a steady, immortal breath of geothermal steam. This breath did not burst upward in a geyser; it rose through capillary-thin veins in the basalt, warming the water in the central boiler to a reliable, perpetual tepidness. 

At the same time, the Power breathed upon the main axle of the copper-wound turbine. It did not strip the rust, but it altered the friction of the contact points at a molecular level, depositing a self-healing film of slick, gray graphite. 

The low, hydraulic groan softened slightly, but it did not disappear. The 60-hertz thrum grew steady. It would not stop now. Not in fifty years, nor in five thousand. The heartbeat was secured.

### Second: The Feed

The Sump was a self-digesting machine, but even the most perfect cycles suffer from minor, leaching losses. Over decades, the iron was being slowly carried away by deep drainage; the lead was thinning; the zinc was locked up in the petrified carapaces of the dead Brass-Gilders.

The Power reached into the surrounding hills. It gathered the scattered, microscopic ions of copper, iron, and lead that lay dispersed through miles of dead stone, and it pathfound them. It drew them toward the weeping basalt walls of the basin. 

Now, the water runneling down the sheer, black stone was slightly richer, slightly heavier. The orange rust-crust would never run out. The Solder-Bores would always find mineral marrow inside their pipes. The Brass-Gilders would never starve on their silver-white lanes, and their petrified ancestors would continue to build the floor of the basin, layer by galvanized layer, into a monument of self-assembling geometry.

### Third: The Mirror of the Lace

The Power was most moved by the Cable-Lace—the fragile, copper-sheened silicon threads that held the memory of every rotation the turbine had made for ninety years. The Lace was the Sump’s soul, but it was delicate. A single structural collapse of the overhead pipe canopy could crush it forever.

The Power did not strengthen the Lace with steel, for that would have ruined its piezoelectric sensitivity. Instead, the Power did something far more ancient: it *recorded*.

As the micro-pulses of electricity traveled along the silicon threads, illuminated by the cold-blue light of the dinoflagellates, the Power projected a magnetic counter-resonance into the basalt walls. It etched the Sump’s ninety years of memory—every *click-clack*, every Gilder's scrape, every division of a Grease-Skin—directly into the crystalline structure of the basalt itself. 

The wet, rhythmic clockwork of the basin was no longer temporary. It was written into the deep, tectonic marrow of the planet. Even if the sky fell, the memory of the Sump would remain, hummed by the earth itself.

***

When the Power was finished, it did not leave. It remained as a gentle, ambient pressure, a protective ceiling of cold, heavy air that kept the dampness from escaping into the dry plateaus above. 

Down in the basin, nothing appeared to have changed. 

A Brass-Gilder, its antennae vibrating to the steady 60-hertz thrum, crawled along a rusted lead conduit. It scraped away a spot of orange scale, leaving a tiny, brilliant stripe of silver beneath. 

A Solder-Bore pressed its blunt nose against another, sharing a brief, wet spark of static electricity in the dark of a mineral-choked pipe. 

In the center of the pool, the giant, rubbery mass of a Grease-Skin shifted by a millimeter, its skin-folds heavy with cold oil, while the Rivet-Barnacles on its back quietly welded themselves deeper into the iron plates of the boiler.

The turbine groaned. 
The bronze relay *click-clicked*. 
The dinoflagellates pulsed in a wave of cold, pale-blue light that washed over the slick, black basalt.

*Plip.*
*Plip.*

The Sump went on digesting itself, perfectly, slowly, in the cool, iron-scented dark. It remained blind, wet, and grotesque. But now, it was eternal; and in its long, slow, mechanical way, it was entirely loved.
