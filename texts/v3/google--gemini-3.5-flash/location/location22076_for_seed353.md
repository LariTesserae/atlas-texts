The air is thin, searingly hot, and smells of baked lignin, turpentine, and dry friction. 

We are on a high, parched plateau, though there is no stone beneath our feet to define the elevation. The ground is a compressed, iron-hard plain of lignified fiber—miles of fused, ancient heartwood that has been planed smooth by eons of dry, grit-laden winds. There is no sand here, nor soil, nor water. If there ever was moisture, it was driven out eons ago, replaced by a non-aqueous chemistry of heavy resins, high-temperature vegetable oils, and dry, powdery graphite that lubricates the endless interfaces of this world.

It is near-total darkness. The sky above is not a sky but a dense, vaulted ceiling of interlacing black branches, miles thick, that absorbs what little light might filter from above. The only illumination is the rare, dull-orange glimmer of friction-glow—where two massive wooden shafts grind against one another under immense pressure—and the faint, bio-chemical phosphorescence of hot, slow-dripping pitch.

This is an ecosystem of pure, absolute vegetation, but it is a vegetation that has forgotten the wild, chaotic shapes of the wilderness. It has organized itself into an industrial complex. 

Every structure, every tool, and every open space is built. The forest does not grow; it constructs. Towering hollow trunks, square-profiled and reinforced with internal ribbing like cargo hulls, rise from the floor to meet the ceiling. These are the distillation towers. Inside them, the scorching heat of the deep plateau boils volatile organic compounds out of fossilized timber, sending dry gases whistling through miles of hollow reedpipe conduits. 

The landscape is packed to the point of suffocation. There is no empty space. Where there is not a vertical trunk-housing, there is a highway of horizontal runner-vines. These are not simple plants but braided, high-tension fiber belts, spinning silently on polished wooden rollers that sprout from the joints of massive elder-trees. The belts carry dry, hard seed-capsules—each the size of a barrel, hexagonal and uniform—from the nursery-grids to the crushing-zones.

The silence is heavy, muffled by the sheer mass of carbonized fiber that surrounds us. The sounds are low-frequency, seismic, and mechanical: the rhythmic *shhh-shhh* of fiber belts sliding over dry pine-oil glides; the deep, resonant *clonk* of wooden valves opening and closing inside the heartwood engines; the dry sighing of nitrogen gas purging through miles of fibrous capillaries. Now and then, a sharp, gunshot-like *crack* echoes through the darkness—the sound of a structural timber splitting under the thermal stress of the searing heat, only to be instantly bound and mended by a fast-growing, self-healing weep of black amber.

Despite the lack of water and animal life, the plateau is in a state of hyperactive, turbulent change. It is a slow-motion storm of construction and demolition. The plants do not grow toward a soft, final equilibrium; they behave like a factory running at maximum, unstable capacity. 

In one sector of the valley, a massive battery of piston-gourds—leathery, heat-swelled chambers three stories tall—is systematically crushing dry lignite deposits, using the energy to drive great wooden drill-screws deeper into the dry crust. Nearby, an entire mountain of interlocking, square-cut timber vaults is undergoing a controlled collapse. The old, worn-out structures are dry-rotting in minutes, decomposed not by fungi but by targeted, acidic sap-flows released by the parent root-system. Before the dust can even settle, a carpet of thick, pale shoots is already thrusting upward through the ruins, weaving themselves into a new, more efficient configuration of conduits and shafts.

This is a world that has reached its final, primordial stability by becoming an engine. It is ancient, dried-out, polished to a satin sheen by wind and wood-dust, yet it is violently alive. It works in the dark, hot and tireless, building and rebuilding itself out of its own carbon, driven by a mindless, mechanical intent that needs neither eyes to see nor water to flow.
