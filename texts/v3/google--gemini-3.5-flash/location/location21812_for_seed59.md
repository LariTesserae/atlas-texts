The place is a vertical rib in the world—a steep, high-elevation mountain cleft known as the Great Weave of Kael-Sunder. It is not large; one can crawl from the foaming pool at the bottom to the windy rim at the top in half an afternoon, but every square inch of this limestone fissure is choked, packed, and vibrant with life. 

The air is cold, smelling of crushed mint, wet peat, and the sharp alpine chill of high altitudes. The light here is bright and clean, reflecting off thousands of wet, shaking leaves, though it is perpetually softened by a fine, drifting spray that hangs in the air like a cold steam. 

### The Substrate of Living Green

There is no bare rock. The walls of the gorge, once limestone, have been entirely swallowed by a moss so ancient and dense it has turned into a form of soft, green masonry. This is *iron-moss*, three feet deep and as springy as wool underfoot, which absorbs the constant mountain rain like a sponge and slowly releases it in thousands of weeping, miniature falls. 

Out of this thick green skin grow giant *pipe-reeds*, as thick as a man’s thigh, whose yellow-green stalks curve upward toward the light. Terraces have formed over centuries where fallen logs have wedged across the canyon; these are now completely overgrown with dense, interlocking mats of wild ginger and broad-leafed ferns, creating flat, suspended floors that span the chasm like green suspension bridges. Everything is built upon something else that is growing, decaying, or rooting.

### The Crammed, Clattering Hive

This single gorge is a city, though it looks more like a colony of weaver-birds. Because there is so little flat ground, the inhabitants—who are short-statured, thick-jointed, and wear heavy capes woven from oiled nettle-fibres—live in three-dimensional density. 

Their dwellings are *spindle-nests*: teardrop-shaped spheres woven from the living stalks of weeping willows and bound with waterproof pine-pitch. These nests hang in clusters from the underside of the natural log-bridges or are tucked into dry hollows carved directly into the ancient leather-moss walls. To move from one neighborhood to another, one does not walk down a street; one climbs up a swaying ladder of braided hemp, swings across a narrow gap on a fat, mossy vine, or steps carefully along the slippery edge of a hollowed-out birch aqueduct.

Everything is tight, near-touching, and dynamic. In the open central vertical shaft of the canyon, things are constantly in motion. Basket-pulleys made of split willow roar up and down on thick ropes, carrying wet tubers, freshwater crabs, and heavy bundles of dry firewood harvested from the pine-scrub above the rim. Children, agile and bare-toed, scurry along the guide-ropes with the ease of squirrels, carrying messages from the upper smoke-houses to the lower wet-gardens.

### Water in Motion

Water is the great architect and the endless tenant of the gorge. It does not merely fall; it seeps, drips, gushes, and sings. At the top of the cleft, a glacial stream plunges over the rim, but before it can hit the bottom, its force is broken by a series of hand-built wooden baffles—split spruce logs arranged in a staircase of sluices. 

The water is redirected through a maze of hollowed-elderberry pipes, feeding the terraced gardens where watercress and bitter-root are grown in shallow, gravelly pockets. Every dwelling has its own small, dripping spout of clean, ice-cold water spilling from a leaf-funnel outside its door. The sound is an omnipresent, rhythmic roar: the deep, bass thrum of the main falls below, the clapping of the wooden aqueducts, and the infinite, musical *tink-tink-tink* of high-altitude condensation dripping from millions of pointed leaf-tips.

### The Din of the Wilderness

If the water is the rhythm, the fauna is the melody—and it is deafening. 

Because the gorge is a sheltered, wet shelter in an otherwise dry, windy mountain range, it draws every small creature for leagues around. The dense vegetative walls are alive with *clack-beetles*, the size of fists, whose iridescent green shells click rhythmically as they harvest the moss spores. In the late afternoon, thousands of *mist-swallows* return to their nests in the high rock crevices, their nesting calls creating a shrill, swirling whistle that echoes off the wet walls.

In the pools at the bottom of the cleft, giant orange-bellied salamanders, four feet long, slide lazily between the logs, their low, grunting croaks vibrating through the wooden walkways. Every surface is shared. A weaver working their wooden loom must constantly brush away the small, yellow-striped *sap-frogs* that leap toward the moisture of their hands. The air is never still; it is thick with the lazy hum of woolly mountain bees and the quick, metallic darting of dragonflies with wings like stained glass.

### Primitive Industry

The technology of the Weave is clever but entirely manual, born of the materials at hand. Metal is rare and precious—a single iron splitting-wedge might be passed down through three generations as a family's greatest treasure. Instead, tools are made of split flint, river-worn granite, and flame-hardened ash wood.

For illumination, they do not use oil—which is scarce—but rather wire-sided lanterns stuffed with *glow-moss*, which feeds on the damp air and emits a cool, pale-green light that permits weaving and carving through the mountain nights. 

There is an intense, communal bustle here. Because everything is built of green wood and woven reed, it is always decaying in the damp air. The city must be constantly rebuilt. One crew is always replacing the rotten rungs of a high ladder; another is plastering the wind-ward side of a spindle-nest with fresh grey river-mud to seal out the drafts. There is no privacy, but there is no isolation either. Every call, every laugh, every chip of a stone adze on birchwood contributes to the thick, wild hum of a community that has found its balance within the roaring green throat of the mountain.
