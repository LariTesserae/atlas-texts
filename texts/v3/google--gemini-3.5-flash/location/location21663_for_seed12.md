In this place, the horizon does not exist. There is only the *Thick*, a warm, perpetual sky-sea suspended high above an invisible floor. It is not air, and it is not the deep, cold ocean of a seabed; it is an elevated, planetary envelope of dense, brackish water, kept at a constant, blood-warm temperature by the volcanic thermal plumes rising from below. 

Through this emerald twilight rise the spires.

### The Raw Stone

The geology of this world has no history of wear. The spires are colossal, hexagonal pillars of dark, crystalline basalt and raw, obsidian-slick ironstone. They have no rounded edges, no pebble-strewn beaches, and no sedimentary slopes. They are sharp, blocky, and geometric—crystalline structures that feel as though they were pushed out of a planetary kiln only yesterday. Their surfaces are sheer and glassy, occasionally fracturing into razor-sharp steps that climb vertically into the wet gloom. Because there has been no wind erosion and no dry frost to cleave them, the stone remains in its embryonic state: monumental, black, and perfectly defined.

### The Floating Town: *Kael-Tide*

Between and around these massive black columns, a dense human settlement clings to the stone. This is not a scattering of rafts, but an organized, stacked city of basket-work and bone. 

Because the basalt is too hard for primitive tools of flint and bone to carve, the inhabitants build outward instead of inward. They have constructed thousands of spherical, bulbous dwellings woven from water-bamboo and the thick, fibrous ribs of giant sea-lilies. These "pods" are lashed directly to the sheer stone faces with heavy, resin-coated ropes of braided hemp. 

The pods are layered like grapes. One family’s floor is another’s curved ceiling. Long, swaying foot-bridges made of buoyed timber and creeping vines stretch across the gaps between the spires, bridging distances of eighty or a hundred yards. Because of the water’s buoyancy, these bridges do not sag downward; instead, they float slightly upward, curving toward the surface like inverted arches.

The technology here is physical, manual, and quiet. There are no metals, save for rare, unworked chunks of native copper used as plumb-bobs or anchor weights. Tools are fashioned from the hard, enamel-like teeth of the rare deep-gliders, or from the razor-sharp basalt flakes that chip off the spires during rare seismic tremors. To cook, the inhabitants descend to the lower reaches of the spires where thermal vents bubble up. They truss their food—mostly starchy kelp-tubers and small, soft-shelled bivalves—into clay pots wrapped in wet netting, lowering them into the boiling sulfurous streams until they are soft.

### The Vegetation

The water is a soup of life, but it is botanical, not animal. Broad-leaved kelp forestation fills the voids between the basalt pillars. These are not thin weeds, but massive, trunk-like structures—bladder-woods—that grow straight up toward the dim source of light. Their leaves are wide, oily plates of amber and yellow-green that float horizontally, forming organic roofs that filter the already dim light into a soft, mossy green darkness.

Every surface of the raw basalt that is not cleared by human hands is covered in a thick, spongy carpet of pale blue-green bryophytes. These mosses do not need soil; they feed directly on the mineral-rich water, secreting a mild acid that slowly, invisibly begins the multi-millennial process of breaking down the glass-sharp stone.

### The Light and Sound

The light is a constant, changeless dusk. The sun is nothing more than a pale, milky smudge that drifts across the upper ceiling of the water-mass once every thirty hours, barely distinguishable from the bioluminescent glow of the deep-water algae. There are no shadows, only gradients of bottle-green and charcoal-grey.

The sounds of the town are muffled, carrying the peculiar, bass-heavy acoustics of an underwater chamber. The sharp *clink* of a flint tool against basalt travels for miles, a tiny, metallic chime that registers in the teeth of those who hear it. The human voice does not carry well; it is swallowed by the wet density of the air-water, so the citizens speak in a low, click-heavy dialect of sibilants and throat-tones that cut through the fluid medium. Mostly, there is only the slow, rhythmic *creak-creak* of thousands of woven hemp ropes chafing against the wet basalt as the city settles in the minimal currents.

### The Slow Dynamics

Nothing templates speed here. To move from one spire to another is a deliberate, swimming crawl, using hand-piles and guide-ropes. If someone falls from a bridge, they do not plunge; they drift downward, falling at the speed of a settling leaf, until they can catch a lower vine or inflate a bladder of gas harvested from the kelp-forests to lift themselves back up.

The only change is the slow, seasonal breathing of the vents below. Every few months, the water temperature rises by a degree or two. The kelp-forests swell, their bladders turning from dark green to a bright, translucent gold, and the town quietens even further, entering a collective, languid hibernation until the water cools back to its steady, blood-warm baseline. In this high, wet cradle of stone, time is measured not by days, but by the slow, silent growth of the moss on the un-worn rocks.
