The mountains do not possess the rounded, ancient shoulders of old land; they are young, hostile, and sharp. They rise like shattered teeth into a thin, violet-blue sky, their edges so crisp they seem to slice the very wind that howls about them. This is a geological youth writ vast—miles of sheer granite, basalt needles, and slate cliffs that have not yet been softened by the patient hands of rain and epochal rot. Talus slopes of raw, razor-sharp gravel spill down into vertical abysses, their grey-black stones still smelling of the deep earth from which they were violently thrust.

Upon these raw, soaring peaks climbs a city of impossible density and primitive weight. 

It is not a city of iron or glass, but of stone, bone, and grease. Thousands upon thousands of low, flat-roofed dwellings are stacked directly upon one another, clung to the vertical faces of the range like a crust of mountain-scale barnacles. Every flat surface is a roof for the home below and a terrace for the home above. The construction is dry-stone masonry, heavy blocks of slate and dark diorite fitted together with agonizing precision, their gaps stuffed with mountain moss and frozen mud. Heavy beams of gray, slow-growing pine—carried up from distant valleys so long ago they have turned to the hardness of iron—shore up the overhanging floors. 

Because space is a luxury of the flat world, the houses are packed so closely that their eaves touch, creating narrow, tunnel-like crevices that serve as streets. These passages climb the peaks in dizzying staircases, chipped directly into the living granite by hand-held stone chisels. The city is a vertical labyrinth that spans not merely a hillside, but three entire mountain summits and the serrated ridges that connect them, a continental settlement clinging to the edge of the sky.

The air here is bitterly, dryly cold, a permanent frost that settles deep into the marrow of the stone. Yet, it is a damp environment in its own quiet way. Water does not flow in rivers; it seeps. It is found in the greasy condensation that forms on the dark slate walls of the lower alleys where the cloud-deck occasionally drifts through the streets. It is found in the blue, diamond-hard seeps of ice that choke the deep rock chimneys, which the inhabitants block-mine with horn-headed picks. Every roof is tilted slightly inward to channel the rare, dry snowfall and the heavy, dripping mist of the mornings into carved stone cisterns. The water is heavy with rock-dust, cold enough to crack teeth, and smelling of deep earth.

Vegetation is a sparse, stubborn afterthought. There are no forests here, nor fields of grain. In the cracks of the masonry and the sheltered shadows of the stone terraces, gray-green lichens sprout like scabs. A withered species of alpine saxifrage, with leaves like tiny leather cups designed to catch the frost, clings to the north-facing walls. Here and there, in tubs of hand-carried valley soil, grow stunted, twisted shrubs with needles of deep blue, kept alive only for the oily berries they produce once every three years.

Yet, despite the barren stone and the freeze, the mountains crawl with life. 

The city is a massive, crowded hive of beasts and people, living in a dense, shared warmth. Fat, long-haired mountain goats with horns curved like scimitars stand on the stone roofs, their hooves clicking rhythmically against the slate as they graze on the lichens. Packs of low, heavy-bodied dogs with woolly white coats sleep in the doorways, their breath rising in thick, rhythmic plumes of steam. In the narrow gaps between the stone tiers, great condor-like birds with iridescent black plumage nest in stone niches; they are so accustomed to the human presence that they pluck strips of dried meat directly from the drying-frames on the terraces. Down in the darker, lower alleys, herds of shaggy, humped-back oxen are stalled in dry-stone vaults, their bodies radiating a thick, musky heat that is the primary source of warmth for the families who live directly above them.

Everything is marked by a deep, manual simplicity. There are no wheels; the steep stairways and perpendicular drops make them useless. Goods are moved by human backs or the broad, scarred shoulders of pack-beasts. The tools of this place are made of dark green jade, chipped flint, hard river-pebble hammers, and the heavy thigh-bones of the great mountain birds, bound to ash-handles with strips of wet hide that have dried to iron-like tension. Ropes are woven from the coarse, greasy hair of the mountain goats, smelling forever of wet wool and wood-smoke.

For all its density, the city is a quiet place. The thin air doesn't carry sound well, and the cold encourages a quiet conservation of breath. The loudest sounds are the dry *clink-clink* of a mason’s stone mallet far up the ridge, the deep, hollow grunt of a stalled ox, and the constant, soft hiss of the dry wind brushing over millions of stone shingles. People move slowly, with the rhythmic, plodding stride of those who live where oxygen is a scarce commodity. There is no rush; time seems to stretch and freeze like the ice in the crevices. The seasonal changes are minimal—only the shift from a bright, blinding summer glare on the high glaciers to the long, blue-shadowed dark of the high-altitude winter.

At midday, under a sun that is brilliant but offers no warmth, the city looks like a colossal, multi-tiered carving of the mountain itself. The slate roofs glisten with a thin sheen of melting frost, and thousands of small, grease-lamp fires send thin, horizontal threads of blue smoke out into the cold, still air. It is a monument of immense, slow labor—a place where humanity and beast have huddled together in the cracks of a young and giant earth, refusing to fall.
