The world is an ocean suspended where a stratosphere should be: a planetary-scale shelf of cold, emerald-tinted water held aloft by a structure that is entirely alive. 

There is no stone here, no silt, no clay. Every solid thing is cellulose. The landscape is a network of colossal, water-logged botanical conduits, some as wide as continents, branching through a crystalline abyss. This is the Siphon-Swell, a high-altitude sea of fresh, glacial-cold water that never warms, never freezes, and never falls.

Space here is vast, silent in its distances yet deafeningly loud at its nexuses. Because the density of the environment is sparse, one can swim or drift for leagues through empty, sunlit water before encountering any sign of the vegetative scaffold. But when a conduit does appear, it is monumental. These are the Great Piston-Cedars—smooth, green-black pillars of petrified fiber that rise from invisible depths below and pierce the ceiling of the world. 

The tech of this wilderness is entirely organic, yet its function is ruthlessly industrial. 

Within the hollow cores of these massive trunks, giant wooden pistons, driven by some deep-well geothermal pressure, slide up and down in a perpetual, microsecond-perfect cycle. They do not grow; they do not decay. They simply pump. The sound is a low, rhythmic thrumming that vibrates through the water for hundreds of miles—a deep, metallic *thump-hiss* as millions of gallons of water are compressed and forced through natural valves into the lateral branches. 

Every branch is a pipeline, its bark polished by millenia of rushing water to a glass-like hardness. At the junctions where these branches meet, the vegetation swells into colossal, bulbous refinery-nodes. These nodes operate like chemical factories, their internal chambers lined with bioluminescent yeast-skins that glow with a steady, cold green light. Here, water is desalted, de-oxygenated, or enriched with thick, sugary sap before being sent along its designated channel. The nodes hum with the high-pitched spin of wooden turbines, driven by the sheer velocity of the internal currents. 

The light never changes. It is a perpetual, crisp morning. The sun, locked at a sharp, angled zenith, casts long, hard shafts of brightness through the cold water. These beams are populated by slow-drifting, perfectly spherical green spores that never settle, suspended forever in the water's neutral buoyancy. There is no weather inside this ocean, only the artificial currents generated by the pumping plants. There are no seasons, no tides, and no night. The system exists in a frozen, time-invariant equilibrium. A leaf that falls from a rare, high-pressure ventilation spray is instantly shredded by organic filtration fans, its compounds chemically harvested and reallocated to the node walls within minutes, leaving the water as sterile and clear as melted ice.

Because the world is so empty between its living towers, the fauna has adapted to the industrial layout of the plants. They do not hunt or roam so much as they maintain. 

Translucent, glass-shelled crustaceans, the size of carriage houses, crawl along the exteriors of the pipelines on jointed, chitinous legs. Their movements are rhythmic and precise. They scrape away the microscopic algae that threatens to clog the external intake pores, their pincers clicking in a ceaseless, synchronized static that fills the water column. They do not mate or age; they are budded from the bark of the refinery-nodes fully formed, programmed with the singular task of maintenance.

Deep in the blue voids between the pipelines, giant, bladder-like leviathans float motionless. These creatures are entirely blind, their bodies consisting of little more than a vast, sail-like membrane of tough, leafy fiber and a series of internal chambers. They act as passive storage vessels, absorbing excess pressure from the pumping conduits and releasing it back into the system during local fluctuations. They hum at a frequency that matches the Piston-Cedars, a long, low, drone that sounds like a sustained organ note vibrating through an empty cathedral.

There is a strange, clean smell to this world—the scent of pine needles submerged in ozone, combined with the sharp, metallic tang of iron-rich water. Water flows through the heart of the plants, and the plants breathe the water, and the animals clean the skin of both. Nothing is born that was not already planned by the network; nothing dies that is not immediately reclaimed by the filters. It is a pristine, cold machine of leaf and fiber, thrumming endlessly in the high, bright quiet.
