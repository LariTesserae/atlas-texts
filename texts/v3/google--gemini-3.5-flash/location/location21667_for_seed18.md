This is the interior of the Great Conduit, Segment Eighty-Two.

It is a vertical world, a singular chimney of monumental proportions but narrow width. If you were to stand upon the iron grating of the maintenance catwalk, the space would feel no wider than a small parish church—perhaps thirty meters from wall to wall. Yet, if you look straight up or straight down, the boundary disappears entirely into a sheer, dizzying void. There is no visible sky and no floor, only a vertical drop of several kilometers, illuminated by a pale, pearlescent daylight that filters down from some distant, obscured skylight far above.

The density of this place is zero. It is an emptiness defined strictly by its margins. There are no floating islands, no mid-air bridges, no cross-beams to break the fall. The center of the conduit is a vast column of nothing but humid air. The only things occupying this central gulf are three thick steel cables, greasy and black, that hang perfectly plumb through the center of the shaft, vanishing into the gray mist both above and below. 

Everything here is constructed. Every square inch of the boundary wall is a monument to industrial masonry and heavy metallurgy. There is no granite, no sandstone, no native earth. Instead, the walls are made of massive, interlocking plates of cast iron, bolted together with rivets the size of dinner plates, and banded with great structural ribs of green-patinated bronze. 

But this is not a dry, sterile engine. It is a warm, weeping greenhouse. 

Steam, vented from some unfatigued boilers leagues below, rises constantly through the shaft, clingy and humid. The temperature is that of a warm summer night in a swamp—heavy, rich, and smelling faintly of wet coal-ash, cedar oil, and lubricating grease. This steam condenses continuously against the cold iron walls, creating a perpetual weeping. Fine streams of water trickle down the rivets, collecting in the crevices between plates, dripping from the undersides of the maintenance galleries in a slow, rhythmic rain.

Where there is moisture, warmth, and light, life has taken hold with a terrifying, silent vigor. 

The walls are not black or rust-brown; they are a deep, plush emerald. A thick carpet of mosses, liverworts, and broad-leafed ferns has colonized the iron plates, rooting in the accumulated soot, rust-scale, and grease that have settled over centuries. Great, ropey vines with thick, rubbery leaves that shine like polished leather crawl along the seams between the bronze plates, their tendrils wrapping tight around copper conduits and wire-bundles. Some of these plants seem to have adapted specifically to the machinery; their roots don't merely cling to the iron, but seem to secrete a mild acid that etches into the metal, fusing plant and plate into a single, calcified crust. 

Because the machinery here moves so slowly, the plants have had time to swallow it. A massive gear, five stories high, is set into the western wall. Its teeth are choked with a dense, spongy species of lichen that softens its mechanical outline until it looks like a ancient stone monument. The gear is not dead; it is turning. But its movement is almost imperceptible—perhaps one tooth every three hours. If you watch it closely, you can see the lichen on the teeth being slowly, silently compressed against the brass housing, oozing a clear, aromatic sap that smells of pine and parakeet oil, before the gear tooth pulls away, leaving a flattened green paste that slowly begins to regrow.

The air is not quiet. It is occupied by a sound so loud it ceases to be a sound and becomes a state of body.

It is a low, thundering roar—the collective output of a thousand gargantuan heat-exchangers, steam-pistons, and rotary fans operating miles below. The noise is a steady, oceanic drone that vibrates the soles of your feet through the iron grating. It hums in your teeth. It is too loud for screaming to parse; if you speak, the sound is instantly swallowed by the deep, bass thrum of the machine. The fauna of this place have adapted to this deafening environment. They do not sing, and they do not cry out. 

Small, lizard-like creatures with pale, translucent skin and oversized claws scuttle across the sheer vertical moss beds. To communicate, they do not make noise; instead, they tap their hard, bone-tipped tails against the iron plates, sending vibrations through the metal that can be felt by other lizards miles up the shaft. 

Thousands of small, dull-winged moths, their wings patterned like oxidized copper, flutter through the green ferns. They do not fly into the open void of the central shaft, where there is nothing to cling to; they stay within three inches of the green walls, feeding on the sweet, oily condensation that drips from the copper valves. High above, nesting in the hollow, rusted interiors of defunct steam pipes, are colonies of small, featherless birds. Their skin is dark and leathery, and they glide between the catwalks on long, bat-like wings, catching the warm updrafts of the steam vents. They are entirely deaf to the roar of the engines, having heads containing no ear canals, relying instead on specialized pressure-sensitive whiskers along their snouts to detect the shifts in the vertical wind.

This is a world that has reached an equilibrium. The steel is eroding, but very, very slowly, held in place by its own weight and the protective coat of mineral-rich mosses that shield it from the worst of the steam-rust. The engines are old, so old that their original purpose has been forgotten, yet they continue to function with a heavy, indifferent momentum. The water drips; the steam rises; the plants grow; the machines thrum. Nothing here has changed in five hundred years, and there is no indication that anything will change for five hundred more. The Great Conduit simply exists, a small, vertical slice of dense green life suspended in an infinite, thunderous iron sky.
