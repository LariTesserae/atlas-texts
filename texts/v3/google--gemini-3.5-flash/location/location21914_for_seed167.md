The world is a single, warm, heavy envelope of saltwater. There is no surface to this sea—or if there is, it lies so far above that the weight of the water has long since compressed any memory of an sky into a myth of thin air. Here, in the low, flat basins of the world-bed, the water is thick, saline, and cradling, heated from below by the slow, rhythmic exhalations of deep geothermal faults. It is a twilight that is nearly absolute black, rated at the barest whisper of light: a velvet darkness where eyes are useless, vestigial beads, and the universe is map-read instead through the skin, the lateral line, and the chemical tongue.

Though there are no tools, no metal, and no glass, this is a city. 

It is an immense, organic metropolis, structured with the density of an ancient, crowded capital. The architecture is not built; it is grown, secreted, and carved by the collective instincts of millions of living things over tens of thousands of years. The material is calcite—creamy, grey-white, and smooth as worn bone. Massive, hollowed-out towers rise from the silt, their walls composed of billions of fused, mineralized shells. They are interconnected by soaring, tubular aqueducts and arched bridges grown from the skeletons of colonial glass-sponges, long since died and calcified into stone-hard lattice. 

The layout is highly organized, a three-dimensional grid of lanes, plazas, and vertical shafts. Because there is no gravity to limit movement—only buoyancy—the city exists in three dimensions. "Streets" are not flat pavements but pressurized current-corridors. Warm, nutrient-rich water is channeled through these pathways, propelled by the coordinated, rhythmic beating of millions of tiny, fan-like sea-pens that line the interior walls. To move through the city is to drift or swim through a series of warm, pulsing pipes, guided by the gentle, directional tug of the water.

Erosion here is a settled, balanced force. The edges of the calcite buildings are not sharp; they have been licked smooth by the steady, unceasing flow of the thermal currents. Every corniced ledge, every entry-pore, and every spiraling ramp is rounded, polished to a dull sheen like river stone. The city has existed in this state for so long that it has become its own geology. The sediment that settles in the deeper basins is not dirt, but a fine, powdery flour of crushed shell and organic dust, which is periodically swept clean by giant, undulating bellows-organisms that live in the city's foundations, pulsing like slow, fleshy hearts to flush the detritus out into the uninhabited barrens.

Vegetation is almost entirely absent. In the deep, warm dark, photosynthesis is impossible. The only plants are sparse, thread-like strands of a pale, rootless algae that clings to the edges of the thermal vents—ghostly, colorless hair that sways listlessly in the hot plumes. 

Yet, the city teems with animal life. The density is startling; every square inch of the calcite structures is occupied. The walls are not bare stone but are carpeted in a living, shimmering skin of barnacles, sedentary anemones, and colonial bryozoans that filter the warm water as it rushes past. 

In the open lanes, the fauna moves in complex, silent patterns. Blind, long-legged decapod crustacea—some the size of dogs, with shells polished white and smooth—clamber along the outside of the towers, their multi-jointed legs making a soft, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* against the stone. Schools of long, ribby glass-fish, completely transparent and carrying only a faint, silver sheen along their spines, dart through the communal chambers in silent, perfectly synchronized clouds. 

Now and then, a massive, slow-moving siphonophore—a colonial organism resembling a living, weeping ribbon of pale blue jelly fifty feet long—drifts down a vertical avenue. It is one of the few sources of light: a cold, faint, bioluminescent green-blue glow that barely illuminates the walls it passes, casting long, crawling shadows of the scrambling crabs before fading back into the velvet dark.

The sounds of this place are hushed, muffled by the immense density of the water. The dominant sound is a low, vibrational thrum—the deep, oceanic hum of the thermal vents, felt in the chest rather than heard. Against this bass note, there are the soft, domestic noises of the metropolis: the wet, sliding *shuck* of giant bivalves adjusting their shells, the gentle *whisssh* of water being pumped through living filters, and the occasional, sharp *snap* of a pistol-shrimp clearing a passage. 

There is a sophisticated social order here, though it is entirely biological. The roles of infrastructure are filled by specialized species. Giant, stationary tunicates serve as waste processors, absorbing the refuse of the mobile populations. Siphon-worms act as valves, swelling to block currents during the periodic, violent heat-surges from the vents, protecting the delicate nurseries of the glass-fish. 

It is an ecosystem mimicking an urban civilization, or perhaps an urban civilization that has dissolved back into its ecological components. It is active, dynamic, and perfectly balanced. The predators—sleek, eel-like hunters that slide through the dark with lateral lines sensitive to the heartbeat of a crab a hundred yards away—do not break the order; they maintain it, harvesting the weak and the old from the edges of the crowded towers.

In this warm, dark, submerged world, there is no concept of progress, no tomorrow, and no yesterday. There is only the flow of the current, the steady, comforting warmth of the earth-heat from below, the scrape of shell on stone, and the endless, silent pulse of a city made of bone and water.
