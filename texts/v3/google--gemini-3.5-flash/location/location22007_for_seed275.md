The world does not have a core of iron, nor crusts of basalt and granite. If you tunnel downward through the dry, leafy loam of the far upper reaches, the soil does not compact into stone; instead, it knits. It becomes a dense, fibrous webwork of root-flesh, then solid lignin, and finally opens into the great, hollowed galleys of the Inner Pith. 

Here, several miles below the outer canopy of the world-tree, lies a cool, dry vault the size of an inland sea. 

There is no water here. The atmosphere is arid, smelling of cedar shavings, dried tea, and bitter oil. In place of rivers, there are slow-flowing veins of amber latex—hard, cold, and glassy at their edges, but moving with glacier-slowness in the deep channels. The floor of the vault is a series of vast, terraced shelves made of ancient, dark-ringed heartwood, dried to the hardness of flint. Because there is no water to carve the landscape, there are no rounded stones or smooth valleys. Where the wood has fractured under the immense weight of the trunk above, the cliffs are razor-sharp, splintered into colossal, vertical spikes of pale yellow cellulose. These raw, jagged breaks are young; they have not been softened by rain or wind. They stand as embryonic mountains of pure grain, waiting for the slow, creeping lichen-crusts to heal them.

The vault is bright, illuminated by a steady, cool glare that has no sun for a source. Instead, massive, swollen conduit-vines—some as thick as aqueducts—run along the ceiling and down the walls. Their translucent skins glow with a chemically intense, pale green light, pumped down from the photosynthesizing leaves miles above. This light is strong enough to cast hard, sharp shadows across the dry plains of the heartwood shelf, but it carries no warmth. The air remains cellar-cool, hovering at a temperature that preserves the dry wood from decay.

Across this vast, hollow territory, a settlement has been established, though it is not crowded. The city is built with a sparse, deliberate geometry, its components scattered across the wooden plains with miles of empty, silent space between them. 

The architecture is entirely industrial and vegetal. Great towers, grown from controlled bamboo-like shoots that have been desiccated and lacquered, rise like hollow chimneys. They do not cluster; they stand in isolated groups of three or four, connected by long, sagging suspension bridges made of woven hemp cables. 

Because there is no coal or oil of a mineral kind, the technology here is powered by the mechanical tension of the world itself. The city’s industry is noisy in its design but quiet in its execution, muffled by the sound-absorbing properties of the wooden floor. You can see the great gantry cranes—spidery configurations of dried ash-beams—lifting massive square blocks of cork from the quarry-cliffs, but you hear only a dry, sliding whistle of ropes and the faint, rhythmic *clack-clack* of wooden gears. 

Power is transmitted across the open distances not by wires, but by massive, rotating shafts of polished pine, supported every hundred yards by wooden gantry towers. These shafts spin silently, carrying the torque from distant, slow-turning turbines that are driven by the rising hot air of the deep thermal vents. Elsewhere, industrial looms the size of cathedrals weave raw flax into stiff, structural canvases, their shuttles flying back and forth with a soft *shuck-shuck* that is swallowed by the dry air almost immediately.

The silence here is immense and acoustic. The ground does not echo. If you drop a metal tool, it hits the hard heartwood floor with a dull, flat thud that dies instantly, without the ringing reverberation of stone. 

Yet, the environment is active, populated by a specialized, dry-bodied fauna. Large, multi-segmented beetles with shells like polished mahogany crawl slowly along the transport cables, their legs tipped with hook-like claws that click rhythmically against the hemp. They carry bundles of dried fiber and jars of refined resin to the factories. In the wide, empty spaces between the urban hubs, flocks of glider-birds with skin like parchment drift on the cool thermal drafts, their wings silent as they hunt for the small, wood-boring grubs that chew through the foundations of the towers.

The city is constantly under construction, but it is also constantly in a state of defense against the very ground it sits upon. The "geology" of this place is alive. Though the air is dry, the walls of the vault are slowly, inevitably expanding. New shoots of iron-hard root-flesh push through the streets like slow-motion geysers, forcing the citizens to run giant, circular saws powered by the spinning drive-shafts to prune back the tarmac of their plazas. 

Every few weeks, the "weather" changes. The great conduit-vines overhead pulse with a deeper, throbbing yellow light. A dry, golden snow begins to fall from the ceiling—not water, but a heavy blizzard of sterile pollen. It covers the industrial yards, the wooden rails, and the roofs of the lacquered towers in a thick, fragrant powder. The factory whistles—made of dried gourds—sound a low, hollow chord, warning the workers to retreat indoors or don their leather breathing-hoods. During these pollen-falls, the sparse city becomes even quieter, the machinery slowing to a crawl as the yellow dust muffles the gears, and the only movement is the slow, silent passage of the mahogany beetles through the drifts, leaving neat, patterned tracks in the gold.
