This is the High Basin of the Slate-Weirs. 

At this altitude, three thousand meters above the salt-flats, there is no earth. There is only the Grid: an endless, terraced expanse of black iron plating, soot-baked brick masonry, and thick, interlocking copper flumes that stretches from one horizon of the pass to the other. It is a regional machine, vast as a city but with no citizens, built to catch, filter, and balance the heavy cloud-harvest of the peaks.

The air is cold—a wet, lung-filling chill that keeps one’s breath suspended in pale plumes. It is a world of perpetual twilight. Overhead, a dense, low-hanging ceiling of bruised slate-colored clouds drags across the topmost gantries, squeezing out a constant, gentle drizzle. This water doesn’t fall so much as it condenses upon every vertical surface, weeping down the rusted flanks of massive, vertical heat-exchangers and pooling in the thousands of shallow brick holding-reservoirs that step down the mountain pass like giant stairs.

Though the scale is massive, there is no emptiness here. Every square inch of the landscape is packed, occupied, and utilized. If a platform is not occupied by a hiss-vent or a cluster of heavy iron rivets, it is choked with vegetation. The local flora is lush, monstrously thick, and wet. It is an ecology of damp-dwellers that have found balance with the iron. Spongy, five-inch-thick mats of emerald sphagnum moss coat the sides of the steam pipes, insulated by the warmth within. Giant, waxy-leafed ferns roll out of the gaps between brick foundations, their frills dripping black oil-water. Ivy with leaves as broad as shovels climbs the iron lattices, its rootlets secreting a mild acid that etches into the lead paint, securing a grip that no gale can shake. Everywhere, the smell of damp peat and rotting vegetation blends with the sharp, cold tang of wet iron and coal grease.

It is a world in constant, violent motion, yet it is almost entirely silent. 

The turbulence of the Basin is mechanical and atmospheric. Heavily counter-weighted water-gates, the size of three-story houses, swing shut on grease-slicked bronze hinges to reroute the rising runoff. Massive, iron-plated pontoons rise and fall in the deeper locks, their movements driving oil-pumps deep within the substructure. High above, on suspension rails that span the valleys between terraces, long trains of automated ore-tills crawl back and forth, shifting thousands of tons of ballast to compensate for the shifting weight of the rainwater. The landscape is a puzzle constantly reassembling itself, sliding, tilting, and draining. 

And yet, you can hear a single drop of water hit a copper leaf fifty yards away. 

The sound of this kinetic chaos is swallowed by the wet green carpet. The moss acts as a universal muffler. The great, earth-shaking thrum of thousand-horsepower steam pistons is reduced to a soft, rhythmic heartbeat felt through the soles of one’s boots rather than heard in the ear. The discharge of high-pressure steam flues isn't a deafening roar, but a long, wet *hiss-shhh* that sounds like the sigh of a sleeping giant, deadened by the hanging ferns. The only sharp sounds are the occasional chilly drips of water falling from an iron girder onto a broad dock-leaf, or the slow, rhythmic *shuck-clunk* of an automatic valve turning its wheel behind a wall of ivy.

Life other than the plants is small and quiet. In the wet dark beneath the copper conduits, where the water is warm from process-run, one might occasionally find a palm-sized iron-snail, its shell a spiral of oxidized brown, scraping algae from the wet rivets. Small, pale, blind midges drift in clouds around the dim, brass-hooded gas lamps that flicker with a cold, yellow-green light along the maintenance walkways. These lamps are the only landmarks in the gloom, their light struggling to penetrate more than thirty yards through the dripping fog before being snuffed out by the dark.

Everything here has the weight of centuries. The iron is not bright; it is black-scaled, multi-coated, pitted by generations of acidic mountain rain, and healed over by moss. The brickwork is smooth at the waterlines, worn down by the quiet friction of billions of gallons of mountain drainage. There is no decay here that is not also a form of growth. It is an industrial wilderness, fully built, heavily engineered, unstable in its details but perfectly balanced in its totality, running its lonely, wet cycles in the quiet of the high clouds.
