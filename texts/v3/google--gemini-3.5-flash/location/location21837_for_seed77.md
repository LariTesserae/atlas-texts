The horizon is a ruled line, perfectly flat, separating a pale violet sky from a floor of endless, sun-baked plaster. 

There is no soil here. There is no sand, no river-silt, no gravel. The ground is a single, continuous sheet of colossal masonry—an infinite plain of hand-smoothed lime plaster and pale, terracotta-colored cement. The work is monumental but simple, lacking any ornament, machinery, or structural metal. It resembles the dry floor of a gargantuan, ancient reservoir that has been cleared of all debris. 

Because the landscape is newly finished—geologically embryonic—there is almost no wear. The corners of the great stepped terraces, which drop in thirty-foot increments every dozen miles, are sharp enough to cut a hand. There are no cracks in the plaster, no heaps of crumbled mortar, no wind-blown dust. The surface is pristine, raw, and sterile. The only textures are the broad, faint sweeps of whatever giant, soft trowels smoothed the lime before it cured, leaving shallow ridges that catch the bright, normal sunlight.

The air is hot, thick, and heavy, hanging low over the basin. At this low elevation, the atmospheric pressure makes the heat feel tactile, like a dry weight pressing against the skin. Yet, despite the heat, the world is remarkably wet. 

The water does not arrive via rivers or deep springs; it is a product of the atmosphere. Every night, as the vast plaster plains slowly radiate their heat into the sky, the air cools, and condensation occurs on a massive scale. Great sheets of dew form on the pale, hydrophobic surfaces. This moisture does not sink into the ground, for the plaster is non-porous. Instead, guided by a barely perceptible slope built into the mega-slabs, the water begins to move.

It gathers in the seams—narrow, three-inch-wide joints that separate the continental-sized tiles. Slowly, silently, the water channels down the terraces into the low-lying basins. By dawn, these basins—which are fifty miles wide but only a foot deep—become shallow, mirror-like lakes. The water is distilled and perfectly clear, reflecting the sky with absolute fidelity.

As the sun climbs, the temperature rises rapidly toward its daily peak. The shallow lakes begin to steam. By noon, a white haze rises from the basins, forming long, linear cumulus clouds that trace the exact grid of the water channels miles below. The weather here is a direct reflection of the architecture.

By mid-afternoon, the clouds grow heavy. They release brief, warm, torrential downpours. The rain hits the hot, dry masonry with a soft, collective hiss, instantly cooling the surface for a few moments before running off into the channels to repeat the cycle.

Between these continental tiles, there is much emptiness. One could walk for days across a single, vast terrace of cream-colored plaster and encounter nothing but the wind. The silence is profound. There are no leaves to rustle, no insects to hum, and no birds to call. The only sounds are physical: the low, rushing sigh of the hot wind across the flat expanses; the rhythmic, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of the plaster expanding and contracting under the sun; and the soft, distant murmur of rainwater sliding over a terrace edge.

Life has only just begun to touch this world, and it is sparse. It exists exclusively in the narrow joints between the giant masonry blocks, where moisture lingers longest before evaporating. 

Here, in the vertical seams, a pale, thread-like moss has taken hold. It is a dry, fibrous organism, light greyish-green, that grows flat against the plaster walls like veins of jade in marble. It has no leaves or stems, only tiny, capillary-like roots that drink the mineral vapor rising from the curing lime. In some of the wider junctions, where three or four continental plates meet, the moss forms small, bouncy cushions that smell faintly of wet chalk and salt.

There are no animals to graze on this moss, no burrowing creatures to nest in the seams. The world belongs entirely to the cycle of the sun, the water, and the slow, quiet curing of the plaster. It is a world under construction, waiting in the hot, bright quiet for its first grain of sand to form.
