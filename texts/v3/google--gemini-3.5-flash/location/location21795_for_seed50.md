High up in the needle-thin spires of the Shatter-Spines, where the air should be dry and thin, there exists a hidden, vertical pocket of dynamic madness. It is a chamber no larger than a cathedral’s bell tower, hollowed out of a single towering finger of black rock. This is the *Hollow-of-the-Gush*. 

To enter it is to be instantly deafened. The sound is a continuous, concussive roar that vibrates not just in the ears, but through the jawbone and the soles of the feet. It is the acoustic cauldron of high-altitude water under extreme, chaotic pressure.

The space is almost entirely empty—a vertical void of cool, moist air—yet it is simultaneously dominated by water. Because of some strange localized barometric anomaly and the fierce updrafts rising through the mountain's volcanic vents, water does not fall here in a simple stream. Instead, it enters from a glacial fissure at the top of the chamber as massive, self-contained globules—liquid spheres the size of haystacks, carriages, and small houses—that float and drift through the empty air of the pocket like slow-motion planets. 

These water-spheres are in constant, turbulent motion. They are sucked upward by warm, rising thermal drafts, then grabbed by downdrafts of freezing high-altitude air, sending them careening into one another. When they collide, they do not quietly merge; they impact with the sound of cracking timber, shattering into a million heavy, stinging drops before gravity and surface tension drag the fragments back together into new, wobbling liquid bodies. The air is so saturated with this hyper-dense, atomized spray that to breathe is to drink. It is an atmospheric ocean, a drowned room where the water is everywhere but holds no shape for more than a few seconds.

The rock enclosing this pocket is raw, sharp, and young. It has an erosion value that suggests it was born only yesterday. There are no smooth, water-worn basins or polished river stones here. The walls are made of jagged, vitreous obsidian and needle-point basalt columns that have just finished cooling. They are embryonic—sharp enough to slice open a hand at the slightest brush, their edges glistening with wetness but completely unbowed by the frantic water that lashes them. In some of the deeper wall-fissures, the basalt is still faintly warm to the touch, venting thin plumes of sulfurous steam that smell of the earth’s deep belly.

Human presence here is minuscule, ancient, and primitive. High up, wedged into a cleft between two raw basalt needles, are the rotting remains of a single, small structure: a platform made of rough-hewn pine logs, blackened by dampness and mold. A single rope, braided from coarse mountain grass and dried intestine, stretches across a corner of the void, sagging under the weight of the moisture clinging to it. It is held in place by crude copper spikes, green and crusty with oxidation, hammered directly into the seams of the young stone. Dangling from the center of this rope is a small, empty basket of woven splint-wood, spinning sluggishly in the wind of the water-globes' passage. It is a tool of manual craft, left by some forgotten climber who came to harvest the pure, mineral-filled ice-melt from the center of the floating spheres.

The light is surprisingly bright and clean. Far above, the top of the vertical chimney is open to the stratospheric sky. A pillar of cold, brilliant sunlight cuts straight down through the center of the chamber. When this light strikes the drifting water-globes, they act as massive, moving glass lenses. Prisms of blinding violet, gold, and green sweep across the black obsidian walls, illuminating the wet, raw stone in sudden, silent flashes before plunging them back into shadow.

In this cold, roaring, empty space, life is a rarity. There is no soil, only the bare black stone. Yet, in the cracks where the warm volcanic steam vents, a sparse, rubbery orange lichen has taken hold. It grows in small, round scabs, feeding on the mineral steam and the brilliant, refracted light. 

Once in a long movement of the eye, one might spot a member of the chamber’s only fauna: a "mist-glider." These are pale, ribless, leathery creatures no larger than a hand. Lacking eyes, they cling to the basalt walls with sticky, disc-like pads. When a floating water-globe drifts close to the wall, a glider will launch itself into the dark void, flattening its body to catch the turbulent updrafts. It sails through the air, plunges directly into the heart of a drifting liquid sphere, feeds on whatever microscopic algae lives within the glacier water, and then rides the sphere until it passes close enough to another rock face for the creature to leap back.

It is a place of beautiful, violent instability. The water is never still; the rock is still growing; the sound never ceases its heavy, rhythmic hammering. It is an isolated crucible, high above the world, where wetness, stone, and wind are locked in a perpetual, loud, and lonely dance.
