High on the sheer, vertical face of an unnamed volcanic horn, nestled far above the cloud-line where the air is thin and hot enough to sting the throat, lies a hidden pocket of space no larger than a quiet room. 

This alcove is a geological cradle, so newly emerged from the earth’s interior that its materials have not yet met the softening hand of wind or rain. The rock underfoot is not stone as one typically knows it, but raw, unscuffed black glass and native iron, fractured into flawless, razor-sharp prisms. There are no pebbles, no dust, no soil. Every edge is surgically sharp, clean as a fresh weld, and burning with a deep, radiating heat that rises from the tectonic pressure-cooker below. 

Though no hand has ever worked this place, the landscape is profoundly industrial. The planet has not grown ruins; it has grown machinery. Striations of raw copper rise through the basalt like integrated circuitry, forming natural, massive solenoids that hum with a faint, electric potential. Perfect cylinders of iron slide with agonizing slowness inside sleeves of self-lubricating graphite, driven by the glacial expansion and contraction of the mountain’s hot roots. It is a factory without a designer, an engine of pure geology that produces nothing but its own quiet, rhythmic existence.

There is not a drop of water here. The environment is entirely non-aqueous; the air is a bone-dry mixture of nitrogen and carbon-fluoride gases, shimmering with heat distortion under a harsh, normal sky. Yet, despite the total absence of moisture, the pocket is home to a lush, sparse flora. 

These are not plants of cellulose and sap, but of silica, pewter, and glass. They grow in isolated, dramatic clusters, separated by wide expanses of bare, iron-plated floor. In one corner stands a clump of *needle-palms*, their trunks constructed from nested, overlapping brass pipes that creak as they expand in the sun. Their leaves are delicate ribbons of hammered tinfoil, arranged in dense, heavy crowns that catch the glare and reflect it in brilliant, cold arcs. Nearby, crawling slowly across a seam of raw sulfur, is a sprawling vine of *glass-ivy*. Its leaves are hollow prisms of flawless quartz, inside of which a yellow, non-conductive silicone fluid rises and falls in response to the heat. 

Because the scale is so small and tucked-away, the pocket holds its own distorted rules of reality. Inside this alcove, space behaves like a bent lens. Gravity does not pull straight down; it tilts slightly toward the heavy iron deposits in the back wall, so that the glass-ivy grows outward at a thirty-degree angle into the empty air, suspended over the abyss. Light, too, is warped by the intense magnetic fields generated by the stone engines. A sunbeam entering the alcove does not travel in a straight line; it curves gently, illuminating the undersides of the metal leaves and casting crescent-shaped shadows that slowly rotate even when the sun is still.

There is no animal life here. No insects crawl through the iron-moss, and no birds dare the thin, scorching air of the high altitude. The silence is profound, but it is not empty. It is a quiet of low-frequency vibrations. Every few minutes, a dry *tink-tink* echoes through the chamber as a silica leaf expands in the heat. Underneath that, at the very limit of human hearing, is the steady, 50-hertz drone of the copper-veined basalt, a slow electrical respiration that has not changed its pitch in a thousand years.

Changes occur here on the scale of centuries. A new leaf of spun-aluminum takes a decade to unfurl from its copper sheath. The iron pistons shift perhaps an inch every fifty summers. It is a landscape caught in the moment of its creation, self-assembling with mechanical precision, dry as a kiln, boiling in its own quiet, high-altitude sanctuary.
