This is a place inside a place. 

It is a single, sealed basalt vesicular pocket—a oval bubble in the volcanic deep—measuring no more than fifty paces from end to end. The air here has never touched the sky. It is heavy, desiccated, and intensely hot, registering a dry, kiln-like heat that smells of parched slate and spent matches. 

A central, vacant void occupies almost the entirety of this pocket. There is no dust in the air to catch the light, only an emptiness so profound that the far side of the chamber seems both incredibly near and miles away. True to its pocket-scale, the world ends at the curved basalt walls, yet within this boundary, the density of matter is low; it is a hollow sphere with its contents pushed entirely to the edges.

A dull, rhythmic rust-red glow rises from thin fissures in the floor, providing the only illumination. It is a dim, twilight light that does not carry far, casting thick, soft-edged shadows that stretch upward along the curve of the ceiling. 

Where the red light leaks from the floor, there is life, though not a drop of water has ever existed here. 

The vegetation is lush but entirely non-aqueous. Dense forests of tall, segmented reeds made of brittle, glass-hard silica grow directly from the warm stone fractures. Their leaves are thin, translucent plates of gray mica that do not bend so much as they hinge. They draw their sustenance from the hot carbon gases and vaporous mineral-salts that leak from the earth. These plants grow with an aggressive, snapping speed, their cellular plates expanding in the heat like crystallizing salt. Because they are completely dry, they resemble complex origami structures made of slate and parchment.

This pocket is not wild; it has been organized. 

Clinging to the steeply curved walls of the bubble is a dense, miniature town. It is a work of immense, primitive labor, constructed entirely of hand-molded red clay and carved chunks of volcanic pumice. Hundreds of tiny, box-like dwellings are stacked atop one another like the cells of a paper wasp nest, forming a vertical urban landscape that curves with the geography of the chamber. Tiny, hand-fashioned stairs climb the basalt cliffs; miniature plazas are paved with flat shards of obsidian; and long, sagging suspension bridges woven from tough, hair-like mineral fibers span the gap from the western cliff-dwellings to the eastern terraces.

The scale of the buildings suggests they were built for something small, but there is no fauna here. No insects crawl in the red clay cracks; no lizards dart across the hot stone. The town is empty, weathered by the hot air-currents into a state of smooth, settled balance. The sharp corners of the clay houses have been polished down over centuries by the constant passage of dry wind, leaving them looking like melted tallow or water-worn bone, though they have never known rain.

Though devoid of animal life, the chamber is incredibly loud and in constant motion. 

The thermal vents in the floor do not blow steadily; they breathe. At regular intervals, high-pressure drafts of hot gas whistle through the floor fissures. The wind rushes upward through the forest of silica reeds, causing the mica plates to clatter against one another with a sound like a million dry dice being shaken in a leather cup. 

The woven fiber bridges hum and vibrate like the strings of a harp. Under the baking heat, the clay towers of the town expand, making dry, snapping groans that echo off the curved ceiling. 

Most active of all are the reproduction cycles of the silica plants. As the stalks mature in the intense heat, their dry seed-cones—which look like hollow, charred pinecones—dry to the point of extreme tension. Suddenly, with a sharp, metallic *ping* like a snapped spring, a cone will burst, throwing tiny, glittering silicate spores into the updraft. The drafts carry these bright, glass-like specks through the central void, where they catch the low red light of the fissures and sparkle like falling embers before settling into the next crack to climb again. 

Everything here is in balance: the rising heat, the clattering growth of the dry plants, the gradual polishing of the clay town, and the heavy, windy silence of the empty space at the center of it all.
