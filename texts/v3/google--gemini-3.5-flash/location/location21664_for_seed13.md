The light of this place does not travel so much as it dwells. It is the steady, sharp illumination of a high-altitude afternoon, held at a permanent slant of forty-five degrees. The sky above is a deep, unwashed indigo, so clear that the stars are faintly visible as silver pinpricks even beside the sun. Because the light never shifts, the shadows are solid, bluish-black shapes painted permanently onto the stone. They do not lengthen or contract; they are as much a part of the geography as the cliffs themselves.

This is a landscape of young, raw-edged stone. Great needles of dark, basaltic rock rise thousands of feet into the thin air, clumped together like the crystals of a massive geode. There is no rubble here. There are no scree slopes, no piles of gravel at the bases of the spires, and no dust in the air. The edges of the rock are so sharp they look as if they were fractured only seconds ago, yet they have held this razor-like definition forever. The typical soft-rounding work of wind and time does not occur. The stone does not yield to the air, and the air does not bite the stone.

Clinging to these vertical columns is a sprawling, sparse city of dry-stone masonry. It is built on a scale that suggests a vast population, yet the towers and plazas are separated by wide, breathless gulfs of empty space. Without mortar, cement, or iron clamps, the people have stacked flat slabs of dark slate into impossibly tall, slender towers that sit perched on the lips of chasms. Because nothing ever decays or shifts, these towers do not require maintenance. A wall laid down ten centuries ago remains perfectly plumb, its joint lines as tight as the day the stones were split with copper wedges. 

The technology here is quiet and manual. Braided ropes of tough mountain grass connect spire to spire, spanning hundreds of yards across the abyss. These bridges do not swing; they hang in perfect, frozen catenary curves, unaffected by gusts, because the wind here is a constant, gentle draught that never rises to a storm and never sinks to a dead calm. On the terraces, one sees large, shallow basins carved into the bedrock with hand-held flint and copper chisels. These basins collect the settlement’s water.

Water does not fall from the sky as rain, nor does it flow in rivers. Instead, it exists as a quiet, perpetual condensation. A cold, damp breath rises from the unseen depths of the low valleys, meeting the cool mountain air at this high elevation. Little silver beads of water form constantly on the undersides of the stone bridges and along the tips of the vegetation. The moisture does not run; it sits in perfect, trembling spheres until it grows too heavy and drops into the carved basins below with a clear, metallic *tink*. 

Because of this constant, cool dampness, the vegetation is lush, though it grows in a strangely halted, eternal state. Thick rugs of emerald moss cover the northern faces of the stone towers, feeling as dense and wet as saturated felt. Dwarf conifers with stiff, blue-green needles grow from the cracks in the basalt, their branches laden with tiny, non-drifting cones. Every leaf and blade of grass is clad in a thin sleeve of condensation that acts like a lens, making the plant life glitter under the constant sun. The plants do not grow, nor do they wither. No yellow leaves drop to the ground; no seedlings sprout. The green is static, preserved in its moment of maximum vitality.

Fauna is everywhere, yet it is almost entirely silent. High-crested mountain sheep with coats of thick, oily white wool stand on ledges so narrow their hooves seem to overhang the void. They do not move for hours, their yellow eyes fixed on the horizon, chewing slowly on grass that never diminishes. Thousands of small, glass-winged moths cling to the damp moss, their wings vibrating at a frequency too high for the human ear to register, creating only a faint, cool pressure in the air. Overhead, large-winged raptors with feathers the color of wet slate drift on the permanent thermal currents. They do not flap their wings; they remain suspended in the blue, steering with minute tilts of their tail feathers, silent observers of an unchanging world.

There is a profound hushed quality to the air. The sounds that do exist are localized, small, and distinct: the occasional *clack* of a sheep’s hoof against rock, the steady *plop-plop* of condensation falling into a cistern, the low, breathy whistle of the constant air current passing through the hollow stone towers. Because there is no thunder, no rustling of dead leaves, and no roar of machinery, these small sounds carry for miles through the thin, crisp air, completely clear and undistorted.

In this place, the concept of history has no meaning, because there is no decay to measure it by. The ladders used by the inhabitants are made of green ash wood, bound with raw leather thongs; they look fresh-cut, yet they have stood in the same niches for three hundred years without rotting or loosening. The inhabitants themselves move with the deliberate, unhurried grace of creatures who have no fear of the dark or the winter, for neither is coming. They carve their stone, weave their grass ropes, and watch the silent birds drift across the gap between the spires, living in the middle of a beautiful, cold, and eternal afternoon.
