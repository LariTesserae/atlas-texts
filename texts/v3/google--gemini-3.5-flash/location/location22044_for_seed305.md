The light does not fall; it strikes. At this altitude, the atmosphere is thin as gauze, offering no shield against a sun that hangs like a magnesium flare in an indigo sky. The brightness is flat, hard, and absolute, reflecting off a world made entirely of black glass and white frost. There are no shadows that are not pitch-black, and no surfaces that do not gleam.

This is a landscape of young, raw tectonic violence. The mountains are not rounded slopes but vertical needles—great, jagged sheets of unweathered obsidian and basalt that have only recently torn through the crust. They have the sharp, dangerous edges of freshly shattered bottles. No rains have lived long enough to blunt these peaks; no rivers have carved valleys here. Instead, the water exists as a tense, intermittent grease—a cold brine that drips from high rock-fissures, instantly freezing into needle-like rime or clinging to surfaces as a heavy, viscous condensation. It is a damp world, but it is a dampness of sweat and frost, not of streams. 

And yet, despite the biting, sub-zero cold and the razor-sharp rock, there is no empty space. The landscape is packed so densely that the geography itself seems choked.

Between the basalt needles, filling every chasm and covering every ledge, is a lush, suffocating growth. This is not soft foliage, but "cable-vegetation"—thick, vitreous fibers as stiff as copper wire and as pale as lard. They grow in massive, self-compressing mattresses, miles wide and hundreds of feet deep, bridging the canyons to create a false, trembling floor. These plants do not have leaves; they have hair-thin glass tubes that channel the blinding sunlight deep into their roots, humming faintly as the light heats the sap within them.

The sound is the first thing that betrays the true nature of this place. It is not the sound of wind or water, but of an unimaginable, slow-motion iron foundry. 

A deep, rhythmic, hydraulic thrumming vibrates through the soles of the feet. It is a slow, mechanical pulse—one beat every twelve seconds—accompanied by the long, metallic hiss of high-pressure steam being discharged through biological valves. 

This is the fauna. The mountains are not merely covered in life; they are constructed of it. The valleys between the glass cliffs are packed shoulder-to-shoulder with the *Colossi*—spherical, plate-armored organisms the size of cathedral domes. They do not walk; they are too tightly wedged against one another for that. Instead, they exist in a state of eternal, slow-motion pressure. Their shells are made of bio-grown iron-sulfide, dark and metallic, polished to a mirror sheen where they grind against their neighbors. 

These beasts function as living thermodynamic engines. To survive the extreme cold, they draw the ambient brine into their massive, boiler-like internal cavities, where geological heat from deep fissures cooks the water into high-pressure steam. Every few minutes, a row of a thousand domes will synchronously vent their exhaust through tall, chimney-like tubes of bone and keratin. The steam shoots sixty feet into the freezing air, where it instantly crystallizes into a blinding white mist, only to settle back down as rime-frost upon the cable-vegetation.

The density of this ecosystem is total. To look down from a high spire is to see an undulating sea of iron plates, rising and falling with the agonizingly slow respiration of a million boiler-shells. Beneath this iron floor, in the dark, warm spaces heated by the exhaust, smaller creatures move in frantic, grease-slicked throngs. They are segmented, brass-colored arthropods with limbs that move like small pistons. They do not eat plants; they scrape the mineral grease from the sides of the steam chimneys, their mandibles clicking like sewing machines in a rhythmic, deafening chorus.

Nothing here is human, yet everything feels manufactured. The world behaves like an automated factory that has forgotten its purpose and simply continued to grow, reproducing its gears and boilers in meat and bone. 

The dynamics of this place are glacial, almost geologic. A single step by one of the larger, derrick-legged organisms that straddle the valleys takes nearly an hour. The leg rises, a massive column of fibrous muscle encased in jointed iron, dripping with black-tar lubricant. It hovers in the blinding glare, steam whistling through its pressure-vents, before descending with a ground-shaking *thud* that takes minutes to reverberate through the packed masses below. 

Changes happen over centuries. Sometimes, a basalt needle, top-heavy with frost and cable-plants, will fracture under its own weight. The collapse is not a quick landslide, but a slow, crushing compaction as the dense layers of life absorb the blow, swallowing the shattered glass-rock into the oily, steaming dark below the canopy, where it is slowly ground into sand by the rhythmic shifting of the iron domes.

If you stood on the highest point—a needle of pure obsidian that pierces the thin blue ceiling of the sky—you would see this pattern repeat to the horizon: an endless, blinding chessboard of black glass and white steam, vibrating with the deep, mechanical heartbeat of a planet that has turned life into industry.
