To sit upon the high plateaus of this world is to occupy a shelf of raw, newly minted geology. The elevation is dizzying, but there is no wind to make one sway; the air is thin, warm, and perfectly still. Below and around, the landscape stretches out in a vast, open shelf of monochrome basalt and gleaming metallic seams, extending for dozens of leagues before dropping away into vaporless rifts. 

There is no water. Not a drop of dew, no hidden aquifers, no clouds, and no ice. The sky is an intense, dry violet, holding a sun that burns with a clean, unscattered light. Because there is no water to freeze in the rock-pores, and no rain to scour the stone, there is virtually no erosion. The mountains are not mountains in the traditional, worn sense; they are giant, crystalline blocks, sharp-edged steps, and sheer fracture-panes of obsidian and granite that look as though they were quarried only hours ago. The edges of the cliffs are so crisp they could cut paper. There are no scree slopes, no heaps of gravel, and no dust. Everything is raw, black, and perfectly clean.

In the cavernous distances between these basalt blocks, the landscape is mostly void—vast, smooth terraces of bare stone where nothing has happened for millenniums. But where there is life, it is remarkably, shockingly lush. 

These are not organic plants. They are *metallophytes*—complex, self-assembling structures of brass, copper, and silicate that have sprouted directly from the rich, unoxidized metallic veins of the young rock. Within their isolated, widely spaced sanctuaries, these mechanical thickets grow with an astonishing, crowded density. 

They do not require soil. Instead, they anchor themselves with heavy, leaden counterweights and spring-like coils to the raw stone. From these bases rise trunks made of braided copper cables, which split into thousands of delicate, brassy wire-ferns. The foliage consists of thin, etched silicon wafers that catch the warm, direct sunlight like arrays of tiny, glittering leaves. Many of these plants exhibit the exact geometry of industrial machinery: there are segments that resemble cold-extruded rods, delicate geared wheels that do not turn but are locked in perfect symmetry, and long, hollow pipes that taper into copper needles. They look less like living organisms and more like a high-end automated factory that has been shattered, dissolved, and then replanted to grow as a dense, motionless garden.

There is a strange, industrial warmth to these thickets. The copper plates and brass wire absorb the steady heat of the sun, radiating a dry, metallic warmth that smells of hot solder and warm silica. 

This world is completely silent, save for two things. The first is a rare, minute *tink*—the sound of metal expanding under the steady sun, a sharp micro-fracture of a silicon leaf or the settling of a copper branch. The second is a faint, almost imperceptible electrostatic hum that vibrates through the rock where the metallic thickets are densest, a passive current generated by the temperature difference between the sun-baked brass leaves and the deep, cold stone roots.

No animals move through these copper forests. There are no insects to pollinate the gear-blossoms, no birds to nest in the wire-mesh branches, and no microbes to rot the fallen brass leaves. When a silicon leaf eventually snaps off from its copper stem, it falls to the basalt floor and lies there forever, uncorrupted, bright and sharp as the day it formed. 

Everything exists in a state of eternal, frozen equilibrium. Because there is no water to rust the iron, no oxygen to tarnish the copper, and no weather to wear down the stone, the landscape is time-proof. The mechanical plants do not grow in the sense of cellular division; they are complete, poised, and locked into their forms. It is a dry, high-altitude greenhouse of clockwork and mineral, perfectly still, basking in the warm, perpetual light of an empty sky.
