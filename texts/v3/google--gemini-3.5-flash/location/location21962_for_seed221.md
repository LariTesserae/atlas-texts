At forty thousand feet above the world’s obscured floor, embedded deep within the structural marrow of a colossal, sky-spanning pylon, lies Compartment 9-C. It is an intimate, claustrophobic pocket of absolute fabrication—a chamber no larger than a cargo elevator, tucked away behind three-inch-thick plates of milled chromium-steel. 

There is no soil here. There is no sand, no clay, no fallen leaf. The floor, the ceiling, and the four slightly curved walls are composed entirely of interlocking industrial components: gray, unpolished sintered iron plates, hydraulic risers, and heavy-gauge copper conduits that run in parallel tracks like the veins of some frozen titan. Because the environment has suffered no erosion—its age held in a state of embryonic, pristine stasis—every metal edge is razor-sharp. The lathe marks on the massive hexagonal bolt heads are so crisp they could shear skin; the weld beads between the plates are bright, bumpy ribbons of unweathered nickel, showing no signs of decay, rust, or rounding. Everything is brand new, yet frozen in place, a monument to an immediate and permanent "now."

It is bitterly, terribly cold. The temperature hovers just a fraction above freezing, kept there by the constant passage of liquid nitrogen through cooling jackets behind the walls. Because of this extreme chill, the air is thick with a heavy, condensing fog that never clears. Water is everywhere, but it does not pool. Instead, it drips from the overhead valve-clusters in a perpetual, icy drizzle, while a fine, pressurized mist sprays from a hairline fracture in a high-pressure line. This moisture clings to the vertical metal plates in a shivering, viscous sweat, mixed with the faint, sweet scent of machine oil. On the colder iron seams, this condensation freezes into delicate, pipe-hugging sleeves of black ice that crack with a glass-like chime under the weight of their own accumulation, only to be instantly replaced by the weeping atmosphere.

The noise in Compartment 9-C is a physical presence. It is a deafening, 130-decibel monotone that has not changed its pitch, volume, or timber in who knows how many centuries. The sound is composed of two forces: the rhythmic, skull-rattling *thrum-shudder* of a massive pneumatic piston operating somewhere just beyond the eastern bulkhead, and the relentless, high-pressure shriek of cold air escaping a tiny, precise calibration-port in the center of the floor. The scream of the air is so loud and so constant that the dripping water does not fall in straight lines; the sound waves physically deflect the droplets, forcing them to dance and skate sideways across the wet metal plates in ribbed, static patterns.

In this screaming, freezing, iron-bound dark, life has found a way to saturate every available square inch—though it is a life devoid of greenery. Vegetation is almost entirely absent, represented only by a few pale, thread-like strands of a dry, oil-drinking fungus that nests in the shadowed spirals of the unused bolt-threads. 

But the fauna is overwhelming. 

The entire surface of the compartment—every pipe, every valve-handle, every wet flat of sintered iron—is covered in a thick, vibrating carpet of "solder-mites." These are pale, chitinous creatures about the size of coat buttons, with flat, translucent bodies that reveal cold-adapted, copper-tinted hemolymph pulsing within. They do not crawl so much as they shiver, packed side-by-side like tiles on a roof. There are millions of them in this tiny room. They feed upon the condensed heavy-metal lubricants that weep from the seals, and they drink the freezing water that slides over the chromium. 

Because the sound in the room is so deafening and unchanging, the mites have synchronized their internal biology to its frequency. They do not move individually; rather, a collective tremor passes through the white sheet of their bodies in perfect time with the piston's *thrum-shudder*. When the amber light of the single, recessed sodium-vapor lamp behind its wire cage catches them, the surface of the walls seems to ripple like a greasy, pale-pink liquid. 

Beneath this carpet of mites, in the tight, wet gaps where the steel plates meet, thin wire-worms—blind, silver, and no thicker than a sewing needle—thread themselves through the seams. They move in slow, endless loops, clearing the dust of microscopic metal shavings that flake off from the high-pressure fittings.

Nothing here changes. The light remains a dim, stagnant mustard-yellow, never flickering, casting long, unmoving shadows of the pipe-clusters across the shivering floor. The cold mist continues to bead on the razor-sharp welds. The mites continue to vibrate in their millions against the freezing iron. It is a tiny, hidden cathedral of noise and wet metal, perfectly preserved at the top of the sky, operating under absolute rules of pressure and stasis that have no need for a world outside.
