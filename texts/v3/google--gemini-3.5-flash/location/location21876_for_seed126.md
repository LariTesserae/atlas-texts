The sky does not end in a curve; it dissolves into a flat, blinding-white horizon that remains at exactly eye level, no matter how far one walks.

This is the Platform. It is an endless, low-lying expanse of engineered substrate that stretches across a world-sized surface, built so long ago that its materials have entered their final, stable state of decay. There is no soil here, no clay, no bedrock. The ground is a titanic mosaic of hexagonal tiles, each several meters wide, made of a dense, vitrified ceramic that feels like warm, dry horn to the touch. Under the intense, omnidirectional glare of a sky that has no visible sun but glows with a uniform, soft brilliance, the tiles gleam with a flat, pearlescent lustre.

Eons of micro-abrasion—caused not by sand, which does not exist here, but by a fine, electrostatic soot—have worn down every sharp edge. The seams between the tiles, once perhaps razor-thin joins in an incomprehensible planetary machine, have been smoothed into shallow, gentle troughs. In some places, the tiles have melted together under the patient pressure of deep geological time, forming vast, glazed plains that ripple like pale, frozen cream.

The environment is remarkably warm, holding a dry, radiating heat that rises from the floor plates. It is a sterile warmth, completely devoid of the scent of damp earth or rotting leaves. Instead, the air carries a faint, dry smell of hot quartz, ozone, and old, stable polymers.

Though the horizon is flat and the elevation is low, the landscape is far from empty. It is intensely dense, crowded not with mountains or trees, but with an endless, repeating architecture of low-profile technical forms. Every square meter of the Platform is built. Translucent domes of yellowed, scratch-resistant mineral-glass rise like bubbles from the surface, housing silent, intricate arrays of copper-colored rods. Long, flat conduits, polished to a dull silver by the warm winds, run in parallel lines toward the horizon, occasionally dipping into shallow trenches or intersecting at complex, multi-tiered junctions. These layers go deep; looking down through the gaps in the shifting tiles, one can see tier upon tier of inactive machinery, conduits, and dark, glass-like fibers, extending miles into the dry gut of the world.

There is no water. The concept of liquid has no place in this ecology. The weather is dry, driven by thermal cycles and electromagnetism. Twice during each diurnal phase, the ground plates polarize. A quiet, shimmering curtain of violet static rises from the seams of the tiles, standing three feet high like a field of glowing grass. When this happens, a low, sub-audible vibration hums through the soles of the feet—not a sound, but a physical pressure that makes the teeth ache slightly.

In this dry, brilliant world, vegetation has adapted as a mineral phenomenon. In the shallow troughs between the tiles, where the electrostatic soot has gathered into thin, dry drifts, grow sparse clusters of "glass-weeds." These are not organic plants but fine, hair-like filaments of flexible silica and selenium. They do not have leaves; instead, they sprout tiny, hexagonal plates of blue-grey crystal that turn slowly to track the brightest quadrant of the glowing sky. They make a faint, dry *tink-tink* sound when the thermal wind stirs them, like tiny glass beads spilling onto a stone floor.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, and what little remains has been integrated into the maintenance of the platform. Once every few miles, one might encounter a "spinner." These are flat, segments of dark, warm ceramic, no larger than a shield, that glide millimetres above the tiles on a cushion of magnetic repulsion. They have no eyes or limbs; they move with an agonizing, patient slowness, their undersides whispering against the ceramic as they scrape away the electrostatic soot and deposit it into the deep, dry vents of the platform. They are completely indifferent to visitors, moving around any obstacle with a slow, clumsy dignity before returning to their infinite, silent cleaning.

For all its scale and the complexity of its underlying systems, the world is hushed. Under the blinding, shadowless sky, the only sounds are the rhythmic, sighing gusts of the warm wind, the occasional glass click of the silica-weeds, and the deep, silent breath of the platform’s ancient internal systems—systems that continue to cycle through their routines, long after the programmers have drifted into the same dry dust that blankets the tiles.
