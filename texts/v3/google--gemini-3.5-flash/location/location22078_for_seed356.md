The atmosphere here does not sit; it presses. It is a warm, unvarying weight, thick with the scent of wet starch, raw turpentine, and the sharp, mineral tang of copper-rich water. There is no sky. Above, there is only an infinite vault of interlocking, ribbed cellulose—a ceiling of black-green plates pressed together so tightly that not a single draft of external air, nor a single photon of astronomical light, has ever slipped through. 

This is a world made entirely of plant, but it is not a forest. It is an engine of continental scale, constructed from wood, sap, and fiber, operating in absolute, pitch-black silence.

The floor is not soil, for soil requires decay, and here nothing dies and nothing rots. Instead, the substrate is a vast, continuous plain of living, corrugated bark, hard as cured ironwood and slick with a fine, oily sheen. The terrain is low-lying, flat, but textured with incredibly sharp, fresh features. Because there is no wind, no rain-wash, and no animal life to wear them down, the edges of this world are razor-keen. Newly grown ridges of crystalline resin rise from the floor like obsidian shards, their angles geometrical and perfect. They form hexagonal chambers, long aqueducts, and deep, rectangular sumps, all shaped by the self-sculpting habit of the vegetation.

Everything is packed close. To move here is to squeeze between fluted column-trunks that rise mere inches from one another, or to duck beneath low, horizontal conduits of swollen bark. These conduits—mile-long pipes of living xylem—are under immense hydrostatic pressure. If one were to press an ear to the dark, wet wood, there would be no wood-sound, but rather a deep, sub-audible vibration: the infinite, mechanical throb of millions of gallons of sap being pumped at high pressure through the plumbing of a continent. 

It is an industrial landscape grown from seed. Great, bulbous structures resembling colossal pistons sit nested inside woody sleeves. They do not move with the frantic speed of iron machinery, but remain locked in a state of high-tension equilibrium, holding back forces of incredible hydraulic weight. Elsewhere, massive, tightly braided cable-vines, as thick as ancient oaks and tensioned to the ringing point, span the darkness between pillars, stabilizing the immense weight of the vaults above. There are no chimneys, yet from high-pressure leaf-valves, hot, sterile steam occasionally vents into the dark, condensing instantly on the cold, sharp edges of the resin-spikes.

The water does not fall; it weeps. It exists as a constant, heavy sweat upon every surface. Large, spherical drops of water, thickened by plant mucilage into a gel-like consistency, cling to the undersides of the conduits, swelling until they are too heavy to hold, then falling with a slow, viscous *clop* into the black wells below. These wells are perfectly still. Their surfaces are like dark glass, reflecting nothing but the absolute void above them. There are no ripples, no currents, and no splash, for the water is too heavy and the air too still.

There is no sound here but this occasional, rhythmic dripping and the low, low hum of the hydraulic pressure—a sound so deep it is felt in the bones rather than heard by the ear. It is a quiet that has never been broken. There are no insects to click in the dark, no birds to nest in the vaults, no burrowing things to stir the black pools. The world is sterile of animal life, occupied only by the unceasing, unfeeling metabolism of the green machines.

And nothing changes.

Time here has no teeth. Because there are no seasons, no day and night, and no life-cycles of birth and death, the landscape exists in a frozen, eternal present. The leaves—dense, needle-like clusters of dark, waxy crystal—do not drop. They do not turn yellow, nor do new shoots sprout to replace them. The system reached its optimum configuration of pressure, chemical synthesis, and structural support eons ago, and there it stopped. It does not grow larger, for there is no space left to occupy; it does not shrink, for its resources are recycled with perfect, closed-loop efficiency through the organic pipes.

The only hint of illumination is not light, but a cold, chemical memory of it. At the joints where the great xylem-pipes meet the vertical columns, a faint, bioluminescent smear of dull, oily purple clings to the bark. It does not cast shadows; it does not show the way. It merely outlines, in the dimmest possible terms, the sheer, towering geometry of the columns, rising up and up into the warm, wet weight of the silent dark.
