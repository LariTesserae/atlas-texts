The descent into the Sinking Rift does not end in stone, but in timber. 

Three hundred meters below the rim of the plateau, the limestone walls taper and vanish, swallowed completely by a colossal, self-contained architecture of wood. This is not a forest in the traditional sense, but an unbroken, subterranean solid of ancient, slow-growing ironwood that has filled the great rift from wall to wall. It acts as both floor and ceiling, mountain and valley. The terrain underfoot is composed of massive, horizontal trunk-roads and ribbed plates of petrified, dark-grey bark, frozen so hard by the ambient sub-zero temperatures that they ring like cast iron under a boot.

The scale is immense, cavernous, yet strangely empty. Because the growth is so massive, the individual supports—the primary pillars of this wooden world—are spaced far apart, leaving vast, cold voids of dark air between them. You can stand on a ridge of dense, fibrous knotwood and look down into a gulf three miles long, where the only visual anchors are the distant, vertical columns of sister-trunks rising up into a ceiling of frozen, black needles and interlocking limbs.

The temperature is an absolute, dry cold that needles the sinus. Yet, there is water. It exists in tiny, highly localized pockets—condensation that gathers on the undersides of the great wooden vaults and falls in sporadic, heavy drops, or small, black pools of tannin-heavy water that sit in the hollows of the bark. Because this underground wood-world is geologically young—formed perhaps only a few centuries ago when a sudden geological subsidence dropped a thriving ecosystem into this deep, cold sink—the water channels have not had time to wear smooth paths. Instead, the rills are sharp, jagged fissures split by frost-wedging, cutting clean, raw, splintered runnels through the fibrous peat. 

The air is dim. The only natural light is a faint, pale grey that sifts down from the distant, mile-wide mouth of the rift, casting long, sharp-edged shadows. In the deep sections, this daylight fails entirely, replaced by the occasional flickering yellowish hue of low-voltage utility bulbs strung along heavy copper cables. 

These cables belong to a sparse but heavy industrial presence. Every half-mile or so, the silent majesty of the timber is interrupted by human engineering: small, zinc-roofed generator shacks bolted directly into the heartwood of the pillars; suspension bridges of rusted steel wire spanning the dark abysses; and massive, iron-housed condensation collectors that hum with slow, mechanical purpose. These machines are built of heavy plates, riveted together and stained with frozen grease, designed to run for decades without intervention.

But the defining characteristic of the Sinking Rift is not its size or its cold. It is the noise.

The sound is deafening, a physical pressure that vibrates through the soles of the feet and rattles the cartilage of the ribs. It is a double-voiced roar. The first is mechanical: the distant but massive ventilation turbines mounted miles away at the rift’s blocked northern bottleneck—huge, eight-bladed fans designed to prevent cold damp from rotting the structural heartwood. They scream with a continuous, high-pitched mechanical howl. The second voice is organic: the deep, seismic groaning of the frozen wood itself. Millions of tons of timber, flexing under the slow, crushing weight of the shifting cliffs above, creak and pop. When a major structural limb settles, the sound is like a cannon shot, echoing through the empty space for minutes, followed by the soft, dry hiss of falling frost-needles.

Nothing moves quickly here. The plants do not sway; there is no wind other than the static, industrial draft of the turbines. The trees, locked in their freezing subterranean vault, grow by fractions of a millimeter each decade. The ice on the black pools does not melt, but slowly thickens, trapping the occasional fallen needle in perfect, glassy amber.

Even the fauna has conformed to this slow, deafening stasis. White wood-boring beetles, the size of a man’s hand and covered in a thick, insulating down, cling to the bark near the warm copper conduits of the generators. They move so slowly that they appear to be part of the wood itself, their legs lifting and setting down once every several minutes, indifferent to the thunderous mechanical roar that fills the cold, dark air around them.
