The plateau does not end. Under a sky the color of cold charcoal, the high tables of weathered basalt stretch for thousands of miles, flat-topped and sheer-sided, separated by chasms so wide that the opposite rims are lost in the warm, perpetual gloom. 

The air here is thin but heavy, carrying a constant, humid warmth that smells faintly of peat, wet iron, and scorched tallow. It is not a burning heat, but the deep, radiating warmth of a massive engine room that has been left to idle for centuries. There is no true day. Occasionally, a pale, copper-colored smudge appears in the high haze—the sun, filtered through leagues of suspended soot and moisture—only to vanish without ever rising high enough to cast a sharp shadow.

Water is a rare, precious grease. It does not fall as rain from clouds, but condenses out of the warm air when the high-altitude winds strike the cold iron flanks of the land’s great movers. It gathers in the shallow, wind-scoured depressions of the basalt: black, stagnant pools no larger than a horse, their surfaces skimmed with a film of natural paraffin. Elsewhere, the rock is bone-dry, covered in a fine, velvety dust of volcanic ash and iron filings that clings to the skin like soot.

There are no roads here, no paths, no huts, and no monuments. The land is entirely unbuilt, untouched by the hand of any designer who understood steps, doors, or windows.

And yet, the landscape is industrial. 

Miles apart from one another, separated by vast tracts of empty stone, the *Great Striders* move. They are not vehicles, nor are they monuments; they are part of the geography. These are colossal, blind structures of cast iron, black steel, and riveted bronze, some as large as low hills, that crawl across the basalt plains on massive, multi-jointed piston-legs. They have no cabins, no controls, and no interior spaces meant for breathing things; they are solid, humming blocks of gears, drive-shafts, and slow-turning flywheels. They graze upon the plateau. As they move—at a pace of perhaps a mile a day—their massive undersides scrape the basalt, crushing the rock into powder, extracting trace ores through heat and gravity, and depositing long, cooling ribbons of glassy slag behind them. 

The Striders are weathered. Their iron skin is pitted by centuries of condensation; their joints are thick with green-black grease that has oxidized into a crust as hard as pine resin. They are as much a part of the natural cycle as the wind. 

Around these iron giants, a sparse but stubborn ecology has taken root. 

In the joint-lines of the Striders, where the friction generates a constant, oily heat and the condensation drips continuously, grows a tough, hair-like vegetation known as soot-moss. It is a deep, non-reflective black, absorbing whatever faint light filters through the sky. In the tracks left by the machines, where the basalt has been ground into rich, mineral-dense dust, lonely thickets of wire-reed grow. Their stalks are hollow, segmented tubes of silica that clatter like dry bones when the wind rushes over the plateau.

Fauna is abundant, though it lives in the long shadows. Because the world is dark, none of these creatures have eyes. They navigate by the deep vibrations of the Striders and the warmth of the cooling slag. 

Great herds of legless, segmented slide-grubs—each as thick as an oak trunk—follow several miles behind the machines. They eat the scorched lichens and the mineral salts left in the fresh slag-trails, their bodies shielded by tough, chitinous plates that rattle against the stone. 

Higher up, in the complex, skeletal scaffolding of the Striders’ upper frames, thousands of small, blind leather-wings roost. They resemble hairless, black kites. They do not fly so much as launch themselves into the thermal updrafts created by the machines' vents, drifting on the warm currents to catch the heavy-bodied oil-moths that feed on the Striders' grease.

Down on the stone plains, between the far-separated machines, the silence is immense. The atmosphere is so vast, and the space between things so great, that sound does not carry; it is swallowed by the empty air and the soft carpet of ash. 

Every twenty minutes, however, the silence is subtly altered. The ground shudders. It is a low, bone-deep vibration—the synchronized stroke of a three-hundred-ton piston on a Strider ten leagues to the south, answered seconds later by the faint, metallic *clink* of another giant far to the west. The sound is not loud; it is a pulse, like the heartbeat of the plateau itself.

A traveler on these high plains would walk for days through the warm, damp dark, seeing nothing but the flat, black basalt underfoot and the occasional pool of oily water reflecting the dark sky. Then, over the curve of the horizon, a mountain of iron would slowly rise. It would not notice the traveler. It would lift its massive, grease-slicked leg with a groan of metal that sounds like a winter gale, plant it three cubits forward with a soft, crushing thud, and continue its infinite, solitary work of grinding the world to dust.
