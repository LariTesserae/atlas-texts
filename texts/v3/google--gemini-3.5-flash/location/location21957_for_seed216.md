### The Loom of the Cloud-Sills

The sky here is not an empty blue, nor is it a ceiling. It is a series of infinite, hollow chambers defined by grey-green cables, each timber as thick as a cathedral isle, stretching from one horizon-mist to the next. This is the Loom: a world-sized lattice of living wood, hand-spliced, braided, and knotted over eras so distant that the oldest timbers have fossilized into a state resembling wet green marble.

There is no ground. If there is a bedrock to this world, it lies miles below, sealed beneath a mile-thick layer of dead, compacted peat and the perpetual steam of the lower cisterns. Here, at the high elevation of the Sills, the air is thin but heavy with a warm, muggy breath that smells of boiled tea, resin, and wet feathers. 

#### The Architecture of the Graft

Every surface is plant, and every plant is made. 

To crawl along a road is to crawl along a three-cord plait of living ironwood, its fibers trained over centuries to grow in a spiraling, interlocking rope. Where two great cables cross, they do not merely touch; they have been grafted together by hand. One can see the ancient scars of these unions—gouty, swollen knuckles of bark larger than houses, bound with rotting hanks of coconut-fiber twine and stabilized with wedges of fire-hardened bone. 

The technology of this place is stubborn, manual, and endless. There are no engines, no metal brackets, no gear-works. Instead, there are millions of miles of weathered hemp rope, securing the younger branches to the old. There are thousands of thousands of wooden pegs, driven into drilled cambium with mallets made of river-pebbles lashed to oak roots. The people who tend this place—though they are rarely seen, living in the hollowed-out burls of the vertical trunks—work with flint-bladed adzes and bone needles three feet long. Their progress is measured in inches per generation: a new bridge across a five-mile void is started by a great-grandfather who bends a single green shoot, and finished by a great-granddaughter who ties the dead leader-rope to the far terminal.

The wood is old. It has weathered into deep, grey furrows that hold pools of brown, tannin-rich water in every crease. Lichens and liverworts cover every inch of the top-facing surface, creating a damp, springy turf that muffles every step. 

#### The Wet and the Hollow

Humidity is the primary inhabitant. It does not rain in drops; rather, the clouds drift through the open cells of the lattice like slow, blind whales. As they brush against the rough wool of the mossy cables, they squeeze out their moisture. Water runs everywhere—weeping from the undersides of the great branches, dripping in long, silent chains of pearls into the grey void below, or pooling in the suspended hollows of giant, cup-like leaves that hang like baskets from the joints.

In the vast spaces between the woven highways, there is a profound emptiness. A single cell of the Loom might be five miles wide and ten miles high—a grand, cube-like hall of misty air bounded only by four vertical columns of braided vine and a floor and ceiling of woven thatch. Looking out into these spaces, one feels the scale of an insect inside a wicker basket. The light is a constant, dim twilight, filtered through twenty layers of canopy above. It has no direction; it seems to emanate from the mist itself, a soft, leaden glow that turns the wet bark into a dark slate.

#### The Softening of Rules

In these high, empty voids, the laws of the physical world have grown damp and loose, like old twine left too long in the rain.

Gravity does not pull toward a center; it pulls toward the nearest mass of wood. If you slip from a high cable, you do not fall forever into the steam; you drift slowly toward the nearest vertical column, your descent curving like a leaf on a draft, until you emerge into its gravitational skin and cling to its wet moss. 

Water behaves with a strange, liquid curiosity. In the corners where three cables meet, giant spheres of water—the size of haystacks—hover without falling, held in place by the mutual breath of the leaves. These "wells" are perfectly clear and warm; silver-bodied fish with feather-like gills swim from the interior of the wood, through the bark, and into these floating globes, then back again without breaking their motion.

Time, too, behaves like wet wood. In the deep hollows of the burls, where the air is perfectly still and smells of yeast, a traveler might sit for an hour to mend a leather sandal and emerge to find that the vine they were resting on has grown three feet and grown green fruit, while their companions have grown no older at all.

#### Silence and the Silent Lives

The world is remarkably quiet, its soundscape absorbed by the infinite mattress of moss and wet leaf-mold. The few sounds that survive are small:
* The rhythmic, heavy *tock... tock... tock* of a water-heavy branch bending and releasing its load into a lower pool.
* The wet, soft hiss of a cloud sliding through a mile of pine-needle thatch.
* The occasional, deep *groan* of the entire lattice shifting its weight against the wind—a low-frequency vibration that is felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard in the ears.

Yet, the Sills are teeming with life. Thousands of species of moths, some with wingspans like sails, drift through the empty cells. They have no mouthparts; they live only to lay eggs in the rotting heartwood of the joins and die, their velvet bodies falling into the mist like giant, grey leaves. 

Small, lungless lizards with six legs cling to the undersides of the cables, their skin matching the speckled green of the cup-rafts. They do not chirp or hiss; they communicate by tapping their ribs against the bark, sending private, rhythmic codes through the wood that only those connected to the same branch can feel.

Occasionally, one passes a "cradle"—a massive, spherical nest woven from green willow-whips, suspended in the center of a void by a hundred guide-lines of twisted rawhide. Inside, something large and warm is breathing. You can see the steam of its respiration rising from the air-holes in the weave, joining the general mist of the Sills, silent and slow.
