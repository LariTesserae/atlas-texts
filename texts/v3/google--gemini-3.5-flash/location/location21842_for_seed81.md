At this altitude, the air should be cold, but it is not. Instead, a heavy, suffocating heat presses down from a sky that is never seen. The sky here is a flat sheet of bruised, dark-grey condensation, hanging low over a landscape of steep basalt terraces and black glass valleys. It is a high-altitude greenhouse, trapped in a permanent, dim twilight where the light is so scattered and weak that shadows have no clean edges; they merely dissolve into the wet, warm air.

The scale of the place is vast, but it is a strange, hollow sort of vastness. Though classified as urban, there is no congestion here. The density is near-zero. Between the monumental works of construction lie miles of empty, gleaming black stone surfaces, swept clean by moisture and wind. It is an industrial landscape built for giants, or perhaps designed to be populated only by the steam itself. 

Gigantic structural grids—iron-black and swollen with years of mineral buildup—rise from the deep chasms. These are not buildings with rooms and windows, but colossal skeletons: towering lattice-works, elevated pipe-gantry roads that span ten miles without a single junction, and circular cisterns the size of small lakes, their rims rimed with orange salt-crusts. The entire region feels like a massive distillation engine that has outlived its creators, continuing to run on its own slow, geothermal clock.

There is no green. Not a single blade of grass, no moss, no lichen clings to the wet masonry. In place of vegetation, the mineral world has mimicked life. Where the warm, chemical-heavy water runs down the flanks of the massive iron pilings, it has deposited thick, dendritic crusts of white silica and pale sulfur. These formations branch like cold, leafless trees, growing in silent, calcified groves along the drainage channels. In some areas, the basalt floors are cracked, and from these fissures grow clusters of long, needle-sharp quartz tubes that whistle faintly when the pressure in the earth shifts.

Everything is wet. The air is so saturated that any surface cooler than the ambient steam instantly breaks out in a heavy, rolling sweat. Water does not fall as rain here; it condenses directly onto the undersides of the great high-altitude gantries, forming fat, greasy drops that hang like pearls before falling into the void below. The paths—when there are paths—are slick, black, and constantly running with shallow, warm rivers. These streams do not erode the land into jagged ravines; they have long since found their permanent courses, polishing the ancient concrete and volcanic glass into smooth, undulating channels that lead to the great central reservoirs.

The quiet is profound, yet it is not a dead silence. The atmosphere is too thick for high-frequency sounds, swallowing them within a shifting mist. Instead, the landscape speaks in low, muffled murmurs. There is the wet, heavy *plip-plop* of condensation hitting metal basins hundreds of feet below. There is the deep, stomach-dropping sigh of steam venting from some miles-distant pipe, a sound like a great beast clearing its throat in another room. Occasionally, the mountain itself shudders slightly, and the massive, rusted iron counterweights of some slow-moving mechanical weir will creak, shifting three inches over the course of an hour with a dry, metallic scream that is instantly dampened by the fog.

Life is exceedingly rare, and what exists does not belong to the familiar categories of the world below. Sometimes, on the wet underbelly of the elevated pipes, one can see the slow, pulsing movement of "grease-slugs"—large, translucent, fluid-filled sacs that have no eyes or limbs. They cling to the hot metal, slowly absorbing the sulfurous grease and mineral grime that coats the machinery. High above, in the iron rafters that disappear into the grey ceiling of mist, long-bodied, wingless gliders—creatures resembling ribbon-like parchment—drift on the rising plumes of hot air, falling from girder to girder in absolute silence, seeking the warmth of the steam vents.

This is a world in equilibrium with its own decay. The immense iron pipes and concrete basins have been weathered to a state of geological permanence. The rust has thick, stable layers that protect the metal beneath; the concrete has been calcified by the hard water until it is tougher than the basalt it rests upon. There is no sense of ruin here, only of an eternal, lonely functioning. The water rises as hot steam from the cracked belly of the high plateau, climbs into the dark metal rafters of the empty sky, condenses into greasy black droplets, and slides down the rusted skin of the silent towers, back into the earth. It has done this for centuries. It will do this until the heat deep in the stone finally goes cold.
