The air inside this pocket is so hot and thick with moisture that it behaves like a wet lung. 

We are suspended in a single, five-meter-wide chamber—the *Hollow-of-the-Loom*—perched at an altitude so extreme that the atmosphere outside the wicker walls should be freezing and thin, yet here it is a sweltering, pressurized steam bath. There is no earth underfoot. There is no stone. Everything around, above, and below has been built, but built with the frantic, clumsy haste of a species using only thumbs, fiber, and river-silt.

The floor is a dense, springy raft of green timber poles, split by hand and bound with coarse, hairy vines that still bleed bitter white sap. The gaps between the logs are packed with raw, red clay that has never known a dry day. It is embryonic masonry, so fresh that the deep, double-handed finger-grooves of its makers are still sharp, wet troughs where hot, mineral-heavy water pools and bubbles. There is no erosion here; the world has just been slapped together. The edges of the wood are sharp-splintered and green; the clay has not been smoothed by wind or worn by feet. It is a space still slick with its own creation.

Because the space is so small and packed, there is no room to stand fully upright without pressing one’s shoulders into the weeping ceiling. The ceiling is a dome of woven osier and beaten copper plates—crude sheets of metal hammered out with round river-rocks, showing every uneven blow of the stone. Through the seams of these plates, hot water weeps constantly. It does not fall straight down.

Here, gravity is unstable, buckled by the extreme pressure of the height or some regional breakdown of physical laws. The water drips *upward* from the floor-crevices in fat, lazy beads, drifting toward the copper dome like reverse rain, only to hit the metal, condense into hot streams, and crawl sideways across the ceiling before spilling horizontally into the center of the room. It forms a central, hovering globule of warm, brown water that turns slowly in the air, suspended by the conflicting currents of the chamber’s heat.

Through this floating knot of water, the light is bent and scattered. The illumination is dim, a twilight of muddy oranges and bruised greens. It filters through thick panes of translucent animal bladder stretched across tiny, round port-holes, and through the dense canopy of vegetation that choking-ly fills every corner of the room. 

The plants do not merely grow here; they are structural. Thrown into the wet clay as seed or braided into the cordage while still living, they have exploded under the influence of the heat. Fleshy ferns with thick, tongue-like fronds that feel like warm leather crowd the corners, their undersides coated in a gray, velvety fuzz that drips grease. Moss—saturated like a drowned sponge—swells from every joint in the log-floor, pulsing visibly as it drinks the rising steam. The air smells of boiled cabbage, wet rope, hot tin, and the sharp, ozone tang of lightning.

And the sound is relentless. 

It is a loud, vibrating roar that comes not from the wind outside, but from the very pipes of the chamber. Primitive conduit systems—hollowed-out birch logs wrapped in raw hide—run along the walls like exposed veins. They hiss and whistle as boiling water and pressurized steam are forced upward from some unimaginable depth below. The floor underfoot thrums with a deep, low-frequency growl, like a massive wooden gear grinding against wet clay. Occasionally, a loud, wet *thump* echoes through the timbers as a pocket of air passes through the water-lines, shaking the hanging ferns and sending a shower of warm droplets flying horizontally across the space.

The world outside the bladder-windows does not offer relief; it offers a spatial paradox. If you press your face to the wet membrane of the eastern port-hole, you do not look out onto a mountain range or an open sky of clouds. Instead, through the thick, swirling mountain-steam, you look directly across a twenty-foot gap into *this same room*, turned sideways. You can see the copper dome of your own ceiling acting as the western wall of the opposite structure, and if you wave your hand, a figure through the steam waves back three seconds later, its movements slightly sluggish, as if traveling through a thicker draft of time.

Fauna is nearly non-existent in this hot, wet box, save for the *valve-lice*. They are small, flat creatures the size of a human palm, with shells that look like wet, charcoal-colored slate. They have no eyes or legs; they live by clamping their circular mouths over the leaking seams of the steam pipes, their rubbery bodies rising and falling like crude leather bellows as they strain the mineral-crust from the whistling vapor. When the steam pressure rises too high, they let out a high-pitched, flatulent squeal, releasing the excess gas before their shells can crack.

Nothing here is static. The water cycle is so accelerated that a cup of water spilled on the floor is drawn up into the ceiling-globule within ten minutes, only to be absorbed by the tongue-ferns, sweated out as steam from their stomata, and condensed back onto the copper walls. The clay walls are constantly sliding down in slow, red mud-glides, only to be pushed back up by the growth of the aggressive moss roots that bind them. 

It is a place built by hand, kept alive by tension and steam, suspended in an attic of reality where the rules of the earth have been forgotten, and only the heat remains.
