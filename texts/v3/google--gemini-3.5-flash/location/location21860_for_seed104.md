Here, the sky is not air, but a warm, heavy suspensor of emerald-tinted water that has long since swallowed the peaks of the ancient world. 

We are at the summit of things, though there is no sky above to prove it. The elevation is immense—a high, drowned plateau that sits above a forgotten, toxic base-atmosphere miles below. Up here, the water is pure, amniotic, and thick. It is a vast, floating ocean held in place by laws of gravity that have grown tired and lenient with age. The temperature is a constant, humid warmth, like the inside of a closed flower.

### The Terrain

Beneath the endless, dim-lit volume of water lie the mountaintops. They are not jagged or sharp. Eons of slow, circular currents have ground them down into massive, pillow-like domes of grey basalt and dark green serpentine. These peaks are like the backs of colossal, sleeping beasts, smoothed and polished until they reflect the faint, internal glow of the water itself. 

There are no cliffs, only long, undulating slopes that descend into the hazy green distance. Every dip in the stone is filled with a dense, fine silt that has the texture of velvet. If you disturb this silt, it does not rise in a cloud; instead, it rolls down the slopes in slow, heavy ribbons, behaving more like mercury than mud, before settling back into its ancient grooves.

### The Atmosphere and Light

Light does not fall from above in rays; it is suspended in the medium itself. The water is saturated with a tiny, self-luminous phytoplankton that glows with a persistent, copper-green twilight. It is a dim, permanent afternoon. There are no shadows, only gradients of density. Where the moss grows thickest, the green deepens into near-black; where the water is clear, it is a pale, warm amber. 

The pressure is high but comforting—a steady, wrap-around weight that removes the burden of posture. One does not walk across the domes; one drifts slightly above them, nudged by thermoclines that move through the space like slow, massive drafts in an old house.

### The Vegetation

The plant life is luxurious, heavy, and silent. It does not grow upward toward a sun; it reaches outward, following the warm chemical rivers that thread through the water. 

Colossal kelp-forests, their stalks as thick as ancient oaks and soft as skin, form vast, horizontal networks. They do not have roots. Instead, they hold onto the smooth stone domes with massive, suction-cupped pads. Their leaves are wide, purple-grey ribbons that drift in the current, whisper-soft, constantly shedding a fine, sweet-smelling mucilage that keeps the water thick and slow-moving.

In the valleys between the stone domes, giant bladder-ferns grow in dense, tangled clumps. These plants are built of pale, translucent spheres the size of carriage wheels, each one filled with a warm, dry gas. Within these spheres, one can see tiny, mossy ecosystems thriving in the dry interior, completely sealed off from the wet world outside—microcosms of dry land, floating in the deep.

### The Weirdness (The Breakdown)

The physics of this place have softened under the weight of its age. Gravity does not have a single destination. In certain pockets of the water, "down" shifts ninety degrees to the left; you can find yourself swimming horizontally along the side of a basalt dome as if it were a flat plain, while the kelp-forests beside you stand out horizontally like flags in a stiff breeze.

Distance, too, is dream-like. The water possesses a strange refractive quality that distorts space. A ruined stone archway that appears to be miles away through the amber haze can sometimes be reached in three or four slow strokes of the arms. Conversely, a patch of glowing moss at your fingertips may require minutes of crawling toward it, the space stretching out like chewing gum as you move.

Time does not tick; it pools. The water holds memory. Occasionally, you will swim through a "pocket" of old water that tastes of cold pine needles or dry dust—remnants of the atmosphere from before the flood, preserved like bubbles in amber. Inside these pockets, you can hear the faint, dry rustle of wind through leaves that died a million years ago, only for the sound to vanish as you pass back into the warm, silent wet.

### The Built Elements

The marks of mind are rare, ancient, and nearly reclaimed by the vegetation. There are no cities, only single, inscrutable gestures.

On the highest dome of the plateau sits a series of giant, jade-colored stone rings. They are fifty feet in diameter, suspended in the water by nothing at all, floating in a perfect, vertical line. They are worn as smooth as river pebbles, encrusted with tiny, star-shaped yellow anemones. They do not hum or glow; they simply exist, aligning with some long-vanished star that once shone down before the water became the sky.

In other places, one finds staircases carved directly into the basalt domes. They wrap around the mounds in wide, gentle spirals, leading upward into the empty water, ending abruptly in mid-medium. The steps are so deeply eroded by the currents that they look like melting wax.

### Fauna

Animal life is nearly absent, having surrendered the world to the plants. There are no fish, no darting creatures, no sharp teeth. 

Once in a great while, a *Skein* will pass. These are the only large animals left: incredibly long, ribbon-like beings, perhaps three miles in length and only a few inches wide. They are completely transparent, visible only by the way they warp the light and the slow, rhythmic pulsing of their violet nervous systems. They do not swim; they drift along the thermoclines, absorbing nutrients directly through their skin. As they pass, they emit a low, vibrations-only sound—a sub-bass thrum that vibrates in the bones of your chest, lasting for hours after they have gone.

### The Sound and Dynamics

The dominant state is silence, but it is not the hollow silence of a vacuum. It is a dense, muffled quiet, like being wrapped in thick wool. The only sounds are the occasional, wet *pop* of a gas-fern releasing a bubble of sweet air, the dull, tectonic sigh of the plateaus settling under the weight of the water, and the brushing of your own skin against the warm medium.

The world is not static, however. It operates on a slow, biological cycle. Every few weeks, a "Spore-Tide" occurs. The kelp-forests release billions of tiny, glowing blue seeds. For days, the warm water is filled with a dense, glittering snow that drifts horizontally across the plains of smooth stone. During this time, the water grows even warmer, smelling faintly of jasmine and wet copper. The landscape becomes a single, breathing lung, inhaling the nutrients, exhaling the blue light, before settling once more into its ancient, amber-green sleep.
