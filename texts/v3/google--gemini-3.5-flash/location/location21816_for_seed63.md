High on the shoulder of a dead, volcanic horn, well above the reach of any weather that carries moisture, lies the Basin of the Floor.

It is a small place—scarcely larger than a parish churchyard—sunk into a depression between basalt ribs. No water runs here. The air has the heavy, bone-dry kiln-warmth of a baker’s oven, smelling faintly of heated iron, bitter salt, and the sharp, vinegar-sting of dry chemical decay. 

There is no sky to speak of, only a high, heavy ceiling of dry, black dust that hangs suspended in the thin air, snuffing out the stars. The light is a flat, soot-colored dark. The only illumination is a rare, sluggish copper spark that drifts upward from the volcanic fissures below, dying before it reaches the rim.

Here, the earth is not made of stone or soil, but of movement. 

At first glance, the ground appears to be a shifting, irregular shale of deep purple and charcoal-grey. But the shale is alive. The entire basin is packed, wall-to-wall, edge-to-edge, with an overwhelming, suffocating density of small, dry-bodied fauna. There are no plants here; no root has ever found purchase in these hot crevices, nor has any leaf unfolded to catch the non-existent sun. Instead, the basin is a living, non-aqueous engine of chitin, plate, and thread.

They are flat, segmented creatures, the size of hand-spans, overlapping like slate tiles on a roof. They slide over and under one another in a state of constant, fluid pressure. There is no empty space. To stand in the basin is to stand upon a writhing, three-foot-deep carpet of hard-shelled life that fills every depression, rises up the walls of the basalt cliffs, and pools in the corners like dark liquid.

The sound they make is not a roar or a chatter, but a vast, whispering dry friction. It is the sound of millions of brittle, polished carapaces sliding over one another—a soft, rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck* that never ceases. It sounds like grain dropping into a deep silo, or a dry gravel slide that never quite comes to rest. Occasionally, there is the sharp, tiny *snip* of a tooth against shell, or the dry whistle of a spiracle breathing the hot, sulfurous air, but these are small details lost in the sea of friction.

The rules of this place are simple, closed, and ancient. 

The basalt walls surrounding the basin are smoothed and grooved—not by the action of water or wind, but by the relentless, million-year rubbing of these millions of bodies climbing, slipping, and wearing the mountain down. This is an old, settled system. The creatures do not starve; they feed upon the fine, lime-rich dust that sheers off the cliffs and the continuous, silent rain of organic soot that drifts down from the higher, unseen peaks. 

They are active in a slow, circular dance. Across the floor, great slow eddies form—swirls where the creatures are sucked down into the deeper layers of the pile to molt, while others are pushed outward to the margins to cool against the stone. 

In the center of this shifting mass stand the only marks of anything prior: three small, circular enclosures built of dry-stacked basalt slabs. They are ancient, ruined structures, half-collapsed, their stones licked round and glass-smooth by the passage of billions of hard bellies over the centuries. They look like wells, or small altars, but they are empty. Now, the living floor rises and falls against them like tide against a breakwater, occasionally spilling over the low rims to fill the small, dark interiors with a boiling mass of legs and plates before crawling out again. There are no tools here, no signs of the hands that stacked those stones, only the smooth, unyielding basalt and the beasts that wear it away.

In the dark, a localized pattern emerges: when one segment of the floor is pressed too tightly, a faint, cold-green phosphorescence flashes briefly between the plates—a spark of friction-light that ripples across the basin like dry lightning across a summer plain, gone in a heartbeat, leaving the valley in its warm, whispering dark once more.
