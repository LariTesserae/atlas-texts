To stand at the edge of the plateau of Olaus is to look upon a metropolis of water and stone that has forgotten the concept of the tool. 

The scale is continental. From the high, thin-aired vantage of the rim, the landscape stretches to the sharp horizon in a dense, geometric gridlock of dark grey basalt and pale, damp siltstone. It presents the unmistakable silhouette of a city: thousands upon thousands of blocky, vertical towers, stepped terraces, and deep, linear canyons that mimic avenues. Yet there is no steel here, no mortar, no glass, and no copper. These structures — some the size of modest keeps, others rising like windowless, flat-topped skyscrapers hundreds of meters into the cold sky — are packed so closely together that the shadows between them are narrow, dripping slot canyons, barely wide enough for a shoulder to pass through. 

The entire terrace-city is wet with a heavy, constant seepage. At this elevation, the air is cool and thin, but the ground is saturated. Water does not fall as rain so much as it condenses directly onto the cold faces of the stone. Every vertical wall is a weeping wall. Droplets bead on the dark, polished surfaces of basalt, coalescing into tiny silver veins that run down the stone faces to feed the vast network of level-floored channels below. In the "streets" between the towers, the water pools in shallow, silt-bottomed fens. There are no fast currents here; the land is too old, its grades weathered down over geological epochs into a perfect, self-contained balance. The water moves with a lazy, almost imperceptible drift, filling stone basins, spilling over low, hand-flat weirs, and evaporating back into the bright, clear sky.

The vegetation is exceptionally sparse, limited to what can survive on wet rock under a hard sun. There are no trees, no grasses, no flowering plants. Instead, the stone is patterned with thin, incredibly tough films of crustose lichen—hues of lead-grey, dried blood, and sulfur-yellow—that grip the basalt like dried paint. In the corners where the wind deposits fine silt, flat mats of dark, liver-colored moss sit like wet velvet, holding water like sponges. 

Because of the thin air and the absence of wind-catching foliage, the silence is immense. The world here operates at a hushed, micro-acoustic level. Under the bright, normal light of the high sun, the only sound is the liquid choir of the plateau: the infinite, tiny *tink-plip* of condensation dropping from overhead ledges into the standing water of the plazas, and the soft, flat squelch of gas bubbles escaping the silt beds. 

The landscape is not dead, but its dynamics are geological and atmospheric rather than animal. The heat of the sun on the dark stone creates a powerful, silent engine of evaporation. By mid-afternoon, thin ribbons of white mist begin to rise from the damp alleys between the towers, twisting upward like smoke from chimneys. By late afternoon, these mists have organized into a ceiling of low, bright clouds that rest on the flat tops of the monoliths, turning the deep chasms into cold, dripping tunnels. 

Fauna is nearly absent. If one watches a single basin for hours, they might see the slow, gelatinous glide of a slate-grey flatworm across the submerged stone, or the sudden, erratic skip of a tiny springtail on the water’s surface tension. High above, riding the rising thermal currents generated by the dark, sun-warmed blocks, a solitary, wide-winged scavenger might drift, silent as a scrap of charred paper, searching for some rare sign of life that has failed in the higher passes.

There is no trace of the builders of Olaus. If they were biological, they worked in an era before the mountain was lifted to this height, carving the terraces and channel-grids with something simpler than metals—perhaps using only the cleavage planes of the rock and the slow, guided erosion of water. The edges of the stone are rounded and soft now, polished by millions of years of damp wind and the gentle friction of moss. The city has become its own mountain range; the mountain range has become its own city. It is a place that feels entirely complete, balanced in its quiet dampness, waiting for nothing.
