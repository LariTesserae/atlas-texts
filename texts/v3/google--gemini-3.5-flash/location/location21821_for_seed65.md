### The Sky and the Light

At this altitude, the sky is not blue but a dark, saturated indigo, nearly black at the zenith. The air is so thin that it fails to scatter the sun’s rays into a general haze; instead, the light falls in sharp, parallel beams of blinding intensity. The sun is a small, furious white coin. It does not warm the air, which remains cool and thin enough to make the lungs ache, but it heats the surfaces it strikes. 

There are no shadows with soft edges here. Every boulder, cliff-face, and ledge casts a shape of absolute, ink-black darkness, creating a stark, binary landscape of brilliant white and deep void. The glare off the polished rock is constant, forcing any eye to squint or fail.

### The Terrain and Erosion

This is a world of dolomite terraces, suspended countless miles above an invisible lowland. The elevation is extreme; the horizon curve is visible in the distance, dipping slightly at the margins of the sky. Looking down from the rim of the great terraces, there is only a pale blue abyss, empty of clouds, ending in a floor too distant to possess detail.

The stone has achieved an equilibrium of erosion. The wind has spent millions of years carving the cliffs into aerodynamically perfect curves. There are no loose rockfalls, no gravel slopes, and no jagged, fresh breaks. Every surface is smooth, hard, and slightly greasy to the touch, resembling aged bone or candle wax. 

Great horizontal shelves, some three miles wide and twenty miles long, step down the side of the massif like the stairs of a sleeping titan. Each shelf is flat, swept clean of dust by the ceaseless gales, and pierced at regular intervals by vertical shafts—natural blowholes formed by centuries of wind-boring.

### The Sound

The landscape does not merely host sound; it is defined by it. The noise is a solid, physical weight. 

An unchanging, high-altitude gale rushing from the northern void strikes the terrace system at a constant angle. As this air mass passes over the flat plains of stone, it is forced through the thousands of vertical shafts and hollow flutes carved into the cliff faces. The entire mountain range behaves as a colossal pipe organ. 

The sound is a deafening, low-frequency thrum that vibrates the marrow of the bones. It is a roar without variation, a continuous, mechanical rumble like the exhaust of a great engine, overlaid with a high, metallic whistling where the wind shears across the sharp edges of the upper shelves. 

Because the wind never stops, never shifts its direction, and never loses its velocity, this roar is a permanent condition of existence. It is the silence of this world. To hear anything else, one must climb down into the deep, dead-air pockets of the crevasses where the wind cannot reach.

### Water and Vegetation

Water exists only as a phantom. It does not run in streams, nor does it pool on the flat, wind-scoured terraces. Instead, it is found in the deep, narrow fissures that split the dolomite. 

During the brief, cold nights, the temperature drops rapidly. The thin air loses whatever heat it held, and moisture condenses out of the atmosphere, coating the interior walls of the chasms with a thick, glittering rime of frost. 

In the morning, as the fierce sun climbs, the frost melts, sending thin, dark ribbons of dampness trickling down the vertical stone seams. These wet tracks are the only oases in the desert of light.

Clinging to these damp, dark cracks are the only plants that can survive: a species of black, wire-like moss that grows in tight, hard scabs. It has no roots, only tiny pads that glue themselves to the dolomite. It does not grow outward; it spreads in flat, circular rings that take centuries to widen by an inch. The moss is dry and brittle to the touch, feeling more like iron shavings than plant matter, but when the morning meltwater touches it, it softens slightly, turning a dull, bruised purple before the dry wind parches it once more.

### The Fauna and Their Technology

Despite the emptiness of the stone, the sky is crowded. Thousands of large, pale creatures—the *Vanes*—inhabit the cliffs. They are built like glider-planes, with long, narrow wings made of a semi-translucent, leathery membrane that stretches between three jointed fingers. Their skin is pigmentless, a chalky white that renders them nearly invisible against the dolomite when they fold their wings.

The Vanes do not flap. They cannot; their breastbones lack the deep keel for flight muscle. Instead, they ride the permanent updrafts of the mountain face, hanging motionless in the roaring air currents for hours, their black, lidless eyes searching the stone below.

They are communal but silent, communication being impossible over the roar of the wind-organ. They communicate instead through rapid, rhythmic movements of their wingtips, flashing the dark undersides of their flight membranes against the white stone.

They are tool-users, though they build nothing. 

On the flat terraces, in the spots sheltered from the direct force of the gale by low ridges of dolomite, are the *knapping-grounds*. Here, the Vanes gather to work. They search the deep chasms for nodules of black obsidian—a glass-hard, volcanic stone that is not native to these limestone slopes, carried up from the hot depths of the earth by some long-extinct geothermal process.

Using their heavy, parrot-like beaks and their dexterous hind-claws, they strike the obsidian nodules against the hard dolomite, chipping them into sharp, leaf-shaped scrapers. They do not use these tools to kill—they are scavengers of the large, soft-bodied grubs that live deep within the damp moss-fissures—but to pry, scrape, and wedge the hard moss away from the rock to reach the moisture beneath. 

Around these knapping-grounds, piles of black glass shards have accumulated over millennia. Because there is no soil to bury them and no rain to wash them away, these fragments sit in bright, glittering heaps on the white stone, pristine and sharp as the day they were struck. They are the only sign of intellect in the landscape: thousands of neat, black circles of broken glass, spaced at regular intervals along the bone-white terraces.

---

### The Rules of the World

In this place, time feels like an illusion. Because the wind is constant, the light is cloudless, and the geological structures have reached a state of perfect resistance to the elements, there is no change. 

A stone dropped from a terrace falls into the blue void, disappearing without a sound. The white gliders hang in the air, seemingly frozen in place against the dark sky, their wings vibrating with the tension of the gale. The roar of the mountain organ remains at the same decibel, day after day, century after century. 

It is a landscape locked in a cold, bright, deafening equilibrium—a machine that was started so long ago that the concept of a beginning, or an end, has been worn away by the wind.
