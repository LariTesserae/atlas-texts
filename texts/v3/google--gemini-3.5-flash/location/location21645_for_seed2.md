The world is a flooded cage, infinitely wide, cold, and meticulously manufactured. 

Here, there is no seabed of sand or silt. There are no volcanic trenches or limestone continental shelves. At the lowest navigable depths of this world, the floor is an endless, flat plateau of interlocking structural steel plates, zinc-coated grates, and pristine, gray ceramic tiles. Everything has been built. The horizons are defined not by landmasses, but by colossal, vertical trusses—pillars of hollow steel twenty yards thick—that rise from the floor and vanish into the bright, water-logged sky miles above.

Despite the total dominance of the machinery, the space is profoundly empty. The density of these structures is extremely low; a traveler could swim for hours through the cold, pale blue medium without encountering anything but the single girder they departed from and the distant, hazy silhouette of the next, fifteen miles away. The water is exceptionally clear, preserved at a constant, crisp temperature of forty degrees Fahrenheit. Under the high-intensity glow of massive floodlight arrays—grids of thousands of sodium lamps mounted directly to the vertical girders—the deep-water void is illuminated with a hard, surgical clarity. There are no shadows humped with mud or hidden by sea-fog; everything is sharp, clean, and visible across miles of open water.

The structure is young. It has not had time to decay. There are no pitted surfaces, no orange veins of rust flaking into the currents, no warped plates or buckling joints. The welds are clean, showing the neat, stacked-dime pattern of recent robotic fabrication. The stenciled serial numbers—three-foot-tall block letters in industrial yellow—are still sharp and legible through the water.

And yet, this mechanical desert is loud with the business of movement. 

The sound is a heavy, physical presence that vibrates through the bone. It is an orchestra of heavy industry operating at a planetary scale. From somewhere fifty miles to the west, the slow, rhythmic *thrum-shudder* of a massive water-displacement turbine beats a subterranean pulse through the liquid. Closer, the high-pitched, metallic whine of an automated intake valve registers as a constant buzz in the teeth. Every few minutes, a long-travel hydraulic piston somewhere in the overhead scaffolding fires with a deafening, hollow *clack* that echoes through the vast empty corridors of the grid, followed by the long, rushing sigh of pressurized water clearing a ballast line.

These mechanical processes generate powerful, predictable weather systems within the sea. There are no tides governed by a moon, but there are "drafts"—rivers of fast-moving, icy water drawn toward distant intake screens. These drafts function as wind, bending the sparse biological growth that has managed to take hold in the seams of the metal plates. 

The vegetation is minimal, opportunistic, and tough. In the narrow, millimeter-wide gaps between the floor tiles where organic sediment has managed to settle, thin ribbons of dark green bladderwrack and pale, threadlike hair-algae find a foothold. They do not form forests or meadows; they exist as sparse, lonely fringes of green lining the cold joints of the machine, shivering violently whenever a high-speed draft passes over them.

But where the plants struggle, marine animals have discovered an artificial paradise. 

The structural steel has become an infinite reef. Millions of blue mussels and barnacles have anchored themselves to the vertical faces of the trusses, their small shells forming a jagged, protective sleeve around the base of the metal columns. In the vast voids between the girders, life moves in enormous, glittering clouds. Schools of silver herring and Atlantic cod, millions strong, use the open spaces of the grid to migrate, their movements coordinated in perfect, sweeping curves that catch the yellow glare of the floodlights like spilling coins. 

Dogfish and small, sleek sharks patrol the empty water between the pillars, hunting the schools with a quiet, mundane efficiency. They are joined by thousands of common squid that hover in the bright light cones of the sodium lamps, their chromatophores pulsing with neutral, quiet grays as they wait for smaller prey to be drawn into the light by the artificial currents.

There is nothing magical or strange about this place. The water behaves exactly as water should; the fish eat, sleep, and breed as they would in any natural ocean; the machinery operates on simple, heavy-gauge pneumatics and electrical currents. It is merely a world where the natural earth was never invited, or where it was paved over entirely before the pumps were turned on.

If you float in the center of one of the great twelve-mile squares of the grid, suspends half a mile above the floor plates and three miles below the ceiling, the world feels like a silent, brightly lit cathedral of glass and lead. The water around you is still and empty. But if you press your ear against the cool casing of a nearby coaxial cable running down into the dark, you can hear the planet-machine breathing—a low, mechanical groan that never sleeps, driving the cold currents forward through the endless, illuminated iron.
