The world here does not have a crust of granite or sandstone; it has a deck. 

For miles in every direction, the landscape is an endless, low-lying expanse of interlocking iron plates, heavy steel grating, and colossal, poured-concrete slabs. There is no soil. Yet, under the influence of unimaginable age, this entirely manufactured environment has begun to mimic the geomorphic collapse of an old-world marsh. 

The plate-joints and expansion seams, meant to remain flush, have long since buckled and sagged. The weight of millennia has pressed the low-lying plains into shallow, wide depressions where the metal has bowed downward, forming artificial basins. In these hollows, water gathers. It is a slow, cold, condensing world—the temperature hovers in a perpetual drafty chill that draws water out of the dark air and lays it in a slick, greasy film over every vertical surface. 

Everything drops. Cold rain, heavy with the taste of sulfur and dissolved zinc, sifts down from a sky that is not a sky at all, but a distant, vaulted ceiling lost in the near-total darkness. The ceiling weeps. Hidden high in the gloom, miles above the deck, massive pipelines—some as wide as highway tunnels—have cracked under their own weight. From these fractures, black waterfalls plunge into the abyss, breaking into fine, freezing spray before they ever hit the ground.

The sound is not a background element; it is an physical presence that occupies the ears, the throat, and the hollow chambers of the bones. It is a deafening, layered roar. Miles away, monstrous, automated pumping stations—resembling hollow, black iron cathedrals—struggle against the rising water. Their pistons, three stories high, slam down with rhythmic, earth-shaking thuds that send circular ripples across the miles of shallow pools. Steam safety valves, frozen in the "open" position, scream into the dark with a high, whistle-like hiss that never wavers. Underneath it all is the deep, structural groan of the deck itself, shifting and settling as the iron supports far beneath the water basin slowly fatigue and shear.

Because there is no natural substrate, the vegetation that has taken hold is highly specialized, sparse, and strange. It appears in the crevices where the concrete slabs have crumbled into grey, alkaline gravel, or where the iron plates have exfoliated into thick flakes of red rust. It consist of "rust-moss"—a dry, fibrous scale that is orange-brown and smells of copper—and long, hair-like black weeds that grow in dense, greasy tresses from the open drainage grates. These weeds do not seek sunlight, for there is none; they thrive on the low-voltage electrical currents that still leak through the wet, deteriorating copper conduits deep within the floor. They twitch rhythmically when the pumps cycle. 

The horizon is vast, open, and intensely lonely. The density of features is very low; there are no crowds of buildings, only rare, solitary monuments of dead industry. A traveler might walk for hours across the wet, ringing iron flags, through water that rises to the shins, before encountering anything other than the horizon. 

Then, out of the dark mist, a structure will loom: a single, colossal boiler, five hundred feet tall, pitted and blistered with corrosion, standing like a dead volcano. Or a row of colossal, skeletal crane-gantries, their cables dangling into the dark pools like the search-threads of deep-sea anemones. These structures do not feel built so much as geological; they have been weathered by centuries of salt-spray and acid rain until their sharp, machine-carved edges have rounded out into the shapes of wind-worn bluffs. The rivets have dissolved into smooth, soft bumps; the iron ladders have sagged into draped loops like heavy vines.

There are no animals. No birds wheel through the dark, vaporous sky; no fish swim in the chemical-rich pools that collect on the iron plates. The only movement belongs to the water and the machines. 

The dynamic of this place is a slow, mechanical water cycle. The rain falls; it drains through grates into a labyrinthine underworld of wet basements, forgotten holding tanks, and flooded turbine halls; it is heated by the lingering geothermal baseload of the deepest foundations; it rises as steam through massive, screaming chimneys; it condenses on the cold iron sky; and it falls again. It is a closed, automated loop that has forgotten its purpose, operating on a scale so vast that it has become its own climate.

Occasionally, the weirdness of the place reveals itself in the behavior of the pools. Because the deck is layered over endless empty shafts and flooded machine-vaults, the water does not always lie still. Sometimes, a localized failure deep below will cause a pool to drain instantly, sucking down into a grated vortex with a wet, gurgling roar that echoes for miles. At other times, the pressure reverses, and a geothermal geyser of rusty, boiling-hot water geysers thirty feet into the cold, dark air from a corroded manhole, screaming like a dying engine before collapsing back into the cold, quiet marsh of iron.
