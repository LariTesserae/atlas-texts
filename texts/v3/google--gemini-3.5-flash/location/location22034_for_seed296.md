The cliffs here do not crumble. They are made of a dark, fine-grained basalt that has had its sharp edges licked smooth by three hundred million years of mist. There is no loose scree at the base of these walls; everything that could slide, drop, or shatter did so during some forgotten geological epoch. What remains is the core: a series of immense, bulbous spires and deeply fluted hanging basins that sit five thousand meters above sea level, locked in a perpetual, cold communion with the lower clouds.

This is the cleft of Saint-under-Sough. 

The air is thin and tastes of wet iron, chimney soot, and the cold, green scent of crushed liverwort. Because the elevation is extreme, the clouds do not merely pass overhead; they drift through the streets and under the floorboards of the town. They arrive as silent, drifting walls of white that smell of the high snowfields further up the range, leaving a glittering glaze of condensation on every vertical surface.

### The Liturgy of Water

Water is not a torrent here, nor is it a lake. It is a slow, vertical seepage. 

The stone faces of the spires weep. Millions of tiny, microscopic fissures in the ancient basalt act like capillaries, drawing moisture from the passing cloud-banks and expelling it in slow, bead-by-bead sweat. This water does not stream down in waterfalls; it clings to the stone, running through the dense velvet carpet of black-green moss that covers eighty percent of the landscape. 

The inhabitants of Saint-under-Sough have harnessed this weeping. Every building, every walkway, and every industrial tier is fitted with copper catch-lips—gutterings that hug the natural contours of the rock like metal ivy. These lines feed into thousands of lead-soldered cisterns, which drip with a rhythmic, metallic *tink-plip, tink-plip* that forms the underlying heartbeat of the cleft. 

The dampness is absolute but quiet. It is the dampness of wool that never quite dries, of iron hinges that grow thick collars of orange rust, of leather boots that must be greased every three days with mutton tallow to keep them from sprouting white webs of mold overnight.

### The Sough and the Shingle

The town itself is a vertical crust of human presence. It occupies a three-kilometer crescent where two spires lean together, their summits nearly touching like two old monks whispering over a desk. 

The architecture is layered, dense, and heavy. Houses of dark slate and wet pine are stacked five, six, seven tiers high, each one’s roof serving as the foundation or the terrace for the one above it. They are bolted directly into the ancient basalt with dry-fitted iron pins as thick as a man’s thigh. Between the houses run "the crevices"—narrow, stair-stepped alleys where the sky is reduced to a gray thread. 

The technology here is heavy, wet, and rhythmic. It is an industrialism born of coal-smoke and gravity. 

Because the water falls continuously from tier to tier, the town is driven by hundreds of small, sluggish water-wheels made of iron-bound larch. They do not spin quickly; they turn with a heavy, groaning *thrum-click, thrum-click*, their axles lubricated with thick black tallow that smells of sheep and oil. These wheels power the cable-ways—the "creakers"—which pull wooden buckets of coal, wet laundry, and silent, bundled passengers up the sheer faces of the spires.

Every half-hour, a low, leaden bell rings from the boiler-house on the third tier. The boiler is a monstrous, rivet-studded iron cylinder that burns peat and low-grade lignite hauled up from the valleys below. It exists solely to pump low-pressure steam through a web of insulated copper pipes that run beneath the main wooden walkways. Without this steam, the steps would freeze into black ice; with it, the walkways are warm to the touch, constantly hissing as the condensation hits them, rising in gentle white plumes that smell of hot metal and wet cedar.

### The Cold Greenery

Despite the cold and the altitude, the cleft is choked with life. It is not the life of flowers or broad-leafed trees, but a prehistoric, slow-breathing flora that thrives on basalt and coal-dust.

The mosses are the true masters of Saint-under-Sough. They are not mere skin; they are thick, springy mattresses, three feet deep in the corners of the terraces, colored in shades of bruised plum, oxidized copper, and wet soot-black. If a man sits too long on a bench, the moss will begin to grow over his heels within a week. It digests the iron chains, the stone stairs, and the wooden shingles, turning everything into a soft, rounded green hummock.

Out of this moss grow the "grease-ferns"—low, fleshy plants with fronds that look like oiled leather. They do not have seeds; they drop heavy, gelatinous spores that stick to the soles of boots and the coats of the few animals that live here. Beside them are the dwarf conifers, their trunks twisted into knots by the prevailing wind, their needles so dark they appear black in the twilight of the cleft.

There is a strange relationship between the industry and the flora. The soot from the boiler-smoke does not kill the plants; it feeds them. A specific type of gray-blue lichen, known locally as "chimney-wool," grows exclusively on the rims of the active brick stacks, drawing sustenance from the sulfur and carbon in the smoke.

### The Strange Inhabitants

Fauna is sparse. There are no horses, no cows, no dogs. The paths are too steep, the air too thin.

In their place are the "chimney-ravens"—massive, heavy-beaked birds with feathers so greasy they repel the rain like shingled oilcloth. They do not fly so much as hop and glide from one warm steam-pipe to another, their throat-pouches stuffed with scraps of tallow-soaked leather and dried cod. They are silent birds; they do not caw, but make a dry, clicking sound with their mandibles, like two dominoes being struck together in a closed drawer.

Below the walkways, in the dark, wet spaces where the copper drains empty into the rock flutes, live the grease-grubs. They are pale, blind caterpillars as thick as a finger, which feed on the lard-based grease that drops from the cable-pulleys. The children of the cleft fish for them with wire hooks, using them to oil their boots or selling them to the boiler-men, who boil them down into a fine, non-clogging oil for the clockwork regulators of the steam engines.

### The Soft, Heavy Dynamics

Life in Saint-under-Sough is slow, rhythmic, and muffled. 

The sheer weight of the damp air and the carpet of moss swallows sound. A scream fifty feet away is heard only as a soft gasp; the heavy iron hammer of the blacksmith on the fourth tier sounds like a wooden mallet falling on turf. The only sounds that carry are the low-frequency vibrations—the deep, sub-audible hum of the great boiler-house, the slow *thug-clank* of the cable-ways, and the constant, whispering hiss of the steam lines.

The weather changes not by hours, but by minutes. The wind will shift three degrees to the west, and instantly the cleft will fill with "the shroud"—a fog so thick that a man cannot see his own knees. During these times, the town stops. People stand where they are on the warm walkways, listening to the drip of the copper gutters and the click of the ravens, waiting for the wind to shift again and reveal the black, ancient ribs of the spire rising out of the white wool.

There is no sense of progress here, nor of decay. The town has reached its final, stable form. The iron is rusting, but it is replaced with iron of the same weight and shape. The wood is rotting, but the moss holds the walls together until new pine can be bolted in. Safe above the wars and changes of the lower plains, the cleft simply breathes—in and out, steam and fog, coal-smoke and mist—locked forever in its cold, wet crease of rock.
