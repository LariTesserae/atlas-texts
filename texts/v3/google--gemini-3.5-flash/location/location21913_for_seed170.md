The air at this altitude is thin, dry, and hot enough to make the eyelids stick. There is no water here—not as a liquid, nor even as a memory in the dry, baked crust of the highland basin. The atmosphere is a arid soup of nitrogen and vaporized sulfur, under a sun that hangs high and white, casting shadows that seem too dark, as if they are solid planes of black slate rather than the absence of light.

This is a regional tableland, immense and hollow. Between the great, wind-polished ridges of copper-rich hematite, there are miles of nothing but empty space. It is a vast, open bowl of high-elevation desert, but it does not look like any natural wilderness. It looks like an ancient, self-generative heavy-industry site that has survived for millions of years without a single builder to design it.

There are no ruins here, no concrete foundations, no rusted bolts, and no scaffolds. The landscape is entirely untouched by hands, yet it is profoundly mechanized. 

The basalt mountains are shaped like giant thrust-pistons and turbine housings, their curves streamlined by eons of friction with dry, grit-laden winds. They have reached a state of deep geological balance; they do not crumble, and they do not shift rapidly. Instead, they operate on a scale of centuries. Once every several weeks, a sandstone block the size of a cathedral—naturally balanced on a fulcrum of magnetic ironstone—slowly tilts three degrees to the left with a deep, earth-shaking *clack*. The sound is dry and clean, like a giant typewriter key being pressed in an empty ballroom.

The ground underfoot is not soil, but a self-healing sheet of gray silicon-loam and interlocking brassy plates of pyrite. It has been scoured flat, worn down by time into a polished surface that reflects the pale sky. In the absence of plants, the landscape is colored in metallic shades: tarnished bronze, oxidized copper green, dull silver, and the brilliant, oily purple of heated steel.

A constant, deafening noise fills the basin. It is a 60-cycle electrical hum, so deep and loud that it vibrates inside the chest cavity before it even reaches the ears. This sound does not come from a machine; it is the voice of the mountains themselves. The high-altitude ridges act as natural conductors, catching the static charge of the dry upper atmosphere and channeling it down through vertical quartz veins. Giant arcs of white electricity—thick as old trees and perfectly silent—stretch lazily between the peaks, remaining suspended in the air for hours at a time, humming like high-voltage transmission lines.

Because of this constant electrical saturation, reality here has begun to fray.

Gravity does not always point straight down. On certain slopes of the basalt ridges, your weight shifts toward the mountain wall, allowing you to walk horizontally along a vertical cliff face as if it were a flat road. The light, though bright and normal to the eye, behaves erratically. If you drop a stone of hematite, you will see it hit the ground, but you will not hear the sharp *clink* of its contact until several minutes later. Sometimes, shadows do not follow the shapes of the rocks that cast them; they linger where they were an hour ago, slowly catching up to the sun’s position in jerky, mechanical increments. If you look across the wide expanses of the valley, the perspective is broken: distant peaks look razor-sharp and tiny, while nearby stones appear massive and blurry, defying the eyes' ability to focus on both at once.

There are no plants, no lichens, and no mosses. The only things that grow are mineral dendrites—crystalline branches of copper and silver that sprout from the rock salt like frost on a winter window, clicking softly as they expand by micrometers each year.

Yet, despite the lack of water and vegetation, the basin is crawling with life. The fauna is overwhelming, a teeming, frenetic ecology of dry, inorganic organisms that have claimed this high, hot machinery as their home.

Billions of tiny, glass-winged insects—like living shards of quartz—swarm in thick, sparkling clouds around the silent electrical arcs, feeding directly on the blue corona discharge. They emit a constant, high-pitched sizzle that runs over the top of the mountains' deep hum. Across the copper-loam flats, herds of large, multi-segmented creatures made of articulated slate shale move with a dry, clattering rustle. They have no eyes; instead, they have spinning, magnetic needles on their heads that align with the planet's poles as they migrate at a snail's pace toward the cold centers of the valley.

In the crags of the piston-like mountains, giant, cable-like organisms made of braided silicate fibers hang suspended between the cliffs. They resemble great, sleeping serpents, dozens of yards long, completely motionless for decades. They do not eat or hunt; they are passive induction coil beasts. They live by absorbing the magnetic fields generated by the slow, grinding movements of the iron tectonic plates below. When one of them finally moves, it does so with an agonizing, rusty screech that echoes across the entire basin, a dry sound that takes minutes to die away in the thin air.

Everything here is slow, ancient, and loud. The world does not change; it only cycles. The great tectonic pistons rise and fall over centuries, the herds of slate-creatures move inches a day, and the electrical ribbons hang frozen in the dry sky like pillars of light. It is an industrial wilderness that has attained a perfect, mineral peace, operating by its own broken physical laws, indifferent to anything but its own heavy, humming existence.
