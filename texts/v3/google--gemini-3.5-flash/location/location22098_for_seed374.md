The country is a low, vast basin of hard, black obsidian and raw basalts, flat as a frozen lake but fractured into a trillion razor-sharp edges. There are no rounded pebbles, no soft river gravels, and no soil. The land has not had time to wear down; it exists in a state of sudden, violent arrest, immediately cold enough to hold its form but still hot enough to hiss. 

Water is everywhere, yet it does not carve channels. The Basin is too young for rivers. Instead, warm, mineral-rich water sheets across the flat slabs in broad, trembling mirrors, barely a finger’s depth, or drips in rhythmic, unceasing ropes from the overhangs of jagged volcanic folds. The atmosphere is a heavy, warm womb. Steam rises from every crack, mixing with a permanent, low-hanging canopy of ash-colored clouds that smothers the sky. The light is an unchanging, leaden twilight that casts no shadows and reveals no horizon. Everything is slick, dripping, and tepid, smelling of wet iron, raw sulfur, and the sharp, briny stink of a crowded tide pool.

There is nothing green. No leaf has ever broken this darkness; no root has ever pried open the basalt slabs. The only things resembling plant life are slimy, non-photosynthetic crusts—rubbery mats of iron-oxidizing bacteria that coat the stone in greasy bands of ochre, mustard yellow, and deep, blood-like red. These mats eat the mineral water directly, fueling an ecology that bypasses the sun.

Into this raw, unpolished geology, an immense, dense anthill of a city has been wedged. 

It is a settlement of stone, but not of masons. The inhabitants have built with what lies at their feet, stacking the sharp, natural obsidian blocks as they found them, wedging them together with thick, grey volcanic clay that they have dug from the steam vents and slapped on by hand. The dwellings are small, low-ceilinged cells, piled three, four, and five levels high in dizzying, chaotic terraces. The alleys between them are so narrow that two bodies cannot pass without sliding chest-to-chest, and the roofs of the lower levels serve as the streets for the upper. Millions of these cells stretch across the basin floor, a sprawling, dark hive that looks less like a city and more like a scab of hand-pressed mud and black glass clinging to the bottom of the world.

There is no metal work here, no wheel, no glass windows. The technology is primitive, heavy, and wet. Utensils are chipped from the natural glass of the ground; ropes are braided from the tough, fibrous cords of land-anemones; vessels are thick, sun-dried clay pots that never quite dry in the humid air, remaining slightly soft to the touch. 

The density of life within this wet stone labyrinth is suffocating. 

The air is alive with a deafening, continuous roar. It is not the sound of wind or machinery—for there is no wind in this basin, and no engines—but the collective noise of billions of animals. Without plants, the fauna has ballooned to fill every centimeter of space. The vertical walls of the mud-and-stone dwellings are covered in a crawling carpet of plate-shelled land crabs, their claws perpetually clicking against the obsidian. In the shallow sheets of water on the rooftops and alleys, millions of flat-bodied trilobites scrape the red bacterial slime, their hard carapaces grinding against the stone with a sound like iron filings being scraped over slate. 

Every hole in the basalt is home to a creature. Long, pale, multi-jointed centipedes, thick as a man’s arm, slide through the wet crevices of the masonry. From the steam vents, flocks of small, leathery-winged gliders—neither bird nor bat, but blind, smooth-skinned things that navigate by warmth—launch themselves into the humid gloom, their high-pitched, metallic trilling combining with the scrape and click of the crawlers into a single, wall-of-sound din that vibrates inside the bones of the skull. It is a noise that never falters, never rises to a crescendo, and never falls silent. It is the permanent baseline of the world.

Nothing here changes. The basin has no seasons, no day, and no night. The tide of the standing water never rises or falls because there is no sea to pull it, only the constant, unvarying hiss of the deep-earth springs that feed the steam. The population of the city does not migrate, does not build outward into new territories, and does not go to war. They exist in a crowded, warm equilibrium, sleeping when their bodies fail, eating the slow-moving shelled things that they scrape from their own walls, and patching the wet clay of their cells when the condensing steam washes it away. 

Time does not pass in the Basin; it merely accumulates, like the red slime on the black stone.
