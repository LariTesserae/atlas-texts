This is a world that has only recently finished rising. 

At an altitude so extreme the air is thin enough to make the lungs ache, a wilderness of razor-sharp basalt needles and obsidian slabs thrusts toward a pale, sun-bleached sky. The geology here is embryonic, raw, and hostile to the touch. The mountains have no shoulders, no rolling slopes, and no soil; they are vertical splinters of dark volcanic glass, untouched by the smoothing fingers of deep time. The edges are so new they can slice through leather like carbon-steel razors. Nothing has been rounded by wind; nothing has been softened by rain.

And yet, wrapped around these black teeth is a city.

It is an urban sprawl of immense, vertiginous density, a vertical metropolis of cold-rolled iron, brass plating, and heavy, dark-fired brick. The settlement clings to the needles like iron filings to a magnet. Houses are stacked vertically, the roof of one forming the foundation of the terrace above, anchored directly into the unweathered glass of the peaks with massive, multi-ton tension cables. The sky between the peaks is a dense web of iron suspension bridges, dangling utility conduits, and heavy steel guide-ropes. 

The air is hot—a dry, radiant heat that rises from the volcanic roots of the range far below, but the city itself is perpetually damp. Because of the extreme altitude and the intense thermal updrafts, clouds do not drop water here so much as they are torn apart by the sharp architecture. Moisture exists as a constant, heavy condensation. A warm sheen of grease-slick water coats every vertical surface of black stone and rusted iron. It runs down the basalt in quiet, rhythmic pulses, dripping from the rims of copper gutters into cisterns made of lead. Thick, insulated pipes—the city’s rusted veins—crawl over every surface, hissing softly as they carry boiling sulfurous water and pressurized steam from the depths to the high residential sectors.

There is no green. In this mineral empire, the concept of a leaf or a root is entirely absent. The only growths are inorganic and saline. Out of the mortar-joints of the iron towers and the shadowed crevices of the obsidian cliffs, strange, brittle formations of silicate and salt crawl outward. They look like pale, branching corals or stiff, white frost-flowers, but they are hard as granite, growing atom by atom as the mineral-heavy condensation evaporates in the hot sun. They are dry, dead, and beautiful, clicking like fingernails when the thermal drafts brush past them. 

The heat makes the horizon warp. Looking out from the high galleries across the valley-sized expanse of the settlement, the distant sectors shimmer through layers of rising heat. Far below, in the deep rifts between the needles, the true industry of the state resides. Giant, slow-turning flywheels, three stories tall and cast from solid iron, turn on grease-slicked axels. They rotate with an agonizing, patient majesty, driving long reciprocating rods that reach miles up the cliffs to pump water, lift coal, and hoist the massive wooden platforms of the gravity-elevators. 

Despite the industrial density, the world is remarkably quiet. The thinness of the air deadens high frequencies; there is no screaming of metal or howling of whistles. Instead, the landscape breathes in a low, sub-audible rumble—the deep, rhythmic *thump-thump* of piston houses buried deep in the basalt, and the wet, heavy *drip-clink* of condensation hitting copper catch-basins. People move through the iron ladders and the narrow, wet alleys with a quiet, deliberate slowness, their boots wrapped in thick felt to avoid slipping on the wet glass or waking the silence. 

Life is rare, specialized, and metallic. In the shadowed spaces behind the steam pipes, one might spot a rust-mite—a creature the size of a fist with a shell of dull, oxidized copper, moving with the sluggish clockwork gait of a wind-up toy as it feeds on the greasy mineral deposits. Overhead, on the vast thermal currents that rise between the iron-clad peaks, giant, leathery-skinned gliders drift. They have no feathers, only dark, ribbed membranes that stretch ten yards across, catching the hot steam plumes and riding them for hours without a single movement of their wings. They make no sound.

There is a strange, slow-motion elasticity to this place. Because of the intense heat and the weight of the iron city, the basalt needles themselves are under immense, shifting stress. Very slowly—over the course of months—the towers lean. A street that was level in the spring will tilt by three degrees by autumn. The citizens do not panic; they simply adjust the tension on the great iron cables, turning massive bronze turnstiles with long lever-bars, drawing the buildings back into alignment with a series of deep, metallic groans that echo through the stone once every few weeks.

In the late afternoon, when the normal light begins to golden, the wet obsidian cliffs catch the rays and turn the entire valley into an arrangement of black mirrors and brilliant, blinding streaks of copper light. The steam rises, glowing like incense, and the silent, heavy city sits suspended between the furnace of the earth and the empty, high blue of the sky, waiting for the slow turn of the world to cool the glass.
