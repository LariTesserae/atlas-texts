Here, there is no earth. There is only the Shelf: a vast, high-altitude matrix of cold-rolled steel, galvanized zinc plates, and interlocking brass assemblies that extends in every direction until it dissolves into the white glare of the horizon. 

This place is young, or perhaps it is renewed constantly; its edges are razor-sharp. There are no rounded corners, no paths worn smooth by footsteps, no soft pits of rust. The stamped patterns on the checker-plate floors are crisp enough to cut leather. Every nut is tight; every rivet head is a perfect circle of untouched silver. The air is cool—chilled by the altitude—and carries the odor of fresh industrial grease, cold solder, and the sharp, clean scent of ozone.

Although the scale of the Shelf is regional, stretching across endless terraces and sky-bridges that span miles-wide chasms, the environment is incredibly dense. Structures crowd one another. You cannot look through a gap without seeing five more layers of infrastructure behind it: forest-like clusters of conduits, vertical radiator fins, suspended catwalks, and heavy, slow-moving pendulum weights that stabilize the towers. 

***

A flat, brilliant white light fills the world. It does not come from a sun, but from giant, luminescent plates set directly into the undersides of the overhead terraces, miles above. This light is persistent and shadows are short, hard, and strange. Because of the density of the metal around you, the light bounces and refracts, creating a luminous haze.

Yet, despite the colossal machinery, it is almost entirely quiet. A deep, heavy silence hangs over the terraces. The sound of a metal plate expanding in the cool air carries for miles, a lone, crystalline *ping* that echoes and vanishes without a sister sound to answer it. The air is too thin, or perhaps the geometry of the metal is too complex, to allow sound to travel in waves; everything is muffled, absorbed, dead-ended by the sheer density of the structures. Even the wind, when it rises, makes no whistle—it merely pushes against your skin like a cold, silent weight.

Moisture exists here in delicate, local pockets. Because of the chill, condensation forms in glistening, bead-like paths along the undersides of the copper pipes. It collects in small, perfectly square reservoirs stamped into the floor-plates. This water is clear, though it tastes faintly of tin and oil. Strikingly sparse vegetation has adapted to these damp corners: not green weeds, but flat, vinyl-textured lichens that grow in yellow and grey hexagons across the non-slip surfaces of the stairs. They mimic the geometric regularity of the metal beneath them, sliding slowly across the plates over weeks, leaving clean, ungreased metal in their wake.

***

In this quiet, sterile-looking machinery, fauna is surprisingly abundant. Small, jointed creatures—looking like combinations of crabs and typewriter parts—clatter softly along the undersides of the railings. They have no flesh; they are made of thin, stamped tin and brass wire, their movements powered by the static electricity that accumulates on the dry surfaces of the metal towers. They feed on the grease that leaks from the slow-turning journals of the massive gears, licking the black lubricants with tongues of copper mesh. 

There are "birds" as well—sheared-metal kites that carry no feathers. They do not flap their wings; instead, they lock their flat, steel pinions and glide on the thermally generated updrafts rising from deep, warm ventilating shafts. They drift in absolute silence, like floating razor blades, their mirrored polished surfaces catching the glare of the overhead lights and throwing sharp beams across the lower decks.

But the most striking thing about the Shelf is not its life, or its scale, but the way reality behaves within its borders. 

As you walk, the laws of distance and perspective begin to slip. You might look down a long, straight catwalk that stretches for three miles to a towering boiler house. As you take ten steps forward, the boiler house does not get closer, but the catwalk beneath you begins to widen, until you realize the boiler house is actually only three feet away, small enough to be held in your hand, sitting on a tiny pedestal. When you reach out to touch it, your hand appears normal-sized to you, but its shadow on the floor is sixty feet wide.

Up and down are not fixed directions here; they are properties of whichever surface you are currently looking at. If you shift your gaze to a vertical pillar crowded with copper pipes, your balance shifts with it. Suddenly, the pillar is a floor, and the catwalk you were just standing on is a sheer wall rising to your left. The transition is seamless, accompanied only by a slight, cool pressure in your inner ear. 

The machinery itself moves with an almost geologic slowness. There are flywheels here the size of cities, their outer rims studded with teeth that mesh with other city-sized gears. They turn so slowly that their motion is invisible to the eye; you only notice they have moved when, after hours of walking, a portal that once looked out onto an empty sky-glen is now blocked by a wall of solid, grey-painted iron. 

Nothing wears out. Nothing decays. Under the bright, shadowless illumination, the metal constructs of this high world continue their patient, silent, self-contained operations, indifferent to the impossible angles they form as they slide through each other without a sound.
