### The Cradle of the White Plinths

The world here has not yet learned how to decay. 

At an altitude where the air should be thin and dry, the basin of the White Plinths is instead choked with a heavy, cool moisture. The ground is not made of old granite or weathered basalt, but of *marrow-stone*—a soft, chalky, semi-crystalline mineral that is slowly, persistently rising from the deep interior of the mountain. It is a landscape under construction, entirely devoid of erosion. There are no scree slopes, no rounded river pebbles, and no soil layers built from millenniums of rot. Instead, the white stone rises in smooth, wet, bulbous knuckles and sharp, crystalline steps that look as though they were poured and set only yesterday. 

Water does not flow in rivers here; it weeps. It bubbles directly out of the porous, un-weathered marrow-stone faces, coating every vertical surface in a shimmering, three-millimeter film of cold, mineral-rich moisture. The air is cool—a crisp, lung-chilling dampness—but the light is strong and direct, casting sharp, pale shadows across the basin. Because the stone is so white and the water so clean, the daylight multiplies, bouncing between the vertical walls until the entire basin glows with the interior brightness of a salt cellar.

Inside this amphitheater—which is small, no larger than a cathedral or a modest public square—stands a city of ghosts and scaffolding. 

Despite the near-total absence of inhabitants, the basin is organized with dense, complex infrastructure. The construction is layered, vertical, and sprawling, but composed entirely of primitive, pre-metal materials. Giant pillars of hand-woven wicker, some seventy feet tall and thick as ancient oaks, are lashed to the raw stone with cords of braided hemp and boiled leather. These hollow, basket-like towers are connected by a labyrinth of suspension bridges made of split pine and green vines. 

The architecture is highly sophisticated in its planning but elementary in its execution. There are tiers of empty dwellings carved directly into the soft, unformed marrow-stone using flint adzes and fire-hardened wooden wedges. There are complex, gravity-fed aqueducts made of hollowed logs, designed to catch the weeping water from the high walls and funnel it into vast, empty cisterns below. Every surface is structural; every ledge has been notched or peg-bored to accept a timber. Yet, there is almost nothing filling these spaces. The city is a skeletal loom, a dense nest of pathways wrapping around a central void. You can stand on a high sky-bridge and look through hundreds of empty doorways, down through three tiers of unoccupied rope-walks, straight to the wet floor of the basin.

Vegetation has claimed this empty framework with aggressive, silent fury. Because the rising stone is so rich in virgin nutrients, plants grow with monstrous vitality. Mosses do not form carpets here; they grow in thick, wet, sponge-like bolsters the size of sheep, clinging to the wicker towers and the limestone steps alike. Ribbons of bright green strap-ferns, five feet long and dripping with condensation, hang from every wooden joint. A low, succulent ground-cover with fat, translucent leaves covers the bottoms of the walkways, squishing underfoot like wet fruit. The green is so intense, so saturated by the cold water and the brilliant mountain light, that it seems to vibrate against the stark white of the emerging stone.

And the basin is extraordinarily loud.

The noise is not organic, but mechanical and elemental. The hollow wicker towers act as giant flutes for the mountain wind, producing a deep, whistling drone that vibrates in the chest. Beneath this wind-song is the relentless, polyrhythmic clatter of water: the sharp *tink-tink-tink* of droplets hitting the crystalline stone, the hollow sloshing of the log-aqueducts, and the roaring boom of the spillways where the cisterns overflow into the empty air. Every sound is caught by the hard, un-eroded walls of the basin and bounced back, amplified, until the air feels physically dense with vibration.

Animacy, however, is nearly absent. There are no birds in the sky, and no insects hum among the dripping ferns. The only sign of animal life is the occasional *slid-click* of a high-altitude karst-snail—creatures with heavy, porcelain-like shells that graze with agonizing slowness on the moss-bolsters, taking three days to cross a single wooden bridge. 

Nothing changes quickly here. The rising of the marrow-stone is a movement measured in inches per century. The wooden City-Nest, though built of perishable materials, does not rot because the water is too cold and too sterile to support the fungi of decay; instead, the wicker and the vine-ropes are slowly mineralizing, turning to stone as the wet lime from the rock seeps into their fibers. The town is petrifying even as it stands empty, a monument to an urban planning that has no one to house, preserved in a cold, loud, green womb of newborn earth.
