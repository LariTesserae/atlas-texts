This is the interior of Vault 42-A, an ancient, subterranean pumping station and pressure-regulating chamber, now permanently and entirely drowned. Water fills every cubic inch of this space, from the curved iron floorplates to the vaulted, ribbed ceiling forty meters above. It is not ocean water, but the heavy, mineral-fretted rainwater of a lost drainage system, cool and thick with centuries of dissolved iron and lime. 

The vault is small—scarcely the size of a parish church—and entirely bounded by walls. There is no horizon here, only the massive, inward-curving bulkheads of cast iron and poured volcanic concrete. Nothing about this place is natural. Every surface was drawn, cast, Riveted, and bolted. Yet, it has existed in this flooded state for so long that the water has claimed the architecture as its own, polishing the sharp flanges of the steel girders until they are as smooth and rounded as river-stones. The heads of three-inch rivets, once sharp and hexagonal, have been worn down into gentle, flat mounds of dark oxide.

The temperature is consistently, penetratingly cool—the chill of wet iron in a deep cellar. Under this cold weight, the water is not still; it moves in a complex, rhythmic cycle of drafts and swells. These currents are driven by the slow, blind efforts of three massive, water-jacketed turbines set into the southern bulkhead. They turn at perhaps three revolutions per minute, their forty-foot bronze blades churning the dark water with an immense, low-frequency hum that vibrates more in the chest than in the ear.

The sound here is a constant, heavy physical presence. It is a chamber of metal resonance. There is the wet, rhythmic *clack-hiss* of a water-lever releasing pressure somewhere behind the walls; the deep, resonant *thrum* of the iron ceiling acting as a sounding board for the machinery; and the sharp, metallic *ping* of loosening scale drifting down to strike the grates below. It is a loud environment, but because of the density of the water, high frequencies are swallowed quickly, leaving only a wet, roaring bass that makes the teeth ache.

Light is scarce and monochromatic. There are no windows to the upper world. The only illumination comes from a series of heavy brass-housed lanterns bolted to the vertical support columns. Most have long since gone dark, but a few still glow with a faint, dying orange filigree—the work of ancient, silt-clogged galvanic piles that refuse to die. This orange light does not carry far through the green-gray suspension; it pools around the brackets, illuminating drifting clouds of fine rust and the pale bodies of the vault’s inhabitants.

Life has colonized this metal tomb, though it is a sparse and cold-blooded ecology. There is almost no vegetation. A thin, greasy velvet of brown diatoms coats the handrails and the faces of the pressure gauges, and occasionally, in the slow-moving eddies near the ceiling, one can find a small, stringy cluster of water-moss, pale white from the lack of sun. 

But the fauna is abundant and busy. Thick clusters of white, trumpet-shaped freshwater bryozoans have settled inside the dormant machinery, their delicate, feathery crowns filtering the iron-rich water. Across the floor-grates, large, blind crayfish—wholly transparent, their internal organs visible as tiny amber organs—clamber over one another to pick at the grease-caps on the main-shaft bearings. Small, pale cave-fish with needle-thin bodies dart through the holes in the strainers, hunting the water-fleas that breed in the warmth of the friction-joints. 

Everything here exists in a steady, mechanized equilibrium. A school of blind fish flashes through the blades of a slowly turning flywheel; they do not get crushed because they know the rhythm of the machine, navigating by the change in water pressure as each bronze blade sweeps past. The ecosystem has grown around the iron, adapting to the hum of the pumps as if it were the tide.

There are no doors out of this chamber that are not sealed with three-foot-thick sluice gates, and no air to be found. If there was once a purpose to this massive vault—to keep a city dry, or to feed a boiler-works that has since crumbled to dust—that purpose has been forgotten. The machines do not care. They continue their heavy, water-logged strokes, wearing themselves away grain by grain, in the dark, cold, loud cathedral of the pump-room.
