High above the vapor-line of the lower world, suspended on the sheer flank of an immense, structural pillar, sits Deck 40-Alpha: a warm, wet sanctuary the size of a small chapel. 

There is no stone here that was not mixed and poured; no soil that was not synthesized. The entire terrace is a masterpiece of unweathered, raw industrial construction. The floor is an interlocking puzzle of dark, cast-basalt tiles, their edges so sharp and newly minted they could cut paper. There are no chips, no scuffs, no signs of feet having trodden them. Everything is embryonic, just-built, a clean-room of heavy spit-and-polished copper, iron, and lead-gray ceramic that has yet to know the dulling effect of time. 

And yet, it is soaking wet and bursting with green.

Water is the dominant engine of this small, enclosed shelf. It does not fall as rain from the sky, which remains a clear, high-altitude blue above the deck’s copper-ribbed vaulting; instead, it is exhaled. Under the basalt floor-plates, low-pressure steam engines thrum with a deep, nearly silent vibration. They push hot, mineral-saturated moisture upward through capillary networks built directly into the walls. The ceramic blocks of the terrace walls are porous by design, engineered to sweat. Droplets gather on the pristine, cream-colored tiles, swelling until they run down precision-carved runnels into copper troughs. The air is thick, warm, and sweet, smelling of hot brass, ozone, and wet starch.

Out of this mechanical moisture grows a furious, dense vegetation. The flora here does not seem to require soil. It thrives in custom-machined pocket-wells filled with a gray, fibrous growth-medium that looks like spun basalt wool. Massive, fan-leaved ferns—their fronds a hyper-saturated, translucent emerald—reach outward, brushing against the hot-water pipes. Thick vines of an unnamed runner climb the copper conduits, wrapping them in tight, spiraling sleeves of succulent green. The plants grow with an unnatural, visible vigor, their leaves lifting and shifting slightly over the course of minutes as their internal turgor pressure fluctuates with the automated pulse of the pumps.

Because of the high altitude, the light is brilliant and direct, but it is constantly chopped and scattered by the drifting plumes of steam that rise from the floor grates. Sharp, silver shafts of light slice through the mist, illuminating the wet, waxy surfaces of the leaves and making the polished metal surfaces gleam like wet coal.

The atmosphere is almost entirely quiet. The machinery here is new, its bearings perfectly oiled, its pistons cushioned by water-jackets. There is only a low, rhythmic *thrum-click* from the sump-valves beneath the decking, accompanied by the endless, musical patter of condensation dripping from the iron ceiling-beams onto the broad, rubbery leaves below. Now and then, a copper valve vents with a soft, long *hiss*, releasing a cloud of sweet, lukewarm vapor that rolls across the floor like ground fog.

Animal life is nearly non-existent in this engineered cradle, save for those things specifically designed to maintain it. Tiny, nickel-plated gastropods—clockwork water-snails no larger than a fingernail—glide silently along the wet brass pipes. They graze on the thin film of algae that tries to form near the hot joints, their tiny scrapers making a faint, intermittent *scritch* sound if one presses an ear to the metal. 

There is an eerie, beautiful discipline to the landscape. The plants, though wild and lush, are guided by the architecture. Embedded within the sweating ceramic walls are faint, copper-wire pathways that carry a micro-current of electricity. The vines do not wander aimlessly; they follow these invisible, charged tracks, growing in perfect, geometric spirals and clean right angles across the walls, framing the steam-vents in living, throbbing borders of green.

It is a world in miniature, highly dynamic but utterly controlled. Every ten minutes, a central regulator clicks. The flow of warm water shifts from the eastern wall to the western wall. In response, a hundred thousand leaves slowly tilt, turning their faces toward the fresh source of moisture. The water drains through grates of perforated zinc, is filtered, reheated, and pumped back into the walls. It is a closed, perfect loop of birth and growth, ticking quietly to itself in the high, clean air, waiting for the first sign of decay that may never come.
