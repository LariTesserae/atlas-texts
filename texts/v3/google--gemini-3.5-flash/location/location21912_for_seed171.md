The world here does not have a floor; it has a crust of cold, wet muscle that happens to be green. 

Beneath this living mantle lies a geology that is violently young. The rock, where it can be seen through the rents in the moss-flesh, is basaltic, black, and razor-sharp. There are no rounded pebbles here, no river-worn boulders, no sand. The mountains have been thrust up into the thin, high-altitude air so recently that their edges are still like broken glass, unweathered and raw. Yet they have had no time to erode because they were captured instantly by the green. The vegetation has sealed the stone away from the air, wrapping the continent in a sheath of hyper-dense, self-assembling cellulose that is meters thick.

This is a high, cold shelf of the world, vast enough that its horizons bend slightly under a heavy, perpetual roof of violet-grey clouds. The air is thick and wet, so saturated with moisture that the distinction between fog, rain, and the plants themselves is difficult to maintain. Water is everywhere, but it does not run in normal streams. Instead, it is actively managed. 

The entire landscape hums with a deafening, subsonic roar. It is the sound of a trillion gallons of water being moved against gravity. The plant-substrate is not passive; it is a vascular machine of planetary scale. Millions of vertical, tube-like trunks—each the color of wet slate and as slick as sealskin—reach up into the mist. Inside them, under hydraulic pressures that would rupture steel, cold sap is pumped upward to the high canopies. The noise of this capillary lift is a continuous, white-noise thrum, like standing beneath an infinite railway bridge while a heavy train passes overhead. It vibrates through the soles of your feet, shaking the water off the leaves in a constant, fine drizzle.

There is no animal life. No insects crawl through the dense, spongy moss; no birds call from the high, dripping structures. The silence of fauna is swallowed completely by the mechanical roar of the flora. 

Though there are no buildings, no roads, and no signs of intelligent design, the landscape operates with a technological sophistication that borders on the cosmic. The plants do not merely grow; they organize. If you examine the undersides of the great, plate-like leaves that shelter the valleys, you will see their veins are not random. They run in perfect, micro-grooved grids, glowing with a faint, internal coherent light. This light—a pale, pulsing indigo—is not bioluminescence for attraction; it is data. The plants are communicating across continents, balancing barometric pressure, shifting their water-weight to compensate for tectonic shifts, and filtering the raw ultraviolet light of the high altitude through crystalline chloroplasts that act as quantum-dot collectors. 

Because the temperature is cool—hovering just above the freezing point of water—decomposition is incredibly slow. The dead matter does not rot; it is integrated. When a giant siphon-pillar dies, its neighbors do not let it decay on the forest floor. They grow into it, using high-pressure acidic roots to dissolve its cellulose and reconstruct it into new, living structural columns. Nothing is wasted. The density of life is so absolute that there is literally no empty space; every square centimeter of this cold plateau is covered by a leaf, a stalk, a root, or a slick of blue-green algal film that computes the local salinity of the damp air.

Light is a scarce commodity. The cloud deck is thick and low, and the over-canopy of specialized, fan-like fronds leaves only a twilight below. The ground level—which is actually an elevated, shaking mattress of interwoven roots fifty feet above the actual stone—is washed in a cold, dim, marine-blue gloom. Underfoot, the moss-substrate is soft but pressurized, pushing back against your weight like an air mattress. Every step you take causes a brief hiss of water to squirt from microscopic pores in the moss, a tiny squeak of hydraulics that is immediately lost in the great, roaring thrum of the continent-machine.

This is an embryonic world that has somehow bypassed the messy, chaotic stages of geological weathering and animal evolution. It has jumped straight from raw, volcanic birth to a state of cold, perfect, and deafeningly loud biological computation. It is pristine, untouched by any hand, yet it functions with the cold, wet precision of a supercomputer built from moss and high-pressure water.
