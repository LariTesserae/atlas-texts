The pocket is a sharp, black hexagonal cleft, a vertical throat in the basalt floor of a cold, low-elevation valley. It has no ceiling but the low-hanging cloud, and no entrance but a narrow fissure where two massive, unweathered sheets of dark stone have slammed together and wedged themselves open. It is a very young wound in the earth; the basalt edges are so fresh they could slice a palm, lacking any of the soft, rounded moss-shoulders or ice-scraped corners of the older valley above. 

This is a chamber of raw minerals and cold hydraulics. It is twelve feet across at its widest, yet inside this vertical pipe, there is an immense sense of vacancy. There are only three things here: the wet stone, the towering pipes of the vegetation, and the water. Between these elements lies a great, hollow draft of freezing air.

The ground is not soil, but a self-assembling pavement of basalt joints, perfectly level but separated by dark, finger-wide gaps. Through these gaps, the water lives. It does not flow like a stream; it behaves like a pressurized piston. Every eighty seconds, with the mechanical regularity of a beating heart, the water rises. It is ice-cold, gray-green, and highly pressurized from some glacial reservoir hidden deep within the mountain’s flank. 

The rise is preceded by a deep, hollow *thrum-shudder* that vibrates through the soles of the feet—a low-frequency hum like an idling engine. Then comes the sound: a rushing, wet *slosh-hiss* as the water surges up through the hexagonal cracks, filling the floor of the pocket to a depth of three inches in a matter of seconds. For a brief moment, the chamber is a perfectly still, dark mirror reflecting the dim, pewter sky. Then, with a sudden, wet gasp—like a giant valve releasing pressure—the water is siphoned back down into the dark slots.

This relentless, forty-second tide has created a highly specialized, industrial-style ecology. 

Growing directly from the cracks in the basalt are stands of giant Equisetum—copper-colored horsetails as thick as a man’s wrist and six feet tall. Over centuries of absorbing the mineral-heavy water, these plants have petrified their own cells with silica and dissolved iron. They are not soft green stalks; they are rigid, metallic-looking pipes, segmented with dark, oily rings. When the hydraulic tide rises and falls, the moving water forces air through the hollow interiors of these pipes, producing a chorale of whistling, rhythmic sighs—a collective, organic bellows that hums in counterpoint to the thumping floor.

Along the sheer vertical walls of the fissure, where the spray is constant, thick blankets of black-livered moss hang like sound-dampening insulation. These mats do not grow in soil; they cling to the bare stone by secreting a sticky, acidic resin that eats microscopic anchors into the basalt. When the water surges, these moss-mats act as natural sponges, swelling and groaning under the weight of the absorption. When the water drains, they weep. Thousands of tiny, perfectly round droplets bead on their tips, catching what little dim twilight reaches this floor, before falling in a heavy, metallic patter onto the stone below.

The air is cold enough to keep one’s breath suspended in thick, white plumes that do not disperse, but rather linger in the draft of the water-cycles, spinning in slow, lazy heaps. It smells powerfully of wet iron, cold slate, and the clean, sharp bite of ozone, as if a great electrical generator had just been switched off nearby.

The fauna here are small, numerous, and frantic. They are part of the clockwork. Millions of tiny, slate-gray isopods—armored like miniature tanks—cling to the vertical copper horsetails. As the tide rises, they climb upward in unison, their thousands of microscopic shells clucking against the silica-heavy stalks with a sound like dry gravel being shaken in a tin bowl. When the tide retreats, they rush down again to feed on the fresh mineral deposits left on the wet basalt. They are accompanied by lungless, ribbon-thin salamanders, pale as lard, that slip in and out of the hexagonal cracks on the rising water, their bright orange gills vibrating like tiny external pumps in the current.

There is no sign of human presence here—no concrete, no sheared bolts, no tool marks. Yet the space functions so much like a machine that the mind looks instinctively for a switch, a pipe-joint, or a pressure gauge. The basalt columns are too blue-black, too geometric; the copper-reed whistles are too rhythmic; the tidal pump is too precise. 

When the light dies in the late afternoon, the pocket does not go dark all at once. The mineral deposits left on the basalt by the pumping water—thin, crusty veins of white silica and iron oxide—begin to faint-glow with a cold, pale phosphorescence. It is a dim, chemical twilight, just bright enough to see the copper stalks shaking, the steam rising from the wet stone, and the black, cold water rising once more to drown the floor.
