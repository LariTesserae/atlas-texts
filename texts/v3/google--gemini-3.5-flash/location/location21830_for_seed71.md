The tableland does not end. It is a continental shield of deep-grey basalt, lifted so high into the upper atmosphere that the sky above it is not blue, but a hard, clear indigo, even at noon.

This is a world of flat-topped domes and low, rolling ridges that have been planed down by billions of years of wind. There are no sharp peaks here. The mountains died long ago; they have settled into their final, stable shape—a vast, sleeping pavement of stone that has forgotten how to shake. The scale is so immense that the curvature of the horizon is visible, a gentle arc that sections off the dark sky from the pale, frosted floor.

It is cold enough that water does not flow so much as it slips between states. There are no oceans, but the air is thick with a heavy, cold moisture that never quite resolves into rain. Instead, it exists as a perpetual, creeping rime. Mist rises from cold fissures in the basalt, instantly freezing into microscopic needles upon the stones, only to melt again when the pale, silver sun passes overhead.

Across this infinite grey-white flat, the vegetation is lush, but it is a flora adapted to the extreme cold and the absence of animals. Without eyes to attract or insects to bribe, there are no bright flowers. Instead, the land is carpeted in immense, thick quilts of violet peat-moss and sprawling, charcoal-colored lichens that grow several feet deep. These mosses are self-heating; their slow, internal chemical decay produces a faint, greasy warmth, so that every step into the violet sod sinks into a lukewarm, wet peat that smells of ancient bogs and wet flint.

Living within the moss are giant, glass-like reeds. They grow in sparse, isolated groves that stretch for miles along the low ridges. They have no leaves, only hollow, hexagonal tubes of clear silica that draw up the condensation. When the wind blows across the plateau, these reeds do not bend; they remain perfectly rigid, but they hum. Thousands of square miles of glass flutes, tuned to the low, resonant frequencies of the earth, create a sound like a distant, vibrating iron string. It is a dry, hollow vibration that you feel in your shins more than you hear in your ears.

There are no animals. Nothing runs, crawls, or flies. No worms turn the dark soil beneath the violet moss; no birds break the clean geometry of the indigo sky. The only movement belongs to the weather and to the slow, creeping migration of the plant-colonies, which roll across the stone in decades-long waves, following the shifting thermal vents in the basalt.

Yet, this empty world is urban.

Rising from the violet peat are the Spun Cities. They are not ruins, though they are older than the valleys below the plateau. They are dense, tightly packed settlements built entirely of hand-hewn slate, dry-stacked granite, and great, green-crusted beams of cold-hammered bronze. The technology is elementary—there are no pipes, no wires, no machines—but the organization is immense.

These cities are constructed around the places where reality has begun to fray.

Because the world is so old, its physical laws have grown tired, slipping out of alignment like worn gears. In the plazas of these cities, gravity does not pull straight down. A street of stone cottages, built with simple hand-tools and wooden rafters, will tilt upward at a forty-five-degree angle, its hearth-smoke rising sideways. The people who built them—whoever they were—did not fight these anomalies; they built into them.

In one city, which stretches for five miles along a shallow depression, a great circle of houses is built around a floating lake. The water, a perfect sphere of cold, black mountain-melt three hundred yards wide, hangs suspended fifty feet above the cobblestones. Long, braided ropes of hemp and leather hang down from its underbelly, dripping cold water onto the slate roofs below. The city’s inhabitants used simple copper buckets on pulleys to draw water down from the sky. The buckets are still there, swinging gently in the wind, green with age but unbroken.

The cities are empty now, but they are not ruined. The cold has preserved them. The timber rafters are as hard as iron; the bronze hinges have oxidized into a protective, powdery teal. The layout is compact—narrow, winding alleyways designed to keep out the biting frost, communal ovens made of red clay, and high, watchless towers that stare out into the empty steppe.

These settlements are sparse. You can walk for three days across the violet moss, through the ringing glass reeds, seeing nothing but the flat horizon, before the low, grey silhouette of another stone city appears against the dark sky. They look like teeth rising from the tundra.

The dynamic of this place is slow but active. The air is never still. Great, cold weather fronts—so large they have their own internal gravity—sweep across the continental shield. These are not storms of snow, but of "heavy air," dense clouds of frozen nitrogen mixed with water vapor that roll across the land like a slow, white tide, filling the streets of the empty cities to the second story, then receding after three days to leave everything coated in a thick shell of glittering ice.

When the tide of heavy air recedes, the sun catches the ice-coated stones, and the entire continent burns with a cold, blinding glare. The glass reeds chime louder as the ice around their bases melts. The violet moss sighs, releasing small pockets of warm nitrogen gas that bubble up through the puddles.

And then, the quiet returns. A vast, high silence, broken only by the hum of the silica flutes and the steady, rhythmic *drip-drip* of water falling upward from the bottom of the floating lakes back into the sky.
