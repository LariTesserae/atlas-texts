The ground here is not earth. It is an endless floor of hand-fired terracotta tiles, each three spans wide, laid out in a staggering herringbone pattern that has settled, buckled, and cracked over centuries. Where the tiles have slumped into hollows beneath, shallow, cold pools of water have collected. There is no soil, no sand; the dark grit that accumulates in the seams is nothing but brick-dust and the fine, calcified husks of deceased water-dwellers, ground down by the slow rocking of the masonry.

This is a sunken basin, perhaps eighty paces across, bounded on all sides by monumental walls of dry-laid granite blocks. The walls rise thirty feet into a perpetual, slate-gray fog before disappearing into the upper gloom. Every stone bears the distinct, rhythmic tooth-marks of hand-chisels. Long ago, this was a closed chamber, but the vaulting above has collapsed, leaving only a few massive, sagging stone ribs that arch overhead like the skeleton of a beached leviathan. Down through this broken throat falls a dim, diffused light, cold and shadowless, barely strong enough to illuminate the corners of the enclosure.

The air is cool, smelling of wet clay and old cellar-damp. Your breath forms brief, shivering plumes of white that linger in the stagnant drafts. It is a wet world, but not a rainy one. Instead, the water is a rising, weeping presence. It bleeds from the joints of the granite walls in steady, silent sheets, turning the dark stone black and slick. It drips from the underside of the ruined stone arches in rhythmic, heavy plops that strike the standing pools below with the bright, metallic ring of coins falling onto stone. 

Because of the acoustics of the brick enclosure, these drips do not sound faint. They are amplified, echoing off the wet masonry until the basin is filled with a loud, chaotic percussion. Underneath this watery chime is a constant, low-frequency vibration—the sound of wood and stone groaning under the shifting weight of water somewhere deep below the floor tiles.

There are almost no plants here. In the deep joints where the mortar has long since rotted away, one might find a rare, thread-like weed, pale yellow and devoid of leaves, clinging to the damp clay. A thin, blackish slime—more bacterium than moss—coats the waterline of the granite walls, making them look as though they have been dipped in ink. 

Yet, the basin is crowded with life. 

It is an ecology of shells and wet skin, thriving in the cold dark. In the shallow pools that cover the terracotta floor, thousands of small, ivory-white snails crawl in slow, intersecting paths, their shells clicking against the tiles like dry beans spilled on a table. When the water level shifts—which it does regularly as the invisible drains beneath the floor sigh and fill—the snails move in unison, a creaking, shuffling carpet of calcium.

Larger shapes move in the deeper water near the walls. Pale, blind crayfish, nearly translucent, battle silently over the carcass of a drowned cricket. They have unusually long, whip-like antennae that they lash through the water, sensing the vibrations of your footsteps on the masonry yards away. On the dry-ish margins of the buckled tiles, thousands of flat, gray woodlice—some as large as a man’s hand—huddle in dense, overlapping shingle-patterns, their jointed armor dryly scraping whenever a cold draft passes through the vault.

The world is active, driven by simple, mechanical cycles. Every hour or so, a heavy copper float-valve, buried deep in a wall niche and green with verdigris, reaches its limit with a loud, metallic *clink*. Instantly, a torrent of icy water gushes from a carved stone spout near the top of the wall, cascading down the masonry steps in a foaming sheet that floods the basin's floor by two inches. The snails instantly retreat into their shells, rolling in the sudden current like gravel, while the woodlice scramble upward onto the higher, buckled ridges of the tiles, creating a frantic, collective clicking sound that drowns out even the roar of the water.

Then, just as suddenly, the flow stops. The water begins to gurgle downward through dozens of hand-drilled weep-holes in the floor tiles, whispering as it drains into some deeper, unseen level of this massive, hand-built puzzle of stone. The snails emerge, the woodlice crawl back down to the damp tile-seams, and the cold, dripping quiet returns, punctuated only by the heavy, echoing beat of the ceiling-water hitting the terracotta below.
