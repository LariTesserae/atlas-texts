This is a room no larger than a boiler, buried deep inside the low foundations of a gargantuan, hollow structure. Through the narrow seams where the walls of this pocket do not quite meet, there is only a vast, silent, empty dark—miles of cold, vacant space where nothing has been installed. But here, within this single, tight chamber, there is a miniature wilderness of wet metal and choking green.

The space is entirely manufactured, though it has long since forgotten the sharp lines of its assembly. The floor, walls, and curved ceiling are constructed of thick, overlapping plates of copper, bronze, and structural iron. Over aeons of unvarying moisture, these plates have undergone an extreme, ancient erosion. The rushing, dripping of warm water has smoothed the heavy rivets into low, shiny bumps and hollowed out the thick metal flanges into gentle basins, making the industrial architecture look like the inside of a limestone cave. The rust is not flaky or dry; it has been ground down by time into a slick, dark-red peat that fills the corners. The copper has oxidized into soft, velvety coats of pale-blue verdigris, creating a strange, chalky soil that clings to the curved surfaces.

Water is the dominant medium of the room. It does not fall as rain, but rather weeps from every seam in the ceiling, running down the smoothed bronze plates in thick, warm sheets. It pools on the floor in shallow basins, where it is kept at a constant, blood-warm temperature by the deep-lying heat of the machinery below. The air is thick with a condensing mist that smells of wet iron, sweet copper salts, and decaying humus. 

Out of this metallic silt grows an incredibly lush, crowded flora. Because the temperature never fluctuates and the water never stops, the vegetation has achieved a state of permanent, overgrown stasis. Huge, fleshy ferns with fronds like wet leather hang from the overhead copper pipes, their tips dragging in the pools below. Thick carpets of liverwort and brilliant green star-moss cover every flat inch of the corroded metal, growing so dense that they form a springy, living upholstery over the pipes and valves. There are no seasons here, no cycles of budding and shedding; the plants grow to their maximum limit, their dead under-leaves rotting immediately into the warm, rich muck to feed the new fronds in a closed, perpetual loop.

The light is bright and unwavering, cast by long, thick glass panels recessed into the iron ceiling. These panels, sealed against the moisture with perished rubber gaskets, house ancient, glowing filaments that have burned with the same neutral, yellowish glare for centuries. The light cuts through the rising steam, illuminating the dripping leaves and the glossy, wet surfaces of the bronze with a flat, clinical clarity.

There is almost no animal life here. The environment is too loud, too wet, and too isolated. Only a single species of tiny, shell-less snail—blind and cast in the same pale-blue hue as the copper verdigris—moves with glacial slowness across the wet plates, grazing on the microscopic algae that coats the metal.

The defining characteristic of this pocket is its sound. It is a deafening, continuous roar that fills the room like a physical weight, pressing against the chest and vibrating through the marrow of the bones. It is composed of two layers: the immediate, high-pressure hiss of boiling water escaping from a ruptured conduit just behind the mossy iron plates, and a deep, earth-shaking rumble from the vast, empty voids outside the chamber—the distant, eternal cycling of a massive pump or turbine that never changes its pace. The sound is so loud, so flat, and so completely devoid of rhythm or variation that it eventually ceases to register as noise; it becomes the silence of the room, a permanent, roaring equilibrium that has not shifted by a single decibel in a thousand years.
