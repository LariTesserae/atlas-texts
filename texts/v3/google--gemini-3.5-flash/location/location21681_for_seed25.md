This is the **Lithic Sough**, a vast, low-lying basin locked beneath a shelf of ancient, greasy-grey dolomite. 

Here, the scale is regional—a network of connected valleys and shallow depressions stretching for leagues, covered by a perpetual stone ceiling that hangs low, sometimes dropping to within thirty feet of the floor, sometimes rising into broad, flat-vaulted domes. There is no weather as the upper world knows it. Instead, the sky is a roof of damp, ancient stone, and the horizon is simply the place where the floor and ceiling meet in a distant, crowded haze of biological steam.

### The Terrain and Water

The landscape is built entirely of stone, mud, and flesh. The rock underneath is not jagged; it has been smoothed by two forces acting over millions of years: the slow drip of lime-rich water and the endless, abrasive friction of billions of crawling bodies. Every boulder is rounded like a bar of used soap; the valley floor is a series of rolling, fat-like mounds of grey and dark-brown carbonate, polished to a dull sheen.

Moisture does not fall as rain. It condenses on the cold stone ceiling, gathering in great, heavy lenses before dropping. The sound of these drops is a constant, irregular *tock-tock-tock* that echoes through the dark. Where the drops fall regularly, they have drilled deep, smooth-bore cups into the stone. These cups overflow, sending thin, sluggish ribbons of cold water winding through the dips and swales of the basin. The water is hard, thick with dissolved lime, and cool to the touch—not freezing, but holding the steady, drawing chill of a deep cellar.

There is no soil. Where there would be dirt, there is instead a deep, shifting silt composed of shed carapaces, pulverized stone, and the greasy, grey-black dung of a trillion creatures.

### The Absence of Green

There is no vegetation. Not a single leaf, spore, or blade of grass exists in the Sough. There are no ferns, nor even the familiar shelf-fungi of upper caves. Crucially, there is no light to support them. 

The primary producers here are not plants but massive, calcified colonies of lithotrophic, coral-like animals that coat the lower wet spots. They look like grey organ pipes or clusters of stone fingers, pumping the mineral-rich water through their porous bodies to feed on sulfur compounds and deep-earth bacteria. They are cold, stony, and slowly pulse with a mechanical, involuntary contraction that sounds like wet leather being wrung out.

### The Great Din and the Churn of Life

The darkness is absolute, but it is not empty. It is a crowded, near-solid medium. To move through the Sough is to push through a living curtain.

The fauna is overwhelming, layered density upon density. On the stone floor, the ground itself seems to heave and slide. This is due to the *scritch-carpets*—millions of thumb-sized, flat-bodied arthropods with segmented shells of dull grey slate. They move in vast, multi-layered rivers, crawling over one another three or four deep, seeking the mineral crusts left by the dripping water. 

Above them, clinging to the polished slopes of the basin, are the *harp-mouths*: sessile, sack-like organisms the size of sheep. They have no eyes or limbs, only a broad, basket-shaped mouth strung with taut, vibrating fibers of cartilage. As the cool air drifts through the basin, driven by the collective movement of the fauna, these fibers thrum. 

With so many millions of them, the sound is a deafening, physical pressure. It is not a roar, but a white noise so dense it vibrates the teeth and fills the sinus cavities:
*   The dry, metallic hiss of the scritch-carpets’ legs on polished stone.
*   The deep, cello-like drone of the harp-mouths.
*   The rhythmic, wet *huff-huff* of the giant *siphon-lungs*—bus-sized, bladder-like organisms that anchor themselves to the ceiling, slowly inhaling the humid air and filtering it for microscopic drift-spores before squeezing it out through narrow, whistling vents.

The noise is so loud it acts as a tactile force; a person standing in the Sough would feel their clothing vibrating against their skin, their ears bells of dull pain, their voice swallowed entirely by the hiss and drone before the sound could leave their lips.

### The Weirdness of the Inhabitants

In this world of dark and sound, eyes are an obsolete concept. Nothing here has ever had them. Instead, life is defined by touch, vibration, and chemical scent.

Slow-moving *grease-slugs*, six feet long and covered in a thick, insulating coat of yellow tallow, drag themselves across the high ridges. They are the target of the *stilt-walkers*—spidery, silent carnivores with legs like thin cane glass that stand twelve feet high. The stilt-walkers do not use sound; they are the only quiet things in the basin. They move with incredible, hydraulic slowness, lifting one needle-thin leg at a time and placing it precisely between the scurrying scritch-carpets to avoid alerting their prey. When they find a grease-slug, they drop their soft, star-shaped mouths down like a plunge-pump, liquefying the slug's tallow-flesh with acid before sucking it up.

High above, hanging from the ceiling like grotesque, leathery fruit, are the *lantern-galls*. They are the only source of light, though it is a useless light. They emit a faint, sickly-green bioluminescence from their translucent bellies—not to see, but as a byproduct of their highly volatile digestive systems, which break down carbonate rock into calcium and gas. The gas fills their internal chambers, keeping them buoyant so they do not crush themselves under their own weight. They hang in clusters of thousands, glowing like dying coals in a drafty grate, casting long, shivering shadows of the stilt-walkers across the pale, polished mounds below.

### The Rules and the Dynamic

The Sough is an ecosystem in constant, furious motion, but it is an ancient, balanced machine. The population of the scritch-carpets is kept in check by the sheer volume of filter-feeders and ground-predators, while the stilt-walkers are limited by the slow growth rate of the grease-slugs.

Every few weeks, a "tide" occurs. The heavy carbon dioxide produced by the billions of breathing things settles into the lowest pockets of the basin, creating invisible, suffocating lakes of dead air. When this happens, the fauna must migrate climb-ward. The scritch-carpets roll up the polished slopes in grey, snapping waves, covering everything. The stilt-walkers gather on the highest stone ridges, their long legs tangled together like dead brushwood, waiting for the siphon-lungs on the ceiling to finish their slow pumping, clearing the air and drawing the oxygen down from the upper cracks once more.

This is a world that has never known a sun, a tree, or quiet. It is cool, wet, slick with grease and lime, and so filled with life that there is no room for death to rot; everything that dies is consumed, ground down, and polished back into the grey stone within hours by the endless, noisy crawl of the basin.
