The world here is bounded, held within the hollow heart-wood of a single, titanic vegetable node—a cathedral-sized pocket of space known to its few inhabitants as the *Phloem Basin*. 

### The Substrate and Structure
The ground is not soil, but a dense, packed floor of fossilized peat and interlaced, petrified root-systems, worn smooth by generations of soft-soled boots and the light iron wheels of narrow-gauge trolleys. It yields slightly to the heel, damp and cool, like wood that has spent a century underwater but remains structurally sound. 

The walls of this basin rise eighty feet before curving inward to form a rib-vaulted ceiling. These walls are alive: they are the inner cambium of a colossal, slow-growing organism. The bark is a deep, bruised purple-grey, thick with ridges that have been carved over decades into stairwell treads, shelving, and ornamental niches. At the summit of this chamber, the ceiling is composed of colossal, translucent "skylight fronds." These leaves, each the size of a dockyard crane, filter the sky above into a steady, submarine green daylight, occasionally shifting to a warm amber when the sun outside passes directly overhead.

The entire basin is weathered, settled, and ancient. There are no raw cuts or fresh splinters; where the inhabitants have bored tunnels or anchor-points into the wood, the tree has long since responded by rolling back its outer bark in smooth, grey lips of scar tissue, sealing the iron masonry bolts firmly in place.

### The Damp and the Water
Water is not a presence of rivers or lakes, but of constant, atmospheric transit. The air is cellar-cool (never freezing, but carrying a sharp, damp chill that smells of crushed mint and wet iron). Moisture accumulates on the colossal leaves of the ceiling, condensing into fat, heavy droplets that take several seconds to fall to the basin floor. 

To catch this, a network of copper-wire nets—fine as cobwebs—is suspended across the upper void. These nets collect the fog and channel it down through tiny brass gutters into narrow, vertical copper pipes that cling to the bark like ivy. The sound of this system is a constant, rhythmic *tink-tink-drip* from the cisterns below, muffled by the soft velvet moss that blankets every horizontal surface. There are no puddles on the walkways; the porous peat-floor absorbs any stray droplets instantly, drawing them back down into the vascular system of the outer world.

### The Industrial Infrastructure
Clinging to the lower thirds of the walls is a highly organized, sparse settlement. This is not a chaotic sprawl, but an orderly, tiered industrial hamlet built of blackened pig iron, green-patinated copper, and cured heartwood. 

Because the space is small—scarcely three hundred yards across—every structure is designed with clockwork efficiency. There are no free-standing houses. Instead, three tiers of dwellings and workshops are bracketed directly into the vertical wood-walls, connected by light, suspended iron catwalks that bounce gently when walked upon. 

The technology here is mechanical and botanical. Simple, slow-stroking steam-pistons—fueled by the slow fermentation of leaf-mulch in deep, sealed vats beneath the floor—drive copper shafts that run along the catwalks. These shafts turn wooden gearboxes that power small, clicking looms, timber-saws, and sap-separators. 

Every evening, the "transpiration pumps" are turned on. These are brass-domed engines, the size of haystacks, that assist the natural vascular draw of the surrounding giant plant. They operate with a deep, liquid *chuff-thump, chuff-thump* that vibrates through the soles of anyone standing on the peat-floor. The pumps draw up the bitter, pale amber latex from the tree’s deep roots, refining it into a vulcanized oil used to waterproof fabrics and lubricate the iron gears of the hamlet.

### The Atmosphere and Dynamics
Despite the heavy machinery, the Phloem Basin is mostly quiet. The sheer mass of living vegetable tissue acts as an acoustic sponge; a shout from one side of the basin reaches the other as a soft, dull murmur. The air is thick enough to deaden the clang of iron on iron, turning it into a flat, wooden *thud*.

The population is small, perhaps thirty households in total, and their movements are sparse and deliberate. There is no bustle. A lone operator in oil-stained overalls might be seen on a high catwalk, adjusting a copper valve with a long wrench, or a child might cross an iron bridge carrying a basket of dried, edible fungus.

The dynamics of the place are tied to the plant’s biological cycles. Twice a day, the "sap-tide" rises. The pressure gauges on the copper mains slowly climb into the red; the wooden walls of the basin give off a series of deep, groan-like creaks as the cambium swells with high-pressure fluid. During these hours, the engines are idle, and the residents retreat indoors or sit on their balconies, watching the copper pipes sweat green moisture under the strain. 

Once the tide passes, a natural fog rolls down from the ceiling fronds, cooling the chamber and initiating a brief, five-minute drizzle of heavy condensation—the "room-rain." When the rain ends, the air clears to perfect, green-tinted transparency, and the quiet work of processing, spinning, and maintaining the pipes begins once more.

### Fauna
Animals are almost nonexistent inside the basin. There are no birds or rodents. The only native lives are the *gasket-beetles*—hand-sized, flat insects with dull bronze shells that live near the steam vents. They are slow, blind, and harmless, surviving by consuming the mineral grease that accumulates on the iron joints of the pumps. The people leave them be; they are part of the maintenance of the place, keeping the fittings free of rust. Occasionally, one can hear their dry, clicking legs as they crawl over a cool pipe, a tiny, metallic sound in the green, damp silence.
