Beneath a crust of cold, forgotten stone lies an underworld of immense, silent scale. It is a region of absolute dryness, where the concept of a drop of water has never existed. In its place is a thick, velvet warmth—not the humid heat of a jungle, but the dry, static-charged furnace-warmth of deep earth that has baked for eons without ventilation.

This cavernous void is so vast that its ceilings are lost in a warm, dark haze. There are no clouds, but there is a weather of dust: a perpetual, unmoving suspension of microscopic mica flakes and dry talc that hangs in the air like a fog of ground glass. It does not fall, and it does not drift; the air currents here are closed loops, stable and eternal, maintaining an equilibrium so perfect that a single suspended grain of dust might occupy the same cubic inch of space for three thousand years.

The floor of this world is young and viciously sharp. Because there has never been rain, river, or wind to soften them, the rocks are razor-edged. Vast terraces of black volcanic glass and fractured basalt rise in jagged steps, their fracture lines as clean and lethal as scalpel blades. Great rifts yawn across the plains—cracks five miles deep that were opened during some ancient settling of the planet’s core and have remained perfectly preserved, without a grain of sand tumbling down their sheer, mirror-smooth faces.

And yet, despite the absolute lack of moisture and light, this darkness is crowded. It is dense with a silent, bizarre biology that has no need for water or sun. 

Clinging to the vertical faces of the basalt cliffs are millions of flat, leathery discs. They are the primary fauna of this place: dry, plate-like organisms the size of shields, textured like lizard skin but stiff as horn. They do not breathe, nor do they run. Instead, they press themselves flat against the warm stone, absorbing the dry geothermal infrared heat directly through their bellies. They are so numerous that from a distance—if there were light to see them—the cliffs would appear to be shingled in black dragon scales. They are nearly motionless, packed rim-to-rim in a state of living hibernation that lasts for centuries. 

Among these plate-dwellers crawl larger, multi-jointed creatures with bodies of hollowed, horn-like chitin. They move with an aching, robotic slowness, their dry joints clicking like dry twigs in the dark. They do not have eyes; instead, they have broad, leaf-shaped antennae that detect the minute electrostatic charges of the dust-haze. They feed on the sulfurous mineral crust that exudes from the dry rock fissures, scraping it off with heavy, spade-like forelimbs.

There is no vegetation. There are no roots, no leaves, no mold. There are only the mineral formations and the dry, slow animals that have adapted to live upon them. 

Here and there, scattered across the jagged terraces of black glass, are the marks of some primitive, ancient intelligence. These are not cities, but low, dry-stone huts built of interlocking basalt flakes, stacked without mortar. They are simple, crouching structures, built by hands that did not use tools of metal, but rather shaped the glass rock by crude, patient chipping. 

Inside these low shelters, one might find the remnants of a dry, hand-crafted culture. There are heavy, flat plates of cold-hammered native copper, beaten into crude dishes or simple, dull-edged scrapers. There are ropes made not of hemp or fiber—for nothing grows here—but of the long, hair-like mineral threads of asbestos, braided together with patient, calloused fingers. Every artifact is caked in a fine layer of the eternal mica dust, undisturbed and bone-dry. 

Occasionally, one encounters a beacon-post: a tall, splintered column of dry, fossilized bone or petrified wood, anchored in a pile of heavy stones. At the top of these poles are small, shallow iron cups containing cold, blackened tallow—the rendered fat of some giant, deep-dwelling creature. These lamps have not been lit for age upon age; there is no smoke, no soot, only the dry, grease-scented crust of fuel that has turned to stone in the warm dark.

The silence of this place is heavy, almost physical. It is not a dead silence, but a muffled, crowded quiet. The dry dust in the air absorbs sound, deadening it before it can travel. A stone falling into a rift makes a sharp, dry *clink*, and then the sound is swallowed instantly, leaving no echo. The only continuous sound is a low, vibrational hum that does not travel through the air, but through the bedrock itself—the deep, rhythmic grinding of the world's crust, miles above and miles below, operating at a frequency so low it is felt in the bones rather than heard by the ear.

Nothing changes. There is no morning, no evening, no winter. The warmth remains at the exact threshold of a desert noon; the dust remains suspended; the black plates on the walls remain pressed to the heat of the stone. If an traveler were to stand in the center of one of these obsidian plains, they would feel as though they had walked into a museum of the universe’s first days—a place where the ink of creation dried instantly, leaving the page clean, sharp, and forever dark.
