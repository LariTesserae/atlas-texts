The place is called the Sieve, though it is not a tool but a gorge—a sharp, newly opened fracture in the gray limestone plateaus, no longer than a village street and barely forty paces wide. It feels less like an open valley and more like a cathedral whose vaulted roof was cleanly sheared off by a frost-wedge. 

Water does not merely fall here; it emerges. The vertical walls of the cleft, jagged and unrounded by time, are alive with a cold, perpetual weeping. The stone is not smooth; it consists of raw, step-like shelves that terminate in razor-thin edges, still smelling faintly of struck flint. Down these black, mineral-stained faces, water slides in thin, shivering sheets, collecting in deep, narrow troughs cut into the chasm floor. The atmosphere is an active, condensing cold. Every breath is visible as a brief, gray plume that rises to join the mist hanging permanently twenty feet above the wet slate roofs.

Within this narrow, humid slot, space is an absolute premium. 

An urban density has been forced into this miniature geography. Hundreds of tiny, box-like dwellings—none larger than a ship’s cabin—are stacked directly on top of one another, wedged into the natural crannies of the cliffside like barnacles on a rocky hull. They are constructed from the very stone that fell during the gorge's creation, split into thin, flat shingles and laid without mortar, held together by sheer weight and the stickiness of the river-silt. Where the stone walls cannot reach, pine poles and thick, braided hemp ropes anchor swinging galleries and precarious, ladder-like staircases that bridge the gap from one side of the gorge to the other. There are no streets, only a labyrinth of wet stone ledges and dripping underpasses where one must walk sideways to avoid scraping the bare rock.

The technology is quiet, manual, and heavy. There are no chimneys of brick, only flat slabs of slate propped open with wet wooden wedges to let the pale smoke of turf-fires escape. The tools are bone, antler, and heavy greenstone adzes. Everywhere is the evidence of hand-carving: the steps are notched with rough tooth-marks from flint chisels; the drainage channels are grooved by generations of steady grinding with sand and water. It is a city built by hand-span, piece by piece, crowded so tightly that a person in their hammock can hear the chewing of their neighbor three layers of stone away.

Vegetation is a luxury the gorge cannot afford. The sheer walls and the narrow opening above limit daylight to a strong, silver noon that lasts only two hours before sinking back into a cool twilight. In this perpetual damp shade, no trees can root. Instead, the stone is coated in a slick, gelatinous liverwort that ranges from bruise-blue to a pale, greasy yellow. It smells faintly of cellar-dirt and copper. In the dark corners beneath the bridges, a white, hard-bodied shelf-fungus grows, which the inhabitants scrape off with shell-knives to dry for tinder.

The fauna of the Sieve is rare, silent, and specialized. Occasionally, a heavy, slate-gray salamander, long as a man’s forearm, will crawl out of the dry-stone foundations, its skin glistening with the same mineral grease that coats the walls. It moves without haste, hunting the eyeless, white isopods that live in the cisterns. The people do not bother them; they are considered part of the masonry, keeping the foundations clean of wood-rot. Overhead, the only birds are small, soot-colored swifts that nest in the dry crevices where the heat of the hearths leaks through the stone tiles. Their dry, clicking calls are one of the few sounds that break the silence.

The dominant sound is the water: the heavy, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of condensation falling from the roof-scaffolds into hollowed stone catch-basins, the low, gurgling sigh of the under-floor drains, and the constant, wet brush of wool clothing against wet stone. Voices are kept low, held in by the close walls; to shout in the Sieve is considered an act of violence, as the limestone carries sound like a taut wire.

There is a strange, slow law that governs this place. The water that weeps from the cliffs is not pure rain; it is heavy with dissolved calcium, a petrifying spring that coats everything it touches in a brittle, white crust of travertine. The city is in a constant, slow battle against its own petrification. Every morning, the inhabitants must take their stone scrapers and clear the lime-crust from their leather door-hinges, their wooden rain-gutters, and the rungs of their cedar ladders. If they neglect this for a week, the ladders become thick and white as bone, fused forever to the cliff-face, and the wooden doors are sealed shut into the rock. 

Yet, they use this hardening water to their advantage. To repair a roof, they do not find clay; they lay down birch-bark and let the dripping water petrify it over three moons into a seamless, stone-hard slate. The city is growing itself, calcifying into a single, massive, honeycomb-like fossil wherein the living move through the chambers before the stone can settle around them.
