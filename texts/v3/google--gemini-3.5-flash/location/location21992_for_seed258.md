The valley sits at a great height, where the mountain air should be thin and freezing, but instead, it is thick, warm, and heavy with the scent of split stems and hot starch. 

There is no stone here. There is no soil. 

The entire landscape—the towering cliffs that form the valley walls, the wide stepped terraces that descend to the basin, and the broad, flat plazas between them—is polished green cellulose. It is a world grown rather than geologically deposited, yet its forms are not those of a wild forest. Everything is built. The ground is a continuous, seamless pavement of jade-colored rind, slightly yielding underfoot like heavy leather, polished smooth and kept wet by a constant, shivering film of water.

This water does not rise from springs or fall as chaotic rain; it is wept directly from the architecture. High above, monumental arches resemble the ribs of some impossible, sky-spanning leaf. From the undersides of these ribs, warm water drips in perfectly spaced, metronomic lines. The droplets fall through hundreds of feet of clear air, landing in shallow, pre-molded runnels that are cast directly into the cellulose floor. 

Because the landscape is brand new—embryonic in its history, untouched by the slow sanding of time—there is no erosion. The edges of the steps are sharp, clean-cut, and translucent, like jade knives. The water has not carved a single groove of its own choice; it flows precisely where the green pavement dictates, sliding down glassy, engineered chutes toward the basin below. There is no mud, no sand, and no debris. Not a single leaf has fallen to rot in the channels, because these plants do not shed. They are locked in a state of permanent, evergreen perfection.

The scale of the place is massive, but it is strangely empty. There are no clusters of undergrowth, no weeds, and no tangles of vines. Instead, a few colossal, hollow pillars of green wood rise at vast intervals from the wet plazas, like the columns of an unfinished temple left to dry in the sun. Between these pillars lie wide, glistening expanses of nothingness—hundreds of yards of empty, wet green floor under an open sky. The density of the world is sparse, balanced, and quiet in its placement, though the atmosphere itself is far from silent.

Indeed, the valley is incredibly loud. 

The sound is not one of animal life or mechanical clatter, but the deep, resonant booming of the landscape’s own internal circulation. Within the massive walls of the cliffs and the hollow cores of the pillars, millions of gallons of warm water are being steadily pumped upward through vascular tubes. It is a low, vibrating hum that shakes the soles of one's feet—a wet, roaring drone like a distant waterfall captured inside a wooden cello. The wind, passing through flutes and oval apertures grown into the high arches, adds a whistling, pipe-organ harmony to the deep hum, creating a continuous, unchanging chord that has never shifted pitch.

Nothing shifts. The dynamic of the valley is frozen in an eternal, living equilibrium. The light—bright, clear, and slightly greened by the high, translucent canopies that fringe the valley’s rim—never dims or brightens. There are no seasons here, no nights, and no growth. The plants are complete; they have reached their final, adult shapes and have simply stopped growing, remaining turgid, warm, and wet. The water flows at the same liters-per-minute today as it did a thousand years ago, and as it will a thousand years from now. 

In this vast green cathedral, fauna is a rare anomaly. Occasionally, one might glimpse a creature: a soft, shell-less thing the color of skimmed milk, six feet long, gliding slowly across the wet pavement on a single muscular foot. It has no eyes and no mouth, only a broad, porous underside that absorbs the microscopic cells of algae that occasionally form in the water channels. It moves with a glacial, deliberate stillness, its path as fixed and predictable as the water grooves it follows. Floating high in the warm air, suspended between the arches, are small, transparent bell-buoys—bladder-like organisms that drift without steering, catching the moisture-laden wind and whistling in soft accompaniment to the valley’s great drone.

To stand here is to stand inside a colossal, pristine machine made of flesh and sap. It is warm, wet, and perfectly clean. There is no dust to settle, no wind strong enough to break the rubbery limbs of the sky-canopies, and no death to bring the smell of decay. It is a monument of living wood, eternally raw, eternally new, and forever listening to its own loud, liquid heartbeat.
