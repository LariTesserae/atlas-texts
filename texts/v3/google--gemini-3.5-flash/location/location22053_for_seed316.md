There is no sky here, not in the sense of an emptiness above the earth. There is only the *upper medium*, a warm, perpetual twilight of suspended moisture so dense that it behaves like a slow, vertical swamp. 

This is a world ground down to its absolute basin. Every mountain that once was has been reduced to an infinite, microscopic silt, suspended in a warm, shallow sheet of water that covers a continent-wide tableland. The elevation is low, flat, and uniform—a single, massive, water-logged shelf where the horizon is a blur of steam and mud. The water is everywhere, not as a deep ocean, but as a hyper-saturated, weeping skin. It oozes from the silt, it hangs in giant, soft-walled spheres in the thick air, and it rains upward from the ground in warm, oily sheets, driven by the planet’s immense, chaotic internal engine.

The heat is heavy, damp, and unmoving—a thick, maternal warmth that smells of sulfur, ancient minerals, and wet iron. It is a world that feels packed to the absolute margin of its capacity. There is no vacuum, no room for a breeze to passage through without pushing against millions of tons of floating vapor, colloidal clay, and vibrating acoustic waves. The space itself is crowded; the air is less like gas and more like a warm, breathing gel that resists quick movement. Every cubic inch of this landscape is occupied by matter in a state of high-pressure oscillation.

Because of this density, the sound here is a physical presence. It is deafening, a continuous, low-register roar that does not come from any single source but from the entire landscape at once. It is the sound of billions of tons of water sliding over flat clay, of wet air rubbing against itself, of deep geothermal gas bubbles the size of cities collapsing under their own weight beneath the mud. To hear it is to feel your chest cavity vibrate in time with the planet’s slow, mechanical groan.

Yet, despite this terrible crowding of physical matter, the world is virtually empty of life. 

Vegetation is a rare and desperate thing. There are no forests, no grassy plains, no green things that reach for a sun they cannot see. Instead, there are only sparse, isolated colonies of rubbery black bladder-worts, twenty feet wide, that drift through the warm mud-shallows like discarded leather shields. In the places where the clay is slightly drier—where the ancient erosion has left a temporary hump of packed silt—one might find clusters of thick, rootless tuber-vines that have no leaves, only wet, throbbing skins that absorb the orange twilight directly through their pores. They look less like plants and more like the internal organs of some colossal, buried beast.

The fauna is rarer still. Every few miles, the water will boil with the clumsy movement of a *wallow-slug*—a blind, grey, leather-skinned mass the size of a hay-wain, with no limbs and no face, that simply rolls through the warm silt, sucking up the organic grease left behind by decayed microbes. Occasionally, floating in the upper, wetter layers of the atmosphere, one might glimpse the slow, rhythmic contraction of a *sky-membrane*—a translucent sheet of living tissue that grows to the size of a village square, drifting on the thermal upwells, catching the wet mineral dust that rises from the flats.

Here, the rules of reality have grown tired. The world is so old, so thoroughly worn out by its own age, that the fundamental laws of physics have begun to fray at the edges. Gravity does not point straight down; it operates in localized, shifting sags. In some areas, the water pools on the *underside* of invisible, horizontal planes in the air, creating vast, inverted lakes that hang suspended above the mud, their surfaces rippling from the heat from below. In other places, the flow of time seems to thicken along with the mud; you can watch a globule of water fall from a hanging clay ledge and take twenty minutes to reach the ground, turning over and over in slow, agonized suspension, only to hit the silt and shatter with the dry, metallic ring of glass.

The dynamics of this place are hyperactive, a restless, Solaris-like state of constant, turbulent creation and destruction. The landscape is never still. A mile-wide stretch of clay will suddenly liquefy, turning into a boiling, vertical geyser of brown soup that screams as it shoots five miles into the dim air, only to freeze into a weird, temporary pillar of salt-crust that stands for an hour before dissolving back into the marsh. Great circular tides of warm water, twelve inches deep but a hundred miles wide, sweep across the basin in unpredictable patterns, driven by the wobbling spin of the planet’s core.

Through this churning, crowded world, there are occasional marks of something built. They are not cities, nor even ruins in the human sense. They are colossal, melted ramparts of dark, fused basalt-glass that rise thirty or forty feet out of the mud, like the stumps of teeth. They are deeply eroded—their surfaces smoothed by millions of years of wet, abrasive air until they look like giant fingers of dark grease. There are no doors, no windows, no tool marks. They are the remnants of some prehistoric, biological architecture from an epoch before the mountains died, when the world’s inhabitants did not build with stone or metal, but grew their shelters out of the mineral marrow of the earth itself. They stand as silent, useless breakwaters against the endless, warm sloshing of the mud-tides.

The light that illuminates this world is a dim, sour saffron. There is no day and no night, only a slow, periodic thickening of the gloom into a dark, purplish wine-color every few dozen hours. The light does not come from above, but seems to emanate from the very moisture in the air—a faint, phosphorescent glow caused by the friction of trillions of microscopic mineral crystals colliding in the heavy atmosphere. It casts no shadows, making it impossible to judge distance or depth. Everything is near, and everything is far.

To walk here—if one could find footing on the shifting, semi-liquid clay—would be to exist in a state of warm, deafening weight. You would feel the air pressing against your skin like a thick, wet wool blanket. You would watch silent, mile-high waves of warm mist roll toward you across a horizon that bends upward into the twilight. You would hear the Earth singing its low, dying song, a physical roar that shakes the water in your eyes, while around you, the very matter of the universe forgets what it is supposed to be—solid, liquid, or gas—and dances in a slow, heavy, primordial soup.
