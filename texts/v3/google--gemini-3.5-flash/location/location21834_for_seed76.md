The sky is not blue, but a deep, hard-edged indigo that suggests the vacuum just beyond it. Because of the extreme elevation, the air is thin, crisp, and so dry that moisture is not even a memory. Here, water does not exist in any phase—solid, liquid, or gas. There are no clouds of vapor, only vast, shifting curtains of dust and dry, crystalline pollen that catch the unfiltered sunlight. 

The light is blinding. It pours down from a brilliant, oversized star, striking the high-altitude plateaus with a clarity that eliminates all haze. Shadows are not soft; they are sharp, black ink-slashes cut across a landscape of pale, wind-polished stone.

This is a world of immense, continental scale, where the horizons recede into infinity. One can stand on a high ridge of oxidized siltstone and look out over a drop that plunges twenty miles into a flat-bottomed basin, only for the basin to rise again into another range of plateaus fifty leagues away. Every feature of the land has been shaped by the slow, relentless friction of dust-gas winds over billions of years. The geology is ancient, settled, and in perfect equilibrium. There are no jagged, young peaks; the mountains are vast, flat-topped tables with sides that have been carved into smooth, undulating flutes and bowls, like the interior of a giant’s woodwind instrument.

Through this monumental emptiness, life has achieved a strange, dry abundance. 

Though the land is sparsely populated in terms of individual density—with miles of bare, chalky basalt separating each living community—where vegetation does take hold, it is astonishingly lush. These are "dry-forests," though they contain no wood or sap. Instead of relying on water, the flora here utilizes a non-aqueous, silicon-organic chemistry. 

These plants grow in immense, isolated basins where the mineral dust settles. They look like towering structures of spun glass, copper-colored vellum, and pale green wax. Great ribbon-trees, three hundred feet tall but only inches thick, rise like vertical sails to catch the high-altitude winds. They are hollow, kept upright by internal chambers pressurized with nitrogen gas. Beneath them, carpet-mosses of golden-brown and silver-grey lie three feet deep. If you were to step on them, they would not squish or yield damply; they would crackle like dry autumn leaves, releasing a faint smell of warm parchment and ozone. 

The flora does not rot when it dies. Without moisture or bacteria, there is no decay. Instead, dead structures are slowly broken down by the intense ultraviolet light of the sun and the abrasive wear of the wind. A fallen ribbon-tree lies for centuries, its copper-colored fibers slowly turning to dry, glittering powder that feeds the next generation of growth.

The atmosphere is incredibly quiet. The wind does not howl; it has nothing to friction against but the polished stone and the dry, flexible sails of the ribbon-trees. It makes a sound like a long, drawn-out sigh—a low-frequency *huff* that vibrates in the chest rather than the ears. Occasionally, when the wind changes direction, the giant glass-ferns in the basins knock against one another, producing a delicate, metallic *clink* that carries for miles through the thin, silent air.

Fauna is exceedingly rare. In a three-day journey across the high flats, one might see only a single living creature. They are light, delicate things, built like kites or gossamer lanterns. They do not walk; the gravity of this high-altitude realm is countered by their immense, gas-filled membranes. They drift on the thermal updrafts, filter-feeding on the airborne pollen and dry-spores. They move without sound, trailing long, thread-like sensory filaments that brush against the tops of the ribbon-trees. Their bodies are semi-translucent, glowing like lanterns when they pass between the eye and the brilliant sun.

The movements of this world are grand and slow. There are no sudden storms of rain, but there are "pollen-fronts"—vast, gold-colored weather systems that roll across the plateaus over the course of weeks. These fronts are driven by the temperature differentials between the sunlit tables and the deep, cold shadow-canyons. As a pollen-front approaches, the light shifts from a stark, bright white to a warm, amber glow. The dry-mosses respond to the change in electrostatic charge, opening their waxen pods with a soft, collective *pop* that sounds like the first drops of rain on dry canvas.

It is a world that has never known a drop of wetness, yet it is full of life. It is cool, clean, and perfectly preserved, operating on a clock of wind and light that has run undisturbed for epochs.
