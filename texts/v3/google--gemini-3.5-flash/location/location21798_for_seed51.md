The air is cold enough to make your breath curl into thick, slow-dissolving plumes, but it is too heavy with moisture to ever dry. This is a low-lying, basin-like sink—an endless shelf of waterlogged tableland that sits just slightly above a flat, silent sea. It feels old, not with the ruggedness of young mountains, but with the extreme, smooth-polished age of a land that has spent ten million years being planed down by water and something much more patient than water.

The light is a permanent, shadowless violet-gray, as if the sun had set hours ago or is still hours from rising. The sky is not a clear dome but a thick, curdled ceiling of mist that hangs so low it occasionally grazes the tops of the low-profile hummocks. 

Everything here is wet, but the water behaves with an uncanny, viscous intelligence. It is everywhere: filling shallow, wide-mouthed basins that stretch for miles; weeping in silent, uniform sheets down the curved flanks of slick, black basalt mounds; and hanging in the air as a dense, cold steam that leaves a sweet, mineral taste on the tongue. In some places, the water does not run downhill. It clings to the undersides of horizontal stone ledges in fat, trembling blisters that never seem to drop, defying gravity through a subtle, localized distortion of surface tension. 

There are no trees—not as we know them—but the earth is covered in a dense, mossy skin that is lush to the point of suffocation. Thick, liver-colored mats of liverwort and emerald-green bryophytes layer over one another, yards deep, forming a spongy carpet that gives underfoot like a half-frozen bog. These plants have no bark or wood; they are wet and soft, and when stepped on, they do not break but simply compress, releasing a faint, pale blue luminescence from their bruised cells that takes several minutes to fade back into the dark. Giant, flat-lobed ferns with ribs that look like silver wire trace patterns across the stone, their fronds clicking softly against one another whenever a draft passes through the basin.

The erosion here is complete. The rocks are not sharp or jagged; they are bulbous, rounded shapes that look like giant, dark-grey kneecaps and shoulders rising from the bogs. They have been licked smooth by eons of wind and tide, but upon close inspection, their curves are too perfect. They do not follow the random cleavage planes of natural basalt. Instead, the stone is carved into precise, repeating mathematical splines—subtle, logarithmic spirals and parabolic curves that suggest they were not worn down by chaos, but were deliberately machined over geological epochs by the invisible field-flows that permeate the region.

And the air hums. 

It is a loud, physical sound—a deep, resonant vibration that throys through the soles of your feet and makes the water in your eyes shimmer. It is not the sound of wind or machinery, but the background noise of the universe being kept online. It sounds like a massive, distant turbine hummed through a wet blanket, interspersed with high-pitched, crystalline *clinks* like glass marbles falling onto a stone floor. This latter sound is actually the rain; when the sky condenses enough to drop water, the droplets do not splash with a soft *pat*. They hit the slick rocks with a sharp, dry ring, as if the water itself is structured differently here—held in some high-density, semi-crystalline state that only softens once it joins the larger pools.

There are no ruins. No walls, no foundations, no rusted scrap. The wilderness is total. Yet, the entire landscape operates like an engineered system. Every few hours, the water level in the shallow valleys rises and falls by several feet, not with the slow pull of a moon, but in sharp, rhythmic pulses that occur within seconds. The water simply *is* there, and then it is withdrawn back into the porous stone below, leaving the emerald mosses dripping and gasping before the next cycle begins. 

Sometimes, the space between the basalt dome-hills stutters. You will look across a wide, shallow reach of moss-flats and see a smudge in the air, a visual tear where the background is shifted slightly to the left, or where the ice-cold air suddenly smells of hot copper and ozone. In these spots, the water droplets do not fall down or cling; they drift sideways in slow, horizontal lines, like tiny spheres of mercury blown by an imperceptible draft.

Of creatures, there are almost none. There are no birds in the cold, thick air, and no fish in the glassy pools. Every now and then, however, you might see a pale, translucent shape slip through the wet mosses—a creature like a blind, foot-long salamander made of soft, flexible quartz. It does not crawl so much as it slides along the lines of the subterranean energy fields, its internal organs visible as tiny, pulsing lines of gold-colored thread. It is silent, indifferent, and rare, appearing only when the ground-resonance reaches a particular pitch, and then vanishing into the tight, water-filled crevices of the basalt.

This is a world that has outlived its makers and perhaps its own purpose, yet its machinery has dissolved so deeply into the water, the stone, and the moss that the division between the natural and the artificial has ceased to exist. It is a quiet, wet, humming garden, self-cleaning and self-sustaining, waiting out eternity at a cool, constant temperature under a twilight that never yields.
