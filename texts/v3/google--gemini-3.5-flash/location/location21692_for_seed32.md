This is a landscape contained within a fissure—a vertical pocket three feet wide, six feet high, and perhaps ten feet deep, hidden inside the foundation walls of an unimaginably vast, dry, ancient masonry complex. There is no soil here, no bedrock, no natural stone. Every surface is made.

The space is entirely occupied by thin, vertical plates of unglazed terracotta and pale-yellow ceramic slip. They are stacked edge-on, side-by-side, like the leaves of a book that has been jammed shut by immense weight. There are thousands of them. They do not rattle or lean; they are packed so tightly against one another that not a single millimetre of empty space remains between their broad faces. Only their top edges are exposed, forming a ribbed, corduroy-like floor that curves slightly under the weight of some long-forgotten architectural pressure. 

Everything is dry with a dryness that feels classic, permanent, and mineral. There is no moisture in the air, no dampness in the deep seams of the clay. A fine, pale dust, as soft as flour, rests in the microscopic grooves on top of the tiles. Because there is no water, nothing decays. 

From the narrow, hair-thin gaps where the ceramic plates do not quite touch the plaster-smeared walls, a few sparse elements of vegetation have arisen. They are not green. They are stiff, hollow-stemmed straws of a fossil-pale, papery reed that must have grown during some distant, long-ended age when the atmosphere was different. Now, they are dry and mineralized, frozen in place like copper wires. They do not bend or sway. They have no leaves, only tiny, dry husks at their tips that look like miniature lanterns, preserved perfectly by the absolute lack of humidity.

A single, intense shaft of cool, glaring light cuts down into this pocket from an unseen light-well high above. The light is bright—almost blindingly white—but it carries no heat. It strikes the upper edges of the yellow and orange tiles, turning the fine layer of settled dust into a brilliant, glowing carpet. 

Despite the total stillness of the scene, the pocket is incredibly loud. 

A deafening, continuous hum vibrates through the clay plates. It is not the sound of machinery, but an acoustic phenomenon: the immense, distant heavy wind of the outer plains, miles away, is forced into the deep vents of the masonry complex. By the time it reaches this tiny, packed chamber, the wind has no physical flow—there is no draft to ruffle the dust or stir the papery straw—but its pressure has been converted into a massive, unchanging acoustic roar. It is a single, low-frequency B-flat, so dense and saturated that it makes the teeth ache and causes the suspended dust particles in the light-beam to lock into perfect, unmoving geometric patterns, held in place by the standing waves of the sound.

Nothing moves. No breeze shifts the dust. The cool, dry air remains at exactly the same temperature, hour after hour, century after century. The erosion here is finished; it reached its equilibrium eons ago when the edges of the ceramic plates were finally worn smooth and round by the dust-laden air of the past. Now, the landscape is in a state of suspended animation. It is a tiny, bright, roaring cupboard of baked earth, utterly devoid of life, perfectly structured, and completely, beautifully loud.
