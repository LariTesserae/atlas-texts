High upon a jagged, unweathered ridge where the air is warm and dry as baked flour, there sits a narrow cleft no larger than a village square. This is the Notch of the Tallow-Glands. 

No streams run here; no rain has ever fallen upon these slopes. The geology is young—sharp, fractured plates of pale gypsum and obsidian that have only recently, in the earth’s scale of time, thrust upward through the crust. The edges of the rocks are like fresh glass, unrounded by water, showing clean cleavage planes that catch the bright, steady sunlight.

Yet the Notch is crowded with life. In the absence of water, a dense, lard-based ecology thrives. The vegetation is lush but entirely non-aqueous, consisting of plants that store thick, golden fats and waxy resins instead of sap. Fleshy, paddle-leafed shrubs with the texture of cold butter jostle for space along the mineral ledges. Beside them grow towering, organ-pipe stalks made of a dry, fibrous cork that exudes a heavy, aromatic turpentine. These plants do not wilt; they are rigid and stiff, their leaves coated in a powdery, blue-white dust that protects them from the desiccating heat. They grow so closely packed that their branches interlock, forming a springy, dry canopy of silver and mustard-yellow.

Within this botanical crowd sits an organized, vertical city. It is built without tools, grown and secreted by its inhabitants over generations. The structures are nested into the sharp gypsum crevices like the nests of cliff-swallows, but on a massive, municipal scale. They are composed of a tough, lightweight carton—a mixture of masticated plant fiber, dry mineral dust, and a hardening insect wax that cures in the sun to the consistency of dried leather. 

Stacked five or six levels high, these dwellings form a continuous, cellular terrace. Small, round doorways open onto narrow ledge-streets. Spiral ramps made of hardened resin wind around the trunks of the cork-trees, connecting the lower apartments to the high nests. Bridges of woven, hair-like root fibers span the gaps between rock walls. It is a city of high density but zero technology: every arch, cell, and tunnel has been shaped by the mouths, gland-secretions, and patient limbs of the community.

The inhabitants are everywhere, though they make virtually no sound. They are soft-limbed, multi-segmented creatures about the size of rabbits, covered in a dense, velvet-like pile that dampens any noise of their movement. They move with an extreme, deliberate slowness, climbing the resin ladders with the steady composure of chameleons. Along with them, thousands of small, wingless paper-bugs crawl over the outer walls of the hive-homes, their dry chitin sliding soundlessly over the wax. Flat, disc-shaped grazers drift like slow snails across the buttery leaves of the shrubs, harvesting the surface fats.

An absolute, heavy silence rules the Notch. The atmosphere, rich with volatile organic vapors and devoid of moisture, behaves like a room lined with wool. The sound of a falling stone is deadened instantly upon landing in the soft, dry leaf-litter of the yellow shrubs. There is no wind to rattle the stiff branches—only a warm, rising shimmer of heat that carries the sweet, heavy scent of beeswax and dry pine. 

Everything here changes at a patient, glacial crawl. The wax towers grow by mere millimeters each year as the velvet-clothed builders deposit their secretions. The sharp gypsum cliffs do not crumble; they wait, pristine and jagged, under the clear sky. In this warm, high-altitude sanctuary, life exists in a crowded, silent state of suspension—a busy, complex civilization built of grease and fiber, ticking over in perfect, quiet slow-motion.
