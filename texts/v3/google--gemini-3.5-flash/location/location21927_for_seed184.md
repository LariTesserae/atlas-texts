Here, the water is not an ocean, nor is it a lake; it is a heavy, warm broth, thick with dissolved copper, tannins, and the slow-release grease of old machinery. 

It filling a cavernous, sunken district—perhaps an ancient pumping station or a deeply buried distillery—shoved deep beneath the bedrock. The space is small, roughly the size of a three-story town square, but it is so densely packed with infrastructure and growth that there is no sense of open volume. There are no vistas here. Instead, there is an intricate, suffocating maze of cast-iron balustrades, rotting brick flues, and massive brass boilers that have settled into the silt at slightly tilted angles.

The temperature is that of a stagnant hot spring, hovering at a constant, sleepy warmth. This heat does not rise from volcanic vents, but radiates from the slow, insulated ticking of ancient geothermal pipework buried in the floor. One or two of these pipes have ruptured, discharging rhythmic, pencil-thin streams of scalding mineral water that rise in shimmering, coiled threads through the colder, greener waters above.

Everything has been worn smooth. The sharp teeth of the great gears are now rounded and bulbous, looking more like rows of knuckles than mechanisms of iron. A century of wet salt and soft, acid currents has dissolved the corners of the brick stairs, transforming them into a series of melting wax tiers. The metal surfaces are coated in a layer of lime-scab and rust-slime that is cool and soapy to the touch.

The vegetation is relentless, occupying every cubic inch that the iron has left vacant. It is not marine kelp, but something more terrestrial that has adapted to drowning. Thick, ribbon-like ferns with leaves the color of bruised plums choke the narrow gangways. They do not drift; they hang heavily, suspended by the density of the water. Creepers with bloated, bladder-like stems coil around copper steam conduits, their rootlets boring into the lime scale to drink the warm metal ions. Around the edges of the submerged copper dials, tiny, hair-like mosses grow in perfect, emerald rings, feeding on the weak, galvanic currents that still leak from the dead wiring.

There is very little light. What exists is a dim, greenish-bronze twilight. It filters down from somewhere high above through thick pane glass that has turned opaque with algae, or it leaks from the plants themselves. Some of the larger bladder-ferns glow with a faint, cold, phosphorus bioluminescence, illuminating the ribbing of the iron ceiling like the belly of a leviathan.

Occasionally, the silence is broken, though sound behaves differently here. It does not travel through air; it is felt in the marrow as a low-frequency hum. Every few minutes, a deep, metal-on-metal *thrum* vibrates through the water—the sound of an ancient, heat-driven siphon piston shifting its stroke somewhere behind the brickwork. It is a slow, patient sound, followed by the soft, liquid *gargle* of a giant air pocket being displaced through a flooded pipe. After that, the quiet returns: the sound of water sliding past water, and the faint, dry click of blind, white-shelled snails crawling over the glass of searchlights that went dark before the waters came.

Fauna is nearly absent, as the warm, oxygen-depleted soup supports very little animal life. But if you watch a single rusted valve wheel long enough, you might see a pale, translucent shrimp, its legs like fine glass needles, pick its way through the moss, or a small, legless salamander, completely blind and slate-gray, drift like a piece of sodden birch bark from one pipe elbow to the next.

Nothing rises. Nothing falls. The sediment does not settle because the water is too thick, staying suspended in a permanent, golden-green fog. The world here operates on a clock that has lost its hands, leaving only the slow, heavy swinging of the pendulum to mark the centuries of rust.
