The basin of the Low-Reach floor is a place where time seems to have started twice: once when the black, razor-sharp obsidian bedrock was driven upward by some sudden interior spasm of the earth, and once when the city-builders laid down their silent grid across it. 

It is warm here, a heavy, greenhouse warmth that carries no wind but holds a constant, clinging moisture. Above, the sky is always the color of a bruised plum. No sun is visible, only a diffused, violet-grey luminescence that filters down through a thin shroud of high-altitude dust. The light is so dim that shadow is nearly non-existent; instead, objects simply fade into dark profiles against the pale ground, or glow with their own internal, cold bioluminescence.

On this young, unweathered floor of basalt and volcanic glass—where the steps of the terraces are still as sharp as freshly knapped flint—stands the city. It is not an urban tangle of alleys and tenements, but a regional architecture of immense, sparse geometry. Colossal towers, shaped like three-sided needles or hollow rings, stand miles apart from one another. They are composed of a dark, seamless material that feels neither like metal nor stone, but a pressurized state of matter that absorbs light. Between these towers run broad, perfectly flat plazas of grey silicate. There are no streets, no stairs, and no vehicles. Instead, the air itself seems to assist movement; to step off a ledge is to be caught by a soft, invisible pressure-gradient that carries you gently down to the lower tiers.

The ground is damp, but it does not rain in the traditional sense. The high, advanced architecture of the towers acts as a massive, passive atmospheric engine. They project invisible thermal fields into the low sky, cooling the warm air just enough to force condensation. Water does not fall as drops; it forms in heavy, silver spheres the size of fists that drift lazily through the air between the structures, caught in the micro-gravitational wells of the buildings. Sometimes they collide, merging into larger, floating globs before drift-settling into the cups of giant plants or slipping down the sleek walls of the obelisks in thick, slow-moving ropes of liquid.

The plants have adapted to this floating water with a terrifying, lush enthusiasm. Because the soil is nothing but un-weathered volcanic dust and crushed glass, the vegetation does not root deeply. Instead, it crawls. Massive, muscular vines with leaves the size of skiffs wrap themselves around the bases of the black towers. These leaves are thick and indigo-colored, covered in a fine velvet that catches the drifting water spheres. Below them, carpets of phosphorus-green moss cushion the sharp edges of the raw stone. The plants are so thick in the plazas between towers that they resemble a green-and-purple sea, swallowing the lower thirty feet of the structures in a silent, creeping tide of sap and fiber.

The landscape is mostly quiet. The only sounds are the deep, sub-audible purr of the towering structures—a frequency felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard in the ears—and the occasional, wet *splat* of a floating water sphere bursting against a broad leaf. 

Fauna is exceedingly rare, and what exists here seems to be either artificial or severely modified by the same science that built the towers. Occasionally, one might spot a *Loom-Strider*: a creature with six legs as thin as carbon-fibers, standing twelve feet high, carrying a soft, translucent body that glows with a faint, pulsing yellow light. It moves with a strange, jerky grace across the moss, its needle-like feet avoiding the sharp obsidian edges with perfect precision. It does not eat the plants; instead, it extends a long, gossamer proboscis into the floating water spheres, drinking the condensates before they touch the ground.

Everything here operates on a deliberate, heavy scale. The city-grid is active, but its processes are slow. A tower will slowly pitch five degrees to the west over the course of three days to adjust the condensation fields; a mile-wide plaza of glass will gently hum and glow with a blue light as it self-heals a fissure caused by the settling of the young earth beneath it. 

It is a world of immense, quiet luxury, where the distinction between organic life and high technology has been dissolved. The plants live on the sweat of the machines; the machines are anchored in the raw, jagged cradle of a newborn valley; and the few living things that wander between them are like ghosts in a vast, dark garden that requires neither sun nor rain to flourish.
