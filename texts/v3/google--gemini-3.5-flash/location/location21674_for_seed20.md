The atmosphere does not end where the liquid begins; instead, the sky itself has liquefied. 

At this high, thin altitude, where the air should be freezing and gasping-scarce, there sits a localized ocean. It is an impossible cistern, a suspended sphere of warm, heavy water roughly the size of a cathedral close, held in place by some localized failure of gravity’s architecture. This is the Well. It does not fall down the mountain slopes. It clings to them, wrapping several needle-sharp volcanic peaks in a thick, churning shroud of dark, translucent teal. 

To enter this space is to slide from thin, cold mountain wind directly into a dense, bath-warm medium that immediately fills the ears with a crushing, absolute roar. 

The sound is a physical entity. It is a 3.0-scale deafening screech, a deep-bore sub-bass hum mixed with the violent, wet tearing of currents shearing against each other. The water here is under immense, inexplicable pressure; it does not merely flow, it pulses and shrieks. When the currents collide, they produce a sound like iron plates grinding together under a hydraulic press. Within this pocket of liquid, the noise is so immense that it becomes a texture, vibrating through bone and marrow until teeth ache and the lungs feel as if they are humming in sympathy with the stone below.

Below, the terrain is young, black, and viciously sharp. Five obsidian spires—the Teeth—reach upward into the warm liquid. They have not been smoothed by their aquatic prison; they are freshly erupted, their edges still razor-like, showing the clean, crystalline fractures of basalt that has known no slow centuries of weather. They are hot to the touch, venting pale, sulfurous shimmers of heat that warp the light even further. 

The light is a dim, bruised twilight. It filters down from the outer membrane of the sphere, where the sun tries to penetrate but is scattered into a dull, shifting olive-gray. Everything is silhouetted. The space between the five basalt spires is mostly empty—a drowned hollow of dark, warm suspension.

In this dark, screaming space, there are marks of intention. On the saddle between the three tallest spires, there lie the remains of a small, crude amphitheater or ring. It is built from heavy blocks of the same black basalt, piled without mortar. They are not old enough to have gathered silt, but they are already shifting, vibrating in the furious currents. A few of the blocks have been torn loose by the water and now float—not sinking, but drifting in slow, heavy orbits three feet above the floor, weightless and stupid in the broken gravity. There are no carvings, no socket-holes for wood or metal, no signs of tools. The stones are roughly cleaved, perhaps by hand, perhaps by some forgotten biological force that shaped them before the water arrived.

Reality here is frayed. The liquid does not obey the laws of a terrestrial sea. Because of the high turbulence and the broken physics of the place, pockets of dry, pressurized air—spherical voids—drift through the water like transparent balloons. If you pass your hand through one, it is instantly dry, then instantly wet again. Some of these air-voids are silent; if your head passes into one, the deafening roar of the water vanishes instantly, replaced by a vacuum-like silence so sudden it makes the ears bleed, only for the roar to crash back with a concussive, thudding strike as the pocket drifts away. 

Elsewhere, the water behaves as a non-Newtonian fluid under the extreme, localized vibrations: if struck quickly by a drifting stone, it hardens into a glass-like solid for a split second, splintering into wet shards before melting instantly back into the swirling, warm churn.

Life is nearly absent, unable or unwilling to endure the violent kinetics of the Well. But in the deep cracks of the basalt spires, where the current is slightly masked, there are pale, fleshy things. They are not plants, but sparse, low-saturation organisms—something like blind, translucent sea anemones that have no tentacles, only flat, sucking discs that cling desperately to the glass-sharp stone. They do not drift; they hold fast, their pale bodies twitching in time with the sub-bass vibrations of the water. Once in a long while, a ribbon-like creature, three feet long and completely without eyes or mouth, will be whipped out of the dark void by a sudden cross-current, flailing uselessly in the warm gloom before being thrown against the obsidian peaks and torn to shreds by the young, sharp stone.

Above, at the upper boundary of the sphere, the water meets the thin mountain sky in a violent, steaming froth. From the outside, the Well must look like a boiling, dark jewel impaled on the peaks of a mountain range. From the inside, it is an endless, roaring twilight, where the earth is still sharp and new, and the water has forgotten how to fall.
