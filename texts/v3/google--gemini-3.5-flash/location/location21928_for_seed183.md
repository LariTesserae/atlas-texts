The sun here is an unblinking, naked core. At this extreme altitude, the atmosphere offers no violet buffer; the light is brilliant, hard, and clean, casting sharp-edged shadows that drop down into vertical abysses of unknown depth. 

You are standing within a crowded labyrinth of basaltic spires. They do not rise as isolated peaks, but crowd together like the stalks of an immense, petrified marshland, so dense that in many places you could reach out your hands and touch two different stone towers at once. They are impossibly tall, stretching hundreds of feet upward toward the blinding sky, and plunging just as far downward into a cold, blue-shadowed floor that never receives direct light. The scale is regional; this thicket of stone needles extends as far as the eye can see, a jagged, ribbed plateau carved by eons of wind and a very peculiar hydrology.

There is no green here. The world is built entirely of mineral, water, and light. There are no trees to soften the edges, no mosses to cushion the stone, and no animals to break the silence. Instead, the spires are clad in a skin of non-living, chemical complexity. Massive crusts of white tufa, sulfurous yellow plates, and sparkling sheets of silica drape the dark basalt like frozen waterfalls. In the cool, crisp air, these mineral formations are perpetually wet.

This is a weeping landscape, but the water behaves according to laws that have drifted far from the ordinary.

Because the air is cool and the spires are cold, the moisture in the atmosphere condenses constantly. But rather than running down the vertical faces of the basalt, the water adheres to them in thick, swollen sheets. Gravity here is not a simple, downward command. It is lazy, distracted, or perhaps warped by the dense, crystalline geometry of the mountain. Water climbs. It flows in slow, pulsing ribbons upward toward the sun-drenched tips of the spires, guided by the fine grain of the silicates. 

In the narrow, three-foot gaps between the towers, the air is thick with hovering moisture. Great, perfect globes of water—the size of plums or human heads—drift slowly horizontally, suspended in mid-air. They catch the brilliant, white sunlight, refracting it into tiny, searing rainbows that dance across the dark rock faces. When a gust of wind blows through the spires, these floating reservoirs do not burst; they wobble, stretch like tallow, and occasionally merge with one another or splat with a heavy *clack* against the stone, immediately soaking into the porous tufa.

The sound is constant and loud. The wind does not merely blow; it is shredded. The spires are naturally hollowed by long-established erosion, riddled with vertical vents and horizontal blowholes. As the high-altitude air rushes through this stone comb, the entire landscape sings. It is a vast, air-driven pipe organ, producing a deep, trembling bass note that vibrates in the marrow of your bones, overlaid with the high, glassy whistling of the smaller rock splinters. Underneath this musical roar is the rhythmic, wet percussion of the water: the *drip-clack* of floating spheres colliding, the gurgle of the upward-bound streams, and the dry, metallic hiss of moisture evaporating instantly when it reaches the hot, sunlit tips of the needles.

There are signs of an old, simple intelligence having negotiated this vertical maze. 

Occasionally, you will find a bridge. These are not grand spans, but single, weathered logs or thick slabs of split slate wedged between two adjacent spires, grayed by centuries of moisture and light. They are secured with primitive lashings made of braided mineral glass—fine, metallic-looking threads that have been painstakingly spun from the volcanic residues. 

Carved directly into the wet basalt are small, beehive-shaped niches. Inside them, the rock is dry, shielded by overhanging lips of stone. These hollows contain the possessions of whoever once climbed here: heavy wedges of dark green jadeite, crude copper scrapers corroded to a bright, chalky turquoise, and shallow stone basins designed to catch and hold the floating water spheres. There are no skeletons, no carvings of faces, and no written language—only the repeated, manual scars of flint chisels on the hard basalt, and the grease-smudges of ancient, fat-burning lamps on the low ceilings of the niches.

As you look closer at the stone, you realize the dynamic processes of this place are not dead, but merely geological. The silica crusts are growing. They feed on the minerals carried by the upward-climbing waters. Where the water reaches the bright tips of the spires, it leaves behind microscopic layers of opal. Over centuries, these tips swell, bloom, and eventually touch their neighbors, joining the spires at their summits into a continuous, translucent ceiling that filters the blinding sun into a cool, milky glare.

It is a place of permanent transit. The water rises to the light, evaporates into the thin air, condenses in the dark shadows of the spires, hovers in the gaps, and climbs again. The stone grows, rings with the wind, and slowly, beautifully, dissolves itself.
