This is the great, wet roof of a world. 

Here, at an altitude where the air should be thin and dry, the atmosphere is instead a heavy, cold sponge. A vast plateau—hundreds of miles of basalt terraces and deep, U-shaped troughs—stretches out under a pale, silver-gray sky. The air is cool enough to keep your breath suspended in brief, white plumes, yet it is so saturated with moisture that every surface is slick, weeping, and alive with the slow, cold business of water.

There is no bare stone here. Despite the elevation, the landscape is packed to the limits of its volume with a dense, matted fleece of primitive vegetation. Deep, water-logged carpets of star-moss and rubbery liverworts cover the basalt in layers three feet thick. Walking upon them is like walking on a saturated mattress; each step sinks deep, leaving a dark footprint that slowly fills with tea-colored water before swelling back into shape. From the lips of every stone ledge hang curtains of blackish-green horsehair lichen, dripping water from their tips in a slow, rhythmic metronome.

Though it is entirely wild and untouched by any hand—devoid of paths, structures, or any sign of intelligent design—the landscape operates on a scale and with a logic that feels strangely industrial. The geology itself behaves like a massive, open-air refinery. 

Because of the unique mineral composition of these high plateaus, the mountain water is heavily charged with iron and natural paraffin-like lipids from ancient underground shales. It does not look like ordinary water; it flows with a heavy, lubricating viscosity through channels that have been worn perfectly smooth over millions of years. These natural stone flumes, dark as cast iron and slick with greasy mineral deposits, cut straight grids through the high peat-moors. Rainwater and snowmelt collect in deep, circular basalt basins that resemble gargantuan settling vats, where the cold causes the heavy oils to separate and rise, forming iridescent swirls of copper, magenta, and zinc-blue on the surface before spilling over into the next level of the system.

High-pressure geothermal steam rises from deep fissures in the plateau floor. But because the land is so packed with wet moss and dense peat, these vents do not erupt in messy geysers. Instead, the steam is forced through narrow, natural conduits of calcified mineral tubes—hollow chimneys built up by algae over eons. These chimneys rise from the moss like black iron pipes, venting high-pressure steam in slow, rhythmic exhalations. *Hiss… hiss…* The sound is muffled by the endless soft carpets of bryophytes, reduced to a dry, mechanical sigh that repeats across thirty leagues of plateau, sounding like the distant, synchronized breathing of a sleeping factory.

The scale of this place is overwhelming. The valleys are so wide that they contain their own weather systems; a storm can form, dump a curtain of cold rain over a twenty-mile span of the eastern terraces, and dissipate, while fifteen miles to the west, the light remains a steady, soft, pearlescent glow. Far off, the horizon does not end in mountains, but in the blue, hazy drop-offs of the plateau rim, where the cold tableland ends and the clouds of the lower atmosphere lie spread out like an endless ocean of white wool.

In the damp quiet, sounds are deadened. The thick moss behaves like acoustic foam; a shout would carry only a dozens yards before being swallowed by the cold wetness. What remains are low-frequency sounds that travel through the stone itself: the deep, subterranean thrum of the water-wheels of the earth, the heavy, muffled sloshing of the iron-rich streams sliding through their basalt grooves, and the occasional soft, wet *clack* of animal life.

And there is abundant life here, adapted to this cold, dense, mechanical ecology. 

In the slow-moving, oily channels, large, flat-bodied creatures with hides like wet boot-leather glide through the mineral currents. They have no eyes, but rather rows of lateral sensors that detect the vibrations of the steam-vents. They graze on the heavy iron-crusts that form on the stream beds, their mouthparts scraping the stone with a rhythmic, metallic *tick-tick-tick*. 

Across the peat-moors, herds of massive, slow-moving herbivores—vaguely resembling short-legged, long-necked seals but covered in dense, waterproof down—move through the mist. They are the color of wet slate. They travel in tight formations, their broad, flat feet distributing their weight so they do not sink into the deep moss. As they graze on the rubbery, water-filled leaf-cups, they move with a heavy, silent purpose, their dark eyes reflecting the silver sky. Overhead, flocks of small, stubby-winged birds with plumage like oiled silk dart through the steam-plumes. They do not sing; instead, they communicate with short, low-pitched clicks that sound like the cooling of hot metal.

Everything here is in balance, settled into its form. The erosion is ancient and complete; the sharp edges of the volcanic birth of these mountains were worn away long ago, leaving only these rounded, heavy foundations that seem as though they have always existed in this state of wet, cool equilibrium. It is a world that runs on a slow, cold clock—wet, green, heavy with metal, and endlessly self-sustaining.
