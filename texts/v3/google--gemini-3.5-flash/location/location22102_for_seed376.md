This is a highland world that does not know stone. 

At this altitude, three miles above some forgotten sea-level, the crisp, thin air is cold enough to keep a constant skin of frost on the high ridges. Yet there is no granite here, no basalt, no shale. The mountains are wood. 

The entire landscape—the towering cliffs, the deep-cleated valleys, the step-terraced plateaus that stretch toward a pale, bright horizon—is composed of a single, ancient, multi-layered botanical entity. Underfoot, the substrate is a dense, iron-hard peat of compressed cambium and petrified root system, weathered by centuries of dry glacial winds into smooth, gray, wood-grain canyons. It is an established, deeply eroded geologic form made entirely of plant matter that has long since settled into its own quiet equilibrium.

The air is bright and normal, filled with the flat, white light of a high-altitude sun that offers no heat. Condensation is the primary moisture here; it gathers silently in the cup-like hollows of giant, blue-black leaves, or runs in icy, viscous threads down the vertical fluting of the wood-walls. There are no rivers, only localized, shivering pools of cold, nutrient-rich sap that collect in the basins of dead wooden basins, preserved by the chill.

But if the foundation of this valley is ancient and frozen, its surface is a frantic, silent engine of industry.

The vegetation has evolved to mimic the mechanics of a heavy factory, operating with a frantic, turbulent speed. Across the miles-wide landscape, giant, ribbed stalks—as thick as cooling towers and colored like weathered bronze—grow from the basin floor in a matter of hours. They do not grow through normal cell division, but through a high-pressure hydraulic assembly. Deep within the petrified floor, ogromous root-vacuoles pump pressurized, starch-rich fluids upward through thick, fibrous conduits that snake across the land like insulated power lines. 

These conduits hum with a faint, bio-electric charge. If you touch them, they do not feel like wood; they feel like cold, vibrating rubber, tense with immense internal turgor pressure. They link the massive towers to one another, forming an interlocking grid of biological processing. Here, a three-story bulbous node swells with fermenting chemical heat, hiss-less and self-contained, synthesizing complex waxes. There, a row of bellows-like leaves expand and contract on a five-second cycle, drawing the thin mountain air down into underground root-furnaces, though no smoke ever rises—only a faint, sweet smell of vinegar and cold ether.

This hyperactive creation and destruction is constant, chaotic, and entirely unpredictable. A forest of thin, needle-like spires will shoot up toward the sun, remain rigid for forty minutes, and then suddenly lose pressure, buckling and dissolving into a pool of gray fluid that is instantly sucked back down into the basin floor by thirsty, capillary mosses. A massive dome of orange, cork-like material will bubble out of a ravine, grow to the size of a cathedral, and then be systematically dismantled in afternoon light by a creeping, carpet-like vine that strips its cellulose to build something else. It is a frantic, looping, biological assembly line that is forever tearing down its own products to rebuild them in different configurations.

Yet, despite this violent, industrial-scale churning, the landscape is wrapped in an absolute, suffocating silence. 

The air is too thin, and the materials too soft, to carry sound. The ground is felted with miles of thick, dry, acoustic-dampening moss that swallows every vibration. When a five-hundred-foot balsa tower loses pressure and collapses into a valley, it does so like a ghost—breaking apart without a crack, its soft, air-filled fibers settling into the moss with a muffled, microscopic sigh that is lost five feet away. The mechanical thrashing of the hydraulic vines, the chemical bubbling of the fermentation nodes, the tearing of wood-flesh—all of it occurs in a hushed, dreamlike vacuum. The only indicator of the violence is the heavy vibration through the soles of your feet: a low-frequency, rhythmic shudder, like standing on the roof of a subterranean machine-shop.

Life other than the great plant-organism is exceedingly rare. Once or twice a day, one might see a *strutter*—a small, four-legged creature with a body made of dry, hollow reed-pipes and joints of greasy resin. They are sluggish, cold-blooded things, devoid of eyes or voice, that cling to the warm, bio-electric conduits to harvest the small sparks of static electricity that crackle off the rubbery bark. They move with jerky, clockwork steps, resembling forgotten wooden toys winded down by the cold, before freezing in place for hours to conserve their heat.

There are no paths here, and no signs of anything having ever been built by design. There is only the organic machine, churning its way through a cold, high-altitude eternity, building and unbuilding itself under a clear, uncaring sky.
