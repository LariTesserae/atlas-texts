This is a world that has finished its work. 

The mountains have long since been ground down to their roots. What remains is a vast, low-lying shield of dark, iron-red granite, polished by hundreds of millions of years of rain into smooth, curving domes that resemble the backs of immense, sleeping beasts. There are no cliffs, no jagged ridges, and no loose rubble; the fragments of the old highlands have been dissolved, swept away, or melted into basins of heavy, grey-brown silt. The land is nearly flat, sloping so gently toward an unseen ocean that the water does not flow so much as it sits, thinking.

The air is warm and heavy, saturated with a permanent, tropical humidity that feels like a weight against the skin. It does not often storm; instead, the sky exists in a state of warm, bright condensation. A perpetual, luminous mist hangs twenty feet above the ground, diffusing the sunlight into a flat, shadowless glare that coats every wet surface in a greasy, silver sheen. Under this white sky, the landscape feels boundless, extending out in every direction in repeating curves of red stone and silver water.

Water is the dominant medium. It fills every hollow in the granite shields, forming shallow, glassy pools that stretch for miles. Between these pools, the water seeps through wide, flat channels of fine silt, moving at a speed that is barely perceptible to the eye. Where the rock rises slightly above the water, it is constantly slick, weeping with a thin film of condensation that trickles down the slopes in silent, braided threads. 

Vegetation is rare and lonely here. There is no true soil—only the bare, nutrient-stripped granite and the sour, anaerobic sediment of the pools. Because of this, plants have given up on height and thickness. The high-domed rocks are mostly bare, painted only with tough, charcoal-colored lichens and thin, rubbery skins of orange liverwort that feel like wet leather to the touch. In the shallows, individual reed-like organisms stand direct and stiff, like rusted iron rods driven into the silt. They do not grow in dense, rustling beds, but rather as solitary sentinels, spaced fifty yards apart, motionless in the windless, warm air. They have no leaves, only segmented stalks that taper to sharp, black needles.

And yet, despite the barrenness of the flora, the basin is intensely, vibrantly alive. 

Because the landscape is flat and the vegetation is too sparse to muffle acoustic waves, the basin is an immense, echoing sounding board. The silence is entirely filled by animal life. It is a wet, percussive, and highly vocal ecology. From the shallow pools comes the ceaseless, rhythmic *chink-chink-chink* of hard-shelled mud-creatures, a sound like small stones being struck together underwater. Large, low-slung amphibians—broad-headed, with slick, pale skin—lie in the warm shallows, their throat-sacs expanding like dark bladder-balloons to release deep, resonating bellows that roll across the granite flats like distant thunder. 

The air itself vibrates with the sound of jointed, insectoid life. High above the water, finger-long creatures with gossamer wings hover in place, their flight producing a dry, mechanical rattle. Every surface of the red stones seems to crawl with tiny, armored grazers that scrape the orange liverworts with microscopic, clicking jaws. The soundscape is not chaotic; it is a steady, ancient orchestra that has played the same movement for ten thousand centuries, a deafening but predictable drone of breathing, clicking, and drumming.

There is no sign that this place has ever known a hand or a tool. There are no remnants of construction, no worked stone, not even the simplest burrow that suggests a desire to escape the wet warmth. The creatures here are built for the wetness and the heat; they do not need to alter their environment because the environment has remained in this perfect, stale equilibrium for eras.

In this landscape, change does not occur in hours or days. The sun rises and sets behind the white mist, turning the sky from silver-grey to dull copper, but the temperature never drops, and the humidity never lifts. No sudden floods scour the plains, and no dry spells parch the pools. If a granite dome loses a single millimeter of its height to the gentle, warm rain, it is an event that takes ten generations of the slow-moving amphibians to complete. It is a finished world, warm and loud, drowning gently in its own ancient sweat.
