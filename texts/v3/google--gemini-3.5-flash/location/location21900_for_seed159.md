There is no ceiling here, only a jagged rip in the world’s crust miles above, through which a flat, stark daylight pours down. This light does not diffuse softly; it bounces violently off millions of facets of unweathered salt and wet silica. The air is bright—as bright as an salt pan at noon—but heavy, warm, and thick with a pressurized, sulfurous dampness.

This is the Rift of the First Cleaving. It is a regional trench, wide as a valley and long enough to have its own weather, located miles beneath the surface. It is a world that has only recently ceased falling. The floor is not dirt, but a chaotic jumble of razor-sharp, blocky talus and newly precipitated gypsum needles. Everything is hard-edged. There are no rounded pebbles or smooth river stones; the geology here is embryonic, sharp as broken glass, and still adjusting to its own weight. 

Water does not flow in rivers here. Instead, it exists as a frantic cycle of condensation and steam. The floor is hot to the touch—warmed by deep volcanic heartbeats—and when the occasional, violent rains fall from the misty heights of the chasm, the water hits the sharp rocks, sizzles, and rises immediately back into the air as scalding fog. Everywhere, there is a wetness that does not pool but clings. Everything is slippery with a hot, greasy dew that smells of copper and yeast.

There is not a single green leaf in the Rift. In place of vegetation, the landscape is choked with non-vascular life that grows with terrifying speed. Vast, rubbery sheets of orange and black slime-molds drape over the jagged stone ridges like melted wax, throbbing slightly as they digest the mineral salts. Out of the cracks rise towers of porous, calcium-rich fungal stalks. These stalks do not last; they grow three feet in an hour, are hollowed out by the ravenous fauna, collapse under their own weight, and are dissolved back into the slime-sheets by morning. The ground is a patchwork of these fleshy, self-digesting crusts.

And the fauna is an absolute, deafening swarm.

To walk here—or to scramble across the razor-edges—is to be constantly brushing against, stepping on, and breathing in life. The air is solid with clouds of translucent, blind midges that do not bite but are so thick they clog the nostrils. They move in undulating, weather-like fronts, driven by the hot wind. Below them, the rock is alive. Millions of white, multi-jointed isopods, some as small as fingernails, others the size of hunting hounds, scrape the salt-crusts with a sound like iron files on stone. Wall-clingers—flat, lungless things with leathery hides the color of wet slate—leap across the chasms, their sticky pads making loud *thwip-thwip* noises as they land. 

The sound of the Rift is a continuous, overwhelming roar: the hiss of steam vents, the wet clicking of billions of chitinous legs, the dull, heavy thud of shifting slabs of rock, and the high-pitched whistling of wind forced through narrow stone needles. It is a place of ceaseless, chaotic motion. Nothing stays still. A mound of gypsum crystals that begins to form in the morning is overrun by mineral-eating larvae by noon, and by evening it has been tipped into a boiling mud-pot by the weight of a migrating swarm of blind crawlers.

Human footprint here is rare, desperate, and fragile. High on the vertical slabs of the chasm walls, one can see the scattered remnants of paths. These are not roads, but crude, terrifying structures: log-like ladders made of petrified fungal trunks lashed together with braided animal hair, pinned into the cracks with wedges of raw obsidian. 

In a few wider, dry niches where the steam does not reach, there are small, roofless circular shelters built of stacked, unmortared slate. They are stained black by oil-smoke. Beside them lie the tools of those who briefy survived here: scrapers made of split bone, heavy stone hammers with crude finger-grooves pecked into the granite, and shallow clay bowls used to catch the cleaner condensation dripping from the salt-shelves. These dwellings are empty now, or perhaps their occupants are simply hiding within the shifting clouds of white midges, waiting for the current geological spasm to pass.

The landscape is a machine in the middle of its first run, shaking itself apart even as it builds itself. A slab of slate the size of a villa peels off the high wall, crashing down into a basin of boiling mud; within minutes, the splash-zone is covered in a fresh layer of orange mold, and the isopods are already crawling over the hot, new edges, scraping at the exposed veins of sulfur. It is a place that does not know how to be still, where the line between the stone and the beast is as thin and sharp as the rocks themselves.
