The basin stretches to the horizon under a wash of brilliant, unblinking light. It is a lowland rift, several hundred miles wide, cradled by mountains that are not rounded hills but stacks of raw, black obsidian, razor-edged slab-stone, and blocks of basalt so fresh they look as if they were quarried yesterday. There is no sand here, only a fine, dry ash that rests in the crevices of the stone like spilled flour. No wind moves it. No rain has ever fallen to dull the sharp corners of the rock or wash the dust into streams. The geology is young, violent, and instantly arrested.

Over this jagged, dry foundation lies an astonishing, suffocating abundance of growth. 

It is a jungle, though it contains not a single drop of water. The vegetation does not search for moisture; its roots—fine, copper-colored threads—wrap directly around the raw edges of the mineral plates, drawing sustenance from the weak piezoelectric currents of the shifting crust and the heavy, light-saturated atmosphere. The plants are solid-state, constructed of dense silicate fibers, flexible carbon lattices, and dry, lustrous wax-membranes. There are massive, plate-like ferns that gleam with a metallic, blue-green iridescence; there are thick, towering pillars of dry, orange sponge-wood that do not grow but seem to have materialized in their current, massive state. Vines as thick as a torso drape from rock-shelf to rock-shelf, their surfaces covered in a velvet pile of purple microfiber that registers warm to the touch.

This forest is packed so tightly that from an elevated rock, the basin looks like a boiling, multi-colored seam of foam. The plants press against one another in a silent, static struggle for space, yet none of them are bruised or decaying. There is no rot here, no autumn, no yellowing leaves. Everything exists in a state of high-fidelity, permanent maturity. 

The warmth of the place is dry and ambient, like the heat rising from a processor or a stone baking in an oven. The air is perfectly clear, smelling faintly of warm insulation, paraffin, and the sharp, clean scent of crushed quartz. 

There is an underlying impossibility to the way the landscape holds itself together—physics here has been edited. In the gaps between the dense thickets of glass-leaved shrubs, the light behaves strangely. It does not travel in strict, straight lines; in certain glades, it curves softly, pooling around the trunks of the orange sponge-wood trees in warm, luminous halos, leaving the ground beneath them in sharp, shadowless clarity. 

In some places, gravity seems to hold no sway over small things. Clusters of dry, silver seed-pods drift six inches above the ground, perfectly stationary, aligned in neat, geometric rows as if resting on a shelf of glass. Individual leaves, shed perhaps during some ancient, singular disturbance, remain suspended in mid-air, tilted at angles that defy the earth’s pull. If one were to push them, they would slide through the air with a faint, oily resistance, then stop instantly when the hand was removed, locked back into their coordinates.

This is the work of an invisible, pervasive technology—not built in the sense of walls or towers, but woven into the quantum fabric of the region. The valley is a stabilized field. It is an engineered equilibrium of cosmic scale, where the decay of matter has been suspended or bypassed entirely. The plants do not grow because they are already complete; they do not die because time, in the thermodynamic sense, has been decoupled from this coordinate. 

Sound does not travel well. The forest is nearly muted, as if the air itself were baffled. The leaves do not rustle—they are rigid, and there is no breeze to stir them. If a traveler were to strike one of the glass-ferns, it would not snap; it would emit a brief, high-frequency chime that dies away instantly, swallowed by the dense, dry velvet of the surrounding brush. Sometimes, a low, subterranean vibration hums through the soles of the feet—a deep, resonant sound like a massive turbine spinning thousands of miles beneath the basalt. 

Fauna is almost non-existent. Once in many miles, one might spot a solitary entity: a creature like a dry, multifaceted beetle the size of a shield, made of polished pewter and amber. It does not crawl. It sits on the edge of a black obsidian terrace, its long, needle-fine antennae pointing toward the sky, completely motionless, registering the static charge of the atmosphere. It will remain there for centuries, a silent witness to a landscape that has forgotten how to change.
