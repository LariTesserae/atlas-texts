In this high, heavy place, there is no stone. 

The ground is not soil but *The Weft*: a green, breathing mattress of tangled root-fibers, massive liverworts, and peat-mosses that has grown upon itself for ten million years. It is a continent-scale tableland lifted so high into the upper atmosphere that the lower world is nothing more than a blue, dry smudge seen through rare, vertical rifts in the cloud-shelf. Yet up here, it is balmy and sighing. The air is thick, saturated with a warm, sugary grease of vaporized sap and water that never quite falls as rain, but rather condenses directly onto every surface, running down the giant stalks in fat, silent tears.

Everything is round. Because this plateau has settled into its final, primordial age, there are no sharp peaks or jagged ravines. The centuries have ironed the landscape into a series of endless, low-rolling green domes—each dome the crown of a single, ancient colonial organism the size of a city. To walk means to sink ankle-deep into a warm, springy velvet that smells of yeast and cucumbers. With every step, a small well of amber water rises around the foot and then is slowly reabsorbed by the fibrous floor with a soft, sucking *hiss*.

The light here does not fall; it is suspended. The sun is a pale, bronze smudge that never rises high enough to pierce the perpetual canopy of spores and low-hanging moisture-clouds. It is a world of permanent afternoon twilight, where shadows do not exist and everything glows with a soft, internal emerald radiance. 

In the dimness, one can see the scattered remains of the before-times. They are not built of stone or wood, but are hollow, calcified skeletons of some extinct, mountain-sized siphonophore lineage that must have floated in the air before the Weft grew so dense. These structures look like ribbed, white towers, fifty cubits high, their marrow long ago eaten away by acid-mosses, leaving only calcified lacework. They sit in the green dips of the domes like empty, giant wicker baskets, half-sunken into the peat, their hollow chambers now serving as nurseries for slow-growing, luminous ferns that drip pale milk from their fronds.

There is no metal here, no wheel, no spark. The only tools are biological. Some of the larger, trumpet-shaped pitcher plants have hard, wooden rims that can be broken off and used to scrape the sweet grease from the moss-bark, but mostly, the landscape exists in a state of absolute, unbothered self-containment.

The fauna is rich but strangely quiet, moving through the dense foliage with the patient laziness of creatures that have no predators and no winters. 
There are the *Pith-Walkers*: slow, six-legged beasts whose hides are indistinguishable from the grey-green lichen they feed upon. They move at the speed of a clock’s shadow, their broad, flat feet spreading their weight so they do not break the delicate surface-crust of the Weft. They have no lungs; they breathe through large, pulsing spiracles along their flanks that make a gentle, wet *bellows-sigh* every few minutes. 
Above them, in the warm mist, drift the *Bladder-Sacks*—translucent, gas-filled membranes that look like floating blue melons. They have no mouths; they simply hang in the humid air, filtering the floating pollen and spores through long, sticky threads that trail behind them like gossamer roots.

Sound does not travel far. The world is a giant wool room. The infinite, porous mass of moss and damp fiber swallows noise instantly; if you hallooed, your voice would die within twenty paces, muffled by the green felt. The only sounds that survive are low-frequency hums—the deep, subsonic vibrations of the Weft’s massive root-systems shifting under the weight of the water-tides beneath, and the occasional, heavy *plop* of a condensation-drop falling from a high leaf into one of the thousands of black, deep cistern-pools that dot the valleys between the domes.

These pools are perfectly circular, their water so clear and still that they look like holes cut into the world. They are bottomless, feeding down into the dark, wet interior of the continent-cushion where the old, dead generations of plants have turned into a warm, pressurized broth.

Everything changes with agonizing slowness. A single leaf takes three summers to unfurl, and when it dies, it does not rot so much as it is slowly woven back into the living floor by the relentless, creeping thread-roots. There are no sudden storms, no seasons, only the long, warm, humid breath of a world that has reached its final form and has decided to stay there forever.
