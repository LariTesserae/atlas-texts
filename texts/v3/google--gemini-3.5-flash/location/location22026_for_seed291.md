The basin of Sinking-on-the-Rise lies beneath a cool, high-latitude sky where the daylight is flat, bright, and clean, like light passing through a sheared block of ice. 

From the lip of the surrounding rim-cliffs, the landscape looks less like a geographical valley and more like a vast, flat vat of dark, steaming soup, packed to its brim with iron, stone, and green grease. This is a low-lying sink, thirty miles wide, situated where the world’s crust is still soft, wet, and forming. There are no ancient hills here, no valleys smoothed by millions of years of wind. The stone down in the basin is embryonic—it is raw, black basalt and sharp glass-obsidian that has only just cooled, or is in the very process of extruding itself from the belly of the world. The edges of the rocks are like knives; they have not yet learned how to crumble.

Because the geology is so young and hyperactive, the valley floor behaves like a slow-boiling porridge. Streets do not stay flat for more than a sequence of weeks. Here, a cobblestone road will rise three yards overnight, its stones parting like teeth to show the raw, glistening gray cement of the earth’s newest crust pushing up from below. There, a block of lime-cement housing will tilt slowly backward as a new thermal rib swells beneath its foundations. The city does not fight this turbulence; it has built its entire industrial life around it.

 Sinking-on-the-Rise is packed shoulder-to-shoulder, a dense, multi-tiered urban density. Since there is no room to sprawl outward on the shifting stone, the town has built upward and inward. Huge, copper-clad factories ride on massive, jointed hydraulic legs that can be raised or lowered to compensate for the swelling of the earth. Overhead, a maze of black iron catwalks, heavy cable-trams, and suspended pipeways forms a ceiling so thick it mimics a pine forest canopy. Below them, the streets are narrow, light-sliced canyons where the buildings are squeezed together like books on a jammed shelf. 

This is an industrial world, but it is one that runs in almost total silence. 

The sound of Sinking-on-the-Rise is a muffled, low-frequency purr, easily mistaken for the pressure of one’s own blood in the ears. There is no iron-on-iron clatter. The heavy gears of the factories are carved from dense, self-healing lignum-block and run submerged in deep troughs of cold tallow and seal-fat. The steam pipes are wrapped in three-inch layers of boiled wool, and the great pistons that drive the city’s electrical turbines are cushioned with thick pads of hair-felt. 

This silence is aided by the greenery. Because the air is cold and the earth is warm from its own birth-heat, the valley is a machine of constant condensation. Water does not fall as rain here, but rather aggregates as a thick, cold dew that beads on every surface. It drips from the copper undersides of the walkways; it runs in slow, clear rivets down the flanks of the boiler towers. 

In this wet, chilly soup, vegetation has run riot. It is not a forest of tall trees, which could never survive the shifting ground, but an immense, dense carpet of lower flora. Blue-green peat mosses, four feet deep and springy as goose-down, cover every roof, every inactive iron boiler, every abandoned street wagon. Creeping liverworts and thick-stemmed cable-vines weave through the electrical grids, their fleshy leaves drinking the oily runoff of the factories. This vegetation acts as a massive acoustic sponge. It swallows the sound of the steam-whistles, turning what should be a deafening industrial hell into a quiet, dripping garden of gears.

The relationship between the machines and the ground is strangely intimate. The young stone of the basin has a high electromagnetic sensitivity. Where the heavy copper power-lines of the industrial grid run close to the earth, the raw, rising basalt responds to the current. It does not grow as random lumps, but crystallizes into perfect, faceted cubes and hexagonal columns that mimic the architecture above them. 

The builders of the city have learned to exploit this. If a factory needs a new foundation wall, they do not dig or pour concrete; instead, they run thick copper cables into the raw dirt, turn on the high-voltage generators, and "draw" the stone up from the ground. Within forty-eight hours, a series of dark, glassy pillars will have grown out of the mud, cold and ready to bear the weight of a three-story boiler. This process is unstable, however; if the current is not maintained at a specific harmonic frequency, the stone may become brittle, or begin to swell sideways, slowly crushing the very machines that summoned it.

The fauna of Sinking-on-the-Rise has adapted to this dense, wet, and quiet world. The air is full of pale, blind gutter-moths with wings like wet parchment, which flutter in silence around the blue-gas street lamps. Down in the narrow gaps between the buildings, where the steam pipes keep the moss warm, packs of grease-dogs roam. These are long, low-slung creatures with hairless, oil-repellent skin the color of wet lead. They do not bark; they communicate through low-frequency thrumming in their throats that vibrates through the iron pipes they nest upon.

The people of the basin move through their city like priests in a cathedral of steam. They are dressed in heavy, layered wools dyed dark plum and charcoal, their boots wrapped in oiled canvas to protect them from the sharp, newly minted stone of the streets. They speak in soft, low voices—to shout in Sinking-on-the-Rise is considered an act of violence, a disruption of the delicate, vibrating balance between the cold air, the hot boilers, and the restless, growing earth beneath their feet.

At any moment, the landscape is changing. A crane operator looking out his cabin window at noon might see a new spire of grey, salt-crusted limestone rising out of the middle of a major thoroughfare, pushing a pile of rusted iron scrap before it. By evening, the mosses will have already begun to crawl up its base, and a crew of silent workers will have clamped a copper wire to its crest, directing its growth into the archway of a new workshop. Nothing is permanent here, but nothing is lost; the city simply eats its own geology as it rises, rebuilding itself in silence, hour by hour.
