The air down here is less of a gas and more of an suspension—a thick, warm broth of sulfur, lime dust, and vaporized lubricating oil that clings to the skin like wet wool. We are deep beneath the surface, inside an immense, low-roofed tectonic basin that stretches for miles in every direction, but the scale feels suffocating because of how tightly packed the space is. There is no empty air. Every cubic meter is occupied by hanging copper conduits, rusted iron guide-wires, clusters of pale stalactites, or the thick, grey mist that perpetually rolls off the hot floor.

This is a landscape in its infancy, geologic time caught at the moment of fracture. The rock underfoot is raw, sharp-edged basalt and jagged shale, black and glinting with unweathered crystals of quartz and iron pyrites. It has not been rounded by the passage of water or smoothed by wind; it is a landscape of fresh wounds. Great fissures cross the floor, their edges as sharp as broken glass, though they weep constantly with a mineral-rich, tepid water that leaves a greasy, white crust of calcium where it trickles.

Water is the dominant force, yet it has not had the time to carve valleys. Instead, it pools in hundreds of shallow, interconnected basins, forming a vast, subterranean fen. The water is warm—near body temperature—and constantly agitated by the vibrations of the floor. It drips from the low ceiling in a heavy, endless rain that has no beginning and no end. Every surface is slick, covered in a thin, cold film of condensation that runs down the raw rock walls like sweat.

In this perpetual twilight, vegetation has found no real foothold. There are no leaves, no stalks, no green. Instead, the flora is reduced to the simplest, most primitive forms: thick, charcoal-colored carpets of slime-mold that coat the wet ironwork, and pale, thread-like fungi that hang from the undersides of the rock ledges like wet hair. These fungal webs catch the moisture from the air, beaded with thousands of tiny, glowing droplets of condensation that reflect the dim, amber light of the few scattered industrial lamps.

Those lamps belong to a sprawling, half-drowned network of machinery. Every few hundred yards, the raw geology is interrupted by massive, black-iron pump houses and steam-driven turbines. These are not polished, modern facilities; they are heavy, Victorian-style ironwork, bolted directly into the living rock with giant copper rivets. Great shafts of steel rise into the ceiling, turning with a tectonic slowness, driving pistons that must be the size of houses. Many of these machines are half-submerged in the rising silt, their lower gears grinding through grey mud with a sickening, wet crunch. Thick, black cables run from these stations like the roots of a monstrous iron forest, looping over the jagged stone ridges and disappearing into the steaming black pools.

The noise is a physical wall. It does not exist as separate sounds but as a single, deafening roar that fills the skull and vibrates the chest cavity. It is the bass thrum of the immense, water-logged piston engines, the high-pressure hiss of steam escaping from cracked bronze valves, and the relentless, roaring splatter of water falling into water. Underneath this mechanical din is the wet, clicking chorus of the cavern’s native life. 

And the life here is astonishingly dense. Despite the noise and the dark, the pools and iron structures are alive with movement. Millions of pale, blind cave-crabs, their shells the color of curdled milk, crawl over the rusted machinery in writhing heaps, scavenging the oily film that coats the metal. In the warm pools, thick-bodied, lungless salamanders—some nearly three feet long—drift through the silt, their pink gills fanning the heavy water. High above, on the underside of the wet stone arches, mats of thick-bodied cave-crickets cling to the rock, their long, hair-thin antennae constantly sensing the vibrations of the thumping engines. They do not fly; they simply crawl over one another in a silent, undulating carpet of chitin.

Nothing changes quickly here. The machines have been thumping, perhaps for centuries, slowly becoming part of the geology. Calcite is growing over the iron flywheels; rust is cementing the copper pipes to the basalt walls. The water rises and falls in cycles that span decades, slowly drowning a pump station here, revealing an ancient, sediment-covered iron boiler there. It is a world of heavy, warm stagnation, where the only movement is the frantic skittering of white crabs and the slow, inevitable encrustation of the machines by the very stone they were built to clear.
