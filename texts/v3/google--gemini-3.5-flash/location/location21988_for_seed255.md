The floor underfoot is not soil, nor is it living rock. It is a vast, interlocking plain of hex-tiles, hand-pressed from a coarse gray-red clay and fired to a dry, ringing hardness. Each tile bears the distinct, shallow indentations of fingers that smoothed the wet silt before it was baked. There are no gaps between them, yet nothing is perfectly plumb.

This is the Floor of the Sieve. It extends in every direction, dipping into broad, shallow basins the size of town squares, then rising into steep, terraced pyramids whose steps are too high for a human stride. Every vertical surface is a wall of dry-stacked stone or unfired mud-brick, held together by a thick, dark mortar that smells faintly of fish oil and river muck.

The air is cold—not the sharp, dry cold of a mountain top, but a heavy, cellar-like dampness that clings to the skin. It is dark here. There is no sky, only a ceiling of massive, rough-hewn stone slabs resting on colossal pillars of red brick, miles overhead. What little light exists does not fall from above; it is carried or struck. Occasional niches in the brickwork hold shallow bowls of baked clay filled with congealed tallow. In each bowl, a thick hemp wick smolders with a greasy, yellow flame, casting long, jerking shadows across the masonry. These tiny spots of fire do little to dispel the dark; they merely reveal the immense density of the structures, showing how closely the walls are packed, how narrow the corridors are, and how the entire region is choked with pillars, buttresses, and low-hanging lintels that force a traveler to constantly duck and turn.

Everything here smells of wet clay, cold lime, and the flat, metallic tang of standing water.

Water is a constant, quiet intruder. It does not flow in streams; instead, it weeps. The cool walls are slick with condensation that gathers in the deep grooves between the bricks, running down in silver threads to collect in the corners of the hex-tiles. In the centers of the low basins, the water pool to a depth of only three or four inches—perfectly still, black mirrors that reflect the smoldering tallow flames. If you stand quiet, you can hear the drops falling: *plip, plop, plep.* The acoustics of the high stone slabs turn these small wet sounds into a strange, hollow music that echoes from a mile away.

But the silence is rare. The predominant quality of this place is a heavy, rhythmic noise that vibrates through the soles of your feet. From somewhere deep in the maze of brick, there is a constant, grinding roar—the sound of huge, manual limestone rollers being pushed across flat surfaces by teams of unseen laborers. Accompanying this is the rhythmic *thunk-thunk-thunk* of wooden paddles beating wet clay into wooden molds. It is a world under construction, yet it does not feel new. It feels raw, unfinished, and ancient all at once. The edges of the bricks are sharp and crisp; they have not been rounded by centuries of wind or water because there is no wind here, and the water only trickles. The erosion is still in its infancy, a mere suggestion of wear where the foot-traffic is heaviest, leaving tiny, polished tracks on the gray hex-tiles.

Nothing grows here in the way of plants. There is not a single leaf, blade of grass, or root-tendril. Instead, the damp clay walls are host to a non-vegetal ecology of minerals and chemical growths. In the dark corners, where the condensation is thickest, "salt-wool" blooms. These are long, hair-like filaments of white sodium that grow out of the mortar joints, curling like fleece. They are dry and brittle to the touch, crackling like dried grass when brushed, only to dissolve back into a salty smear if they fall into the puddle below. In other places, the bricks are coated in a thin, leathery skin of liver-colored lichen—not a plant, but a slow, mineral-eating colony that smells of iron and old wet iron rust.

Fauna is rare, and what exists has adapted to the shadow and the stone. Occasionally, a *click-click-click* sound will rise above the grinding of the rollers. This is the path of the slate-backs—flat, multi-legged creatures the size of a hand, with shells made of a dark, layered slate-like material. They crawl slowly along the mortar lines, scraping off the salt-wool with small, scoop-like mouthparts. They are blind, sensitive only to the vibrations of the heavy clay-paddles. If a shadow crosses them, they do not run; they simply press themselves flat into the seams of the masonry, becoming indistinguishable from the dark mortar.

Sometimes, in the larger basins where the water gathers, one can see the ripple of a "grease-skin." These are ribbon-like organisms, three or four feet long, that look like strips of wet, gray parchment. They slide through the shallow pools with a sluggish, undulating motion, feeding on the tallow drippings that fall from the wall-niches. They have no eyes, no bone, only a tough, muscular hide that smells of cold fat.

There is a strange, illogical geometry to this basin-world. The stairs lead to solid brick walls. The doorways are often too narrow for a shoulder, or so low they must be crawled through. Huge, circular shafts—lined with perfect, spiraling courses of brick—descend into the darkness below, but they are dry and choked with hemp ropes that hang down into nothingness, motionless and heavy with moisture. 

Everything here has been made by hand, with the simplest of tools. The marks of wooden trowels, the rough cross-hatching of flint chisels on the limestone blocks, and the uneven sizes of the sun-dried bricks all point to a labor that is manual, immense, and endless. It is a landscape built by an unimaginable number of hands over generations, yet there are no signs of domestic life—no hearths, no kitchens, no waste-heaps. There are only the walls, the tiles, the weeping water, and the distant, heavy work of the rollers, shaping the raw silt into an ever-expanding honeycomb of dark clay.
