The cliff face behaves like a lung, drawing in the humid rising air of the lower valleys and parting with it here, on this high, sun-warmed ledge. 

The place is a small, semi-circular amphitheater, perhaps fifty paces across, carved into the side of a massive, red-gold sandstone wall. Because it is perched so high, the light is direct and full, but it does not bakingly dry the stone. Instead, the atmosphere within the hollow is thick and greenhouse-warm—a heavy, tropical suspension of moisture that clings to the skin like a damp veil. 

Over immense spans of time, the elements have reached a standstill here. The sandstone is completely smooth. There are no jagged rockfalls, no sharp fractures, and no loose rubble; every surface has been lought and licked by water into soft, undulating curves, like the interior of a worn shell. The basin has a settled, ancient geometry that feels permanent, as if it has looked precisely like this for ten thousand years.

Water is everywhere, yet it does not run. It does not rush or babble. It weeps. Along the horizontal bedding planes of the smooth rock walls, water emerges not as drops, but as a thick, viscous glaze. This water is heavy with minerals, sliding down the sandstone with the sluggishness of liquid glass. It gathers on the flat floor of the ledge in shallow, mirror-flat pools that do not ripple. 

In this warm, wet sanctuary, the vegetation is incredibly rich, but it is strangely arranged. There is no tangled undergrowth, no chaotic jungle. Instead, the plant life is gathered into five or six isolated, circular "islands" rising out of the wet, bare sandstone floor. Each island is an architectural marvel of density. They are hummocks of deep emerald moss, so thick and tightly woven they look like velvet boulders. From the crowns of these moss hummocks grow singular, fleshy trunked plants. Their leaves are massive, spade-shaped, and translucent; they do not rustle, for they are thick and rubbery, filled with a clear, jelly-like sap. 

Between these lush, isolated mounds of green lies wide, clean emptiness—swept sandstone and flat, grey-gold silt, glistening under a thin sheets of water.

In the entire alcove, there is no sound of breathing, no hum of wings, no crawl of legs. The silence is absolute, heavy, and profound. There are no birds in the sky above the ledge, and no insects to disturb the still, wet leaves. The only sound in the entire space occurs at long, predictable intervals: a heavy, viscous bubble of water gathers on the tip of a giant waxy leaf, swells until it is the size of a plum, and drops. 

When it falls, it lands in one of several shallow, circular depressions in the stone floor. 

These depressions are not natural. They are the only sign that anything has ever stood here. They are small, hand-ground basins, the size of a shallow bowl, carved directly into the bed-rock beneath the weeping walls. Beside one of the basins sits a simple tool: a flat wedge of dark, river-rolled volcanic stone, one edge patiently chipped to a dull blade, its handle smoothed by the pressure of fingers that departed epochs ago. The tool has been there so long that the weeping water has coated it in a thin, glass-like sheath of calcium carbonate, gluing it permanently to the sandstone floor.

The dynamics of this place are measured in centuries. Because there are no insects or microbes of decay, dead leaves do not rot. Instead, as the old, waxy leaves of the giant plants gently tire and decline, they are slowly bathed in the mineral-heavy water. Over decades, they do not turn to soil; they petrify. The lower layers of the green islands are composed of these mineralized leaves—botany slowly hardening into jade-like stone, supporting the living green heads above them. 

The light remains constant, reflecting off the slick rock and the glass-like pools, illuminating a world that exists in a perfect, slow-motion balance between the growing and the stone.
