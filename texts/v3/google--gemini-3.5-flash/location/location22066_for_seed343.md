The stone of this world died so long ago that its memory has been completely digested. 

Here, at an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, it is instead soft, warm, and thick with the respiration of a trillion leaves. The landscape is defined by its verticality and its emptiness: it is a regional archipelago of colossal, organic pillars, miles high and hundreds of yards thick, rising out of an invisible abyssal floor. There is no solid ground in the horizontal sense; there are only these green towers, spaced far apart like the legs of a monument built for gods who have departed. Between them lie immense, yawning gulfs of empty blue air. 

If you stand on the edge of one of the horizontal shelf-fungi that project from a pillar’s flank, you are looking across miles of nothingness to the next vertical green column. The space is vast, open, and dizzyingly vacant, yet every square inch of the solid surfaces that *do* exist is made entirely of living tissue. 

The pillars themselves are not rock. Underneath their deep carpet of moss and vine lies a skeleton of petrified ironwood—the smoothed, highly eroded remains of primeval trees that grew when the world was young. Over millions of years, wind and moisture have rounded these giant structures until they resemble massive, green-furred fingers pointing at the sun. There is no soil. Life grows upon life, a pure-plant substrate where ancient, fibrous root-mats form the crust, and generations of decaying ferns provide the bedding for new seeds.

The water of this world is an elusive, vertical traveler. It does not flow in rivers; there are no lake basins at this height. Instead, the water exists as a cycle of condensation. Every morning, warm mists rise from the unseen depths below, clinging to the mossy sides of the pillars. The vegetation is designed to trap this moisture. Giant, ribbed leaves shaped like funnels collect the dew, channeling it into deep, interior wells hollowed out of the fibrous wood. From these aerial cisterns, water drips downward in long, silver threads that fall through the empty chasms between towers, evaporating back into mist before they ever find a bottom. The air is damp, parching slightly at noon, only to become slick and glistening again by twilight.

The light is steady and golden, muted only when a drifting cloud-shelf passes through the spires. It reveals a world in constant, deafening motion. 

The silence one might expect from such an empty sky is entirely absent, replaced by a wall of sound so dense it behaves like a physical pressure. The pillars are hollowed by ancient wood-boring insects and centuries of wind, creating natural flutes that hum with a deep, oceanic bass whenever the drafts shift through the gulfs. Layered over this low drone is the sharp, chaotic chorus of the fauna. It is an overwhelming, swarming ecology. Six-winged gliders with membranous hide leap from the high shelves, their screeching mating calls echoing off the distant pillars. Trillions of leaf-mimic cicadas click in rhythmic waves that travel up and down the vertical forests like static electricity. Strands of ballooning spiders, drifting on silk sails miles long, hum as the wind passes through their filaments. Every crevice of the moss is alive with small, climbing things—soft-shelled crabs that feed on damp leaf-mould, and long-necked lizards that lash their tails against bark to signal their territory.

Human presence here is small, scattered, and precarious. Clinging to the sheltered underbellys of the giant shelf-fungi are tiny clusters of huts. They are constructed from hollowed stalks of giant bamboo-ferns, lashed together with dried vine-fibers. There are no nails, no metal brackets, no masonry. The people who live here use tools of carved bone, sharpened fire-hardened wood, and weighted stones used to loom the tough silk of the wind-spiders. 

To move between the pillars, these inhabitants do not sail or fly; they rely on the ancient, dead vines that span the gulfs. These vines, some as thick as tree trunks, have grown over centuries from one spire to another, self-grafting where they meet. They hang like massive, sagging suspension bridges across the open sky, slick with green mold and trembling constantly from the vibrations of the wind and the scuttling of multi-legged creatures.

The life of this place is active, fast-paced, and indifferent to its own height. A fallen leaf does not hit the ground; it drifts for miles, becoming a temporary raft for a colony of aphids before it disappears into the lower mists. A hand-carved wooden bowl, dropped from a dwelling, is lost forever to the warm gray void below. Everything is designed to cling, to weave, or to float. The world has settled into this ancient, stable state—a high-altitude sky-garden where the earth was forgotten long ago, and only the green, the wet, and the roaring wind remain.
