The horizon does not fade into blue distance; instead, it terminates in a low, sharp step-up of white limestone blocks, rising to meet a solid, luminous sky of unblinking gold. 

Every square inch of this low-lying basin is built. There is no soil, no sand, no raw mud. The ground is an endless flat terrace of hand-dressed pale stone and heavy terracotta tiles, laid out in vast, interlocking squares that press against one another with razor-sharp seams. The tool marks of the builders are fresh—distinct, shallow grooves from flint chisels and stone adzes are visible on every surface, still dusted with a thin film of pale masonry powder that has never been swept away or disturbed by wind. The blocks are large, some the size of small carts, yet they have been aligned with a desperate, hand-wrought precision.

Over this immense, paved depression, the water sits. It does not flow in channels or pool in natural hollows. It rests in a perfectly uniform sheet, exactly two finger-widths deep, across wide sections of the stone tile. Elsewhere, the water defies the flat plane: it seeps from the hair-thin joints between the blocks and climbs upward, clinging to the sides of low, hand-carved pillars and dry-stone pylons in a thin, glittering skin that flows toward the sky. It moves against gravity, silent and smooth, coating the vertical stone in a wet, mirror-like gloss before evaporating into the hot, motionless air.

The heat is immense, heavy and wet like the inside of a closed greenhouse. It does not fluctuate. There is no sun disk in the sky, only the all-pervading, shadowless glare of the amber light that illuminates every corner of the structure with equal intensity. Because the light comes from everywhere at once, there are no shadows. The pillars do not cast dark lines across the wet stone; the leaves of the giant plants do not shade the stems below them. Everything exists in a flat, high-definition clarity.

From small, circular holes bored cleanly through the stone floor, the vegetation erupts. It is lush, dense, and unnaturally vibrant, though it grows in a sparse, orderly distribution, leaving wide avenues of wet, empty paving between each cluster. Huge, heart-shaped elephant-ear leaves, thick as cured leather and slick with condensation, rise on stalks that have been bound to low, hand-lashed bamboo frames. The ropes securing them are simple hemp and split vine, tied in neat, repetitive knots. These plants are alive, yet they do not rustle. There is no breeze to move them. They stand in an eternal, sweating stillness, their deep green surfaces gleaming under the golden glare. 

Among the green leaves, the reality of the place shows its fractures. In some places, a vine does not grow outward; it climbs an invisible vertical line in the empty air, winding tightly around nothingness, its tendrils finding purchase on an absent trellis. Droplets of water do not fall from the tips of the broad leaves; instead, they detach and float horizontally, drifting like tiny, perfect glass spheres through the warm air until they touch another surface and are absorbed. A leaf that has been split by some ancient touch remains open, its green sap suspended in mid-air as a row of static, emerald beads that neither fall nor dry up.

The silence here is heavy, cushioned by the wet, hot air. It is not an empty silence, but a muffled one, as if the world were wrapped in wet wool. There are no bird calls, no wind-sighs, no murmurs of running water. 

Yet, life is everywhere. 

Thousands of pale, legless frogs, with skin like wet porcelain and large, golden eyes that do not blink, cling to the vertical water-skins on the stone pillars. They are motionless, save for the rhythmic, silent pulse of their throats as they drink the rising water. On the flat tiles, beneath the shallow, two-inch lagoons, flat-bodied worms, patterned like hand-woven rugs in red and black, glide over the chisel marks. They move without producing ripples, their bodies passing through one another when they meet, briefly occupying the same space without trailing blood or breaking skin. 

Above, hovering in the golden air, are large insects with wings of dry palm-fiber and segment-bodies of polished black bean-shucks. They do not flap their wings; they remain perfectly suspended, drifting only when a warm pocket of air shifts them an inch to the left or right. They make no hum. 

In this valley, time has no purchase. Nothing decays. A fallen leaf, green and heavy, lies at the bottom of a shallow stone pool; it has lain there since the stones were carved, unchanged, its edges as sharp and fresh as the day it was severed. The water that evaporates from the stone surfaces does not form clouds; it simply becomes the thick, sweet-smelling air, which in turn becomes the water that climbs the pillars again. 

It is a world built by hand, young and sharp-edged, but locked in an eternal, warm afternoon where the tools have just been laid down, and the stone has not yet had time to cool.
