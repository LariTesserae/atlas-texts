The high-altitude tablelands of the Upper Sills are never dry. Here, at six thousand feet above the salt-flats, the atmosphere does not form clouds so much as it simply thickens, turning the warm, sulfur-tinged air into a suspended lake of golden vapor. The heat is heavy and close, like the air inside a bathhouse, smelling of wet iron, scalded river-silt, and the faint, sweet scent of warm oil. 

Beneath this perpetual mist lies a landscape of immense antiquity. The rock is a dark, iron-bearing sandstone, weathered over millions of years into smooth, bulbous domes—like giant, half-buried loaves of bread—separated by deep, curving troughs. There are no sharp edges here. The constant condensation has melted the stone’s features, leaving behind a topography of soft, wet curves that gleam in the diffused daylight like the skin of some vast river titan. Thin, warm sheets of water slide silently across these domes, living glazes that never pool but simply weep downward, south toward the great cliffs.

Upon this soft-molded terrace sits the city of Coris-under-the-Crawl. 

It is an urban landscape of massive, heavy-industry scale, yet it is strangely empty. There are no crowded alleys or teeming markets. Instead, the settlement is an archipelago of colossal, dark-brick structures perched on the summits of the stone domes, separated by half-mile expanses of bare, wet rock. These structures are built from heavy, purple-fired vitrified tile and held together by thick seams of black bitumen. They are monolithic—windowless on their lower levels, with upper tiers characterized by rows of deep, arched vents that breathe out columns of pale, dry steam.

Connecting these isolated brick bastions are massive iron aqueducts and pipeline trestles, some thirty yards wide, supported by squat, muscular piers of dressed granite. The pipes do not hiss; they shudder. They are wrapped in sodden felt and bound with green-patinaed copper bands to keep the internal heat from cooking the passing air. 

Inside the city’s margins, the sound is absolute. It is a deafening, physical weight that presses against the chest and vibrates the fluids of the inner ear until balance becomes a matter of conscious effort. There is no single source of this noise; it is the collective voice of the Sills’ industrial heart. It is the deep, basaltic rumble of massive water-turbines buried three hundred feet inside the hollowed-out bellies of the rock domes, where the mountain's internal springs are forced through bronze impellers. Overlying this low-frequency thrum is the rhythmic, mechanical *tonk-clack* of miles-long iron drawbeams, moving at a snail’s pace back and forth across the wet stone to power distant pumps. It is a sound too loud for voices. The few people who populate this place—engineers in heavy, rubberized aprons and brass goggles to protect their eyes from the caustic condensation—communicate through a complex language of hand-flags and colored lanterns hung from the gantry cranes.

Vegetation here is a rare, greasy afterthought. There are no trees, no grasses, no shrubs. Where the warm water runs thickest down the brick flanks of the pump-houses, a black, velvet-textured lichen grows in wide, circular mats. It smells of vinegar and wet coal. In the drier joints of the masonry, where the steam pipes cross, one can occasionally find small tufts of a white, leafless fern that feeds on the sulfurous exhaust. It has no green chlorophyll; its stems are as translucent as fish-scales and warm to the touch.

Fauna is nearly non-existent in this deafening basin. The noise and the heavy humidity have driven away everything with lungs or delicate ears. The only native lives are silent, slow-moving things of the wet stone: giant, blind river-snails with thick, iron-bound shells that crawl along the undersides of the warm flumes, grazing on the black lichen, and small, pale isopods that skitter through the lime-crusts at the edges of the outfalls. They are indifferent to the thunder of the turbines.

The pace of life in Coris is agonizingly slow, matching the geological patience of the water-carved stone. The cities were built to drain the deep heat of the earth and convert it to clean, high-pressure steam for the lowlands, and this process requires little daily change. The great iron valves, ten feet in diameter and turned by ponderous, water-driven wooden wheels, are adjusted perhaps once a year, clicked three notches to the left or right to account for the seasonal shift in the mountain's pulse. The brickwork, though constantly bathed in warm acid-mist, decays so slowly that the masonry crews patch only a single dome-block per decade. 

In the normal, yellow-gray light of midday, the Sills look like an abandoned engine room of some primeval god, left running in the warm mist. The iron pipelines rust into deep, dark oranges; the purple brickwork turns black where the water drips; and the great, smooth stone mounds stand silent and patient beneath the deafening, invisible roar of the water deep within them. It is a place of massive, industrial solidarity—dry inside the roaring, brick citadels, wet on the ancient, weeping stone without, and completely settled in its loud, warm, and empty eternity.
