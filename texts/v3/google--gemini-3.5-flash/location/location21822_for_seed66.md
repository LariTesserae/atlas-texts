### The Heights of Oru

At this elevation, the sky does not feel distant; it feels like a heavy, bright ceiling of wet glass. We are on the high, weathered shoulders of a continental ridge, where the earth has been planed down by eons of damp wind into massive, rounded steps. There are no peaks here—only infinite, humped plateaus of black basalt and grey schist, slick with a perpetual skin of warm condensation. 

The air is warm—not dry-desert hot, but the thick, muggy warmth of a laundry-house or a geothermal basin. Moisture does not fall as rain so much as it occupies the space; it gathers on every vertical surface, weeping down the stone in thick, silent tears, pooling in shallow basins before spilling over the edge into the next canyon.

There is not a single leaf. There is no moss, no grass, no lichen, no wood. To an eye accustomed to green, this world looks initially like a dead quarry, but it is intensely, almost suffocatingly alive.

### The Living Crust

In place of vegetation, the landscape is coated in a thick, squelching mantle of animal life. Every square inch of the wet basalt is claimed. 

The stones are faced with "shingle-crusts"—flat, overlapping colonial mollusks with grey-purple shells that pulse with a slow, three-second respiratory rhythm. Where the water runs fastest down the rock faces, it passes over fields of millions of tiny tube-worms. These worms do not look like worms; they resemble fingers of white leather, each boasting a tiny, bright-red feathery gill-fan that filters the mineral-heavy run-off. They are so dense that from a mile away, the canyon walls appear to be blushing a soft, damp pink.

Higher up, where the mist settles on flat benches, are the "grease-mats." These are colossal, multi-ton colonies of slug-like organisms that move so slowly their travel is measured in seasons. They are grey-white, rubbery, and cold to the touch, scraping the rock bare of mineral salts and leaving behind trails of iridescent mucus that never dry in this damp air. 

Overhead, the warm updrafts from the lower canyons are thick with aerial drifters. They are not birds, but light-framed, gas-bloated kites with translucent skins of pale yellow and blue. They have no legs; they drift on the thermal columns of the city’s exhaust fumes, trailing long, sticky tentacles to catch the high-altitude midges that swarm in trillions near the wet ceilings of the cloud-level.

### The Industrial Terraces

This is a built world, layered directly onto the ancient stone. The city of Oru does not sit upon the plateaus; it is carved into them, bolted to them, and suspended between them. It is an industrial sprawl of staggering proportions, built to match the scale of the mountains.

The architecture is dominated by heavy, wet iron, green-patinated copper, and dark basalt masonry. Because the air is always wet, every metal surface is either rusted to a stable, dark-red crust or painted with thick, black tar. Massive aqueducts—some as wide as four-lane highways—traverse the chasms, carrying the mineral-rich weeping waters of the upper elevations down toward giant, bellowing processing plants.

Everything is mechanical, driven by the weight of water and the expansion of steam. There are no electrical grids here; instead, the city is connected by a web of whistling steam-pipes and massive, slowly rotating drive-shafts. Across the valleys, iron cables the thickness of tree trunks sag under the weight of iron gondolas, which creep across the void at a walking pace, carrying loads of crushed ore and grease-fat.

The buildings are stepped, following the eroded contours of the cliffs. They have no windows—glass is useless here, as it fogs instantly—but are instead fitted with heavy, downward-sloping iron louvers that let in the bright, diffused light while keeping out the constant drip of the condensing air.

### The Sound and the Slow Pulse

The silence of this high country was broken centuries ago, replaced by a dense, layered acoustic environment that never ceases. 

The bass-note of the landscape is water: the infinite, soft *hiss* of trillions of drops sliding over animal shells, and the deep, muffled roar of waterfalls plunge-cleaning the lower structural tiers.

Woven into this is the breath of the industry. Every few minutes, a massive pressure-relief valve on a distant thermal column lets out a dry, high-pitched shriek that echoes off the black cliffs for miles. The great drive-shafts, mounted on stone bearings lubricated with grease-mat oil, produce a constant, low-frequency hum that vibrates in the soles of one’s feet.

But the loudest sound is biological. It is the communal clicking. The shingle-crusts, when they detect a change in light or water flow, snap their shells shut with a hard, wooden *tack*. With billions of them covering the terraces, this sound rises and falls in waves—a crackling, snapping roar like a dry forest fire, moving slowly across the face of the mountainside as clouds pass before the sun.

### The Law of the Place

In Oru, nothing changes quickly. The iron rusts at a calculated, geologic rate. The grease-mats crawl their inch-a-day paths down the channels. The great iron water-wheels, sixty feet high, turn only three or four times a minute, their heavy oak and iron buckets filling slowly from the constant weeping of the high rocks.

The people who live here—small, pale-skinned operators who wear slicked-leather aprons and greased high-altitude boots—move with the same deliberate lack of hurry. To run is to slip on the wet shingle-crusts; to strike something quickly is to break an iron tool against wet basalt. 

They harvest the grease-mats for oil to lubricate their gears. They scrape the tube-worms into massive vats to render them into a high-protein paste that smells of iodine. They mine the deep, wet veins of copper that run like green fat through the black cliffs. 

At noon, when the high-altitude sun is directly overhead, the mist glows from within. The entire city—all thirty tiers of iron, stone, and pulsing pink shell—shines with a greasy, brilliant glare, like a mountain of wet coal that has been decorated with jewels. It is a world without a single blade of grass, yet it is so thick with life and steam that it feels as though it might drown in its own breath.
