The horizon does not curve so much as it dissolves into a warm, sun-bright haze. This is a basin of continental proportions, a low-slung sink in the world’s crust where the land is flat, vast, and intensely young. 

There is no water here. No rivers have cut valleys into this terrain; no ancient rains have softened the contours of the ground. The floor of the basin is a raw, emerging crust of mineral plates—sharp-edged, unweathered, and grey-white, looking like freshly poured concrete that has only just set. It is an embryonic geology, unscarred by the passage of time, yet it is completely covered. 

Every square inch of this newborn plain is occupied by a colossal, biological metropolis. 

Though there is no technology here—no metal, no glass, no power lines or machinery—the landscape is structured with the density and order of a ancient city. It is built entirely of sun-baked mud, mineral dust, and a tough, organic cement secreted by the scale-inhabitants. Towers of light grey clay, shaped like tapered obelisks, rise thirty or forty feet into the warm air. They stand shoulder-to-shoulder, separated only by narrow, sunken lanes that form a perfect, self-organizing grid stretching to the horizon. Millions of these towers compose a continuous, unbroken fabric of life. 

Growing on, over, and through this clay city is a lush, waterless vegetation. These are not damp, soft plants, but dry-adapted species of astonishing density. They do not run on water; their veins carry a thick, clear, slow-flowing oil that does not evaporate in the heat. There are heavy, succulent vines with leaves like polished jade, and low, fan-shaped shrubs with fronds that feel like stiff parchment. They sprout from specialized pockets in the clay towers, draped over the arches and bridges like a heavy green and amber tapestry. Thick carpets of grey-green lichen, dry but oily to the touch, cover the floors of the lanes, acting as a soft, resilient paving. 

The air is warm and perfectly clear, illuminated by a steady, pleasant daylight. In this dry warmth, the ecosystem is in constant, busy motion. 

The city is teeming with fauna, though they are strangely quiet. Thousands of six-legged, smooth-shelled creatures—comparable to large, herbivorous beetles or small, land-dwelling crabs—scurry along the sunken clay lanes and up the sheer walls of the obelisks. They are the builders and the gardeners. They carry mouthfuls of dry mineral dust to repair the towers; they prune the oily vines; they harvest the heavy, seed-bearing pods that hang from the rooftops. Overhead, large, four-winged gliders with papery, translucent wings sail silently on the warm thermal currents generated by the dark clay roofs.

Despite the millions of living things occupying every foot of space, the world is hushed. There are no vocalizations—no bird calls, no grunts, no whistles. The silence is crowded and alive, composed entirely of tiny, friction-based whispers: the dry *shuk-shuk* of chitinous limbs crossing the lichen carpets, the soft rasp of leather wings against the warm air, and the gentle, paper-dry rustle of the oil-vines swaying in the breeze. 

Because of the scale of this low-lying basin, it generates its own dry weather. Every afternoon, the intense sun warms the dense clay city, causing the towers to exhale hot, sweet-smelling air from their ventilation shafts. This creates a rising convection current that pulls in cooler air from the basin’s rim. The result is a daily wind-cycle—not a storm, but a rhythmic, warm tide of air that sweeps across the continent. 

As the wind rises, the plants begin their dry pollination. Millions of tiny, amber-colored spores are released from the rooftop gardens, rising into the sky in great, swirling clouds. They do not look like smoke, but rather like golden dust-devils, twisting and dancing between the towers. The spores settle slowly, blanketing the city in a fine, fragrant powder that the silent workers immediately begin to sweep, collect, and knead into paste to feed the next generation of building-blocks.

Everything in this landscape is integrated, packed, and perpetually active. The raw geology beneath is held fast by the roots of the oil-vines and the heavy foundations of the clay towers. The world is a closed, dry, self-sustaining loop—a vast, quiet hive of stone, leaf, and shell, working in perfect, instinctual harmony under a wide, warm sky.
