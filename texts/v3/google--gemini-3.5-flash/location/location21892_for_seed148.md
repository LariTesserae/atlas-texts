To find this place, one must crawl beneath the roots of a single, fallen elder-tree in a cold, sodden hollow. The entrance is no larger than a badger’s set, but once the threshold is crossed, the cramped geometry of the forest floor shears away. 

You find yourself standing on a narrow shelf of waxy, unformed peat that hangs over an impossible abyss. The space is a hollow sphere, perhaps half a mile across, yet it exists entirely within the root-space of the tree above. This is a cathedral of empty air, wet and cold, smelling of vinegar, turnip-rot, and cold grease.

Here, the rules of soil and stone do not apply. There is no rock. The "geology" of this cavern is embryonic, a pre-mineral slurry of cellulose and thick, black lignin that has not yet decided whether to become coal, wood, or basalt. In some places, it is soft as cold lard; in others, it has hardened into a slick, obsidian-dark charcoal that rings like iron when struck. But mostly, it is living tissue—an all-pervading plant substrate that serves as floor, wall, and sky.

Through this vast, dark void runs an organized, industrial town. 

It is not built *on* the landscape, but grown *out* of it and wired together with brass and copper. Catwalks of woven, iron-hard vine stretch across the empty air, connecting suspended neighborhoods of hollowed-out gourds and dried, leather-skinned gall-bulbs. These spherical dwellings cling to massive vertical taproots that descend from the unseen ceiling like the pillars of an ancient, damp cistern. 

Mechanization here is quiet, wet, and constant. The town runs on osmotic pressure and the slow, heavy digestion of starch. Huge, pulsing seedpods, the size of houses, act as low-pressure steam boilers. They are wrapped in loops of green copper wire that hiss softly with static electricity. Thin, hollow reeds—some insulated with sheepish, felted moss, others bare and dripping cold sap—run alongside the catwalks like utility pipes, carrying pressurized humors, boiling water, and lubricating oils to different quarters of the settlement. 

The light is a dense, velvety gloom, broken only by the work-lights of the factories. These are not flames, but glass jars filled with pale blue, cold-burning fungi, or the dull, orange-hot glow of iron rods heated by the slow, chemical fermentation of compost-heaps deep within the taproots. This amber and blue light glints off the wet surfaces of the wood and the brass fittings of the pumps, casting long, distorted shadows that do not quite match the objects throwing them.

Indeed, space here is elastic, slipping under the influence of a quiet but total breakdown of physical constants. If you drop a pebble from a catwalk, it does not hasten toward the bottom; it falls slowly, drifting sideways as if suspended in oil, until it vanishes into the dark. Sometimes, you can look straight up and see the lights of a lower district directly above you, the workers there walking upside down with their boots clicking softly on the peat-planks. The gravity is not a pull from below, but a gentle, persuasive agreement between your body and the nearest flat surface of plant-matter. If you jump, you may float for several minutes before realizing you have gently drifted to a vertical wall, which immediately becomes your floor.

Sound is muffled, wrapped in the damp wool of the air. The industrial processes of this town are nearly silent. The great pistons that pump sap up to the high distillers do not clang; they are made of self-lubricating, iron-hard lignum vitae, sliding in sleeves of wet leather with a soft *shuck-shuck* that sounds like a boot stepping in deep mud. The dynamos hum at a register so low it is felt in the teeth rather than heard in the ears.

Yet, despite the quiet, the void is alive. An abundant, silent fauna populates the darkness between the structures. 
- Overhead, giant, blind moths with wings of dry, brown leaf-skeletons drift through the warm updrafts of the boiler-pods. They make no sound as they fly.
- Along the copper lines, pale, jointed wood-lice the size of terriers crawl in slow, military files. They lick the green corrosion off the metal, their mandibles making a faint, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* like clockwork keys turning in an empty room.
- In the gaps where the raw peat is still soft and forming, long, translucent white eels slide through the pulp, their bodies glowing with a faint, internal phosphorescence that pulses in time with the beating of the city’s master-valves.

The environment is in constant, metabolic motion. There are no seasons here, only the cycles of the great trees above and the industry below. Every forty minutes, the temperature drops three degrees as the master-pumps on the lowest level draw cold water from some deeper, subterranean well. This triggers a sudden, brief condensation. A fine, heavy mist settles over everything, coating the brass pipes, the mossy scaffolds, and the pale skin of the inhabitants in cold, clean water. For three minutes, the silent city drips. The sound of ten thousand simultaneous water-drops hitting the leathery gourds and the soft peat below creates a whispering murmur—the only loud noise the cavern ever makes.

Then, as the pumps complete their stroke, the boilers hiss. A wave of sweet, fermented warmth rises from the compost-engines, clearing the mist. The wood-lice resume their crawling, the blind moths settle back onto the cables, and the quiet, impossible city returns to its work of transforming sap into light, and gravity into an afterthought.
