High on the flanks of an unnamed, sky-piercing column of raw black glass, there is a fissure. 

To find it, one would have to scale vertical miles of razor-sharp basalt, passing through layers of scalding, sulfurous clouds that cling to the mountain’s knees. But the mountain itself is not the landscape. The landscape is inside the fissure—a hidden pocket of space no larger than a traveler’s travel-chest, wedged six thousand meters above the unseen valley floor.

Here, the air is thick, hot, and heavy with a wetness that feels like breathing soup. The temperature is a constant, oppressive swelter, kept near boiling by deep geothermal veins that thrum through the young, unweathered stone. The rock here has no history; it is a fresh wound in the earth, sharp-edged and glossy, lacking any of the soft, rounded features of ancient hills. Every ridge within this tiny hollow is a knife-point of black volcanic glass, still smelling of the forge.

Water does not fall here as rain; rather, the atmosphere is so choked with moisture that the air itself seems to be condensing continuously. Beads of scalding water swell on the glass walls, growing like heavy fruit before they slip downward—or, more accurately, sideways.

For within this micro-grotto, physics has lost its grip. 

While the pocket is physically microscopic compared to the mountain harboring it, the space inside does not agree with its external boundaries. If you peer into the three-inch-wide crack at the back of the hollow, the perspective bends. The crack does not terminate; instead, it reveals an impossible, labyrinthine network of miniature glass canyons that seem to stretch for miles, all contained within a space you could cup in two hands. 

Gravity here is a suggestion, a drunkard’s path. Water droplets do not fall toward the center of the earth; they crawl in slow, spiraling paths across the obsidian ceiling, clinging to the stone by some altered capillary force, or they hover in mid-air, spinning slowly like wet, transparent marbles. Sometimes, a bubble of boiling water will float past your cheek, perfectly spherical, enclosing a tiny, frantic ecosystem of its own before it touches a sharp glass outcrop and silently bursts.

There are no plants. The concept of a leaf or a root has no place in this mineral uterus. In their stead are massive, wet crusts of bright cadmium-orange and neon-purple bacterial mats that coat the glass like velvet. They do not photosynthesize; they feed on the heat and the sulfur, pulsing with a slow, metabolic rhythm that mimics the breathing of a sleeping beast.

But if there is no flora, the fauna is overwhelming. The pocket is a congested metropolis of miniature, frantic life. 

Thousands of tiny, translucent organisms—none larger than a fingernail—swarm the wet surfaces. There are glass-sharded mites with legs like optical fibers that click silently as they crawl over one another in dense, writhing piles. There are flat, pale worms that glide through the hot condensation films like grease on a griddle, their bodies so clear you can see the boiling juices moving through their tracts. From the larger hanging water-globes, tiny, hair-like crimson hydras reach outward, waving their tentacles in search of invisible spores. 

It is crowded to the point of suffocation. Every square millimeter of the glass wall is occupied, layered with shells, segmentations, and pulsing membranes. Yet, the chaos is strangely quiet. The entire micro-environment operates in a deep, damp silence. There is no wind, no roar of the great heights outside. The only sounds are the occasional, microscopic *tink* of a crustacean’s shell hitting the glass, the muffled *hiss* of a tiny pocket of steam escaping a pore, and a low, sub-audible hum—less a sound and more a vibration—that rises from the mountain’s hot heart.

In a small, dry alcove near the front of the fissure, where the heat is slightly less intense, lies the only sign of intent. 

It is not a building, nor even a permanent shelter, but a collection of primitive, manual tools. They are so small they could be mistaken for debris, yet their arrangement is deliberate. A dozen tiny splinters of obsidian have been meticulously chipped and bound to the hollow shafts of insect limbs using fine, sticky threads harvested from the web-spinning worms. Next to them are three half-spheres of dried, hardened bacterial rind, hollowed out to act as bowls, one of which still holds a smear of grey, greasy mineral paste. 

The tools are worn. Someone, or some *thing* with fingers no thicker than pine needles, has been working here. They have been scraping the orange mats, harvesting the white mineral salt that crystallizes where the hot springs weep, and perhaps hunting the larger violet jellies that lounge in the deeper, gravity-defying puddles.

To stand before this crack in the high glass mountain is to look at a world that is still being born, teeming with an intense, silent, and crowded biology that has bypassed the green evolutionary paths of the lower world entirely. It is a hot, wet room of glass and steam, where the rules of space have begun to unravel, and where something small and patient is already learning how to use a knife.
