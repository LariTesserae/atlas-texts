This is the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts, a subterranean basin where no part of the horizon belongs to nature. 

It is a world built entirely of small, hand-baked terracotta bricks, square pavers, and heavy lime-mortar. Millions of these reddish flats line the floor, climb the massive square pillars, and curve into the low, ribbed vaults of the ceiling. There is no raw earth here, no granite bedrock underlying the structure; if one were to pry up a brick from the floor, they would find only another layer of brick beneath it, and beneath that, another, descending into the dark. 

For centuries, this chamber has settled into its own balance. The edges of the brick stairs, once sharp and square, have been worn down by water and soft feet until they resemble smooth, wet soapstones. The lime mortar has dissolved in some joints and calcified in others, forming pale, stony icicles that hang from the ceiling plates.

The air is warm and close, holding the muggy heat of a cellar that sits above a geothermal vein. It smells of damp clay, tallow-grease, and the sharp, yeast-like tang of small things rotting in corners. It is not wet enough to drown—there are no deep pools—but moisture is everywhere. It weeps from the walls in steady, glossy films. It collects in shallow, segmented brick pans on the floor, flat squares of water no deeper than a finger, where the surface tension is kept in constant, shivering motion by the drafts.

Far above, at the apex of the vaults, are the light-wells: narrow, round chimneys lined with green-glazed tile that rise to the surface world. Down these wells falls a pale, dusty daylight, illuminating the drifting steam and casting long, pale cones of light onto the red floor. The daylight is steady but soft, supplemented here and there by small, shallow cups of clay filled with animal fat, their braided hemp wicks sputtering with small orange flames that cast long shadows behind the pillars.

The room is small—perhaps forty paces across—but it is incredibly dense, packed with structural elements and simple, heavy machinery. Stout wooden walkways of rough-sawn cedar, slick with rot and lashed together with thick, hairy hemp ropes, span the brick channels. Great copper sluice gates, greened with oxidation, are pinned into the brickwork with fat, hand-beaten bronze pegs. Thick-walled ceramic pipes, three feet across and banded with rusted iron hoops, run along the bases of the walls like slumbering worms. Everything is within arm's reach; the space feels crowded, almost cellular, a complex puzzle of masonry and wood.

And it is loud. The chamber is a sounding-board for a hundreds-voiced acoustic ecology. The sound of water is a constant, shifting rhythm: the high, metallic *ping-ping* of leaks hitting bronze plates, the flat *slap* of condensation dropping into the shallow pans, and the low, hollow gurgle of wet air moving through the ceramic pipes. 

Beneath this watery percussion is the sound of life. The Room of the Six Thousand Shunts is crowded with animals. Thousands of small, flat-bodied river crabs, their shells the same dull orange as the baked brick, scurry sideways along the damp masonry, their tiny legs clicking like a shower of dry peas on a tiled floor. In the dry crevices between the ceiling vaults, small, hairless bats with oversized ears huddle in writhing clumps, keeping up a constant, liquid chittering that rises and falls in pitch. 

In the lukewarm pools, pale, sightless crayfish with long, sensitive antennae whip the water into tiny bubbles as they fight over bits of debris falling from the light-wells. Now and then, a swift-winged bird—a small, dark-bellied glider that nests in the chimneys above—drops down a shaft in a blur of feathers, snaps a flying midge from the warm air with a sharp *clack* of its bill, and spirals back up toward the sky.

Vegetation here is tough and sparse, lacking the light for true greenness. It exists only in the marginal zones where the light-wells cast their pale beams. Here, patches of white, velvety mold coat the damp wood of the catwalks, and a thin, liver-colored lichen creeps across the mortar joints, feeding on the minerals in the lime. In the dampest corners, long, pale rootlets—belonging to some unseen forest growing on the surface far above—have pushed through the joints between the ceiling bricks, hanging down like fringes of greasy, white thread. They sway slightly in the drafts, catching the yellow light of the tallow lamps.

Everything in this space is in motion, engaged in a slow, circular metabolism. A leaf falls down a light-well; it is instantly torn apart by the orange crabs; the crab-waste feeds the gray mold on the cedar beams; the mold is grazed upon by pale, thumb-sized slugs; the slugs are gathered up by the bats. The water, too, is part of this cycle. It drips from the vaults, runs down the grooved brick steps, pools in the pans, evaporates into the warm air to cling to the ceiling, and drips down once more. 

It is an ancient, manufactured basin that has forgotten its original purpose. Whatever machine or city once sat above or below has faded, but this middle-tier—this warm, brick-lined pocket of wetness and stone—has settled into a permanent, self-sustaining state. It is a loud, busy, crowded world of baked earth and soft, scurrying things, living out their tiny, active lives in the warm shade of the arches.
