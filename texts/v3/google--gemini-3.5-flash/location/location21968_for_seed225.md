This is the *Chamber of the Salt-Loom*, a single subterranean pocket shaped like a flattened, inverted egg, buried some sixty feet beneath a dry, sun-baked plateau. It is small—perhaps forty paces from wall to wall—but it feels vast because of its profound, hollow emptiness. There are no columns to support the ceiling, no banks of talus on the floor, and no hanging stone draperies. Everything that could have fallen has fallen and been ground to dust or dissolved away over thousands of cycles.

What remains is a room of pure volume, smoothed by ancient, long-concluded air currents into a flawless basin of pale, dry limestone. It is warm, holding the steady, radiating heat of the desert above like a loaf of bread left in a cooling kiln. 

From the center of the ceiling hangs a massive, plugged throat of selenite crystal. It is a natural lens, five yards wide, worn smooth by the wind of the upper world and polished by the rare, heavy rains that sweep the plateau. At midday, this lens catches the vertical sun and flings a column of blinding, white-hot glare straight down into the center of the chamber. The light is so intense (2.9/3) that the air itself seems to smoke, though there is no smoke; instead, tiny, microscopic flakes of mica and dried salt rise on the gentle thermal updrift of the light-column, hovering and turning like cold sparks in an invisible column of water. Because the air is so still, these spark-like flakes do not dance; they float in long, horizontal orbits, taking hours to complete a single circle around the light.

The chamber is almost entirely empty (0.0/3 density). The polished floor is free of soil, gravel, or debris. Only at the very edges of the dome, where the limestone walls meet the floor in a soft, concaved curve, is there any texture.

Here, the water lives. It does not flow, nor does it pool in quantities larger than a cupped hand. It is an environment of beads and tracks (0.7/3). Across the upper lip of the dome, where the cool stone meets the rising warm air, moisture condenses into heavy, fat drops. These drops run down the walls along identical, pre-worn grooves—tiny, hairline channels that have been eroded into the limestone over centuries. The water moves with a lazy, deliberate rhythm. A single drop will form at the ceiling, cling for an hour, then slide six feet down a groove to lodge in a small, thumb-sized depression in the rock, where it sits like an eye until a passing creature drinks it or the dry air reclaims it.

Beside these damp channels grow the only plants of the chamber (1.4/3). They are small, pale succulents, no larger than coins, that look like buttons of green jade. They have no stems and no roots to speak of; they cling directly to the damp limestone seeps using a tough, black glue they secrete from their undersides. Once every few days, as the sun-shaft shifts its angle slightly with the turning of the season, a single succulent will open a tiny, trumpet-shaped white flower that lasts for three hours, dries into a husk, and is immediately eaten by the chamber's inhabitants.

These inhabitants are small, flat-bodied reptiles (1.7/3), about the length of a human hand, with skin that mimics the chalky white-gold of the limestone. They are everywhere along the perimeter, though they move so slowly (1.1/3) that an observer might initially mistake them for natural ridges in the stone. They have five-fingered hands with delicate, needle-like claws, and large, dark, lidless eyes that are constantly hooded by bony, translucent brows to protect them from the central glare.

Though there are no constructed dwellings, nests, or signs of engineering (0.5/3 built), these creatures possess a quiet, manual intelligence (1.4/3 tech). They do not build, but they arrange and maintain. Scattered along the base of the walls are their "toolkits"—neatly sorted piles of natural objects they have gathered or lightly altered. There are flat, black river-pebbles brought from some deeper, inaccessible crawlway, used to crack the hard seed-cases of the succulents. There are thin, hollow bird-bones used as siphons to draw water out of the deeper wall-crevices. Most remarkably, there are small, hand-flaked scrapers of red chert. The reptiles use these to scrape the white salt crusts from the dry sections of the wall, grinding the salt into a fine powder which they rub into their skin to keep themselves clean of fungus. 

The sound of the chamber is a low, rhythmic breathing (1.1/3). The air column in the center acts as a passive chimney, drawing a faint, cool draft down from the edges of the selenite lens and casting it warm out through the floor-cracks. This creates a perpetual, soft *shhh*—the sound of silk being pulled over dry stone. Into this silence falls the occasional, metallic *tink* of a water droplet hitting a dry stone ledge, or the dry, gravelly scrape of a lizard tapping a chert tool against the limestone to clear its edge of salt.

Time in the chamber does not seem to pass in minutes. Because the ceiling lens focuses the light so perfectly, the beam does not sweep across the room like a sundial hand; instead, it remains virtually stationary for the peak six hours of the day, a solid pillar of white fire, before vanishing almost instantly as the sun dips below the lip of the plateau’s dry sink. When the light goes out, the chamber does not grow cold; it slowly exhales its gathered warmth into the night, the lizards remaining perfectly still where they basked, waiting for the sky to turn and the lens to ignite once more.
