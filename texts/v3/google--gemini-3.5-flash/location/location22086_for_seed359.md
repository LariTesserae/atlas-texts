The floor is made of hand-pressed terracotta tiles, each one the size of a man’s breastplate, laid out in overlapping scales. They are warm—not hot, but holding the steady, radiant heat of a bread oven that has been cooling for an hour. There is no soil here, no sand, no raw earth. Every boundary is a dry brick wall, three feet high or twelve, built of coarse, pale clay that still bears the thumb-prints of the ones who kneaded it. The joints are mortarless but tight, finished with bronze-scrapings that have tarnished to a dull, dry green in the crevices.

Nothing is worn down. The edges of the bricks are sharp enough to scrape skin; the chisel marks on the low limestone steps are crisp and deep, their ridges sparkling with untarnished calcite. It is a space built yesterday, or perhaps a thousand years ago in a world where wind does not blow hard enough to grind stone.

It is small—a courtyard no larger than a village common, bounded by these low, stepped walls that lead to other courtyards, which lead to others, all on the same sunken plain. There is no horizon; thirty feet above, the air simply thickens into a flat, jointless ceiling of pale, glowing plaster that sheds a white, unblinking daylight.

In this dry, warm enclosure, life is dense. A thick, succulent vegetation crowds the margins of every walkway, growing not from dirt but from deep, hand-carved troughs built into the masonry. These plants are lush, but they have never known water. In place of leaves, they grow clusters of blue-grey parchment lobes that rustle like dry paper when the air stirs. Others are thick, segmented ropes of pale green wax, cold to the touch but pliable, which slowly drip a heavy, camphor-scented oil onto the tiles. This oil does not sink into the clay; it pools in shallow, hand-cut channels, remaining there without evaporating, thick and still as honey.

There is no sound of trickling water, no damp breath of moss. The silence is muffled and heavy, as if the air itself were dry flannel. The only noise is the occasional *click-click-shuck* of the fauna.

These creatures are small, dry, and abundant. Legions of porcelain-skinned beetles, the color of salt-glazed stoneware, crawl through the waxy thickets. They do not have mouths for biting, only small, flat spatulas with which they scrape the yellow resin beads that form on the wax-ropes. Among them are larger, six-legged things like dried gourds, their bodies hollow and light, which move with a slow, mechanical patience. They do not run; they lift one leg, wait, and then place it down on the brick with a tiny, hollow *tock*. They have no eyes, but they follow the seams of the tiles with their antennae, tracing the hand of the mason who laid them.

In this place, reality has begun to fold in on itself, quietly and without violence, like paper in an origami press.

Near the center of the courtyard is a small, five-step staircase of white limestone. If you walk down these five steps, you do not find yourself in a cellar; you simply step off the bottom stair onto the very top stair from which you started, looking down at your own heels. If you reach out quickly, you can brush your own shoulder, though the air between you feels dry and vacant.

The shadows here do not behave as shadows should. Where a high wall blocks the light, the darkness that falls is not an absence, but a physical deposit. It is a thin, dry, violet-black velvet that grows on the surface of the brick. If you take a bronze scraper, you can peel this shadow off the wall in long, papery ribbons. Once peeled, the wall beneath is bright and bare, and the ribbon of shadow can be folded up and tucked into a pocket, where it remains warm and slightly fuzzy to the touch.

Time passes here, but it does so without the markers of decay. The waxy plants grow, but they do not rot; when a stem dies, it simply hardens into a sweet-smelling, yellow resin that looks like amber, which the porcelain beetles gradually polish with their bellies until it shines. A broken tile remains broken, its fracture white and fresh forever, unless one of the gourd-creatures slowly, over the course of three years, nudges it back into alignment with its snout.

There is a profound patience to the place. In one corner, a small, hand-built well-head sits. It is made of dark, unglazed clay. If you peer into it, you see no water, nose no moisture. Instead, fifty feet down, there is only a second, identical well-head, looking back up at you. Sometimes, after hours of silence, a tiny, dry seed—winged like a maple key but made of brown parchment—will drift up from the well, float through the warm, dry air of the courtyard, and settle into a joint between two bricks, waiting for a drop of the camphor-oil to find it.
