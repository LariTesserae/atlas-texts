The rift is a vertical wound in the high summit. 

It is narrow—less than twenty paces across at its widest point—but it drops straight down into a lightless chasm that smells of wet granite, cold iron, and old lubricants. Here, at an altitude where the air should be thin and quiet, there is instead a violent, roaring density. The space is a pressurized chamber of shadow and kinetic force.

The walls of the rift are made of black, glass-sharp basalt. This is young stone, recently split by tectonic stress or the brute force of what lies within it. The edges of the rock are razor-like, unworn by time or weather, showing clean, crystalline faces that reflect nothing but the dull, occasional orange of friction-sparks. Down these cold walls, water doesn't flow so much as weep. It is a damp environment, filled with a heavy, clinging condensation that beads on the rock and runs in thin, oily ribbons down the metal plates wedged between the cliffs.

There are no buildings here. There are no ladders, no doors, no catwalks, and no human-scaled design. Yet, the chasm is jammed with colossal, raw industrial machinery. 

Huge, solid-iron piston shafts, three stories tall and coated in black grease, plunge vertically into the dark with a rhythmic, ground-shaking *thrum-shudder, thrum-shudder*. Massive flywheels, their rims jagged with heavy square teeth, spin in the narrow clearances between the rock faces, throwing off a fine, cool mist of grease-tainted water. Thick copper conduits run like giant roots along the basalt, vibrating so intensely that they hum a low, bone-vibrating B-flat. This is not a factory designed for people; it is a naked, autonomous engine, a raw process of extraction or compression that has been bolted directly into the mountain’s throat.

Coexisting with this grinding iron is an explosion of cold-hardy vegetation. In the perpetual dark of the crevice, plants have claimed every square inch of stationary surface. Thick, rubbery liverworts and pale, blue-green ferns drape over the cold metal casings, their fronds glistening with oil-spotted condensation. Dense carpets of moss, four inches deep and sodden as sponges, cushion the vibrating rock ledges. 

This greenery does not sit still. The ecosystem is in a state of hyperactive, turbulent motion. The violent drafts pushed up from the depths by the plunging pistons create a howling wind that whips the ferns into a frenzy. The plants thrash against the cold iron and the sharp stone, their leaves tearing and growing back with equal speed in the nutrient-rich, damp air. Giant, pale tendrils of climbing vine wrap around steam pipes; they are scalded to black rot at one end while aggressively sprouting new, pale shoots at the other. It is a cycle of frantic growth and mechanical destruction, played out in seconds.

The air is cool, almost cold, refrigerated by the extreme altitude and the wet wind, but it is thick with the smell of wet earth and hot, vaporized oil.

There is no light here except for the rarest, briefest occurrences. High above, the jagged ribbon of the sky is permanently choked with gray mountain cloud, rendering the bottom of the cleft pitch black. The only illumination is functional and accidental: the dull, cherry-red glow of an unlubricated bearing rubbing against a sleeve of brass; the blue-white hiss of high-pressure steam escaping a cracked bronze valve; and the shower of orange sparks that erupts when a vibrating iron conduit scrapes against the sharp basalt wall. These brief flashes cast leaping, monstrous shadows of the thrashing ferns across the wet rock.

The sound is a wall. It is not a single noise, but a complex, deafening architecture of friction and power. There is the deep, infrasonic thud of the main pistons that vibrates the teeth in your skull; the high-pitched, metallic shriek of ungreased guide-rails; the wet, rhythmic slapping of wind-whipped leaves against iron plating; and the constant, furious hiss of condensed water boiling off the hot cylinder heads. It is a mechanical storm that never clears.

Nothing living moves here under its own power. There are no birds nesting in the iron lattice, no insects crawling through the thick moss, no worms in the damp silt accumulation. The environment is too violent, too loud, too constantly shaken by kinematic energy for animal life to take hold. There is only the rock, the machine, and the green—all locked in a dark, cold, wet struggle at the top of the world.
