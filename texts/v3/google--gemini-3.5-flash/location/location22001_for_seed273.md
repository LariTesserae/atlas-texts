The world is a lattice of iron, bronze, and rivets that stretches from a bottombox of boiling shadow to an high-altitude grid of bright, white light. There is no stone here that was not cast, no soil that was not gathered as dust in the corners of girders, and no water that did not first travel through a pipe. 

### The Structure and the Light
This is an endless vertical landscape, a mountain range reconstructed out of heavy engineering. Towering bulkheads of sheet steel, vast as tectonic plates, rise miles into the warm air. They are not flat; they are stepped, buttressed, and channeled with millions of maintenance ledges, structural flanges, and projecting gantries. 

Blinding, hot sunlight pours down from above, slicing through the massive cross-braces of the upper levels to paint the lower tiers in alternating stripes of brilliant glare and deep, cool shadow. The air is thick and warm, smelling of boiled oil, wet copper, and the sweet, heavy rot of dense vegetation. Because the air is heated by the sun-baked iron, it rises constantly, carrying with it a fine mist—a perpetual condensation that clings to the undersides of every beam and platform.

### The Hydrology of the Machine
Water does not fall as rain from clouds, but as run-off from the cooling systems of giant, slow-throbbing machinery housed in the higher spires. It travels downward in a complex, accidental river system. It cascades down copper drainage flumes, overflows from concrete settling basins, and drips in heavy, continuous sheets from the lips of overhanging platforms. 

In the joints where massive beams meet, the water pools. These are the "cistern-hollows"—deep, square pockets of stagnant, warm water, sometimes seventy feet across, where the surface is covered in a carpet of thick green duckweed. Below these pools, the water is dark and iron-scented, home to pale, blind crayfish that scurry over the submerged rivet heads.

### The Overgrowth
Nature has occupied the machine. Wherever two plates meet, a seam of rust has formed, and in that rust, seeds have taken root. 

The vegetation is incredibly lush. Waxy, dark-green ivy with vines as thick as ship’s cables wraps around the structural pillars, strangling the iron in a tight, emerald grip. Giant ferns, their fronds ten feet long, sprout horizontally from the vents of dead boilers, swaying slightly in the rising hot drafts. Where the condensation is most constant, the metal is entirely hidden beneath three inches of wet, spongy moss that behaves like a carpet, muffling the footsteps of anyone who climbs the ladders.

Orchids—vividly orange and purple—grow in the damp nooks of the electrical junction boxes, their roots feeding directly on the warm, greasy water that leaks from the seals. There is no bare metal visible that is not either hot to the touch or constantly scraped clean by moving parts. Everything else is green, brown, and dripping.

### The Inhabitants and the Noise
The landscape is far from silent; it is a loud, rhythmic wilderness. 

The background is a deep, structural baritone: the slow, immense *clank-shudder* of heavy counterweights dropping in shafts miles deep, and the steady, lung-like *hiss-sigh* of low-pressure steam vents. This mechanical breathing is so slow and regular that after an hour, the ear ceases to hear it as noise, translating it instead as the pulse of the world itself.

Sharp against this bass are the sounds of life. Tens of thousands of birds—broad-winged swifts with feathers the metallic color of wet slate—nest in the safety of the girder-hollows. Their shrieks and whistles echo down the long, open canyons of steel. Millions of large, bronze-backed beetles clamber over the hot copper pipes, their legs making a dry, clicking sound like falling gravel. 

### The Established Order
This place is old, and it has found its balance. This is not a ruin; it is a weathered ecosystem in equilibrium. The rust is not destroying the structures; it has reached a thickness where it seals the iron beneath from further decay, forming a dark, protective scab. 

The giant gears—some as large as city squares—turn so slowly that their movement is barely perceptible to the eye. A single tooth may take a day to pass a given point, grinding with a deep, groaning vibration that shakes the ferns growing on its hub. The plants have adapted to this slow motion; they grow around the rotating shafts, their vines twisting in spirals that match the direction of the spin.

Everything here is close, dense, and layered. To look out from a gantry is not to look at an empty sky, but to look through a forest of wires, pipes, steam plumes, and hanging gardens toward another iron cliff miles away, where a thousand small waterfalls catch the bright sun as they tumble into the green-choked depths.
