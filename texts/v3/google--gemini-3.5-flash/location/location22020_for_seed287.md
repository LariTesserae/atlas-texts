The air down here is heavy, thick as wool, and hot enough to make the eyes sting. At a depth where one would expect the clean, cold draft of stone passages, there is instead a stifling, stagnant heat, like the interior of an oven that has been left to cool but never quite managed it. 

This is the Caldera-Grotto, a singular subterranean chamber the size of a collapsed cathedral. It is an enclosed basin of weathered basalt, smoothed and scooped out by some forgotten, ancient hydro-geological violent era that has long since subsided into absolute stillness. Now, the space has settled into a deep, domestic antiquity. The stone is rounded, its edges soft, resembling dark, melting wax where it meets the floor.

But the grotto is far from empty. It is crowded to the point of choking with two things that have grown to rely entirely on each other: heavy, iron-bound industrial decay and a violent, sunless jungle.

Overhead, through the thick, rust-scented fog, hangs a canopy of copper-colored ferns and broad, spear-shaped leaves that are almost black. These plants do not need the sun; they are lithophytes and metal-feeders, their roots thick as thighs, wrapping around the massive, sweating iron pipes that crisscross the vault. The vegetation is lush, but it is a dry, fleshy sort of greenness—the leaves are thick, waxy, and cold to the touch, designed to trap every drop of sweat that beads on the ironwork.

Water here is a scarce but persistent tenant. There are no streams or pools, only a constant, rhythmic condensed weeping. The extreme heat of the earth below vaporizes the moisture from deep fissures; this steam rises, strikes the cold iron pipes and the stone ceiling, and falls back down in a slow, heavy, hot drizzle. It collects in the hollows of giant brass valves and the rusted cups of dead generators. Each droplet strikes with a loud, metallic *clink* or a soft, fat *splat* on the broad leaves below. 

The grotto is intensely loud, though it contains no voices. The sound is a mechanical ecosystem. Every few minutes, a massive, half-blind piston somewhere in the floorboards—driven by the deep geothermal pressure—rises with a deafening, metallic shriek that vibrates through the soles of one’s feet. It reaches the apex of its stroke, holds for a long, agonizing moment, and then drops with a dull *thoom* that shakes the dust from the black ferns. Following the impact comes the long, whistling sigh of a pressure valve releasing steam: a dry, throat-clearing *hssssssss* that lasts for water-clock durations, filling the vault with a billowing, parching mist.

There are no animals here. There are no beetles in the rotting leaf-mold, no blind fish in the copper-rimmed condensation pools, no bats hanging from the low-slung iron girders. The silence of life is absolute, which makes the mechanical violence of the machines feel oddly deliberate, like the breathing of a huge, brainless beast.

Scattered through this dense, iron-and-leaf jungle are the marks of human labor—or whatever labor built this place before the plants took it over. Half-shattered brick hearths, their interiors vitrified into black glass by old fires, serve as planters for clumps of pale, trumpet-shaped fungi that glow with a faint, curdled phosphorus. Rusted catwalks, their iron grates partially dissolved by acidic condensation, hang suspended by chains that have grown so thick with rust they resemble red coral. A single, three-story steam boiler sits in the center of the chamber like a fallen tower, its copper skin green with verdigris, its firebox now packed tight with the dry, fibrous tubers of the black ferns.

The light is dim, local, and colored like amber and old blood. There is no day or night, only the faint, steady glow of bioluminescent molds that coat the undersides of the metal plating and the dull, orange light from deep geothermal vents that open and close in the basalt floor like slow, sleepy eyes. This orange light catches the rising steam, turning the air into a shimmering, incandescent curtain that thickens and thins with every lazy breath of the great piston.

Everything here happens on a scale of decades. The rust flakes off the boilers in sheets that take fifty years to accumulate. The black ferns slowly, cell by cell, dissolve the iron bolts to drink the mineral rust, replacing the structural metal with their own calcified roots. The machines clank and whistle, performing some incomprehensible, closed-loop task that produces nothing but heat, noise, and steam, long after the creators have gone. It is a world in equilibrium: a metal-and-leaf furnace, roaring softly in the deep dark, perfectly content to be forgotten.
