The horizon does not exist as a line, but as a curved lattice.

This is a world of bone-dry, high-altitude scaffolding, a planetary-scale scaffold grown entirely of living wood. There is no soil, no sea, and no stone. If you were to drop a plumb line through the gaps in the floor under your feet, it would fall for three miles through cold, brilliant blue air before striking another tier of the network, and then another, down into an unilluminated abyss where the wood is dead, pressurized, and petrified into the world’s core.

We are on the upper terraces, where the air is thin, cool, and violently bright. The light is a sharp, unblinking glare that smells of ozone. It strikes the limbs of the world at steep angles, casting long, sharp-edged shadows across voids that could swallow mountain ranges.

### The Material and the Erosion

The substrate is *Lignum Silex*—silica-saturated timber, cold to the touch and polished to a dull sheen by eternal dust-storms. It is not brown or green, but the color of weathered zinc and dry salt. For thousands of miles, these wooden beams—some as thick as peninsula-chains, others as delicate as copper wire—arch and interlock. 

They have been weathered by centuries of dry friction. The edges of the great trunks are not rough with bark; they have been sanded by dry, airborne crystalline spores into aerodynamic curves, channels, and interlocking sockets. Where two immense limbs rub together in the wind, they do not wear away; they deposit self-sealing layers of gray, lubricating graphite-sap that smells of flint and dry resin.

There is no water. The air is so parched that skin would crack in minutes without protective oils. Instead of moisture, the system runs on atmospheric static and dry, gaseous exchange. The plants do not drink; they inhale the high drafts, extracting nitrogen, sulfur, and airborne particulates through millions of tiny, whistling pores that cover the wood like the pores of a sponge.

### The Organic Engines

Though there are no buildings, no roads, and no scrap of human iron, the landscape is vibrantly, deafeningly industrial. The vegetation has evolved to harness the wild, cold winds of the upper atmosphere through mechanical processes.

To your left, a timber pillar three hundred yards wide rises into the blue. It is hollow. Within its core, a series of natural, wooden piston-valves—grown from fibrous, rubbery membranes—slide up and down in a rhythmic, deafening *thrum-thrum-thrum*. Driven by the pressure differences between the warm lower voids and the cold upper drafts, these giant biological pumps compress dry air, channeling it through whistling pipelines of hollow bamboo-like structures to feed distant, subterranean roots.

Everywhere, there is rotation. Where the wind is strongest, the branches do not end in leaves, but in broad, interlocking fans of stiff, dry reed-fiber. These fans spin. They are organic turbines, connected by thick, twisting vine-shafts to deep internal nodules. As they spin, they hum—a rising and falling scream of friction that vibrates through the very wood beneath your feet. 

The forest is an generator. The friction of the spinning turbines and the dry wind rubbing against the silica-coated needles creates a massive, continuous electrostatic charge. Blue sparks, thick as tree trunks, leap lazily across the wide gaps between the arches, casting brief, artificial noon-glints across the landscape. The plants feed on this electricity, absorbing the lightning through copper-veined leaves that rattle like sheet metal in the gale.

### The Sound and the Movement

The noise is total. It is not a roar of water or a scream of wind, but the clattering, grinding, thudding symphony of a world-sized workshop. 
*   The *clack-clack-clack* of miles-long chains of seed-pods, swaying in the wind like heavy iron cables.
*   The deep, infrasonic groan of the main structural arches adjusting to the centrifugal forces of the planet’s rotation.
*   The dry, high-pitched whistle of wind passing through millions of natural Venturi tubes grown into the sides of the trunks.
*   The occasional, sharp *crack* of an electrical buildup discharging into a lower beam, followed by the dry sizzle of scorched resin.

It is impossible to speak here; the air itself vibrates so intensely that your teeth ache with the resonance of the wood.

### The Sparse Void and the Fauna

Despite the sheer mass of the vegetation, the world feels empty. The density of the structures is low; the gaps between them are vast deserts of sky. You might stand on an arch forty meters wide and see nothing else but three or four similar arches on the distant horizon, crossing the blue vault like the rings of a gas giant. The emptiness is as solid a presence as the wood.

Within these gaps, the fauna exists in a state of constant, buoyant navigation. 

They are dry-bodied, chitinous creatures, clean of sweat or moisture. Some are like giant, hollow kites made of paper-thin skin stretched over frames of light, hollow bone; they hook themselves to the turbine-shafts, riding the wind-currents created by the spinning trees, filtering the dry, dusty air for drifting spores. 

Whip-tailed, insectoid cleaners—shining like polished brass—scuttle along the main arches in long, silent lines. They have no eyes, only sensitive vibration-pads on their feet. They move from joint to joint, clearing away the dry leaf-shards and graphite-dust that accumulates in the natural gears of the wood, keeping the mechanical joints from seizing. They do not bleed when crushed; their interiors are dry, filled with a powdery blue dust that smells of sulfur.

### The Dynamic Churn

Nothing is static. The landscape is in a state of chaotic, self-regulating creation and destruction. 

As the wind direction changes over the continents, the tension in the great vine-cables shifts. If a cable tense to its limit, it does not snap quietly; it explodes with a sound like a cannon-shot, sending splinters the size of galleons spinning through the empty air. Immediately, the surrounding wood reacts to the loss of tension. Glands in the broken stumps swell, excreting rapid-growing, fibrous foam that hardens within hours into a new, pale structural brace, stabilizing the arch before the collective weight can collapse it.

The sky is never clear of debris. Dry, yellow dust—the ground-up remnants of old, collapsed arches—drifts in massive, mile-long clouds, reflecting the brilliant sunlight in curtains of gold. These dust-storms are the soil of the sky; they carry the copper-rich minerals and dry seeds from one end of the scaffold to the other, where they lodge in the joints of the living turbines, sprout without water, and begin the endless work of turning wind into motion, and motion into power.
