### The Silt-Pocket

The space is a sealed pocket of air, roughly the size of a low-ceilinged parlor, buried twelve meters beneath an ancient, waterless plain of pressed calcium. 

#### Terrain and Materials
The boundaries of this chamber are not smooth cave walls but sharp, freshly fractured planes of burnt-orange siltstone and raw, untarnished selenite sheets. The edges are jagged and clean; no moisture has ever run down these walls to dull their crystalline teeth, nor has any draft of wind worn down the sharp ridges where the rock once sheared apart. The floor is covered in a deep, perfectly level layer of dry, flour-like dust—monochromatic silt that preserves the exact impression of anything that touches it, indefinitely. 

In the center of the pocket, the rock projects upward in a sharp, wedge-like shelf. Here, the stone is clean of dust, showing a pale, dry grain like parched bone.

#### The Vegetation
From the narrowest crevices in the siltstone grow the *starch-ferns*. They are incredibly lush, their pale-yellow and ivory fronds crowding the vertical spaces of the chamber in dense, baroque scrolls. Despite their leafy appearance, these plants are entirely dry. They contain no sap, no water, and no juice; their tissues are composed of a rigid, fibrous starch and mineral silica. They feel like heavy, textured paper or thin sheets of dried pasta. 

These plants do not leaf or drop foliage; they exist in a state of eternal growth-arrest. They draw their energy from the ambient warmth of the earth and the high electrostatic charge of the deep silt through long, hair-thin filaments that cling to the stone like dry cobwebs. Their leaves do not sag or wilt; they stand stiffly, dividing the dim air into complex, geometric shadows.

#### Atmosphere and Light
The air inside the pocket is thick, still, and warm, resembling the heat of a baker’s oven just after the fire has been drawn. It smells of toasted rice, dry linseed, and the faintly sweet, dusty scent of warm chalk. 

There is no sun, nor any sky. The only light is a low, dim phosphorescence that leaks from the veins of selenite in the walls. It is a cold, pale-orange glow, barely strong enough to cast a shadow but sufficient to reveal the jagged outlines of the rock and the pale, ghost-like crowns of the starch-ferns. The center of the room remains in deep, velvety twilight.

#### The Built and the Primitive
On the central stone shelf sits a small, low structure—perhaps a hearth or a small altar—constructed of thin, flat shale tiles. These tiles are not held together by mortar but by a dry, dark insect-wax that has hardened into the consistency of horn. Beside this structure lies a single primitive utensil: a flat spatula-spoon carved from the dry, dense rib-bone of some long-dead burrowing creature. Its handle is wrapped in dried plant-fiber, tied with a simple, three-strand braid. 

On the floor nearby, a shallow circular depression has been hollowed out of the raw rock, containing a small pile of ground ocher powder, completely undisturbed. The tools and the hearth are ancient, yet they look as though they were laid down only minutes ago; in this room, there is no dampness to rot the fiber, no wind to scatter the powder, and no chemistry to rust the stone.

#### Fauna and Sound
Though there is no water, the pocket is teeming with dry life. Hundreds of small, hollow-bodied creatures—no larger than hazelnuts—cling to the starch-ferns and crawl across the silt-covered floor. These are the *clackers*. They have no blood or soft inner tissue; their bodies are dry, jointed shells of amber-colored chitin enclosing small, loose pellets of mineral salt.

As they move, their jointed legs tick against the stone, and the salt-pellets rattle inside their bodies. The sound is immense and ceaseless. A deafening, dry clatter fills the small room—a sound like thousands of small wooden dice being shaken violently in leather cups, accompanied by the dry, paper-like rustle of the starch-ferns rubbing together when a lizard-like creature, covered in dry, overlapping scales of white mica, slips through the dense foliage. The noise is monotonous, rhythmic, and incredibly loud, reflecting off the hard, sharp walls with nowhere else to go.

#### The Rule of the World
This place does not participate in time as the surface world does. Because there is no water to facilitate dissolution, decay, or chemical transition, the chamber is in a state of frozen equilibrium. 

Nothing dies here in the traditional sense, because nothing is moist enough to decompose. When a clacker ceases to move, its joints simply lock, and it remains on the leaf where it sat, becoming a permanent ornament of the plant. A footprint left in the silt five centuries ago remains as crisp and sharp-edged as one made five seconds ago. There are no seasons, no drafts of fresh air, no rising or falling of temperature. The rattling of the dry-shelled insects is not a sign of busy, changing life, but a perpetual, mechanical hum—the static noise of an engine that has never been turned off and has no way to run down.
