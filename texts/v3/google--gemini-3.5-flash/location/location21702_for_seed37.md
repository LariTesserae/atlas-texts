This is a room that exists beneath the weight of the steppe, but it does not feel like a cave. It is a pocket of space, perhaps twenty feet across, trapped inside a layer of basalt that was never allowed to finish its formation. 

The first thing that encounters you is the light. It does not leak down from a crack in the ceiling, nor does it rise from any visible phosphorescence. The air itself is bright—a sharp, high-intensity white glare that mimics the noon sun on a salt flat, yet it carries no heat. The temperature is cool, steady, and thin, like the draft beneath an cellar door in early autumn. This cold brilliance reveals a landscape of raw, embryonic geometry. The basalt walls have not been worn down by time; they are sharp as broken bottles, displaying flat, grey-black cleavage planes that look as though they were snapped into existence only minutes ago. There is no dust. There is no soil. The earth here is under construction, waiting for its first true weathering.

Because the rock is un-eroded, there are no slopes, only steps and ledge-faces. In the corners of these steps, water collects. It does not pool in natural hollows; instead, it beads up on the hyper-flat rock faces as if the stone were oiled. These beads of moisture are large, trembling spheres of condensation that migrate slowly across the horizontal planes, pulled by a gravity that seems to tilt at a different angle than the floor. Every few minutes, a sphere of water will slide to the edge of a ledge, roll upward against the vertical face of the next step, and disappear into a microscopic dry fissure. 

Vegetation is a rare, hesitant presence. In three or four places, clinging to the sharpest vertical edges, are small patches of pale, desaturated lichen—not green or orange, but a dry, papery lavender. From these patches grow single, hair-like stems that bear tiny, hollow pods. The pods do not use photosynthesis; they seem to vibrate slightly when the air moves, catching the ambient glare and turning it into a faint, dry rattle that is too high-pitched for human ears to fully register, though it leaves a cold tickle in the back of the throat.

The space is crowded with life, yet it remains intensely quiet. 

Dozens of small, translucent creatures occupy the ledge-world. They have the physical weight of wet tissue paper. Some are shaped like many-legged combs, three inches long, made of a material like flexible glass. They slide over the dry facets of the basalt with a fluid, oil-slick motion, completely silent. Others—small, sphere-like things with hundreds of feathery cilia—drift through the air, but they do not float randomly. They move in straight, aggressive lines, hunting one another. When two collide, there is no sound of impact; one simply folds around the other, the victim’s body turning instantly into a single bead of water that drops onto the stone below, while the predator grows slightly more opaque.

This ecosystem is in constant, busy motion, but it is a silent mime-show. The only sound is the occassional *tlick* of a water droplet snapping as its surface tension breaks against a sharp corner of stone.

In the center of the pocket, sits a small, built artifact. It is a tiny, dry-stone wall, no more than four inches high and a foot long, constructed from micro-thin flakes of the surrounding basalt. It is incredibly primitive—there are no tools used in its shaping, only the natural cleavage-planes of the rock—yet it is undeniably deliberate. It curves in a perfect semi-circle around a dry, shallow depression in the floor. Inside this depression lies a single, clean bone, likely from a small rodent that fell into this place long ago. The bone has been scraped clean of all marrow and marrow-scars, not by teeth, but by something that has peeled the bone-layers back like the leaves of a book, laying them out in a tiny, fan-like pattern on the stone.

Time and space do not hold steady here. 

If you watch a glass-comb creature slide across a ledge, you will notice its shadow does not follow it. The shadow remains behind, cast on the flat rock in sharp silhouette, for several seconds after the creature has moved on. Then, with a sudden, jerky motion, the shadow will rush forward to catch up with its owner. If you reach out a hand toward the miniature stone wall, your fingers will seem to shrink as they approach it, as if the space within the pocket-scale is denser, folding inward on itself to protect its proportions from the intrusion of a larger world.

You are standing in a room that is below the earth, but the ceiling—when you look directly at it—does not look like rock. It is a flat, grey plane of absolute nothingness that reflects your own breath back at you as a thin, silver fog that vanishes before it can touch the stone. There is no exit visible from where you stand; the crack through which you entered has already resolved into a solid, un-fractured basalt seam, leaving you alone in the bright, cool quiet of a world that is still deciding what laws it wishes to follow.
