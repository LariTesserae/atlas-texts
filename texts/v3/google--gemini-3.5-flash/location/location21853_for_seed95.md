To stand at the summit of the Oru-Vatta is to realize that the world has finished its work. 

At this elevation, nearly three miles above the salt-flats that rim the continent’s hem, there are no peaks. The sky does not meet a jagged line of needles; instead, it rests upon a vast, undulating pavement of ancient, pinkish-gray granite that has been planed down by three billion years of wind and light. The mountains have settled into their final, comfortable old age. There are no cliffs left to fall, no loose scree to slide. The land is a sequence of colossal, gentle hummocks—domes so wide and low that their curvature matches the curve of the planet itself. 

The air is thin but surprisingly warm, smelling faintly of toasted starch and old lime. It does not move in gusts, but in a lazy, unidirectional drift that takes three days to cross the plateau. 

Water here is not an active agent. There are no rivers, no springs, no torrents. Instead, the plateau breathes moisture. Every evening, as the sun dips beneath the horizon and the dry stone cools, a thick, warm blanket of mist rises from the invisible lowlands, curling up the long slopes of the domes. It does not rain. Rather, the mist settles on every horizontal surface as a heavy, tepid grease of condensation. By midday, under the steady, normal light of a yellow sun, this moisture has retreated into the thousands of shallow, bowl-like depressions in the granite, forming warm, glass-clear puddles that dry to a sticky glaze before the next evening’s breath arrives.

There is almost no green. The vegetation is sparse to the point of invisibility: dry, papery lichens that look like peeling grey paint on the undersides of the rock lips, and occasional tufts of stiff, wire-like hair that grow in the deepest crevices, colored the dark orange of rusted iron. 

Yet, the landscape is crowded. It is crowded to the point of suffocation, not by plants, but by an overwhelming, silent kingdom of animals.

Because there are no trees to cast shade and no soil to burrow into, the fauna has become the geology. The stone is covered in a living, shifting crust. They are soft-bodied, slow-moving creatures of immense variety but uniform quiet. Millions of flat, disc-shaped organisms, the size of dinner plates and the color of wet slate, lie tiled over the granite like living shingles. They do not walk; they slide on a microscopic film of mucus, moving perhaps three inches a day to follow the shifting margin of the drying puddles. Beneath them, in the endless cracks of the ancient stone, are layered millions of tiny, velvet-skinned cylinders—creatures without eyes or limbs that pulse with a slow, four-second breath, drawing the dampness of the mist through their pores.

The air above the domes is not empty. It is thick with drift-globes: translucent, bladder-like animals the size of pumpkins, filled with warm, biological hydrogen. They have no wings, only fringed skirts of fine, lavender cilia that beat with absolute silently, keeping them suspended twenty feet above the rock. They bump into one another in the light breeze, rebounding with the soft, rubbery *clop* of wet leather, but they make no vocal sound.

Indeed, for all the density of life—thousands of beating hearts and sliding bellies per square yard—the Oru-Vatta is almost perfectly silent. There are no throat-sacks, no chirping legs, no teeth to grind. The only sound is the collective, low-frequency rustle of millions of soft bodies dragging themselves over smooth stone: a sound like dry autumn leaves being stirred by a broom three rooms away. Underneath it is the rhythmic, communal sigh of the drift-globes, a dry, papery whisper that rises and falls with the warming of the day.

Scattered across this immense, undulating plain are the only signs that this place was ever touched by anything other than slow biological drift. They are not ruins in the human sense—there are no arches, no straight walls, no paved squares. Instead, there are the "Hollows." These are clusters of tall, hand-scooped sandstone pillars, standing twelve to fifteen feet high, arranged in loose, concentric rings that stretch for miles across the depressions. 

The pillars contain no tool marks. They appear to have been shaped by hand or tongue from the river-silt of an epoch before the plateau was lifted so high. Each pillar is bored through with hundreds of smooth, spiral pipes, like giant wind-flutes, though they are too clogged now with the nesting bodies of shell-less snails to make any sound when the breeze passes. The creatures use these ancient structures as nurseries. Inside the dark, vertical shafts, protected from the sun, the soft-bodied things pile six deep, their moist hulls glinting in the pale shadows of the sandstone. 

There is no metal here. No glass. No bone-tools or clay pots. If the builders of the pillars had minds, they left no record of their thoughts other than these massive, thumbed heaps of mud, now hardened to the consistency of iron, slowly being licked away by the condensation of ten thousand centuries.

Time on the Oru-Vatta does not run; it pools. The transitions are so gradual they are nearly imperceptible. The sun rises and warms the dome; the slate-colored discs migrate two inches to the east; the drift-globes rise ten feet into the thinning air; the puddles shrink by half an inch. Then the evening comes, the mist rises, the discs migrate two inches back to the west, and the globes sink to rest on the warm granite. 

It is a world that has arrived. Every niche is filled, every surface is occupied, every process has been worn down to its most efficient, slowest gear. It is a warm, dense, silent machine of flesh and wet stone, ticking so slowly that a human lifetime would appear to it as nothing more than a single, bright flash of lightning across an endless, gray afternoon.
