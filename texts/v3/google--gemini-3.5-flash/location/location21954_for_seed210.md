The basin sits high in the throat of the jagged, black-peaked range, a secluded oval amphitheater no larger than a small hamlet. Here, the air is thin, cold, and heavy with a greasy, mineral-scented mist that smells of wet slate, ozone, and cold grease. The peaks surrounding the basin are raw, newly split teeth of basalt; they have no rubble at their bases, no rounded shoulders from centuries of wind. Their edges are as sharp as fresh scalpels, gleaming with a dark, wet gloss under the uniform iron-gray sky. It is a young place, physically—geologically born only moments ago in the grand scale of things—yet it feels anciently, unyieldingly fixed.

There are no ruins here, no roads, no foundations, and no signs of hands having ever shaped a stone. Yet the landscape hums with an immense, non-anthropogenic industrialism. 

Out of the deep, black fractures in the valley floor, cold water weeps continuously. It does not flow in streams or gather in muddy mires. Instead, it rises as a pressurized, micro-fine spray from tiny, perfect apertures in the rock, or hangs suspended in the air in dense, uniform grids of floating spheres that do not fall. These liquid beads, each the size of a grape, hum with a faint electrostatic charge, spinning slowly in place. When the cold mountain draft blows through them, they do not drift; they remain locked in their geometric positions, vibrating against the wind.

The vegetation is lush but sparse, arranged in isolated, grand clusters that rise like altars from the bare basalt floor. Between these groves stretch wide, empty avenues of dark, wet stone, polished to a mirror finish by some process that has left no sediment. Within the groves, the plants are thick and turgid, but they are not made of soft cellulose. There are giant ferns with leaves of ribbed, green-tinned brass that hiss like steam radiators when the cold mist touches them. From the center of these ferns rise tall, fluted pillars resembling ironwood, but their trunks are segmented by rotating, self-lubricating collars of dark bronze. These organic gears turn against one another with a low, wet *thrum-clack, thrum-clack*, drawing up mineral-rich water from some subterranean pressure-cooker. Underfoot, the moss is a thick carpet of copper-hued wire, springy and cold, which crackles with static electricity when stepped upon, discharging tiny blue sparks that smell of burnt sugar.

The fauna is extraordinarily abundant, but it exists in a state of absolute, clockwork stasis. 

Throughout the groves and across the stone lanes, thousands of small, segmented creatures move. There are lizard-like things with bodies of cold, burnished pewter and joints of flexible black rubber. They scurry across the wet basalt, their needles of legs clicking in a rapid, rhythmic cadence that sounds like a room full of busy weavers. Overhead, in the low, dim canopy of copper leaves, sit flocks of birds with wings of thin, overlapping steel leafing. They do not sing; they emit a constant, high-frequency whistle, like steam escaping a pinhole vent. 

But none of this life is choosing its path. 

If you watch a pewter lizard, you will see it march exactly twelve paces to the edge of a wet pool, dip its snout three times into the water, turn at a perfect ninety-degree angle, and march twelve paces back. It has done this for as long as the valley has existed; it will do it forever. The birds do not fly; they tilt their heads in unison every forty seconds, their steel feathers clicking like sliding bolts, then return to their frozen, staring posture. If a falling leaf of tinned brass lands on a lizard, the creature does not flinch; it simply carries the weight on its back until the next loop of its path shakes it off at the exact same point in space.

Time has no depth here; it is a flat, vibrating ring. The water that weeps from the basalt cliffs rises, condenses into the spinning spheres, and is drawn into the copper ferns, only to be hissed back out as steam that instantly cools back into the rock fractures, resetting the loop. 

The sound of the basin is immense, a deafening but entirely unchanging wall of noise. It is the chorus of a massive, automated ironworks that is producing nothing but its own existence. There is the heavy, hydraulic thump of the basalt slabs shifting fractions of a millimeter beneath the earth; the dry, metallic chattering of the pewter lizards; the high, clean whistle of the steel birds; and the deep, watery gurgle of oil-slick pools being sucked down into the rock and regurgitated elsewhere. It does not rise or fall in volume. There are no seasons here, no day or night, only the permanent, shadowless twilight cast by the iron sky.

In this high, cold room of stone and metal, the world has achieved a terrifying, beautiful equilibrium. It is a biological factory that has never seen a designer, a biomechanical marsh that has never known decay. Everything is loud, everything is wet, everything is teeming—and nothing will ever happen here for the first time again.
