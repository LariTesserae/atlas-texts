In this low, sweltering basin, the world is unfinished, yet it has already decided never to change. 

The ground is composed of raw, embryonic stone—bulbous mounds of dark, vesicular basalt and smooth, skin-like grey clay that look as though they were squeezed from the earth’s mantle only moments ago. There are no sharp cliffs, no deep-cut riverbeds, and no gravel plains. The rocks have not yet learned how to crumble; they are rounded, glossy, and slick, resembling giant, half-cooled loaves of dough frozen in the act of rising. The earth here is under construction, a geologic nursery where the crust is perpetually setting but never dries. 

Water does not fall from the sky in storms; instead, it is a permanent inhabitant of the air. A dense, lukewarm condensation clings to everything, turning the atmosphere into a heavy, pearlescent soup. The humidity is absolute. Water weeps continuously from the bellies of the bulbous stone mounds, pooling in wide, shallow depressions that have no outlets. These pool-edges are not muddy banks but carpets of lime-green liverworts and spongy mosses that swallow the foot several inches deep in wet, warm resistance.

Above, the light is filtered through an unchanging canopy of steam and thick, yellow-white cloud. There is no sun, only a sourless, sourceless gloaming that never brightens and never fades. In this perpetual twilight, the heat is heavy and physical, holding the basin at a constant, suffocating temperature that smells of sulfur, decaying starch, and hot sap. 

The vegetation is monstrously lush, though it represents an ancient, non-flowering biology. There are no trees with bark or rings. Instead, giants of the fern family and towering horsetails with hollow, green-jointed stems shoot sixty feet into the heavy air, their ribbed trunks crowded so close together that they touch. Below them, massive, fleshy-leaved plants with the texture of cold rubber overlap in layers, catching the constant drip of condensation and funneling it downward in small, rhythmic splashes. Everything is swollen, turgid, and glossy with moisture. 

Through this dense, green-grey gloom, the fauna exists in an overwhelming, vibrating mass. Indeed, the basin appears to contain more flesh than air. The ground is a crawling carpet of jointed leg and lacquered carapace. Giant, slow-moving millipedes, three feet long and the color of wet obsidian, wind their way through the moss-towers. In the shallow, warm pools, pale, blind amphibians with feather-like external gills hang motionless just beneath the surface, or crawl sluggishly over the submerged clay. 

The air is thick with a low, vibrating drone—the stridulation of millions of heavy-bodied, six-winged insects that cling to the horse-tails, their bodies twitching in time with a collective pulse. There are no high-pitched bird calls or howling predators here; the acoustic space is filled with a dense, textured roar of small noises. It is the sound of wet skin sliding over wet stone, the rhythmic *clicking* of mouthparts, the dull bubbling of warm mud-vents, and a strange, low sighing—like the exhalation of a giant lung—that rises from the deeper, reed-choked hollows where the water is thickest. 

This landscape exists in a state of frozen equilibrium. There are no seasons, no tides, and no wind to stir the giant fronds. The basalt mounds do not erode; the rain does not wash the clay away; the plants do not die back in winter. The cycle of birth and decay has been compressed into a single, continuous, hyper-dense moment that repeats itself infinitely. It is a regional cradle, miles across, where life is trap-nested in its own wet heat, thriving in a dense, primeval twilight that has never known a tool, a path, or a change in the weather.
