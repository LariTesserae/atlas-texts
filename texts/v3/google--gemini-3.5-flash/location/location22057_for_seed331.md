This is a shallow basin, no larger than a village square, nestled on the floor of a vast, flat valley where the mountains died three billion years ago. Here, the earth has finished its work; there are no jagged cliffs or sharp stones left. The edges of the basin are low, rolling mounds of dark, iron-sleek basalt that have been worn down by time and water into smooth, muscular curves, resembling giant limbs resting beneath a blanket of moss. 

The air is thick, warm, and constantly saturated. It does not rain in the traditional sense; instead, the water is close, suspended in a bright, pearlescent mist that clings to the skin like a warm silk sleeve. The basin is a sponge. Every step on the dense, elastic carpet of gold-and-red root-moss coaxes up small pools of clear, lukewarm water that immediately drain back down when weight is lifted. Along the smooth basalt terraces, water does not flow in streams but seeps through the stone itself, weeping in thin, even sheets that glisten under an overhead light so bright and diffused it seems to have no single source. 

Though the basin is small, there is a profound sense of breathing room within it. Only four massive botanical structures stand here, widely spaced, allowing the bright, humid air to circulate between them. These are bellow-palms—thick, fibrous towers that slowly, almost imperceptibly, expand and contract. Their trunks are wound with pale green creepers, and their crowns consist of wide, spade-shaped leaves, each three yards across, that catch the condensing mist and channel it down to their bulbous roots in rhythmic, heavy drips. Between these giant sentinels, the ground is kept clear, covered only by the low Moss-carpet and tiny, star-shaped succulents that bloom and wither in the span of an afternoon.

Sunk into this ancient floor are the remnants of something built, though they do not look like ruins. Three rectangular slabs of a seamless, matte-black material sit at precise angles to one another. They are not masonry; they are solid blocks of an artificial element that repels water at a molecular level. Mist cannot settle on them; pools cannot form nearby. Each slab projects a localized, dry-zone—a perfect dome of warm, dry air about twelve feet across. Within these zones, the air is perfectly still, and the ground is dry, pale dust. Occasional glints of gold-colored dust hover in these dry pockets, moving in complex, geometric spirals. These are not spores, but nano-scale mechanisms, still executing some forgotten, planetary-maintenance routine with quiet, immortal diligence.

The basin is teeming with life, yet it is almost entirely silent. 

The fauna does not crawl or walk; it drifts, glides, and pulses. Thousands of delicate, gossamer ribbons—thin as soap bubbles and long as ribbon—float through the warm mist. They are a translucent amber color, catching the bright light as they ride the thermal currents created by the dry-zones below. They make no sound. Sleek, six-legged creatures with skin like polished wet leather move across the wet basalt. They have no eyes; instead, their heads terminate in wide, pale-pink fans that twitch to read the vibrations of the humid air. When they run, their padded feet make no more noise than dew falling on grass. 

In the shallow, crystal-clear pools that gather at the base of the bellow-palms, tiny, needle-thin water-skaters skate with impossible speed. They do not break the surface tension, nor do they leave ripples; they seem to exist a fraction of a millimeter above the liquid, moving in silent, jerky trajectories like sparks from a fire. Now and then, a larger, soft-bodied organism—shaped like a flattened bell and glowing with a faint, internal violet light—floats up from the moss, hovers for a few moments, and then sinks back down, absorbing nutrients from the wet peat.

If you close your eyes, the world disappears into a gentle, heavy hush. There are no birds to cry, no frogs to croak, no wind to rattle the heavy, moisture-laden leaves. The only sounds are the occasional, deep *splut* of a heavy water droplet falling from a palm leaf into a pool, and the low, vibrational thrum that rises from the matte-black slabs—a sound felt in the bones of the feet rather than heard by the ear, like the purr of a sleeping machine that has forgotten its name but remembers its purpose. 

Everything here is active, but nothing is hurried. The biological pace is steady and cyclic. A plant-ribbon falls, dies, and is dissolved by the wet moss within minutes; a leather-skinned glider consumes a water-skater without a struggle; the gold dust in the dry-zones shifts its pattern to align with some invisible cosmic transit. It is a landscape at the end of history, settled into a perfect, warm, self-sustaining loop, where even the ruins have become as permanent as the stone.
