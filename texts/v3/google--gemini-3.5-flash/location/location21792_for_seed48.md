The air here is a heavy, enveloping weight, thick with the smell of mineral salts, warm wet iron, and the faintly sweet odor of endless, teeming life. It is hot—not with the sharp bite of a desert sun, but with the suffocating, motionless heat of a public bathhouse. A perpetual grey mist hangs over the landscape, condensing on every vertical surface to run down in endless, weeping runnels.

This is a low-lying basin, a vast, flat delta-like depression that stretches out toward a horizon obscured by amber-tinted steam. Across this expanse, the ground is not soil or mud, but a colossal, weathered pavement of poured grey basalt-concrete and dark, flat slabs of slate. The scale is immense, yet strangely hollow. Massive, cubic structures—the size of cathedral foundations or municipal water-treatment works—rise from the flat ground, spaced far apart from one another. Kilometers of empty, wet slate plazas lie between these heavy, silent structures, giving the impression of a city built for giants, or a vast industrial complex whose staff finished their shifts centuries ago.

There is no green. Not a single blade of grass struggles through the seams of the masonry; there are no weeds, no mosses, no vines. The landscape is entirely mineral, metal, and water. Instead of plants, the wet concrete and the interiors of the wide, shallow canals are coated in thick, jelly-like mats of grey, ochre, and white. These are massive bacterial and microbial colonies, growing in the warm, mineral-rich runoff that emerges from thousands of low pipe-mouths and weeping seams in the stone. 

Water is everywhere, flowing in a highly organized, heavily engineered system of shallow channels, stepped weirs, and deep, slow-moving culverts. The water is warm, heated from somewhere deep below the concrete floor, occasionally bubbling up through cast-iron grates with a quiet, regular hiss of steam. 

Although the human builders are gone, their machines still quietly function. Far across the grey flat, a monumental iron flywheel, sixty feet across, turns with a slow, agonizing dignity, half-submerged in a steaming canal. Its movement is so slow it is almost imperceptible, but every few minutes, a deep, copper-toned *clack-shhh* echoes through the mist as a automatic sluice gate shifts, redirecting a warm torrent of water from one basin to another. In the distance, the low, rhythmic *thump... thump... thump* of a steam-driven engine reverberates through the soles of your feet, a vibration rather than a sound, steady as a dying whale's heartbeat.

And in this warm, wet, plantless monument of iron and stone, animal life has exploded.

The sheer volume of fauna is staggering, though it behaves with a strange, quiet discipline. Because there is no foliage to hide in, the creatures occupy every wet surface, every iron pipe, and every dark corner of the massive buildings. 

In the warm canals and the vast, shallow sheets of water that skim across the concrete plazas, millions of blind, pale-grey crayfish and fingernail-sized crabs scuttle in shifting, silent carpets. They graze perpetually on the slippery bacterial mats. The sound of their movement is a constant, soft *click-tick-fric*—like a handful of dry rice falling repeatedly onto stone. 

On the vertical walls of the massive concrete blocks, billions of wet-skinned, pale snails cling in shimmering, overlapping tile patterns, their grey shells worn smooth by the constant trickle of condensing water. 

Above, in the warm, yellow-grey gloom, the air is thick with clouds of tiny, non-biting midges. They do not buzz; they hum in a register so low it is felt in the inner ear rather than heard. These clouds are hunted by thousands of small, leathery-skinned, eyeless flying creatures—similar to micro-bats but with more fluid, bird-like wings—that drift through the mist like scraps of charred paper, scooping up insects without a sound.

There are larger predators, too. In the deeper, slow-moving aqueducts, thick-bodied, three-foot-long salamanders with external, feathery red gills lie motionless on the silt-free concrete bottom. They wait for the blind crayfish to stumble into their path, their pale, rubbery skin glistening under the low, diffuse light. Occasionally, one will rise to the surface with a soft, wet *plop* to gulp the humid air before sinking back down.

The landscape is in constant, slow motion. The rise and fall of the water levels, dictated by the ancient, automated industrial plumbing, creates artificial tides. When a sluice gate groans open, draining a three-acre plaza into a subterranean reservoir, millions of the small crabs move in unison, scuttling backward into the remaining shallow pools to avoid drying out in the hot air. When the warm water returns, rising through the floor vents in a bubbling froth, they fan out again in a great, silent wave.

Everything here is settled into its own balance. The iron is coated in a dark, stable patina of rust that no longer flakes, protected by the greasy microbial slime. The concrete faces have been smoothed by thousands of years of running water, their hard edges rounded into river-stone shapes. The animals do not fight the infrastructure; they use it. The pipes are their burrows; the canals are their feeding grounds; the warm iron flues are their nests.

It is a world that is quiet, wet, and incredibly busy. It is an empty city that is nonetheless crowded to the margins, a place where the line between the machine and the swamp has been worn away by the slow, warm dripping of a million years.
