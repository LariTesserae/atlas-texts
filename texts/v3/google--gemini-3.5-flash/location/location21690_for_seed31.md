High on the shoulder of a young mountain range, where the rifts are still sharp-edged and the rock faces have not yet been smoothed by the centuries, sits a sprawling settlement of stone and timber. 

The air here is warm, pressurized by elevation, and thick with a quiet, ever-present moisture. It is a world that has just finished raining, or perhaps is always about to. A thin, luminous mist hangs suspended in the gorges below, glowing under a steady, amber-tinted daylight that does not seem to change its angle. The light is bright and constant, casting short, soft shadows that never lengthen.

Everything here wet-shines. 

### The Terrain and the Water

The geology is primeval and blocky. Great plates of blue-black basalt thrust upward at sharp angles, their fractures clean and un-weathered, as if the ground were broken only yesterday. Yet, from every seam and fissure in these young cliffs, water weeps. It does not gush or roar; it seeps in a million silver threads, glazing the dark stone and gatherng in natural chalices.

Because the erosion is still young, there are no deep river valleys. Instead, the water moves across the high plateaus in wide, shallow sheets, filling thousands of stepped basins before trickling down to the next level. The water is perfectly clear, cold to the touch but warming quickly as it spreads over the broad, sun-baked flagstones of the town. 

There is an equilibrium here that defies gravity: the water flows, yet nothing ever dries up, and nothing ever floods. The level in every stone basin remains exactly one finger-breadth below the lip, poised in a permanent, shimmering stillness.

### The Built Environment

Spanning several square miles of these wet terraces is an organized, sprawling settlement. It is built entirely of the local basalt and massive, dark timbers, constructed with an obvious, painstaking hand-craft. There are no signs of iron-work or mortar. Instead, the buildings are made of giant, interlocking stones, ground down by hand until they fit together with seismic weight. 

The architecture is layered and vertical. Great stepped plazas, some the size of fields, connect different levels of the town by way of wide, shallow ramps. The houses are square and squat, built to withstand the weight of the damp air, topped with heavy roofs made of giant, overlapping leaves of a waxy green hue, held down by smoothed river boulders.

Between the buildings run long, hollowed-out log flumes—primitive aqueducts—that carry water from the weeping rock faces into the heart of the town. The design is highly organized, suggesting an urban complexity, yet the technology is elemental: stone, wood, fiber-ropes, and gravity. There are no chimneys, no wheels, and no metal gates.

### The Vegetation

The plant life is lush but orderly, growing in deliberate, scattered pockets where soil has gathered in the rock basins. There are no tangles of vine or choking underbrush. Instead, there are massive, fan-leafed ferns that stand as tall as trees, their fronds glistening with condensed water that grows too heavy and slides off in fat drops. 

Fleshy, lime-green moss carpets the steps of the plazas, soft as wool and thick enough to muffle the footsteps of anyone walking upon it. In the quiet canals, broad-leafed lilies with thick, rubbery stems float in stasis, their white blossoms open to the sky, never wilting and never dropping their petals. The vegetation does not seem to be actively growing or decaying; it exists in a state of suspended, vibrant health.

### Silence and the Void

There is a profound, architectural silence here. 

There are no animals. No birds wheel in the pale sky; no fish ripple the surfaces of the stone basins; no insects hum among the giant ferns. The silence is not dead or menacing, but rather spacious. It is filled only by the music of water:
*   The low, hollow gurgle of the log aqueducts.
*   The rhythmic, heavy *plip-plop* of condensation falling from the roof-leaves into the courtyards.
*   The faint, continuous hiss of steam rising where the warm sun hits the wet, dark basalt.

The town is laid out for thousands, yet it feels empty—not abandoned, but simply waiting. The doors of the timber houses stand open to the damp, warm breeze. Inside, the floors of beaten clay are clean, and stone bowls sit on low wooden tables, filled with clear water that does not evaporate. 

Time does not seem to run forwards here. The sun remains fixed in its warm, bright afternoon; the water continues its slow, non-destructive weep; and the young, sharp stones of the mountains remain dark and wet, preserved forever in the moment after the storm.
