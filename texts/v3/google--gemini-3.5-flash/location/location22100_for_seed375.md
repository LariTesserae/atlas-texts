In a shallow, low-lying basin no larger than a small hamlet, a city is growing. It has no architects, no workmen, and no tools. 

The air here is cold and absolutely dry. There is no moisture to cling to the skin, no dew to settle on the sharp-crested ridges, and no vapor to soften the light. The basin is filled instead with a light, microscopic drift of grey-blue wax-dust that blankets the flat ground like dry snow but refuses to clump. It sits in the channels and lanes, a perfectly level floor of powder, so dry that it offers no resistance when disturbed, flowing like liquid helium but without a sound.

Rising out of this waxy floor are the structures. They are built of a semi-translucent, lavender-grey mineral that resembles a cross between volcanic obsidian and dry, compressed bone. The material grows in strict, geometric habits: hollow hexagons, sheer vertical planes, and impossibly thin, cantilevered spans that cross from one cluster to another like highway overpasses. 

Because the landscape is young and unweathered by wind or rain, these structures are razor-sharp. The edges of the hexagonal towers are clean, crystalline fractures that have never known the softening effect of water or friction. There are no chips, no rounded corners, and no detritus of decay. Everything is brand new, recently emerged from the cold crust below.

The layout is undeniably urban. It mimics a dense, multi-layered town with a terrifyingly logical infrastructure. There are clear, wide avenues of black carbon-sand that wind between the blocks; there are elevated pedestrian-like walkways that wind around the towers, and deep, square ventilation shafts that plunge straight down into the silent earth. But it is empty. There is an enormous amount of space between these towering structures, a hollow, clean-swept feeling as if a population of millions was expected but never arrived.

The light is a constant, dim twilight. It does not come from a sun, but from a faint, cold phosphorescence inherent to the lavender-grey mineral itself. The towers glow with a muted, inner light, casting long, sharp-edged shadows across the waxy grey lanes.

Though there is no wind to speak of, the basin is intensely active. The landscape is in a state of rapid, silent metamorphosis. The mineral towers are growing in real-time. If you watch a single cantilevered bridge for more than a few minutes, you can see its tip extending, molecule by molecule, a pale violet crust creeping across the void toward an opposing tower. Beneath the floor, the earth shifts with an organic, hydraulic pressure. Plates of the mineral slide over one another with a slow, glacial momentum. 

Yet, there is no sound. The silence is absolute, a thick, heavy vacuum-like hush that swallows all acoustics. The porous waxy dust on the ground acts as a perfect sound-absorber; any noise that might be made by the scraping of these massive stone plates is instantly muffled and killed at the source. If a stone plate fractures and falls, it strikes the waxy floor and sinks into it without a whisper, the event communicated only as a faint tremor through the soles of your feet.

Vegetation is sparse, appearing only as dry, black, hair-like fibers that sprout from the seams where the mineral plates meet. These fibers do not use photosynthesis, nor do they draw water. Instead, they are electrostatic; they twitch and bend toward any object that carries a charge, harvesting the static electricity generated by the sliding of the dry plates beneath the basin. They look like patches of stiff black wool, shivering silently in the dim lavender light.

Fauna is rarer still. Every few hours, a creature might emerge from one of the deep, square ventilation shafts. These are long, multi-jointed things, armored in the same dry, lavender-grey chitin as the towers. They have no eyes and no mouths, moving on dozens of needle-thin legs that touch the waxy floor with absolute, soundless precision. They do not hunt or graze; instead, they appear to be part of the city’s self-maintenance. They walk the high, cantilevered spans, using long, whip-like antennae to sweep away the waxy dust that accumulates on the growing edges of the crystal towers, ensuring the lines remain clean, sharp, and perfectly formed. Once their section is swept, they slip back into the dark shafts, leaving the empty, silent streets to continue their quiet, geometric growth.
