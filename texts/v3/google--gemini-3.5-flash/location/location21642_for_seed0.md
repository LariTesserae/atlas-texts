The earth here has only recently stopped tearing itself apart. The basin is a young tectonic wound, a vast rift valley whose edges are composed of sheer, unweathered basalt as sharp as obsidian scalpels. There are no gentle slopes or rounded hills; every drop is a vertical plunge, every ridge is a jagged spine of dark, glassy stone that has not yet known the smoothing hand of deep geological time.

Although it is warm—a heavy, tropical heat that clings to the skin like wet velvet—there are no rivers or wide lakes. Instead, the moisture is a phantom, a constant condensation that swells in the absolute darkness. The air is so thick with humidity that the rock faces sweat, their black surfaces gleaming with a thin, sliding film of liquid. Water collects only in the deepest wrinkles of the stone—small, cold pockets of sweet distillation that gather in the cups of volcanic bowls, instantly sought after and guarded by the things that move in the dark.

Overhead, and indeed filling every cubic foot of the rift, is an almost suffocating density of life. This is not a forest of trees, but an vertical web of fleshy, rootless flora that does not require sunlight to survive. Blackish-purple vines, thick as thighs and soft to the touch, drape from the high rim of the canyon down into the abyss, knitting together to form a false floor dozens of feet above the actual stone basin. They grow by heat and moisture, their surfaces covered in a velvet pile that drinks the sweating air. Underneath this colossal, self-woven ceiling, the darkness is complete, riven only by occasional, rhythmic pulses of cold, pale-orange bioluminescence from huge, globular bladder-plants that hang like swollen fruit.

Because the rock is so jagged and young and the plants so aggressive, the landscape is in a state of chaotic, hyperactive transformation. There is no stillness. Vines crawl several inches an hour, their tendrils dryly scraping against the basalt as they seek out seams of minerals. When a shelf of rock calves off from the canyon wall—an event that happens frequently with a sharp, explosive crack—the vegetation pours into the new void within days, a slow-motion green avalanche.

Underneath the canopy, the sound is not a background element; it is an physical weight. It is a deafening, non-stop din that vibrates the very marrow of the bones. It is the collective voice of a fauna population so dense that the air seems to consist more of wings, chitin, and breath than of gas. Millions of blind, multi-jointed flyers—about the size of a hand—throng the spaces between the vines, their hard wings clicking together like thousands of castanets. Below them, on the stone floor, are things that hum; a low, sub-bass drone that oscillates so intensely it causes the condensation on the rock faces to dance in concentric rings. 

There are no paths, no clearings, and nothing built. No stone has been laid upon another, nor has any iron been smelted. Yet, the chaos is not entirely mindless. Down in the narrow, dark galleries between the basalt teeth, where the moisture pools, live small, grey, social creatures with long, multi-jointed arms and flat, hard-skinned fingers. They do not build shelters—the dense, self-healing canopy of vines is roof enough—but they are tool-users of a strange, frenetic sort. 

At any moment, one can hear the sharp, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of these creatures at work. They find the sharpest fragments of the volcanic glass where the canyon walls have recently sheared, using them as hand-axes to score the thick vines, tapping them for the sticky, sweet sap within. They do not store these tools; they use them once, drop them back into the dark crevices, and move on in their restless, chattering family bands. Some have been observed wedging flat splinters of basalt into the rock cracks to create simple, spring-loaded traps that snap shut on the heavy, blind beetles that crawl through the mulch, but these are crude, temporary things, abandoned to the rot as soon as they have caught a single meal.

In this place, life is cheap, fast, and incredibly loud. A plant will sprout from the carcass of a fallen flyer, grow to the size of a man in a single afternoon, be stripped to its stringy skeleton by a passing carpet of nocturnal larvae, and dissolve into a greasy black smear before the next cycle of condensation begins. The ground is never the same from one hour to the next; it is a shifting, boiling soup of black stone, green pulp, and wet shell, vibrating to the relentless, roaring pulse of a young world that has no time for quiet.
