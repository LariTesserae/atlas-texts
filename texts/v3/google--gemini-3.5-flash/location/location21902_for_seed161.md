### The Saddle of the Warm Silt

The mountains here do not rise in sharp teeth; they have long since lost their youth to the wind. At this extreme elevation, five miles above the distant, dust-choked lowlands, the peaks are immense, rounded domes of lilac-and-grey granite, polished by epochs of blowing grit until they resemble the smooth, humped backs of sleeping beasts. Between two of these giant shoulders lies the Saddle: a hanging terrace of rock no wider than a grand cathedral, suspended over a mile-deep drop-off that disappears down into a warm, amber-colored haze.

Here, there is no hum of humidity, no damp earth, no rain. Water does not exist in this altitude; the air is thin, parched, and dry as bone. Yet the Saddle is not barren. It is thick with a specialized, heavy life that has learned to live without wetness. 

#### The Dense Green-Gold

Every vertical rock face, every roof, and every narrow alleyway in the Saddle is choked with a lush, dry vegetation. It is a flora of tallow-woods, wax-leafed vines, and thick, carpet-like layers of silvery-grey dry-moss. The plants do not draw water from the soil; instead, they have deep, tap-root networks that reach down into ancient geothermal fissures to draw up warm, heavy hydrocarbons and mineral oils. The leaves are thick and stiff as boiled leather, coated in a fragrant, yellowish sheen of natural grease that prevents them from drying out in the wind. 

This vegetation grows in heavy, stacked layers. Great curtains of jointed, hollow bamboo-like reeds hang from the higher ledges, clattering against the stone like bundles of dry arrows when the wind rises. The smell is thick, warm, and sweet—a mixture of hot cedar, dry lavender, and the faint, greasy scent of paraffin.

#### The Urban Hive

Woven directly into this dry-moss jungle is a settlement so compact it resembles an insect colony. Because the cliff-shelf is small, the inhabitants have built upward and inward. Houses of hand-chiseled yellow sandstone and sun-baked silt blocks are stacked five or six deep against the curve of the mountain. One man’s flat roof is the courtyard of the family above him; the chimneys of the lower dwellings rise through the living rooms of the upper tiers, heating the stones from within.

The technology of this place is simple, heavy, and quiet. There are no ironworks, no engines, no wheels. Everything is constructed of timber hauled from the dry scrubforests of the lower slopes, bound together with thick cords spun from the inner bark of the wax-vines. Where metal is used, it is cold-hammered copper, beaten into crude plates to line the fire-hearths or shaped into heavy, blunt knives. 

Water is an alien concept here; the people do not drink, nor do they wash with liquid. Instead, they scrape their skin with curved bone knives and dust themselves with a fine, chalky pink silt that binds with body oils and falls away, carrying dirt with it. They drink the thick, sweet sap pressed from the core of the wax-reeds—a heavy, pale syrup that smells of almonds and leaves a yellow dust on the lips.

#### The Atmosphere of the Saddle

The light is a permanent, muffled twilight. The sky is never blue; at this height, the atmosphere is filled with a suspension of fine, glittering mineral dust that filters the sun into a broad, soft dome of copper-colored light. There are no clouds, only this warm, dry smog that keeps the temperature of the canyon steady and balmy, like the air inside a well-used oven.

Because the rock walls are curved and the houses are packed so closely together, the Saddle acts as a massive acoustic chamber. The space is incredibly loud. The sound is not of machines, but of life in close quarters:
*   The dry, paper-like rustling of millions of wax-leaves brushing against stone.
*   The constant, rhythmic *clink-clank* of small, blue-tailed ground lizards that live in the walls, clicking their tongue-scales against the sandstone to find their mates.
*   The high, metallic whistle of mountain swifts that nest by the thousands in the thatch of the roofs, swooping through the narrow gaps between houses to catch the heavy-bodied oil-flies.
*   The low, resonant drone of the wind passing over the empty mouths of the chasm below, sounding like a finger rubbed over the rim of an immense earthenware jar.

Everything is in motion, but it is a slow, cyclical motion. The people do not hurry; the thin air does not allow for sudden exertion. They move with a deliberate, soft-footed grace along the stone stairways, their wool-wrapped feet making no more sound than dry leaves. 

#### The World’s End

Time in the Saddle does not seem to pass so much as it pool. The stone stairs are worn into deep, smooth basins by the passage of centuries of feet, yet the shape of the town remains exactly the same as it was ten generations ago. There is no room to grow, and no reason to change. The mountains have already finished their erosion; they have reached their final, stable, rounded forms. 

At the edge of the lowest terrace, where the masonry of the outer houses merges seamlessly with the vertical drop of the cliff, one can look down. There is nothing below but the vast, pale floor of the world—a flat, endless desert of white salt and yellow dust, shimmering through the warm, quiet haze, miles beneath this crowded, noisy pocket of life.
