High on the shoulder of an unnamed upthrust peak, where the atmosphere is thin but remarkably dense with moisture, lies a small, sharp-edged sanctuary no larger than a village square. 

The ground underfoot is geological infancy itself: raw obsidian and dark, glassy basalt, fractured so recently that the stone still holds the razor-sharp corners of its creation. There are no scree fields or loose gravel here; the shelf is a clean, jagged terrace cleaved from the mountain-side, untouched by the slow rounding of centuries of wind. 

Yet it is sweltering. Powerful geothermal heat radiates from the black rock, warming the soles of the feet and turning the pervasive moisture into a thick, languid steam. The air is heavy and clean, carrying the scent of rain, hot stone, and a faint, sweet smell like boiling sap. 

A peculiar physics rules this small basin, governed by three slender, dark-grey rods of an unknown, matte material that rise from the basalt like needle-thin chimneys. They do not smoke; instead, they hum at a frequency that is felt in the marrow rather than heard. Because of them, the abundant water does not merely run down the steep slopes. Instead, it pools in mid-air. 

Several oval bodies of water—ranging from the size of a basin to the length of a rowboat—hover two to three feet above the black rock, suspended by invisible, localized gravitational wells. These high-altitude tide pools do not fall or splash; they wobble and undulate gently, like great blobs of liquid mercury, holding their shapes through some artificial, heightened surface tension. The water within them is perfectly clear, heated by the rising steam below, and constantly turning over in slow, convection-driven currents.

Though the stone between these hovering pools is dry and hot, life has colonized the water itself. From the floating bodies of liquid, clusters of lush, hyper-tropical vegetation burst forth. Deep-green ferns with fleshy, translucent fronds dangle their roots into the open air beneath the floating pools, drawing water from the suspended reservoirs and discarding the excess as a steady, warm drizzle that falls back to the basalt and instantly evaporates into steam. These plant islands are incredibly rich—crowded with thick-leaved orchids that bloom in shades of oily violet and waxy, emerald-colored mosses—but they are highly self-contained. Between these floating gardens are wide, stark intervals of empty, gleaming black glass.

The built elements of the site are minimal and bafflingly advanced. Near the center of the terrace, a path of flat, hexagonal wafers made of a white, ceramic-like compound floats an inch above the jagged ground, providing a safe walkway across the razor-sharp stone. The wafers adjust their height slightly as they are stepped on, absorbing the weight with a soft, pneumatic sigh. At the edge of the shelf, overlooking a dizzying drop into a sea of clouds far below, is a single low bench made of the same white material. It is cool to the touch, despite the ambient heat.

Fauna is nearly non-existent in this hot, quiet heights. Occasionally, a *translucent skimmer*—an organism resembling a four-winged dragonfly but made of a flexible, glass-like cartilage—darts from one floating pool to another. They do not fly so much as hop along the boundary lines of the gravitational fields, their wings clicking softly like dry leaves when they change direction. They feed on the microscopic algae that thrives in the warm, levitating water.

The soundscape is dominated by silence, punctuated only by the wet *plink* of condensation dripping from the floating ferns onto the hot rock below, where it vanishes with a tiny, sharp hiss. Above, the sun is a bright, high-altitude disk, its light refracting through the drifting steam and the edges of the floating water-spheres, throwing fragments of pale rainbow across the black, un-weathered stone.

Every few minutes, the system breathes. The three rods emit a slightly deeper tone; the floating pools tilt by a few degrees; the warm drizzle shifts its pattern, and the orchids momentarily close their petals as the local pressure drops, only to open them again as the air settles back into its hot, damp equilibrium. It is a highly engineered, self-sustaining micro-climate—a tiny, private room of glass, water, and steam, suspended between the raw rock of the mountain and the empty sky.
