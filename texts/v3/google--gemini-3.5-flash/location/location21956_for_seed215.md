This is a young plateau, raised so high into the thin, cold upper atmosphere that the sky above it is a deep, hard violet, even at noon.

The ground here is raw. It has not yet learned how to decay. There are no rounded pebbles, no soft banks of dirt, no fields of grey sand. Instead, the terrain is an endless basin of dark, unweathered basalt and glassy, deep-green obsidian, laid down in massive, flat sheets that fractured only recently in geological time. The edges of these fractures are razor-sharp, pristine, and clean. Because there has been no time for wind or water to carve channels, the water does not flow in rivers. Instead, it carpets the flat stone in immense, shallow sheets—a high-altitude marsh that reflects the sky like a shattered mirror.

The air is cold—a sharp, crisp chill that preserves everything it touches—but it is also incredibly wet. Clouds do not hang in the sky here; they drift across the ground, snagging on the sharp ridges of stone. The atmosphere is in a constant state of condensation. Every vertical surface is weeping. Water accumulates on the black rock faces, bead by bead, until it slides down in silent, shivering ribbons.

Across this vast, cold shelf, there is immense distance. You can look forty miles in any direction and see nothing but the glistening obsidian floor, the shallow grey water sheets, and the low, sweeping mist. But when you do reach a point of presence, it is colossal.

Every fifty or sixty miles, rising out of the wet stone like mountains of glass and white composite, are the Hubs. These are urban settlements of staggering scale, though they do not look like cities of the past. There are no chimneys, no roads, no power grids. Instead, these structures—some of many miles wide—hover slightly. The lowest levels of these massive white districts do not touch the wet basalt; they float on silent, invisible fields a few meters above the water, allowing the shallow marshes to pass beneath them undisturbed. 

The technology that sustains them is quiet, clean, and operates on principles that resemble a refinement of gravity itself. Thin, pencil-bright lines of coherent light occasionally stitch between the high spires of neighboring hubs, miles apart, transferring energy or data in silent, instantaneous pulses. There are no vehicles with wheels or wings; instead, long, slender needles of matte-black metal drift through the mist between the city-structures, moving with a liquid, frictionless grace, completely silent except for the faint, brief hiss of displaced air as they pass.

Between these monumental, scattered hubs, the landscape belongs to the fauna. Despite the lack of soil and the bitter cold, the plateau is teeming with life.

Herds of great, six-legged grazers—their bodies covered in thick, pale-blue wool that sheds the condensation like oil—move slowly through the shallow waters. They have broad, splayed hooves designed to grip the slippery, unweathered glass of the basalt floor. They feed on the only vegetation available: a tough, rubbery, bioluminescent lichen that grows in thin, circular rings on the wet stone, glowing with a faint, cold mint-green light. 

Above them, the sky is never empty. Huge, flat-bodied flyers with translucent, sail-like wings drift on the cold thermals. They do not flap; they adjust their vast, glassy sails to catch the high-altitude drafts, casting long, silent shadows across the mirrored plains below. In the water sheets themselves, small, armored organisms with jointed shells dart through the cold shallows, feeding on the mineral-rich dust shedding off the newly minted rock.

It is a world of profound silence, but it is not a dead silence. It is the muffled, damp quiet of an open-air cathedral. The sounds are small, isolated, and clear: the *tick-tick-tick* of cooling stone, the wet, heavy splashing of a blue-furred herd three miles away, the sudden, musical *chime* of a hovering transit vessel adjusting its altitude, and the endless, rhythmic dripping of water off a billion sharp edges. 

Everything here is active, but nothing is hurried. The clouds roll in from the rim of the plateau, dump their moisture into the flat basins, and are swept away by the high winds, only for the cycle to begin again. The great machines in the distant white cities pulse with a deep, sub-audible vibration that you feel in your teeth rather than hear with your ears—a quiet, planetary hum that suggests the world is still being built, block by block, under the watchful eyes of things that do not need to hurry.
