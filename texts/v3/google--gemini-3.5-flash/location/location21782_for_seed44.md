The air in the Sump-Ward is so thick with warm moisture that it feels less like atmosphere and more like a suspended, breathable lake. It is a small, sunken cooperative—no larger than a town square or a deep-set quarry—bound on all sides by the colossal, pillar-like roots of three ancient swamp-cedars. Overhead, their crowns weave together into a living ceiling sixty feet up, filtering the daylight into a heavy, emerald-tinted gloom.

Everything here is wet. A perpetual, warm drizzle falls from the canopy, condensed from the steam of the low-pressure engines that hiss in the shadows. Water does not merely flow here; it is processed, cycled, and absorbed. It weeps from the vertical wooden walls in long, dark streaks, pools in shallow basins carved into the living peat of the floor, and gurgles through a complex network of hollow bamboo conduits and green-crusted copper pipes.

The very ground beneath a traveler’s feet is not stone or soil, but a dense, springy mattress of living liverworts and black peat-moss, compressed by decades of foot traffic into slippery, fibrous cobblestones. The architecture is inseparable from the flora. Dwellings and workshops are not built upon the land, but grown within it. Great, hollowed-out storage gourds, as large as carriages, are grafted directly onto the massive structural vines that crisscross the basin. Their rinds have been cured to an iron-hard, amber translucency, and warm, yellow light from oil lanterns glows through their fibrous walls. Scaffolding and multi-tiered walkways are made from the dried, shelf-like brackets of massive tree-fungi, bolted together with brass pins and suspended by thick, braided hemp ropes that have begun to sprout green shoots of their own.

But this is not a wild jungle; it is an industrial parish. The air is filled with the rhythmic, heavy, and mechanical sounds of a working tannery and distillery. To the left, a massive, twenty-foot flywheel—sliced from a single cross-section of a petrified redwood—rotates with a slow, terrifying momentum. Its wet, dark grain glistens under the flicker of a tallow lamp. It is driven by the steady, scalding exhaust of a deep geothermal vent, converted to mechanical power through a series of copper-jacketed wood pistons that stroke with a wet, swallowing *chug-thump... chug-thump* once every six seconds. 

The smell is a pungent, comforting mixture of hot pine-tar, boiling starch, sweet sugarcane ferment, and the sharp, clean scent of crushed mint used to clear the work-floors of mold. Thick copper pipes, green with verdigris and bound with strips of raw vulcanized rubber, wrap around the living columns of the trees like metal ivy. They thrum with the passage of pressurized sap, drawn up from the deep roots to be refined into lubricants or thick, sweet fuel in the boiling-vats at the center of the square.

There is a patient, unhurried quality to the industry here. Because the materials are alive, they cannot be forced. The boilers run on a slow, low-temperature simmer; if they get too hot, the living wooden housings will char and rot. The gears, carved from dense lignum vitae wood, must be constantly lubricated with animal-fat candles and vegetable tallow to keep them from swelling in the humid air. 

Fauna is nearly non-existent in this steam-beaten pocket. The heat and the constant, vibrating hum of the machinery have driven away most birds and mammals. The only native residents are small, grease-loving brass-beetles that crawl along the copper pipes, licking the condensed oil from the joints, and massive, pale-gray garden snails that are kept by the residents to scour the wooden walkways of invasive algae.

At the center of the ward lies the Sump itself—a deep, circular reservoir where the warm, mineral-rich runoff from the boilers settles. It is covered in a thick carpet of duckweed, broken only by the lazy, rising bubbles of boiling gas from below. From this pool, a series of hand-cranked bucket lines, woven from living willow switch, lift the warm water to the upper terraces, keeping the hanging gardens and the gourd-homes hydrated. 

The light here is constant but muted. It does not change with the hours, but rather with the shifting density of the steam clouds rising from the floor. When the engines vent their pressure, the world disappears into a warm, white blankness, leaving only the smell of boiled wood-pulp and the heavy, bass vibration of the great flywheel turning, slow and blind, in the green-gold mist.
