The ground is not earth, but masonry. 

To tread here is to walk upon vast, interlocking flags of dark fired clay and dressed basalt, laid down so long ago that the joints have grown soft, sealed with centuries of packed leaf-mold and the tiny, persistent roots of black-leaved ivy. There is no raw stone that has not been shaped by a chisel; there is no soil that was not placed here by design. The world is an infinite, terraced monument, a mountain range of plazas, stepped pyramids, buttresses, and monumental aqueducts that stretches beyond the horizon in every direction, climbing up into the thin, warm air of the high altitude.

It is very dark. The sky above is not a vault of stars or a blue dome, but a narrow, high-altitude crevice between towering structures, admitting only a permanent, slate-gray twilight that never brightens. The lower levels of this immense construction are lost in absolute blackness, from which a steady, warm updraft rises, carrying the smell of wet mold, ancient brick, and warm, living skin. 

This is an environment of dampness, though not of water in its grand, flowing forms. There are no oceans or wide rivers here. Instead, water is an intimate, localized presence. It exists as a heavy condensation that coats the undersides of colossal stone arches, gathering into fat, heavy droplets that fall through the gloom in a sporadic, vertical rhythm. It pools in shallow, ornamental basins carved into the terraces—basins now choked with thick, spongy carpets of moss that drink the moisture before it can overflow. The masonry itself acts as a sponge; the porous red brick absorbs the damp, keeping the entire world slightly slick, warm, and humid to the touch.

Vegetation has claimed every square inch of this built landscape, yet it adapts to the lack of light. The plants are dark—deep, velvety greens that border on black, violet-veined creepers, and broad, fan-like ferns that thrust outward from the joints of the stone walls to catch the faint twilight from above. There are no grasses, only sprawling mats of liverwort and thick, springy mosses that cushion the feet. Great, thick-trunked trees, resembling ancient figs but with soft, corky bark, have taken root in the wider courtyards. Their roots do not drill into the stone; instead, they flow over it like wax, wrapping around columns, filling niches, and cementing the ancient structures together in a living, wooden grip.

Because the air is warm and thick with humidity, the sheer volume of animal life is staggering. It is a world of soft bodies, chitin, and feathers. Thousands of species of large, pale land-snails—some as small as gravel, others the size of sleeping dogs—drag themselves across the wet brick, leaving silver trails that catch the dim light. In the hollows of the friezes and the empty window-bays of the towers, millions of small, stout-bodied birds with dark plumage roost. 

The sound is not a background noise; it is a physical weight. It is deafening. The acoustic properties of the stone corridors and vast, open plazas amplify a continuous, roaring wall of biological noise. It is the rhythmic, mechanical clicking of millions of cicada-like insects clustered on the tree trunks; the deep, booming croak of fat, skin-breathing amphibians that live in the wet cisterns; the shrill, piping territorial whistles of the roosting birds; and the dry, papery hiss of endless wings shifting in the dark overhead. To exist here is to be immersed in this sonic sea. It is so loud that the vibration can be felt in the soles of the feet through the brickwork, a constant, low-frequency tremor of life.

Despite the intensity of the noise, the actual movement of this world is incredibly slow. The warmth and moisture create a greenhouse-like lethargy. The large snails move with glacial patience. The thick-trunked trees grow by millimeters each decade, their roots slowly settling into the mortar of the walls, stabilizing rather than destroying them. The high-altitude winds do not howl; they are slow, warm drafts that sigh through the stone arches, carrying the spores of mosses from one terrace to the next over the course of centuries.

There are no tools here. No metal rusts in the damp; no gears turn; no glass reflects the dim light. The builders of this place are gone, or perhaps they simply became something else. What remains is a massive, self-sustaining monument of worked stone and clay, now fully digested by a warm, dark, and screaming ecology that knows only how to climb, damply, toward the grey strip of sky.
