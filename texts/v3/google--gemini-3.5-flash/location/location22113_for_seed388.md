The place is a drowned pocket between four towering walls of soot-stained brick, a vertical alleyway no wider than a canal, known to none but the valves that feed it. It is a micro-basin—a single, enclosed courtyard of steam, copper, and weeping stone, perhaps sixty paces long, where the industrial city has folded in upon itself and settled into a wet, permanent equilibrium.

The air here is a warm, thick soup, smelling of hot tallow, wet coal-ash, and the sharp, sweet tang of crushed mint. It is always mid-afternoon here, or rather, the light never rises above the dim amber of an overcast dusk. The sky is nothing but a grey seam far above, choked by heavy iron pipes that span the gap between the buildings like the trusses of a flooded mine. From these pipes, condensation falls in a constant, heavy drizzle. The drops do not patter; they are fat and grease-slicked, drumming against the broad, rubbery leaves of elephant-ear ferns that crowd the lower gantries.

Water is the ruler of this small space. It seeps from the mortar lines in the brickwork, running down in dark, shimmering aprons. It pools three inches deep over the floor of basalt flagstones, pocketed with green grease and iron scale. There are no drains that work; instead, the water is sucked upward again by ancient, vertical copper pipes wrapped in thick horsehair insulation. These pipes thrum with a low, wet inhalation, drawing the pool back into the boilers somewhere in the dark interior of the walls, only for it to return as steam, hiss out of bronze relief valves, and condense once more on the ceiling-irons.

The density of this place is claustrophobic. Every square inch of vertical space is occupied. Where there is not a copper conduit or a lead-weighted pulley, there is vegetation. It is a lush, wet growth, but it is not wild; it has adapted to the machine. Thick, fleshy vines with leaves like dark leather wrap themselves around steam-mains, using the heat to swell their stems to the thickness of thighs. A species of grey-green liverwort, wet as grease, has filled the gaps between the brickwork, its tiny, cup-like structures catching the greasy condensate and black coal-dust. There is moss here—thick, spongy carpets of it, three inches deep—that grows directly over the iron floor-plates, muffling the vibrations of the engines below but vibrating itself, humming like a green skin.

There are no animals. Not a single fly circles the stagnant corners of the pool. There are no beetles under the rotting wood of the old walkways, no rats in the dry crevices of the masonry, no birds nesting in the iron rafters. The silence of life is absolute, yet the place is loud.

The noise is a heavy, rhythmic percussion that never varies. It is the heartbeat of a machines-world that has forgotten its purpose but cannot stop. A massive, cast-iron drive-wheel, nine feet across and green with verdigris, turns slowly in a recess in the eastern wall. It moves with a wet, heavy *clack-hiss, clack-hiss* as its buckets catch the overflow from an upper cistern. Somewhere deeper in the brickwork, a low-voltage generator—an old, wet-wound dynamo—humms with a pitch that registers in the teeth rather than the ears. Steam whistles from a pinhole leak in a brass elbow, a high, steady note like a distant pipe organ, softened by the wet moss that has grown over the pipe’s wound.

Everything here is old, weathered, and stable. The sharp corners of the masonry were rounded off by fifty years of warm spray before the plants even took hold; now, the lime-leach from the mortar has formed small, calcified terraces along the walls, like miniature travertine pools in an ancient cave. The iron is not bright, nor is it flaky-red with active rust; it has reached a state of black, oily stillness, protected by a layer of anaerobic slime and grease that has baked into the metal under decades of low heat. 

The weirdness of the place lies in this transition. The plants do not seem to be destroying the machinery; they are maintaining it. In some places, the thick, tuberous roots of the vines have grown into the packing-glands of the pumps, their fibrous bodies acting as gaskets, sealing the leaks with their own living sap. A low, yellow filament bulb, encased in a thick glass jar encrusted with lime, glows with a dull orange light; a vine has wrapped around its brass socket, its leaves turned inward toward the glass, drinking the heat, its veins glowing faintly green with the current leaking through the damp insulation.

Nothing changes here on any scale that a human mind would call fast. The flywheels turn at six revolutions a minute, day after year. The ferns grow, die, and turn to black peat in the standing water, forming a rich, oily loam upon which the next generation of ferns takes root. The copper pipes slowly thin as the acidic water eats their interiors, but the calcium from the brickwork coats them from the outside, reinforcing them with stone jackets. It is a tiny, warm, wet temple of industry that has outlived its creators, humming its loud, wet song to the empty brick walls, completely content in its own greasy, mossy decay.
