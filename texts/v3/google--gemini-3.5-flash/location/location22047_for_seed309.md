The vault is called called the Sump-of-Stems, though its builders knew it as Node 12. It is a single hollow chamber inside a subterranean root-system so massive that its outer limits are purely theoretical. Here, below the earth, there is no stone. The floor underfoot is a polished plane of dark, fossilized heartwood, its grain twisting in wide, ancient rivers of dark amber and charcoal-brown. The ceiling, sixty yards above, is a ribbed canopy of living bark, glistening with a slow, heavy sweat that never quite becomes rain.

The air is warm, smelling of boiled starch, hot fat, and the sweet, slightly fermented breath of a yeast-vat. It is damp, but not wet; moisture does not fall so much as it clings. It settles on the skin like oily dew, gathers on copper pipes in trembling droplets, and runs down the fibrous walls in thin, dark lines that resemble veins.

Because this is a chamber of vast, hollowed space interrupted only by three colossal vertical columns, the density of the landscape feels sparse, almost lonely, despite the clutter of human effort clinging to those column-sides. The three pillars—the Stems—are mammoth, hollow tubes of living plant structure, each forty feet thick. They plunge through the floor and disappear into the roof. Around these three pillars, an industrial settlement has spun itself like a series of brass-and-iron cobwebs.

This is a town built on gravity and sap.

Clinging to the furrowed bark of the Stems are layered shanties of steam-bent wood and corrugated iron, connected by swaying rope bridges and iron-trussed scaffoldings. Copper pipes, greened by corrosion, wrap around the trunks like parasitic vines, tapped directly into the plant’s deep, high-pressure sap-veins. There is an unmistakable industrial order to the clutter: steam-pumps, black with grease and insulated with woven hemp-fiber, thump rhythmically against the wood. They are drawing the sap up into brass fractionators, where it is boiled and separated into industrial lubricants, sugar-spirits, and a tough, amber-colored latex that the settlers use for everything from boot-soles to steam-valves.

The technology is clumsy, heavy, and loud. It is powered by the pressure of the root itself; the living vault acts as a massive hydraulic pump. Steam-whistles shriek to signal the pressure-drops, and the *clong-clong-clong* of heavy iron valves opening and closing echoes off the distant, curving wooden walls of the vault. 

But the machines are not the loudest thing here. 

The vault is crawling.

To walk across the open, dark wood floor of Node 12 is to invite a carpet of life to part before you. The fauna is not merely present; it is an environment in its own right. It is a deafening, ceaseless tide of chitin, membranous wings, and pulsing bellies. Millions of flat, shield-shaped beetles, colored like wet bronze, scrape over the pipes to lick the grease-leaks. They are so numerous that their shifting bodies sound like dry gravel being turned over by spade-strokes. 

Above, in the dim, greenish shadow of the ceiling, clouds of giant nocturnal midges—each the size of a sparrow—orbit the sputtering oil-lamps. They do not fly in silence; their wings produce a low, bass hum that vibrates in the teeth of the settlers. From the crevices of the bark, long, pale, multi-jointed tarantula-wasps emerge to hunt the beetles, their hard carapaces clattering against the copper pipes like dry twigs. Smaller, soft-bodied things—slug-like tunicates that glow with a cold, greenish-blue light—are harvested by the bucketful from the lower bark-flutes and crushed into paste to light the darker walkways. 

This abundance of animal life is integrated directly into the mechanics of the settlement. The gears of the sap-fractionators are lubricated not with mineral oil, but with the rendered fat of the shield-beetles, which are swept into hoppers by the thousands. The waste-chutes of the shanties discard organic trash directly into the dark void of the floor, where it vanishes in minutes beneath a seething mound of blind, scurrying scavengers.

The lighting is miserable. No sunlight ever reaches Node 12. The only illumination comes from the dull, yellow flare of oil torches burning refined sap-grease, which cast long, jerking shadows against the bark, and the faint, cold luminescence of the crushed-tunicate lanterns. This creates a world of deep, moving shadows where the boundary between machine, wood, and insect is constantly blurred. A pipe may appear to be pulsing, only for a light to reveal it is entirely covered by a single, living sleeve of black moth-larvae, their bodies rising and falling in unison to pump air through their spiracles.

The dynamic of the Sump is one of fragile, loud equilibrium. Every few hours, the root-structures undergo a "breath"—a massive, systemic shift in pressure. The wood itself groans, a deep, structural creak that drowns out even the steam-pumps. During the breath, hot, pressurized sap-steam vents from the relief valves of the Stems with a terrifying roar, filling the lower twenty feet of the chamber with a thick, sugary fog.

During these vents, the beetles go mad. They swarm toward the steam-outlets in screaming millions to drink the condensed sugar from the scaffoldings, turning the entire settlement into a single, undulating wall of bronze shells. The human workers retreat into their sealed, wood-insulated boxes, waiting out the hot mist and the deafening clatter of the swarm.

When the vent ends, the mist cools and settles. The beetles crawl back into the dark crevices of the wood, fat and heavy, and the workers emerge with shovel and broom to clear the dead ones from the gears, restarting the steam-pumps to draft the next cycle of sap. It is an ancient, weathered cycle, established so long ago that the brass of the engines has begun to take on the greenish, organic patina of the moss that grows around the gaskets. The machine has become a part of the tree; the people have become a part of the swarm; and the Sump continues its heavy, warm, mechanical breathing in the dark.
