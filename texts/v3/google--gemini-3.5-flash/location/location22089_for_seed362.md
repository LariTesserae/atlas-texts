The sky is not a sky, but an infinite grid of glowing white porcelain paneling, five hundred meters overhead, casting a glare so bright and shadowless that the world below seems cast in high-definition ceramic. The air is cool—persistently, artificially so, like the draft of a subterranean vault or an air-conditioned laboratory. It is entirely dry. There is no moisture here, no dew, no mist, no fat drops of rain in the forecast; instead, a faint, sweet smell of dessicated pine needles, dry ozone, and powdered chalk fills the nostrils. It is a world that has never known a cloud.

Below the white glare lies a vast, low-slung plain of industrial terraces, stretching to the horizon and then, impossibly, folding upward into vertical planes that do not drop away but continue to run parallel to one’s field of vision. This is a constructed continent, a world-floor of cream-colored modular tiles made of vitrified ceramic and polymer. There is no soil. Every square inch of the landscape has been engineered, laid down, and bolted into place on a planetary scale. 

Yet, there is almost no bare floor to be seen. The entire industrial infrastructure has been thoroughly, systematically overrun by a dry, botanical architecture. 

It is a pure-plant ecosystem, but not one of green wilderness. The flora here is structural, mechanical, and dry. Out of the seams between the floor-tiles grow massive, square-sectioned trunks of pale yellow wood, their bark resembling the combed plaster of architectural columns. These trunks rise in perfect, mile-long colonnades, supporting horizontal beams of fibrous, self-knitting cellulose that mimic the steel girders of a railway terminus. 

Between these wooden structures, a dense, near-solid web of vegetation fills the space. There are no soft leaves. Instead, the plants are made of pale, rust-colored felt, stiff paper, and dry, segmented tubes that click together like dry bone when the air moves. Some plants are large, succulent gears, three meters across, their ribbed edges interlocking with one another in long, horizontal trains. They turn—infinitesimally slowly, perhaps one rotation every three days—driven not by wind, but by the slow, turgid pressure of dry, resinous starch shifting through their internal capillaries.

Without water, life here runs on an electrostatic chemistry. Beneath the floorboards, massive industrial turbines—shrouded in thick, woolen lichens that absorb their vibrations—hum with a deep, subsonic drone. They pump a fine, fluidized silica dust and pressurized nitrogen gas through a network of copper-lined wood conduits. The plants tap into these conduits with specialized, dry roots that look like fibrous bundle-cables, drawing up the micro-fine mineral dust to build their silica-rich shells. 

The silence is profound, damp and heavy despite the dryness. The dense, felted mosses—which grow three feet deep over the copper pipes and iron flywheels—act as perfect acoustic insulators. The titanic processes of this world occur in near-complete muteness. A piston the size of a cathedral tower, wrapped in a blanket of grey, needle-like creepers, slides down into a copper sleeve with nothing more than a soft, greasy *hiss* of escaping nitrogen. A million turning wooden gears produce only a dry, rhythmic rustle, like a autumn forest being stirred by a phantom hand.

There is a strange distortion to the geometry of this place, an anomaly born of its sheer scale and artificial nature. In the middle distance, where the terraces sink into a shallow, fifty-mile-wide basin, the lines of sight begin to bend. A walk down a straight, plant-choked aisle of looms reveals that the path does not end at the horizon; rather, it loops. One can look up through the canopy of stiff, parchment-like leaves and see, directly above, another terrace of silent, green-painted machinery, its ferns hanging "downward" toward the traveler, though both levels share the same cool, gravity-bound floor. 

Solid beams of light—not merely rays, but physical, architectural pillars of hardened lux—cross the gaps between these overlapping terraces. They look like rods of spun glass, glowing with the same cool, sterile white as the ceiling. The plants treat these light-beams as physical trellises. Creepers with leaves like dry, yellowed parchment spiral up the shafts of light, their tendrils sinking into the illumination itself, drawing sustenance directly from the frozen photons. If you run your hand along one of these light-beams, it feels like polished marble, slightly vibrating, with the dry creepers anchored to it like barnacles on iron.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, and what little exists is indistinguishable from the machine-plants. Occasionally, a flat, segmented creature made of dry, brown lacquer—resembling an elongated trilobite three feet long—will crawl out from beneath a silent piston. It moves without sound on dozens of tiny, brush-toothed legs, sweeping up the fallen silica dust and polishing the ceramic tiles beneath the moss. It has no eyes, only copper antennae that twitch in rhythm with the subterranean turbines. It does not breathe; it simply filters the dry, moving air for spores.

Everything here is old, balanced, weathered, and clean. This is not a ruined world, but a completed one. The brass fittings on the massive botanical looms are not bright, but have oxidized into a soft, velvety brown that matches the dry bark of the wood-girders. The ceramic tiles are worn smooth at the corners where the root-bundles have nudged against them for centuries. There is no decay in the traditional sense, for there are no wet rot-bacteria; when a wooden strut dies, it does not crumble into mold, but slowly petrifies under the constant flow of mineral dust, becoming a pillar of sparkling, green-veined jasper that continues to support the weight of the terraces above.

It is a landscape of endless, bright, quiet labor. The machines grow the plants; the plants turn the machines. Under the cool, perpetual glare of the tiled sky, the world-engine breathes its dry, nitrogen breath, shifting its wooden gears and weaving its trellises of solid light, forever, in perfect, frozen equilibrium.
