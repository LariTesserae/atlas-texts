The horizon here does not lie flat. It climbs, shattering into a thousand gray terraces that rise until they are lost in a thin, cold haze. 

There is no water. The air is remarkably clear but utterly dry, smelling faintly of flint, talc, and the dry chill of a cellar. It is a world of bone-colored salt-pan and dark, volcanic shale, where any moisture has long since been locked away in deep, subterranean crystals or lost to the vacuum above.

At first glance, the landscape presents the dizzying scale of an ancient, abandoned metropolis. Colossal rectangular prisms of blue-gray slate and compacted ash rise kilometers into the dim sky. They are arranged in vast, orderly grids—avenues of empty air five hundred yards wide, crossing at perfect right angles. One could easily believe these were the skyscrapers of a long-dead empire, but a closer look reveals no windows, no doors, no seams of mortar, and no signs of chisel or drill. 

This is the work of mineral deposition and a bizarre, crystalline geometry that operates at the scale of mountain ranges. The towers are solid, cleaved along clean, molecular planes by ages of dry wind. They are connected by dizzying flying buttresses of natural basalt—monolithic bridges that span the chasms, worn smooth and hollowed out by the ceaseless friction of dust-storms that occurred millions of years ago. The erosion is ancient, settled, and complete; the stone has reached an equilibrium with its environment, perfectly balanced against its own impossible weight.

It is cold here. Not a biting, wet freeze, but a dry, preserved chill that keeps the air perfectly still. The light is a permanent, faint twilight. The sun, if there is one behind the bruised, purple-gray sky-sheet, never rises high enough to illuminate the bottoms of the canyons. Instead, a diffuse, shadowless glow emanates from everything, as if the gray stone itself were slowly leaking the light it gathered in some forgotten epoch.

The silence is almost total. It is a heavy, physical quiet that presses against the ears. Only occasionally is it broken by a thin, dry *tick-click*—the sound of a flake of shale peeling away from a cliffside and falling into the void. It does not crash; it simply drops until it is out of sight.

In this place, reality has begun to fray at the edges of its own mathematics. Gravity is not a constant downward pull. It is local, sticky, and confused. Each of the colossal stone towers exerts its own faint, horizontal attraction. If you walk along the side of one of the basalt bridges, "down" gradually tilts. Stand on the vertical flank of a five-mile-high obelisk, and the abyss behind you becomes a horizontal plain, while the distant ground you left behind becomes a sheer wall. 

Between these towers, in the vast voids of the grid, gravity sometimes cancels out entirely. In these silent zones, clusters of sharp, hexagonal dust-motes hang suspended in mid-air, forming intricate, unmoving clouds that look like frozen explosions. If you throw a pebble into these spaces, it does not fall; it drifts in a slow, perfectly straight line until it strikes the next tower days later, or vanishes into the gray mist.

Life here is rare, silent, and unrecognizable. There are no plants. The only things that grow are "creepers"—not vines of wood and leaf, but brittle, hair-thin filaments of native silver and copper that sprout from the cracks in the slate. They grow in fractal, circuit-like patterns across the stone faces, catching the dim ambient light and occasionally flickering with tiny, static sparks. They do not seek water; they feed on the dry, electrostatic tension of the air.

Sometimes, one of the rare inhabitants of this void can be seen. These are the *Platens*—creatures made of thin, overlapping shingles of grey mica, like living, breathing slate. They have no legs or wings. Instead, they glide along the boundaries where the gravity fields of the towers meet and slide against one another. They move without a sound, steering with tiny, clicking movements of their hinged plates, drifting like dry leaves caught in an invisible, vertical river.

Though the world is silent and empty, it is not stagnant. It is governed by slow, tectonic cycles of spatial movement. Every few hours, a gentle vibration runs through the stone—not an earthquake, but a "shear." The towers themselves drift on deep, subterranean tides of space, shifting their alignment by a few feet. When this happens, the suspended dust-clouds in the voids reorganize themselves, snapping into new, geometrically perfect patterns with a soft *shhh-t* sound, like salt being spilled on a drumhead. 

You can stand on the edge of a basalt platform, miles above a bottomless chasm of purple dusk, and watch the distant city-structures slowly slide. There are no machines here, no tools, and no minds to register the passage of time. There is only the cold, the silence, the slow rearrangement of the towers, and the dry, metallic spark of the silver-vines reaching out across the stone.
