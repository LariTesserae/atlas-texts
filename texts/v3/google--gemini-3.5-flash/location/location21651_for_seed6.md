High above the active weather of the lower world, suspended in the rare, cold air of an alpine summit, sits the Sky-Well of Chamber 8. 

Inside, however, there is only warmth and water.

The environment is a single, immense, flooded hall—a cathedral of brass, green glass, and wet, pressurized greenhouse growth. It is entirely self-contained, a gargantuan iron cistern roughly eighty yards long and forty yards high. Sunlight, thin and bright from the high altitude, pours through the massive, arched clerestory windows along the western wall. Through the three-inch-thick structural glass, one can see pale mountain clouds drifting *below* the level of the chamber, but inside, the light is immediately captured, slowed, and emeralded by forty feet of warm, clear, highly oxygenated water.

There is no natural earth here. Every surface is machined, milled, or cast. The floor is a tessellation of heavy, cream-colored ceramic tiles, bordered by thick structural margins of green-lacquered iron. Great rows of brass columns rise from the floor to the vaulted ceiling, their surfaces still showing the sharp, parallel tool-marks of a recent lathing. There are no signs of wear, no rounded corners from centuries of water-flow, no soft coatings of silt or decay. The bolts are hexagonal and crisp; the seams where the metal plates meet are as thin and dark as hair. The place is young, perhaps only weeks or months old, preserved in a state of suspended, pristine operation.

And yet, it is completely choked with plants.

The vegetation is a lush, engineered jungle that seems to have exploded into the space the moment the water arrived. Massive, turgid broadleaves—the color of pale jade and translucent enough to show their internal plumbing—grow directly out of brass-collared wall ports. These are not wild plants, but some form of industrial flora, designed to interface with the machinery. Their roots are clean, hairless cords of rubbery fiber that wrap tightly around copper steam-lines and power conduits, drawing warmth from the dormant electrical currents. Thick, corded vines, like underwater ivy crossed with hydraulic tubing, snake up the pillars, their leaves overlapping so densely that the metal beneath is visible only as a golden gleam in the gaps. 

The density is near-total. To move through the chamber would require parting a thick wall of wet, heavy fronds, sliding between vertical copper pipes and the massive, fleshy stems of water-lilies that rise thirty feet to float against the iron-ribbed ceiling. Every square foot of space is occupied by either structure, mechanism, or leaf. 

Despite the density, there is no chaos. Everything is in order. Giant copper coils, twelve feet across, curlicue through the center of the room, their surfaces perfectly polished and free of scale. Through the water, they hum with a faint, steady electricity that vibrates in the teeth. Large, circular dial-meters with heavy glass lenses are set into the walls at regular intervals; their black iron needles are frozen pointing to the high end of their scales, indicating pressure and temperature with absolute, clean precision. 

The level of technology is heavy, mechanical, and electric. There are no delicate microprocessors here, but rather massive, water-tight relays, heavy iron flywheels that take ten minutes to make a single revolution, and thick copper cables wrapped in vulcanized rubber. Everything is built to withstand pressure, heat, and moisture, clean and un-pitted.

The silence within the vault is profound, muffled by the weight of the water. The typical noises of a aquatic environment—the darting of fish, the frantic clicking of insects, the splash of surface life—are entirely absent. There are no animals here. There is not even the microscopic drift of daphnia or rotifers. The water is chemically pure, sterilized, and enriched only with a thick, sweet nutrient syrup that smells faintly of sugar beets and ozone. The only sounds are the physical breath of the building itself: the deep, subterranean *thrum-thrum... thrum-thrum* of a massive, low-RPM pump cycling water through some distant basement, the occasional sharp *clink* of a copper pipe expanding against its brass bracket, and the slow, rhythmic *shuck... shuck* of a giant piston miles away, sending a slow-motion pressure bead through the water.

These pressure beads cause the giant, translucent leaves to bow and then rise again, a movement so slow it resembles the long, patient exhalation of a sleeping giant. A single leaf might take three minutes to complete its downward sweep and return to rest.

At the center of the hall, suspended like a great brass heart among the vines, is a spherical condenser unit. It is twenty feet in diameter, covered in thousands of tiny, sharp-edged cooling fins that glimmer like wet coin-metal. From its base, a steady, thin stream of tiny, perfectly spherical oxygen bubbles rises toward the ceiling. They do not rush; they climb through the warm, viscous liquid like pearls rising through oil, reflecting the green leaves and the sharp yellow indicator bulbs of the walls as they go. 

Nothing else changes. The light declines slowly as the sun sinks toward the high-altitude horizon outside the glass. The temperature of the water remains a constant, womb-like eighty-four degrees. The plants continue their silent, pressurized drinking from the mechanical ports, their leaves growing a fraction of a millimeter wider each hour, slowly sealing the few remaining pockets of open water with their thick, green wax.
