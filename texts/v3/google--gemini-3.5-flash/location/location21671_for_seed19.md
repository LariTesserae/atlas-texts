The air at the roof of the world does not stir. At an elevation where the sky turns the color of a bruised plum, the great plateau of Silt-and-Iron stretches to the horizon, a tableland so old that its mountains have long since been ground down into low, smooth humps of pale shale. There is no water here. Not a drop of dew rises at dawn—if dawn could be said to occur in a place where the sun hangs forever at a three-quarter tilt, fixed like a brass nail in the vault. The ground is a dry, chalky pan of compressed white salt and fine, talcum-like mineral dust that holds no moisture and yields no scent. 

It is cold, but not freezing; a crisp, dry chill that preserves rather than bites. The air tastes of gypsum and cold iron.

Despite the vast, open scale of the plains, the landscape is intensely crowded. It is not empty space that fills this high desert, but an overwhelming, silent congregation of living things. They occupy every square yard of the salt-pan, packed together like cobbles in a cathedral floor. 

These creatures are various, though all are marked by the same patient, petrified biology. There are several-legged things with shells of thin, dry horn, shaped like upturned boats; there are tall, spindly-legged walkers that stand six feet high but are no thicker than dry reeds, their heads consisting of nothing but single, polished amber globes that catch the eternal light; there are flat, segmented tiles of grey flesh that lie flush against the salt, carpet-dense, carpet-wide, overlapping like shingles. They do not move. If one passes a hand over the amber globe of a reed-walker, the lens does not turn, yet a faint, deep warmth emanates from within, a slow-burning biological furnace that requires no breath.

These animals do not eat, nor do they drink. In the dry, non-aqueous chemistry of this high shelf, they appear to feed on the slow, radioactive decay of the deep salt-beds or the constant, unblinking pressure of the light. They are so numerous that to walk across the plateau is to step from carapace to carapace, from hide to horn. Yet they are completely quiet. 

The silence is massive, heavy, and absolute, absorbed by millions of bodies of felt, horn, and dry fiber. Very rarely—perhaps once every several hours—there is a sound: the dry, fingernail-click of a joint settling a fraction of a millimeter, or the soft *hiss* of a pinch of salt falling from a crumbling ledge. Otherwise, the stillness is like a weight.

Here and there, rising from the carpet of silent beasts, are the scattered marks of an ancient, primitive hand. These are short, dry-stone walls of grey slate, built without mortar, curving in semicircles that signify nothing. There are low, square platforms where the flat stones have been chipped with crude stone hammers, their edges rough and white. Atop some of these platforms sit small, hand-modeled clay vessels—no larger than cups—empty of any liquid, containing only a single, dry seed or a scrap of yellowed, papery vine. These are the only plants: a sparse, brittle lichen that grows not on the stones, but in thin, yellow crusts upon the shells of the oldest, largest beasts, and occasional tufts of dry grey hair that sprout from the cracks in the slate, shaking only when a rare shift in gravity disturbs them.

For gravity here is not a simple rule. 

The reality of the place has begun to fray, settled into a final, stable state where the laws of distance and matter are no longer rigid. In some quarters of the plateau, three or four of the reed-walkers occupy the exact same point in space; their limbs intersect without touching, their amber heads blurred together like a double-exposed photograph. A small shale pebble, if kicked from a ledge, does not plunge downward but drifts horizontally at the speed of a walking man, sliding through the air until it gently adheres to the side of a beast’s flank, sticking there as if the animal’s skin were made of soft tallow.

The horizon is deceptive. Because the air is perfectly dry and free of moisture-haze, things do not diminish with distance in the usual way. A low ridge of salt-hills thirty miles away appears to sit immediately behind a dry-stone wall ten paces distant, the two scales locked together in a flat, theatrical perspective. 

Nothing changes. There are no seasons, no migrations, no births, and no decay. The creatures do not die because they do not wear out; their organs are dry, crystalline structures that do not rub or rust. A great beetle-like entity, its hide the color of old pewter, has its rear legs sunk inches deep into a solid block of grey basalt—not because it has burrowed, but because the stone and the beast have slowly, over eons of perfect stillness, drifted into one another’s molecular coordinates. They share the space now, in a cold, eternal equilibrium.

If one stands on a stone platform and looks out over the landscape, the view is of a vast, silent sea of heads, backs, and lenses, all turned toward the north-northwest. They do not blink. The light, frozen at its late-afternoon slant, casts long, thin, charcoal-colored shadows that stretch across the white pan for miles, never moving, never shortening, like black lines drawn upon a map that someone has forgotten to finish.
