To find this place, one would have to crawl through the fibrous, wet marrow of some greater living entity, but inside the pocket itself, there is only the sensation of an empty, warm room. 

It is a hollow vesicle about the size of a small, low-ceilinged cellar, buried deep in the low-lying root-bases of a world made entirely of vegetation. There is no soil here, no granite, no sand. The floor, ceiling, and walls are a single, continuous enclosure of tough, wet heartwood—dark as oiled walnut, worn to a high polish by the tireless passage of fluids. The chamber is mostly empty air, a sparse and tidy void preserved in the center of an infinitely dense, continental thicket. 

The heat inside the chamber is steady and maternal, like the interior of a greenhouse after a midsummer storm. Water does not merely drip here; it is processed. Through tiny, sieve-like pores in the ceiling, warm water is forced down under high pressure. It comes in a fine, atomized spray, forming a perpetual cloud of warm, mineral-scented mist that hangs in the middle of the room before condensing on the smooth, curved walls. This runoff does not pool; it collects in deep, weathered grooves worn into the floor—grooves that have existed for so long they are as smooth as river glass—and is drawn back into the fibrous grain of the wood with a quiet, sucking thrum.

The room is loud, filled with a steady, deafening mechanical roar that has no human origin. It is the sound of a biological factory at peak production. Inside the walls, just behind the polished wood-skin, huge vascular conduits swell and contract. They pulse with a heavy, rhythmic *shuck-shuck, shuck-shuck*—the sound of giant, fibrous pistons pumping sap upward to leaves three miles above. There is no variation in this rhythm; it is a frozen equilibrium, a perpetual-motion machine of wood and pressure that has run without a millisecond of interruption for thousands of years. 

Running across the ceiling like exposed electrical conduit are long, ropey vines of a pale copper color. These are bio-electric cords. They hum with a dry, vibrating drone that crackles occasionally with low-voltage static electricity. Where the copper-vines knot together, they produce a flat, steady, warm illumination. It is not the flickering light of rot-fire or the pale green of foxfire, but a bright, industrial glare like a dry sodium lamp, casting sharp, unmoving shadows across the slick floor.

In the corners of the chamber, where the mist settles into greasy pools, the fauna of the pocket makes its home. They are small, specialized creatures, numerous but unobtrusive, moving with the repetitive, jerky grace of little wind-up toys. Hundreds of flat, brass-colored beetles, their carapaces polished by the damp air, form long, circular conveyor-lines around the pressure-vents. They are cleaning the pores, scraping away mineral scale with their serrated legs in an endless, clockwork cycle. Beside them, blind, soft-bodied worms shaped like ribbed hydraulic hoses squeeze themselves into the narrow drainage grooves, their bodies expanding and contracting to clear any blockages. They have no eyes, no voices, and no variation in their behavior; they are biological gears in a machine that does not know it is alive.

There is a smell here of hot starch, vinegar, and ozone. It is clean, sharp, and slightly suffocating. Nothing here is decaying. The balance of the pocket is too perfect, too established. The wood does not rot because the pressurized juices flowing through its walls are highly antiseptic, and the temperature never wavers by even a fraction of a degree. 

If you were to stand in this little room, the roar of the vascular engines would vibrate through the bones of your feet. The warm mist would coat your skin in a clean, slippery film within seconds. But there would be nothing to do, nothing to change, and nowhere else to go. The chamber is a finished thought. It is an engine room without an engineer, singing its loud, wet, electrified song in the dark, forever.
