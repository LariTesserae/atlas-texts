To stand in this place is to exist on a single bracket of wet stone bolted to the side of nothingness. 

Above, the sky is an immense, pale-blue vault, empty of everything but rushing air and thin, high cirrus clouds. Below, the mountain wall drops away into a sheer, dizzying void—a vertical mile of gray limestone that dissolves at its base into a distant, sun-whitened floor of mist. There are no sister peaks visible, no opposing ridges; there is only this single, colossal wall of rock and the vast, empty ocean of air that presses against it. 

Yet, tucked into a semi-circular scoop in this cliffside, a tiny, self-contained world has taken root. It is a natural amphitheater, no larger than a village square, carved out by millennia of dripping water and frost-wedging. The stone here is rounded, softened, and deeply grooved, possessing the settled, sleepy quality of an ancient riverbed that has somehow been hoisted into the stratosphere. 

The air is cool, carrying the sharp, nose-clearing scent of wet moss, cold slate, and the faint, ozone tang of high-altitude clouds. Water is the master of this pocket. It does not fall in a single torrent; instead, the entire back wall of the amphitheater *weeps*. A network of hairline fractures in the limestone bleeds cold, clear mountain water, which trickles down through thick cushions of lime-green liverworts and spongy hummocks of sphagnum moss. It pools in tiny, cup-sized basins in the rock, overflows, and runs in a hundred glittering veins across the stone floor before reaching the lip of the ledge. There, caught by the powerful updrafts of the abyss, the water does not fall downward. Instead, it is sheared off into a fine, upward-spraying mist, forming miniature, dancing rainbows that hover and dissipate in the empty air.

Life here is dense, frantic, and crowded into the few horizontal feet of soil that have gathered in the rock depressions. Velvet-soft moss, as thick as a mattress, blankets every surface that isn't slick with running water. From the deeper crevices hang long, dripping tresses of alpine ferns and pale, tubular flowers that nod vigorously in the constant draft. 

This tiny enclave is deafeningly loud. The wind behaves like a physical presence, roaring as it collides with the cliff face, howling through three deep, smooth-bored holes that erosion has drilled clean through the ledge’s rocky lip. These "wind-pipes" act as massive acoustic flutes, producing a low, vibrating drone that shakes the soles of your feet. Beneath this deep bass note is the complex, high-pitched percussion of the water: the heavy *plop-plop* of fat droplets hitting peat, the hiss of the upward-blowing spray, and the wet gurgle of a dozen miniature streams.

Amidst this din, animal life thrives with a frantic, buzzing energy. Millions of tiny, black midges dance in the lee of the rock walls, drawing in flocks of white-bellied swifts. The birds rocket out of the empty void at terrifying speeds, snatching insects from the air before diving back over the edge, their high-pitched, metallic screams slicing through the roar of the wind. Clinging to the vertical, mossy seeps are hundreds of translucent "glass-frogs," their skin so clear that their tiny, beating hearts are visible beneath their bellies. They croak in flat, rhythmic clicks—like heavy wooden dice shaken in a leather cup—their voices rising in a deafening chorus whenever a cloud passes directly through the ledge, briefly soaking everything in a thick, blinding fog.

This is not a wild wilderness untouched by hands, though the hands that touched it were simple, and have long since gone. 

Half-merged with the living rock are the remains of a single, primitive structure—perhaps a shelter or a watch-post. It is a circular wall, throat-high, built of flat slabs of shale stacked without mortar. The stones are covered in gray crustose lichens, making them almost indistinguishable from the natural slide behind them. Within this circle, the floor is dry, covered in a compressed layer of old peat and heather-stalks. 

A handful of ancient artifacts remain, preserved by the cool, damp air. Near the center of the ruin sits a heavy, basin-shaped mortar carved from a single block of dark granite—a stone not found on this limestone cliff, meaning it must have been hauled up from the bottom of the world by sheer muscle. Resting beside it is a hand-pestle of river-worn quartz, its handle smooth from the grip of fingers that have been dust for centuries. High on the cliff wall, wedged into a dry crack above the shelter, are three poles of weathered larch wood, lashed together with dried, braided gut-string. They hold a tattered, stiff sail of grease-cured hide, designed to catch the rising wind and divert the freezing mist away from the sleeping circle. It creaks and rattles rhythmically against the stone, a lonely, mechanical heartbeat.

Everything here is in motion. The weather changes on the scale of minutes. One moment, the ledge is bathed in the brilliant, harsh light of the high sun, which makes the wet moss steam and glisten like emeralds. The next, a violent updraft drags a fat, damp cloud up from the valley floor, swallowing the amphitheater in a cold, silent shroud of white dampness. The frogs fall silent; the swifts vanish into the mist; the wind-pipes change their tune from a roar to a wet, hollow whistle. Then, as quickly as it arrived, the cloud is torn away by the wind, shredded into wisps over the drop-off, and the sun strikes the wet stone once more, restarting the chaotic music of the water, the birds, and the air.
