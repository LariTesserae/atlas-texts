Here, the geography of the world does not end at the shore, because there is no shore. Instead, there is the Hanging Shelf—a colossal, high-altitude plateau of raw, jet-black basalt that rises thousands of yards from the abyssal darkness below, stopping just short of the sky. 

The water here is eighty feet deep above the highest peaks of the shelf, and hundreds of feet deep in its interior valleys. It is a warm, cradling body of ocean, heated from below by the slow, muffled respiration of volcanic vents and from above by a bright, generous sun. The water is so clear that looking outward is not like looking through sea, but through a thick, liquid atmosphere of pale turquoise glass. 

Because the landscape is geologically young, there is no sand. There are no smooth pebbles, no beaches, and no silted basins. The terrain is made of sharp, crystalline obsidian and jagged columns of hexagonal basalt that look as though they cooled and cracked only yesterday. The edges of the stone are razor-sharp, pristine, and dark as coal. 

Yet, this sharp volcanic skeleton is almost entirely clothed in an immense, silent riot of vegetation. Without the grinding force of heavy tides—for the currents here are gentle, slow-breathing things—the plants have climbed and clung to every vertical needle of stone. Massive, fern-like kelps with fronds as thick and supple as leather rise from the black cracks. Unlike earthly seaweeds, these plants are highly structural; they do not merely float, but grow in rigid, branching terraces, creating multi-layered emerald forests that extend as far as the eye can see through the sunlit water.

The density of this green world is overwhelming. There are three or four distinct layers of canopy. At the bottom, clinging to the glass-sharp basalt, are thick, velvet mosses of deep crimson and gold that hiss with tiny, self-generated bubbles of oxygen. Above them rise intermediate forests of pale blue water-bamboos, hollow-stemmed and perfectly straight, which sway in unison like a single breathing lung. Above these, the great fern-kelps spread their wide, translucent green fingers, catching the golden sunlight.

And at the very top, where the water meets the air in a flat, undulating mirror of quicksilver, a strange phenomenon occurs. Innumerable vine-like tendrils grow straight upward, ending in flat, webbed pads that seal themselves to the underside of the surface tension. They do not break through into the dry air; instead, they use the surface film as a ceiling, anchoring themselves to it so that the entire forest seems suspended, held in tension between the sharp black rock below and the silver sky-mirror above.

The light is constant and bright. The sun penetrates clean to the black floor of the plateau, casting long, sharp-edged shadows of the giant ferns across the obsidian valleys. Because the water is so still, the caustics—the shifting networks of bright, concentrated light cast by the surface ripples—do not dance frantically; they drift across the foliage in slow, sweeping arcs, like lazy gold brushstrokes.

For all its lushness, this is a world almost devoid of animal movement. There are no schools of silver fish, no burrowing crabs, no clicking of shrimp. The silence is profound and heavy, cushioned by the density of the water. The only sound is a rare, low-frequency hum—the deep, tectonic vibration of the young mountain roots miles below—and the faint, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of gas bubbles escaping the leaves and rising toward the silver ceiling.

Occasionally, the rare fauna of this place appears: a drift-sponge. These are solitary, translucent spheres, the size of carriage wheels, made of a glassy, lattice-like cartilage. They have no eyes, no limbs, and no visible means of propulsion. They drift with agonizing slowness through the emerald corridors of the fern-forest, their pale blue bodies glowing with a faint, internal light. They do not hunt; they merely absorb the microscopic nutrients that dissolve from the dense vegetation, filtering the warm water as they pass like silent, glowing lanterns.

In the deep valleys between the basalt peaks, the water changes. Here, heavy brine pools—water so saturated with volcanic minerals that it is denser than the sea around it—sit in perfect, unmixing lakes at the bottom of the ravines. These underwater lakes have their own shorelines of raw black glass, and their surfaces are so still they reflect the underwater forests above them, creating a dizzying, double-sided world of green and black. 

Nothing changes quickly here. The seasons are marked only by the very gradual rising and falling of the water temperature by a degree or two over centuries. The plants grow, anchor themselves to the silver sky, release their quiet bubbles, and slowly die, decaying into a fine, sparkling dust that sits in undisturbed drifts in the hollows of the sharp black stone. It is a landscape of suspended animation, warm, bright, and perfectly content in its own slow, wet dreaming.
