The air here is warm, smelling faintly of heated slate, dry grease, and the sharp, clean scent of electrical discharge. It does not carry the dampness of decay or the moisture of clouds. Indeed, there is no water in this world. The deep canyons and basins that plunge miles below the high plateaus do not hold lakes, but are instead filled with deep, silent drifts of micro-fine volcanic glass and dry, self-lubricating graphite dust. When the wind moves across these basins, the dust rises in slow, silent sheets that slide over one another like liquid silk, mimicking the currents of a sea but registering absolute dryness.

This is a landscape of vertical extremes. Great pillars of ochre and dark violet siltstone rise like the legs of colossal tables, their tops lost in the warm, pale gold sky. The stone edges are not sharp; they have been scoured over geological epochs by the dry, grain-laden winds. Every cliff face is rounded, possessing the smooth, melted-wax texture of ancient, weathered buttresses. The scale is so massive that the horizon bends with a slight, visible curvature, and the valleys below are perpetually choked with a warm, blue-shifted haze of suspended minerals.

There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, no lichen, no moss. Where life might have climbed the rocks, there are instead delicate, crystalline growths of sulfur and copper-sulfate that bloom from the dry stone in fragile, turquoise needles. These mineral formations do not grow by cell division, but by the slow, dry deposition of atmospheric salts over centuries. They are cold to the touch, snapping like dry glass under the slightest pressure.

And yet, despite the lack of vegetation and moisture, this world is crowded with a strange, quiet fauna. 

These creatures are inorganic, or perhaps their biology is based on a dry chemistry that has no need for water. Across the high, wind-polished plateaus, herds of *wheel-backs*—segmented, tortoise-like beings with shells of dull, burnished bronze—move with glacial slowness. They do not eat; instead, they press their flat, magnetic bellies against the rich iron veins of the rock, absorbing the weak currents of the earth. In the air between the great spires, huge, sail-like drifters float on the dry thermals. They are semi-translucent, like sheets of yellowed mica, and they steer by tilting their long, glassy ribs to catch the rising heat of the canyons. They make no sound but a soft, paper-like rustling as they pass close to the cliffside.

Woven into this extreme topography is an ancient, sprawling industrial infrastructure. 

It is an urbanism of distance and scale. This is not a compact city of houses and streets, but a network of colossal, automated factories and processing plants perched on the summits of the highest mesas. They are constructed of thick, dark iron plates and heavy yellow brick, their scale matching the mountains they sit upon. Between these isolated industrial nodes hang massive cables—some as thick as tree trunks—that stretch for miles across the canyons, sagging in long, elegant parabolas that vanish into the distant haze. 

The technology here is mechanical and slow. There are no flashing screens or high-frequency hums. Instead, there are giant flywheels, fifty yards across, that spin with a stately, rhythmic rotation, completing a single turn every few minutes. Great pistons plunge into the red earth of the plateaus, drawing up dry mineral powders to be sorted by gravity-slides and massive, vibrating sieves. From the tall, square chimneys of the processing nests, thick, dry gray smoke rises in straight columns before spreading into flat, horizontal sheets in the upper air. 

The fauna has integrated itself entirely with this machinery. 

Thousands of copper-shelled *cable-walkers*—creatures like multi-legged, hollow spiders—scuttle slowly along the high-tension lines that connect the mountain stations. They do not feed on the electricity itself, but on the micro-fine layer of zinc oxide that forms on the wires. In the warm, vibrating alcoves of the great engine houses, small, bat-like organisms with skin like vulcanized rubber hang in dense, silent clusters, warmed by the slow, radiant heat of the steam-less friction boilers. There is no conflict between the built world and the wild; the machines are simply another species of mountain, and the creatures treat the iron girders and the stone cliffs with the same instinctive familiarity.

The world is remarkably quiet. 

There is no roar of combustion. The engines are driven by the weight of falling sand or by the slow, thermal expansion of long metal rods heated by the sun during the day and contracting in the cooler, dry evenings. The sound of the landscape is a sparse, low-frequency symphony: the deep, occasional *clunk* of a giant latch dropping into place miles away; the dry, metallic rattle of a cable vibrating in a gust of wind; the soft, shifting hiss of graphite slides; and the distant, dry clicking of thousands of cable-walkers moving across the iron lattice of the sky. 

Everything moves with a patient, rhythmic delay. Nothing here is urgent. The heavy flywheels have enough inertia to spin for days even if the heat-flow stops. The herds of bronze-shelled creatures can remain stationary for months, anchored to a single spot on a metal-rich ledge, their carapaces slowly gathering a fine coating of golden dust. The processes of production, of life, and of geologic wear are all balanced, locked in a slow, warm, and dry equilibrium that feels as though it has existed forever, and will continue, unchanged, for as long as the sun warms the stone.
