The first thing that claims the senses is not the sight of the land, but its voice. 

It is a deafening, physical roar—not of a single waterfall or a crashing ocean, but the collective, vibrating hum of a flat world leaking. Across miles of grey, near-level bedrock, millions of tiny fissures, blowholes, and limestone siphons are constantly breathing. As the cool, heavy mist presses down from a leaden sky, the earth inhales and exhales the wetness through its ancient, hollow lungs. Every crack in the stone is an instrument. The air pressure, thick with condensation, forces water through these subterranean channels with a wet, flatulent thrumming that shakes the soles of the feet and rattles the teeth in one’s skull.

In this place, the concept of a mountain is a forgotten memory. The land has been planed down by eons of wind and water til it is almost entirely flat, a vast, low-lying floor of blue-grey basalt and dark siltstone. There are no sharp edges left. The rocks are bulbous, rounded like giant, sleeping beasts or river pebbles scaled up to the size of houses. Between these smooth, dark mounds lie vast, shallow sheets of standing water, rarely deeper than a man’s knee, spreading out toward a horizon that is lost in a permanent silver gloom. 

There is no green here. The plant kingdom never took root, or perhaps it died out so long ago that even its fossils have been ground to grey dust. Instead, the world is mineral and fungal. The wet flanks of the basalt mounds are coated in a thick, rubbery skin of liver-colored slime-molds and grey bacterial mats that feel like wet leather to the touch. In the deeper crevices, where the deafening wind cannot reach, grow immense, shelf-like structures of pale, chalky fungus. They do not have leaves; they have gills that slowly pulse, filtering the wet, nutrient-rich air.

Despite the lack of vegetation, the landscape is crawling with life. The dampness and the low, cool temperature—faint and shivering, like a late autumn dawn—suit the cold-blooded and the soft-bodied. 

Across the wet flats, things are moving. Thousands of flat, heavy-shelled creatures, shaped like broad stones and colored the same dull slate as the ground, scrape slowly across the slime-molds. They move on dozens of tiny, bristled legs, grazing on the biological film with a wet, rasping sound that is lost in the larger roar of the earth. Out in the shallow pools, long, pale worms with feathery, bioluminescent gills sway in the slow currents. Occasionally, a predator emerges: a multi-jointed, blind crawler, three feet long, its shell glistening with grease, stalking the grazers by sensing the vibrations of their scraping feet through the rock.

The sky is never clear. It is a wooly, moving ceiling of fog that hangs fifty feet above the water, shifting in slow, cold eddies. The light is a uniform, shadowless twilight, neither rising nor falling, as if the sun had stalled somewhere deep below the curve of the flat earth. 

Here and there, rising out of the shallow water like broken teeth, are the works of some simple, patient intelligence. 

These are low, circular enclosures made of stacked, flat slate stones. They are not grand; they are primitive windbreaks, perhaps eight feet across, built without mortar but fitted together with meticulous, heavy labor. Some are half-sunken into the silt, their walls greened over with slick algae. Inside these circles, the water has been benched back with packed mud and gravel to create small, damp platforms of dry ground. 

On these platforms, one can find the remnants of a very simple existence. There are heaps of cracked, empty shells from the flat-bodied grazers, and primitive tools fashioned from the heavy, mineralized thigh-bones of long-dead aquatic beasts. There are scrapers made of dark, flaked volcanic glass, tied to short bone handles with strips of dried animal sinew. In the center of several enclosures lie cold hearths, filled not with wood ash—for there is no wood—but with the greasy, black soot of burned animal fat and dried, turf-like blocks of compressed fungal fiber.

There are no inhabitants visible in these stone circles now, but they are not abandoned. Every so often, a wet, heavy print—like the mark of a broad, triple-toothed flipper with short, clawed digits—is stamped into the grey mud near the entrances, still sharp and filling slowly with seeping water.

The world moves in a heavy, cyclic rhythm. Every few hours, the subterranean pressure shifts. The deafening roar of the blowholes pitches upward into a whistling shriek, and then, with a great, collective sigh, the holes begin to suck air and water downward. The shallow pools drain in minutes, revealing vast plains of slick, dark mud that glisten under the dim sky. The shelled creatures retreat into the crevices of the basalt mounds, locking themselves down against the drying air. 

Then, as the cold fog thickens into a heavy, weeping drizzle, the pressure reverses. The deep earth groans—a sound that vibrates through the bones of the land—and the water comes gushing back up through the stone valves, foaming, cold, and loud, reclaiming the flats for another cycle of damp, noisy life.
