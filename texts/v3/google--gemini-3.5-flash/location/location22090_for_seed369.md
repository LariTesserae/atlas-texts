The place is a perched basin, suspended like a shallow bowl near the summit of a limestone ridge. It is small—perhaps eighty paces across—ringed by sheer, warm-toned stone walls that rise fifty feet before opening to the sky. Because of its height, the air is thin and clear, yet it is heavy with an unexpected, tropical warmth. Thermal vents deep within the mountain basin heat the soil from below, turning the cold mountain air into a constant, rising column of steam that condenses against the high cliffs. 

Water is the dominant architect here, though there are no running streams. Instead, it drips. The air is so laden with moisture that every surface is slick, sheathed in a thin, shimmering film. On the surrounding stone walls, the water does not run in streams but weeps, emerging from hidden pores in the limestone to form heavy, viscous droplets. These droplets are strange—they possess a high surface tension, gathering into perfect, trembling spheres the size of plums before they finally break free and plunge. 

Because of the basin’s shape and the acoustics of the smooth, weathered rock, the sound is immense, resonant, and slow. It is a percussive cathedral. When a heavy, oil-thick droplet falls from a high ledge, it strikes the flat leaves below with a deep, hollow *tock* that echoes off the limestone. Other drops land in deep, natural stone fonts with a metallic, ringing chime. The sounds do not crowd each other; they are separated by long seconds of anticipation. The air hums with these subterranean, liquid bells.

The vegetation is lush but remarkably sparse, organized like the columns of a temple rather than the tangle of a jungle. There is vast space between each living thing. Only three species thrive here, each reaching monumental proportions. Rising from the floor are several huge, pillar-like succulents, their skin the color of sage, ribbed like Greek columns and entirely free of thorns. From their crowns grow massive, paddle-shaped leaves that cup the condensing water. Across the basin floor stretches a thick, unbroken carpet of star-moss, nearly a foot deep, so dense and buoyant that it swallows the sound of footsteps completely. Spaced far apart, giant ferns with reddish-gold fronds arch gracefully over the moss, their tips dripping into the stone bowls.

There is a sense of great age and stability. The limestone walls are soft and rounded, water-slid into smooth chutes and bowls over millennia. This is not a landscape of violent erosion but of patient dissolving. The shapes are settled, comfortable in their own longevity. 

No structures have ever been built here, yet there are faint, ancient traces of a very primitive intelligence that once appreciated this sanctuary. Near one of the natural stone fonts, three flat stones have been wedged into the limestone fissure to form a crude, terraced spillway, directing the heavy water-drips away from a patch of delicate, yellow ferns. Nearby, a thick, hollowed-out stalk of a giant succulent has been propped against a weeping rock face, serving as a simple conduit to gather clean water into a mossy stone basin. These interventions are so old they have been colonized by the same grey lichen that covers the surrounding cliffs; they are less than tools, more like natural forms slightly nudged by a hand that departed long ago.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. The only movement comes from a species of large, terrestrial snail with a shell like polished obsidian. They move with geological slowness across the wet limestone, their paths marked by silver trails that take days to dry. They do not compete with the silence; they belong to the prevailing slowness of the place.

The light is bright and constant. The high sun strikes the rising mist, diffusing the glare into a warm, lime-gold glow that fills the amphitheater. Looking up, past the rim of the basin, one can see the fast-moving clouds of the high peaks, but down here, inside the bowl, the air is perfectly still. The warmth is cradled by the stone. Nothing rushes. The water forms, swells, hangs, and falls; the moss drinks; the steam rises. It is a world operating on a clock of drips and centuries.
