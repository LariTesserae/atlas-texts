The world is a drowned highland, held in a cold, eternal twilight beneath an iron sky—or perhaps beneath a ceiling of ice so thick that the sun is only a rumor. 

Here, the water does not merely sit; it commands. This is an ocean suspended at altitude, drowning a landscape of sub-alpine plateaus and jagged, basaltic peaks. The pressure is immense, yet it does not feel like the dead weight of the deep sea. It feels like a living, shifting bulk. The water is highly mineralized, viscous, and freezing, hovering just a fraction of a degree above the point where it would lock into ice. It moves in massive, slow-rolling thermal currents that slide over the mountain passes like drowning glaciers.

### The Terrain and Erosion

Below the surface, the landscape is one of ancient, weathered stone. These are not sharp, young volcanic rifts but old fold-mountains, their ridges rounded and smoothed by ages of glacial grinding before the water rose to claim them, and now further polished by the tireless licking of the cold currents. 

Deep, wide valleys stretch between the submerged ridges. The peaks of these mountains rise high into the water column, some coming near enough to the unseen surface that the water pressure lightens, though the darkness remains absolute. The valley floors are covered in a fine, dark silt—the dust of mountain-wear and the perpetual decay of the world's singular life.

### The Weird and Active Vegetation

There are no fish here. There are no crabs crawling in the silt, no worms in the mud, no jellies drifting in the cold currents. The animal kingdom does not exist. 

Instead, the flora has claimed every possible niche, developing a strange, active complexity to fill the silence. The vegetation is lush, but it is not crowded; it grows in vast, sparse groves separated by miles of empty, black water. 

Towering from the basalt ridges are *Glass-Ferns*, translucent structures of biological silica that stand fifty feet high. They do not bend with the currents; they vibrate, their stiff, crystalline fronds humming like tuning forks as the cold water brushes past them. 

Between these crystal groves lie the deep-valley *Siphon-Mosses*. These are massive, bulbous mounds of fibrous, charcoal-green pulp, some as large as houses. Because there is no animal life to distribute nutrients, these plants have developed a primitive, active pumping mechanism. They slowly expand and contract, drawing water in through porous skin, stripping it of minerals, and expelling it back into the dark with wet, heavy thumps. 

In the absolute darkness, the vegetation provides its own light—not the warm gold of fire, but a cold, pale mint-green and a faint, chemical violet. The luminescence does not flood the landscape; it merely defines it. From a mile away across a drowned valley, a grove of *Glass-Ferns* looks like a constellation of cold stars frozen in a green fog.

### The Sounds of the Abyss

This is not a quiet world. The water carries sound with terrifying efficiency, and the landscape is filled with a loud, rhythmic roar. 

Chief among the noises is the deep, tectonic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the *Siphon-Mosses* breathing in unison across the valleys—a sound so low and powerful it vibrates in the belly of the stone. 

Overlaying this rhythm is the high, metallic chiming of the *Glass-Ferns* as they brush against one another in the currents, sounding like a thousand crystal chandeliers swinging in a gale. 

Farther down, where the valleys plunge into deeper trenches, thermal geysers erupt from the earth. They do not steam, but rather hiss violently as superheated, sulfur-rich water collides with the freezing mountain sea, creating a constant, crackling roar like burning pine branches.

### The Built and the Primitive

Scattered across the ridges, highly rare but unmistakable, are the marks of some long-gone, primitive hand. 

These are not grand cities, but rough-hewn, dry-stone structures. Tilted pillars of dark basalt, stacked without mortar, stand on the high saddles of the mountains. They are held together in places by green-weathered, cold-hammered copper bands—thick, heavy, and crude, showing the marks of stone mallets. 

Some of these structures are simple circles, like drowned henges; others are low, beehive-like chambers that now serve as anchorage for the glowing lichens. There are no doors, no stairs, and no skeletons. It is unclear if these were built by land-dwellers before the great flood, or if they were constructed under eighty fathoms of water by some hand that did not require air. They feel ancient, balanced in their decay, having reached an equilibrium with the cold and the dark.

### The Dynamics of the World

The world is in constant, slow motion. The primary dynamic is the "ice-fall." Far above, where the mountain sea meets the frozen ceiling of the world, giant shards of ice occasionally break free, slowed by their buoyancy but dragged sideways by the wind-driven currents near the top. They do not float to the surface; instead, super-saline, freezing waterfalls of brine cascade down from the ice-sheet, plunging through the warmer water below like liquid glass. 

When these freezing plumes hit a grove of vegetation, the plants react. The *Siphon-Mosses* draw inside themselves, closing their copper-colored valves with loud *clacks*, while the *Glass-Ferns* grow brittle, shedding their outermost crystal needles to the valley floor with a sound like shattering mirrors.

Eventually, the brine plume dissipates. The plants reopen. The cold light returns to the valley. The silent, animal-less dark continues its slow, loud, and wet breathing, undisturbed by anything other than the shifting of its own cold waters.
