The place is a vertical throat in the earth, high up where the air of the outer world is thin and freezing, but here, inside, it is warm, heavy, and choked with life. 

This is the Oubliette of the Chimney-Dwellers, a single vertical shaft no larger than the nave of a small, ruined church. It sits thousands of feet above the tree line, a hidden volcanic flue bypassed by the mountain’s death, now acting as a giant radiator for a strange, self-contained micro-climate. From the freezing peaks above, cloud-mist is drawn down into this throat, condensing on the basalt walls in fat, heavy drops that never quite become streams. Instead, the water hangs in patches of slick, shiny grease on the rock, or collects in tiny, cupped depressions no larger than a fist.

The walls are not jagged. Aeons of rising thermal wind, coupled with the soft, relentless friction of millions of bodies, have worn the basalt into curves as smooth and dark as obsidian. Every surface is rounded, flowing, and wet. There are no corners, only deep, polished scoops where the stone has yielded to time.

But you cannot see the stone easily. The chimney is crowded to the point of suffocation. 

Almost every square inch of the vertical walls is occupied. They are covered in a dense, overlapping shingles of living things. These are the *Plinths*—flat, dinner-plate-sized organisms with thick, velvety hides of a deep charcoal grey. They do not have legs so much as a single, massive suction foot that grips the wet basalt. They are packed together like scales on a fish, near-touching, their edges slightly overlapping. Because of the warmth rising from the mountain’s deep core, they do not freeze; they thrive in a perpetual, stale heat, like the air inside an active stable.

Among the Plinths, there is a second layer of life. Tiny, blind, long-limbed creatures—neither quite ape nor crab, but possessing three jointed fingers on each of four limbs—scramble with agonizing slowness over the leathery backs of the larger beasts. These are the Scrapers. They are the keepers of a primitive, non-built culture. They do not build shelters, for there is no room, but they are tool-users. In the dark, you can hear them: the flat, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of basalt pebbles being used to crack open the hard, mineralized shells of the parasites that grow on the Plinths. 

These Scrapers have no metal, no fire, and no homes. Their entire technological existence is defined by the three or four smooth stones they wedge into the natural folds of their skin, and the thin, incredibly tough cords of dried root-vine they tie around their waists to anchor themselves to the rock during the long, slow shifts of the mountain's breath. They use these vine-cords to hang suspended in the pitch-black void, swinging with glacial patience from one side of the chimney to the other to trade stones or groom their kin.

Vegetation is a luxury here, almost non-existent. In the narrow, dripping grooves between the Plinths, where the stone is constantly wet, a pale, thread-like liverwort grows. It has no leaves, only white, hair-like fibers that feed on the nitrates of the animal waste. It glows with a faint, dying phosphorus, providing the only light in the shaft—a dim, sickly violet smudge that does not illuminate so much as emphasize the absolute darkness of the pit below and the throat above.

The noise is colossal, but it is not chaotic. It is a drone so deep and resonant that it vibrates in the soles of your feet and the cartilage of your ears. It is the sound of ten thousand heavy, leathery lungs expanding and contracting in the dark. It is the wet, rhythmic *shuck-shuck* of the Plinths sliding an inch to the left to catch a fresh drip of condensation. It is the constant, low-frequency purr of the Scrapers as they work, a sound like gravel turning in a slow cement mixer. And beneath it all is the occasional, crystal-clear *ping* of a water droplet falling fifty feet down the center of the shaft into the lightless pool at the bottom.

Everything here happens on a geologic scale of time. The Scrapers do not scurry; a single step from one Plinth to another takes several minutes. They raise a limb, hold it poised in the warm, damp air for a hundred heartbeats, and then lower it with deliberate, heavy precision. The Plinths themselves move perhaps a foot a week, migrating toward the shifting moisture patterns on the basalt. Even the air moves slowly, rising in lazy, hot drafts that take hours to exit into the freezing mountain night above.

There is no sky here, only the dark, wet curvature of the basalt dome overhead, where the chimney bends and narrows into a crack too tight for even the smallest Scraper. It is a world of skin, wet stone, the smell of hot wool and ammonia, and the endless, heavy vibration of a thousand hearts beating in the dark.
