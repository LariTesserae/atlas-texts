Down in the low sump of Basin Forty-Two, the air is thick enough to chew. It is a closed, rectangular yard, no larger than a small parish churchyard, locked between four sheer walls of dark, newly fired purple-black brick. 

Because the structures here are young, the edges of the masonry are sharp enough to cut a palm. The mortar joints are fresh, pale, and unweathered, showing no signs of crumble or salt-burst despite the stifling, boiling wetness of the air. The brickwork rises sixty feet into a ceiling of iron grates, through which a dim, slate-colored daylight filters down, choked by coal-soot and steam.

The temperature here is held at a constant, feverish heat. It does not fluctuate with day or night—indeed, there are no nights here, only the perpetual, yellow-grey gloom of the overhead grates. The heat is thick, wet, and smelling of sulfur, tallow, and wet coal ashes. Everything is slick with hot condensation. Boiling water weeps continuously from the seams of the brickwork, dripping down the smooth, dark faces of the walls to collect in shallow, hand-carved basalt runnels that cross the stone floor. These runnels are square-cut and sharp, showing the clean, un-eroded chisel marks of their recent manufacture.

The yard is not crowded. It is organized with a sparse, deliberate geometry. In the center sits a cluster of three massive, upright copper condensers, resembling giant, ribbed urns. They do not shake or rattle, but they hum with a deep, low-register vibration that rattles the teeth in one’s skull. A network of heavy, black iron pipes—each wrapped in wet, gray canvas lagging—straddles the yard, suspended ten feet above the wet basalt flagstones. From their valves, jets of white steam hiss steadily into the air, feeding the perpetual fog.

A few pale, sickly strings of wire-thin grass climb out of the mortar joins where the walls meet the floor, but vegetation is almost entirely absent. The heat and the chemical-rich waters have discouraged plants, save for a thin, greasy skin of rust-colored algae that coats the wet undersides of the copper vats. 

Yet, the basin is alive. The warm, mineral-slick floor is a paradise for a strange, silent infestation of pale-shelled river crabs. They are small, about the size of walnuts, with shells of a translucent, milky white. Thousands of them crawl slowly over the wet flagstones, clattering like dried beans as they climb over one another, feeding on the greasy algae and the soot that settles from the air. Along the lower three feet of the brick walls, fat, segmented steam-worms—dull orange and thumb-thick—cling to the wet mortar, their small, frilled gills pulsing in the hot run-off. High above, near the iron grates where the air is slightly cooler, swarms of heavy-bodied sulfur moths flutter in the steam, their greasy yellow wings catching the dull light of the wire-caged incandescent bulbs that hang from the piping.

There is no breeze. The air is perfectly stagnant, a heavy column of humid iron and sulfur that never lifts. The sounds are loud, mechanical, and entirely unchanging: the wet *hiss-hiss-hiss* of the escape valves, the rhythmic, heavy *chug-thud* of a piston pump working somewhere behind the southern brick wall, and the dry, paper-like rustle of millions of tiny crab legs scraping against the sharp, new stone.

Time has no meaning here. The pipes do not rust; the bricks do not decay; the crabs do not deplete their endless graze of soot and slime. The water rises from the earth, heats in the copper urns, condenses on the cold iron ceiling, and falls back to the stone floor in a rain of warm, greasy drops that has not stopped, and will not stop, for a hundred years.
