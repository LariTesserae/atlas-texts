Above the world’s weather, on a plateau so high the atmosphere feels thin and sharp yet carries an oppressive, radiating heat, lies the basalt labyrinth of the Obsidian Teeth. 

To look across this landscape is to look at a horizon of infinite, overlapping vertical lines. The terrain is composed of millions of hexagonal basalt columns, packed together with almost no space between them. They rise like tightly bundled wheat, towering thirty to fifty meters into the bright, dry air. This is not a flat highland, but a saturated forest of stone. The columns are so densely clustered that a traveler cannot walk between them without turning sideways, squeezing through corridors of smooth, polished rock that have stood in this exact, balanced state of wear for millions of years. The edges of the stone are not jagged or fresh; they are weathered to a satin finish by the constant friction of rising thermals and ancient dust.

The heat here is a heavy, living pressure—a registration of nearly three out of three on the thermal scale. The sun sits high and brilliant in a sky of hard, pale violet, baking the tops of the pillars until they are hot enough to sear skin. Yet, because of the extreme elevation and the thermal drafts rising from the deep, damp lowlands far below, this heat is wet. 

Water does not gather in rivers or lakes here. Instead, it exists as a pervasive, intermittent dampness. Every morning, as the cool air of the high altitude meets the rising heat of the lower basin, a dense, warm fog rolls through the spires. It does not rain in the traditional sense; rather, the water condenses directly onto the basalt. Heavy, fat droplets of mineral-thick moisture form on the stone faces, running down the fluted grooves of the pillars in slow, greasy tracks. Small cup-like pockets, hollowed out of the pillar tops by long-lost water patterns, hold warm, stagnant pools the size of dinner plates. These tiny basins are the lifeblood of the plateau.

Vegetation is a rare, desperate thing in this vertical world. There is no soil to speak of. What few plants exist are sparse and specialized: rubbery, lime-green lichens that coat the northern faces of the basalt, and occasional, corky air-plants that wedge their tough, fibrous roots into the narrow joints between columns. These plants do not have leaves so much as thick, water-storing blisters that swell during the morning condensation and shrivel to gray husks by midday.

In stark contrast to the sparse greenery, animal life is abundant, hyper-adaptable, and strangely hushed. The dense vertical cracks of the columns are a thriving hive. Flat-bodied, scale-skinned gliders—neither lizard nor bird—shimmy effortlessly up the basalt lanes, using wide, sticky pads on their undersides. They move in silent, synchronized ripples across the stone, hunting the millions of hard-shelled, steam-breathing beetles that crawl along the condensation lines to drink. Small, silent flyers with membranous wings coast on the hot thermals that rise between the pillars, dropping like stones to snatch prey before vanishing back into the shadows of the rock-forest.

The silence here is nearly total, despite the immense biological activity. The density of the stone columns acts as a perfect acoustic baffle; any sound made is immediately absorbed, trapped, and deadened within a three-meter radius. The gliders do not cry out; they communicate through subtle vibrations pressed against the rock. The flyers move without the sound of feathers. The only natural noise is the rhythmic, heavy *plip-plop* of condensation falling into the stony cups, and the low, hollow sigh of the hot wind passing over the tips of the needles.

Yet, this quiet is occasionally cut by a deep, subsonic hum—the signature of the few, scattered industrial installations that have been wedged into this wilderness. 

These structures are rare but formidable. Every few miles, a heavy, automated copper condenser station is bolted directly into the basalt pillars, straddling them like a metallic arachnid. These machines are products of a refined, heavy-industrial age. They do not burn fuel; instead, they operate on the intense thermal differential between the baking tops of the pillars and the cool, damp drafts in the narrow crevices below. Heavy turbines, buried deep within the stone clusters, turn with a slow, grinding patience, generating electricity that is carried away by thick, grease-coated copper cables slung between the columns. 

The stations are unmanned. They hiss softly as they extract minerals and distilled water from the morning mists, storing them in massive, spherical brass vats that cling to the sides of the cliffs like giant insect eggs. Every few hours, a mechanical valve on one of these vats releases a puff of superheated steam with a quiet, pneumatic *shuck-hiss*, a brief white cloud that is instantly swallowed by the dry, hot air.

There is a strange, balanced rhythm to this place. It is a landscape of extremes—of dizzying height and claustrophobic density, of mechanical industry and primitive, gliding beasts. Everything here has found its niche. The machines hum in their iron cradles; the gliders hunt in the damp shadows; the water rises as steam and falls as sweat; and the ancient, polished stone holds it all, unmoving, under the glare of the high, violet sky.
