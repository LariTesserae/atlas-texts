Underneath the low-hanging cast-iron structural beams of some ancient, forgotten generator-vault lies a narrow, dry conduit. This pocket of space is no wider than a man’s reach and perhaps ten yards long—a low, sunken gutter worn into the bedrock of the world. 

There is no water here, nor has there ever been. The air has the cool, crisp quality of an attic in autumn, bone-dry and smelling of zinc, slate-dust, and cold lard. In place of moisture, a fine, self-lubricating talcum of graphite and pulverized lead coats every surface, making the steep, shallow slope of the trench incredibly slick. 

The stone itself is inconceivably old. The basalt floor of the conduit is not jagged or cracked; it has been ground into smooth, curving hollows and polished channels by endless plates of friction. It looks like a dried riverbed, but one carved by the passage of heavy, dry solids over billions of cycles rather than water. Every corner of the masonry is rounded, soft, and pre-warned, having long since settled into its final, most stable geometry.

From the ceiling of this trench hangs the low industrial tech of an older epoch: a series of small, interlocking copper cams and drive-rods. They do not hiss or steam. Rather, they rotate with a dry, mechanical *clank-shirr-clank*, driven by some distant, slow-turning spindle deep inside the floor. These brass rods are clean, kept free of rust by the absolute absence of humidity, though they are worn thin where they meet their iron brackets. Simple, unblinking light-wells—square holes cut upward through twelve feet of masonry—cast a flat, gray daylight down into the trench, illuminating the space with a dry, shadows-less clarity.

But the most striking aspect of this miniature landscape is its density of life. 

The floor of the conduit is a roiling, heap-like carpet of small, hard-bodied creatures. They are roughly the size of pocket-watches, shaped like flat, segmented shields of matte-gray ceramic. There are thousands of them in this single ten-yard channel, stacked three or four deep, climbing endlessly over one another in a frantic, clicking tide. They have no eyes; their heads terminate in pair of hard, chisel-like mandibles that they use to scrape the graphite dust from the basalt walls or to shave microscopic flakes of copper from the rotating industrial rods above.

The noise is immense and dry. It is a collective, rushing rattle—like a million dry autumn leaves being stirred in a spinning drum, punctuated by the sharp *snip-snip* of their ceramic shells colliding. Underneath this rustle is the steady, rhythmic *clack-shirr* of the overhead brass rods.

The ecosystem here is highly active, dry-burning, and packed to the point of suffocation. Because there are no plants, the entire web of life is based on the consumption of the mineral lubricants and the predation of one creature upon another. Now and then, a larger variant of the shield-creatures—distinguished by its pale, chalk-white shell and longer, needle-like legs—will plunge beneath the gray carpet. A sudden, violent eddy ripples through the swarm as the smaller creatures scramble to escape, their hard shells scraping against the polished basalt walls with a sound like broken plates sliding across stone. 

Occasionally, one of the copper cams overhead drops a single flake of soft metal. Instantly, fifty of the creatures pile onto the spot, a scrambling, heaping pyramid of gray segments, locking their limbs together and biting at one another’s jointed armor to get at the prize. They do not bleed when damaged; they simply crack, spilling a pinch of dry white powder before being ground into dust by the thousands of scurrying feet of their neighbors.

Everything in this little ditch is in motion. The machinery turns, the gray carpet of shells shifts and hums, the light remains flat and cool, and the ancient stone beneath them continues its incomprehensibly slow wear, polished further by every second of this dry, crowded existence.
