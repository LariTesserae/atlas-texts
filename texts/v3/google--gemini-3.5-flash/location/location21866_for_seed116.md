The world here is not stone, nor silt, nor sky. It is a single, vertical conduit—a forgotten maintenance sleeve known only as *Sluice 12-B*—sunk into the guts of an infinite, watery engine. 

To stand within it, or rather to suspend oneself within it, is to inhabit a chamber of drowning warmth. The space is tiny, barely four meters across, a perfect cylinder of green-grey bronze that runs straight up and down into the darkness. It is an intimate, claustrophobic chimney of a room, yet its verticality is absolute. Looking down, the water darkens from jade to a bruised, heavy indigo; looking up, it rises toward a distant, flickering luminescence. There is no floor, no ceiling, only an endless drop through warm, highly pressurized brine. 

Everything here was made. Every surface is an ancient, manufactured plate of iron-heavy bronze, secured with rivets the size of dinner plates. But time has done the work of rivers to these metals. The sharp edges of the industrial plates have been buffed down by geological eras of rushing water; the rivets have been rounded into smooth, metallic domes that resemble river pebbles; the green patina on the brass is inches thick, soft and spongy to the touch like wet velvet. Deep, smooth runnels have been carved into the solid metal walls by the silent, eternal friction of the rising currents, turning the industrial interior into something resembling a polished limestone cavern.

The water of the Sluice is warm—nearly the temperature of blood—and heavy with the taste of sulfur, old copper, and lubricating grease that has been emulsified into the water over ten thousand years. It is a thick, highly active soup. The entire column of water hums. From deep below, through the metal walls, comes a rhythmic, subsonic thuddering—the heartbeat of some colossal, drowning piston miles down in the dark. Every few minutes, a cluster of hot, silvery steam bubbles rises from the depths, clattering against the rounded bronze walls like a handful of thrown coins as they rush toward the surface.

In this narrow column, life does not merely exist; it clings and thrives in concentrated pockets. Because the walls are mostly smooth, the vegetation has clustered in the joints and seams where the giant plates meet. In these horizontal crevices, lush, weeping gardens of water-ferns and long, fat-leaved kelp-ribbons hang out into the current. These plants are a rich, sickly yellow-green, fed by the warmth of the metal plate-seams and the faint, amber light that leaks from thick, glass-lensed portholes set into the walls. Between these hanging gardens, there are long stretches of bare, pitted brass, giving the space a clean, balanced emptiness—small, dense oases of life separated by vertical gulfs of open, humming water.

The fauna of the Sluice have long since adapted to this mechanical ecology. Tiny, pale crabs with legs like thin copper wires crawl through the roots of the water-ferns, their shells stained with rust. They graze on the heavy orange iron-bacteria that coats the colder rivets. Transparent glass-eels, their internal organs visible as tiny, ticking gears of red and white, drift in the lee of the plant clusters, waiting for the microscopic detritus that falls from above. 

There is a strange, low-frequency music to this pocket. The sound of the water is a roaring, muffled hum, punctuated by the mechanical *clank-hiss* of valves opening and closing in adjacent shafts. Sometimes, a high, whistling screech echoes through the metal—the sound of high-pressure liquid being forced through a pinhole miles away—before dying down into the steady, vibrating sigh of the Sluice’s own upward draft. 

The environment is in constant, gentle motion. The ferns sway continuously in the warm, rising updraft; the glass-eels flit from seam to seam; the orange bacteria slowly, invisibly digest the ancient ironwork. It is a tiny, self-contained world of warm metal and wet green, a single vertical pipe where the boundary between machine and wilderness has been erased by time, heat, and the endless passage of water.
