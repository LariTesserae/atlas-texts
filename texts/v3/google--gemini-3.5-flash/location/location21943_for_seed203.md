There is no stone here. 

You stand upon a ledge of raw, wet heartwood that drops away into five miles of vertical blue-white nothingness. If you were to dig your fingers into the ground, you would find not dirt or gravel, but a dense, interlocking carpet of frozen pale moss, beneath which lies the fibrous, ringed flesh of a colossal root system. 

This is the Unter-Canopy, a subterranean rift so cavernous that it possesses its own cold, circular wind systems. The scale is monumental, yet the density of the space is almost entirely vacuum. It is a vast, air-filled hollow inside a world made of solid vegetable mass. To look across the gulf is to look see-through miles of pale, glinting air toward the distant, ghostly silhouettes of other vertical trunks—some fifty miles wide—stretching from an unimaginable floor to a ceiling of compressed, blackening peat so far above that it looks like a flat slate sky.

The light is blinding, but it possesses no warmth. It does not come from a sun, but from the very wood itself. The cambium of these giant structures is packed with a cold, dry, bio-phosphorescent marrow that glows with the uniform, shadowless intensity of an overcast winter noon. It reflects off the ice-crystals that cling to every surface, creating a silent, shimmering glare that forces you to squint. 

Because of the cold—which hovers constantly just above freezing—the moisture in this world is scarce and precious, behaving in strange, localized ways. It does not rain in the traditional sense; instead, the warmth of the glowing wood occasionally coaxes a fine, sweating condensation from its own bark. This moisture instantly freezes upon meeting the cavern’s drafty air, forming delicate, razor-sharp "frost-fringe" that grows horizontally from the wood like white eyelashes. Sometimes, a thermal draft will rip these frost-fringes loose, sending a blizzard of dry, glittering needles screaming through the empty space between the trunks.

The landscape is violently young. The features here are sharp, jagged, and unweathered. No ancient rains have lived here long enough to smooth the contours. Massive splinters—some the size of cathedrals—have recently torn away from the main pillars, leaving behind raw, yellow-green scars that have not yet had time to grow a protective layer of grey bark. The edges of these fractures are like glass shards, completely un-eroded, clean and lethal.

The dynamic here is one of chaotic, turbulent growth and structural failure. The world is too fast for its own weight. Under the influence of the cold light, the cellulose walls grow with an aggressive, unstable speed. You can hear it if you press your ear to the cold wood: not a heartbeat, but a rhythmic, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of expanding fibers, interspersed with the occasional, deep-throated *BOOM* that echoes across the empty miles as a trunk miles away buckles under its own massive, lopsided weight. The landscape is a slow-motion storm of buckling, snapping, and self-repair.

In the midst of this sterile, colossal glare, the signs of life are few but distinct. 

High up on the face of a vertical fiber-wall, where the wood has split into a series of jagged, step-like tiers, there are faint, scattered signs of habitation. They are small, primitive things, dwarfed by the environment. A series of suspension walkways—not made of metal, but of thick, hand-braided hair-grass—clashes against the smooth curve of the trunk. They are anchored into the bark with massive, fire-hardened wooden wedges. 

Here and there, tucked into natural hollows where the sap has dried into a hard, amber resin, are small, basket-like shelters. They are constructed from woven peelings of inner bark, lashed together with dried vine-tendrils. There is no fire here; there is nothing to burn that isn't their very home, and the air is too thin and damp to easily hold a spark. Instead, the small inhabitants of these nests seem to use the glowing amber of the sap-deposits to warm their hands, scraping at the resinous walls with primitive, hand-carved scrapers made of dried beetle-shells.

The fauna of this world is rare, quiet, and specialized. Once in a great while, a shadow will pass across the glare of the vertical white walls—a drift-glider, a creature with a flat, leaf-like body three meters wide, with no legs or eyes, only long, sensory ribbons that trail behind it as it rides the cold thermal updrafts from the bottom of the rift. It makes no sound. It feeds on the microscopic spores that drift through the air like dust. 

Mostly, the world is quiet. The vast air-volume swallows sound, digesting it in the empty spaces between the pillars. But when the silence is broken, it is broken utterly. A thermal wind-shear will hiss through a gap in the root-mesh with the sound of a tearing sheet, followed by the terrifying, slow-motion groan of a fifty-ton branch shearing off its mount, falling silently through the white glare for ten, twenty seconds, before disappearing into the mist below with a faint, distant thud that vibrates through the soles of your feet.

Then, the cold, brilliant quiet returns. The frost-fringes begin to grow again, millimeter by millimeter, locking the sharp fragments of the world back into a temporary, frozen stasis.
