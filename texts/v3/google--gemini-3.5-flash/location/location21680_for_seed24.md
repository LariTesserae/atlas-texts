This is a basin of hot, dry silt, tucked deep beneath the massive, overhanging brim of a collapsed limestone shelf. 

The air here is thick, warm, and entirely devoid of moisture. It carries the faint, dry scent of parched minerals and ancient, heat-baked clay—a steady, clean warmth, like the air inside an oven that was shut off hours ago but refuses to cool. There is no weather here. No breeze enters this deep pocket, and the air sits as still as water in a sealed jar.

The floor of the basin is a flat, shallow bowl, roughly fifty yards across. It is covered in a fine, heavy dust that has long since finished settling. It does not drift or rise in plumes; it has compacted under its own weight over centuries into a smooth, pale grey crust. Out of this crust rise low, rounded boulders of dark, iron-dense basalt. They are smooth to the touch, their edges softened by some ancient, long-ceased process of erosion that occurred before the shelf collapsed to seal this place away. They sit in the dust like sleeping animals, widely spaced, with several paces of empty, flat ground between each one.

In the deep, velvet gloom of the chamber, there is almost no light. The sky is entirely blocked by the heavy roof of stone, leaving only a soft, featureless twilight at the basin's outer edges where the overhang meets the dark cliffs. In the center, the darkness is nearly complete. The eyes must adjust for a long time before the faint, pale shapes of the dust-flats and the dark silhouettes of the boulders begin to separate themselves from the blackness.

In the dry spaces between the boulders, life has reached a permanent, dormant compromise. There are no leaves, no green, and no roots searching for water. Instead, small, isolated tufts of a wire-like, ash-grey vegetation grow directly from the dry silt. These plants are brittle, like charcoal drawings of desert grass. They do not grow, nor do they rot; they exist in a state of suspended preservation. Their stiff, slender stalks do not move, because there is no wind to stir them. They are spaced far apart, single entities claiming their own small territories of dry earth, utterly quiet.

The silence here is heavy and thick, pressing against the ears with a physical weight. There are no insects to hum, no water to drip, and no loose stones left to slide. Once every few weeks, perhaps, a microscopic grain of salt on the underside of the limestone roof might lose its grip and fall, landing in the dust with a tiny, dry *tick* that is instantly swallowed by the emptiness. Otherwise, the stillness is absolute.

Near the center of the basin, resting on a flat, low slab of basalt, lies the only sign that this place was ever known by any living thing. It is not a building, nor a carving, but a small cluster of three knapped scraper tools. They are chipped from a dark, glassy chert, their edges still sharp, shaped by hands that left them here thousands of years ago. They were laid down side by side in the dust, and they have not moved a fraction of a millimeter since. The hands that made them are gone, but the tools remain, resting in the warm, dark silence, preserved perfectly by the absolute lack of moisture, wind, or change. 

Everything here has found its final place. The rocks are settled, the dust is still, the air is warm and dry, and the darkness holds them all in an unbreakable, timeless equilibrium.
