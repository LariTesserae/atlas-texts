The rock here has no memory of wind. It is too young, too newly thrust into the sky to have lost its sharp edges. These are peaks made of fractures and fresh cleavages, slate that splinters like slate-green glass underfoot, and basalt blocks so sharp-cornered they look as though they were quarried yesterday. Everything is vertical, steep, and jagged, rising into a thin, cold altitude where the air tastes of sulfur and wet iron. 

It is a landscape of cold moisture. The clouds do not hang above the mountains; they drift through them, dragging heavy, wet bellies across the raw stone. Water is everywhere, but it does not flow in rivers or gather in wide lakes. Instead, it weeps. It seeps from the fractures of the newly exposed rock, clinging to the dark stone in shivering sheets before freezing into thin, black glazes of ice. In the small, flat saddles between the peaks, the ground is a cold, black mush of mineral silt and waterlogged gravel, crusted over with a fragile skin of frost that needles under pressure.

Vegetation is a rare, desperate thing. There are no trees to break the wind or soften the horizon. In the crevices where a little silt has gathered, there are patches of wire-thick, black grass that look more like horsehair than plant life. Orange and soot-gray lichens paint the rock faces in splotches, surviving on the minerals dissolved in the constant run-off. 

Yet, the heights are far from dead. Animal life thrives in this high, wet cold, adapted to the verticality and the chill. Herds of heavy-bodied, low-slung hoofed beasts climb the impossible shale slopes. Their coats are thick, greasy, and double-layered, smelling of wet wool and peat; they move with a slow, deliberate grace, their wide, splayed hooves clicking against the stone as they graze on the sparse black grass and lichens. Flocks of fat, pale-winged birds roost in the deep, un-weathered fissures, their feathers coated in a natural oil that repels the constant, freezing mist. They make no sound but a soft, dry clicking of their beaks, a noise that carries through the damp air like pebbles rattling in a cup.

Through this wilderness of rock and mist move the machines.

They are not housed in factories; there are no buildings here, no sheds, no yards, no concrete foundations. The technology of this place is nomadic, un-sheltered, and raw. Massive, twelve-legged crawling rigs—each the size of a coastal freighter—lumber along the ridges. They are automated, self-sustaining industrial beasts, their iron bodies streaked with rust, oil, and wet soot. They have no cabins or windows, for there are no crews to look out of them. Instead, they are all boiler, piston, and generator, humming with a deep, electric vibration that rumbles in the soles of your feet long before you hear it through the thin air.

These crawlers move along the ridges with the same slow purpose as the mountain herds. Every mile or so, one of them will pause, its giant pneumatic legs locking into the raw basalt with a heavy, metallic crunch. A massive, diamond-tipped drill will emerge from its underbelly, grinding into the mountain to extract raw, high-grade copper or tin, spitting out heaps of crushed, sparkling gravel. 

The local fauna are entirely indifferent to them. A herd of the woolly, six-legged grazers will huddle beneath the massive, warm exhaust vents of a parked crawler, their backs steaming in the heat of its copper boilers while the machine’s great piston drives rhythmically above them with a muffled, oily *thwip-clap*. The birds nest in the dead spaces between the machine’s outer cooling fins, insulated from the freezing mist by the ambient warmth of the dynamos.

The scale of the landscape is immense, but it is empty of human presence. Stand on a high, wet ledge, and you can see twenty miles of jagged, gray-and-black peaks disappearing into the low, zinc-colored clouds. The view is a vast, repeating pattern of raw stone, creeping fog, and distant, lonely giants. Ten miles away, the silhouette of another crawler can be seen perched on a knife-edge ridge, its tall chimney releasing a thin, dark line of coal-smoke that is immediately torn apart by the cold wind. 

It is mostly quiet. The density of things is so low, and the space so vast, that sound has a hard time traveling. The wind makes a low, whistling hiss through the sharp stone chimneys. The sheep click their hooves; the water drips. Every few minutes, the silence is punctuated by the far-off, dull *thud-thud-thud* of a pile-driver on a distant peak, or the screech of iron treads on raw slate as a crawler rotates its colossal bulk toward a new seam. The sounds are damp, clean, and quickly swallowed by the fog, leaving only the smell of cold water and hot machine grease lingering in the frozen air.
