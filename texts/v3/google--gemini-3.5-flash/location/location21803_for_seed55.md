The water is not an active force here; it is a permanent, freezing weight. It fills everything, stretching upward for miles to some forgotten ceiling or sky, and downward into the heavy, pressurized dark. It is clear but utterly black, cold enough to sluggishly numb any warmth, yet it never freezes. It is a slow, silent medium that holds all things in place.

Below, the ground is not sand, nor silt, nor volcanic rock. The basin of this world is entirely constructed. If you clear away the thick layers of organic sediment that have settled over millions of years, you find a floor of interlocking, dark basaltic tiles. Each slab is vast—the size of a town square—and carved with simple, shallow grooves to channel the deep currents. Over epochs of water-wear, the sharp edges of these monumental blocks have been rounded. The joints between them have softened into deep, gentle troughs, like the worn flagstones of an ancient cathedral, but written on a continental scale. 

From these deep joints grows the vegetation. 

It is a forest that knows no autumn and no sun. The plants are chemotrophic, feeding on the cold, mineral-rich seepages that rise through the microscopic seams of the tiled floor. Because there is no light to chase, they do not grow toward a surface; they grow outward and upward in dense, tangled columns, forming three-dimensional labyrinths of rubbery, black-veined kelp and thick, pale moss. The fronds are heavy, broad, and flesh-like to the touch, designed to resist the slow, crushing currents. They are packed together so tightly that to move through them is to constantly bushwhack through cold, wet velvet. 

There is no animal life. No fish slide through the dark lanes between the kelp; no crabs scuttle across the tiled floor; no worms burrow into the silt. The silence is thick, broken only by the low, sub-audible groan of the ocean shifting its weight against the masonry, or the occasional soft *shuck* of two massive rubbery leaves brushing past one another in a change of current. It is a world of pure botany and stone, breathing in a cycle so slow it mimics death.

Yet, despite the lack of fauna, the forest is not wild. It has been cultivated, and continues to be held in a state of primitive, manual order.

Everywhere among the kelp-forests are the frames. They are constructed from the heavy, water-logged trunks of petrified wood, dragged from some deeper, older layer of the world and lashed together with thick, coarse ropes braided from split reeds. These wooden scaffolds are not grand or complex; they are held together by simple wooden pegs, heavy stone weights, and basic knot-work. They are the work of hand-tools, of wedges and stone soft-hammers. 

These trellises run for thousands of miles through the darkness, keeping the heavy, black mosses from collapsing into heaps on the tiled floor. Simple, hand-carved stone pegs have been driven into the seams of the basalt tiles to anchor guide-lines made of braided plant fiber. These lines rise straight up through the water, disappearing into the blackness above, weighted with rough-hewn rings of grey slate. 

It is a garden on the scale of a planet, maintained by the simplest possible means. There are no machines here, no engines, no metal tools. The wooden timbers of the scaffolding are dark and slimy with cold-water algae, but they are structurally sound, held together by the sheer weight of the water and the simple, redundant knots of the braided ropes. Who or what tied these knots is unclear; there are no corpses, no dwellings, no ruins other than the scaffolding itself. The work is so ancient that the ropes have begun to mineralize, slowly turning to stone while still holding their form.

The only light is a faint, cold bioluminescence that bleeds from the growth-nodes of the giant mosses. It is not a bright light; it does not illuminate the space so much as it marks the boundaries of the dark. It is a pale, watery green, casting long, unmoving shadows through the dense walls of the forest. If you stand in one of the paved corridors between the kelp-walls, you can see these faint green dots stretching out in perfect, straight lines into the distance, tracing the seams of the great tiled floor until they merge into a single, dim thread and vanish into the absolute black of the deep.

Nothing changes quickly here. A single leaf may take a decade to turn from the pale white of its sprouting phase to the deep, lightless purple of its maturity. The currents, driven by the slow rotational drag of the planet, take centuries to reverse their direction, gently leaning the entire continental forest westward, then, ages later, leaning it back to the east. The water cushioned everything long ago. The world has reached its final, cold, and beautifully constructed equilibrium—a quiet, paved garden drowning in the dark.
