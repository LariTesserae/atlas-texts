The air changes three hundred steps down. The chill of the upper shafts does not fade so much as it is crushed by a sudden, heavy wave of wet heat. 

This is the Sinking of the Ochre Spindle—a subterranean pocket no larger than a municipal square, yet packed with the density of an ancient city center. It exists in a raw, freshly split fault of black-and-grey basalt. The rock faces here are jagged and fiercely sharp; the chisel marks of steam-driven wedge-drills are still clean and white along the corners of the cliffs, unsoftened by time or wind. There has been no time for weathering.

The pocket is a vertical well, perhaps eighty yards across, walled by sheer, newly broken stone. Down in this pit, space is at an absolute premium. The architecture does not sit upon the floor; it clings, stacks, and nests in the crags. It is a jungle of copper-sleeved cabins, iron-grate walkways, and tiny, windowless workshops made of mortared slate, all piled one on top of the other like ballast. There are no streets, only a dizzying web of ladders, chain-hoists, and narrow gangways slick with grease and condensated steam. 

Everything here is damp, though it does not rain. Instead, the heat—radiating from geothermal vents and the slow, heavy boilers of small workshops—draws moisture out of the dark rock. Water gathers on the cold iron plates of the under-structures, forming fat, sluggish droplets that hang like beads before falling into the dark. It is an environment of drips, trickles, and occasional sudden spurts of scalding, oily mist from pressure-relief valves.

The plant life has colonized this moisture with terrifying speed. Thick, waxy ferns with fronds the color of bruised plums squeeze out of every gap between the masonry and the stone. Pale, bone-white creepers wrap themselves around the copper steam pipes, their roots boring into the joints to drink the mineral-rich leaks. These vines do not grow toward a sun they have never known; instead, they grow toward heat. They swarm over the casings of small, slow-turning engines, blanketing the cold-rolling mills and the small mechanical looms in blankets of hair-thin, rust-colored moss. 

The plants act as a massive acoustic sponge. Sound here does not carry; it is deadened immediately by the dense, wet leafage and the thick layers of soot-covered mold that coat the stone walls. A piston stroke—a heavy, blind thump of iron against brass—occurs only once every twelve seconds, and its sound is muffled, reduced to a soft, wet thud that feels more like a pressure wave in the ears than a noise. The voices of the builders, when they speak at all, are low and flat, swallowed by the moss before they can travel ten feet. 

There is an scale-like order to this clutter, but it is one born of intense, cramped necessity rather than planning. Every family or workshop crew occupies a space no bigger than a ship’s cabin. Overhead, the "sky" is a dense thatch of suspension cables, heavy copper power-lines, and the dripping bellies of higher buildings.

Light is a rare, precious fluid. There are no glass lamps; instead, the residents use small, iron-caged burners that consume oil extracted from the waxy ferns. These produce a dim, amber glow that barely pushes past the nearest leaves. Here and there, a pocket of pale green light marks a patch of phosphorescent fungus cultivated on the wet wood of the structural pillars. It is a world of deep, warm shadows, where one must navigate by the feel of the handrails and the heat of the nearest steam line.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, unable or unwilling to navigate the dense, metallic foliage. Sometimes, a blind, white-scaled land crab, no larger than a thumb, can be seen clinging to a slowly rotating drive-shaft, picking away at the grease-traps with tiny, delicate pincers. They do not scurry; like everything else in the Sinking, they move with a mechanical, patient sloth.

Nothing changes quickly here. The boilers are stoked with slow-burning peat cut from wet rifts deeper down; they simmer for months without maintenance. The plants creep down the walls millimeter by millimeter, their roots slowly prying open the seams of the newly laid dry-stone walls. The people live in the same rhythmic, dampened silence, moving through the humid dark with the slow, purposeful strides of divers under deep water. It is a place that feels as if it has just begun, yet has already run out of room to grow.
