The descent is a matter of ladders—spruce logs with rungs of notched birch, lashed together with tarred cord and pinned into the raw vertical faces of the rock.

This is the Rift of the Seventh Column, a massive fault-line sink that drops eighty meters below the level of the surrounding pine barren before widening into a flat-bottomed trench nearly two miles long. Up above, the sky is a clean, pale blue that lets down a steady, neutral daylight; down here, the air immediately loses its dry wind and grows cool and heavy with the scent of wet stone.

The rock is incredibly young, geologically speaking. There is no sand here, no rounded river-pebbles, no soft banks of silt. The walls of the chasm are vertical sheets of deep charcoal-colored basalt that have split along perfect hexagonal joints. The edges of these stone prisms are so sharp they could cut a sleeve. No wind has yet lived long enough to blunt them; no ancient rains have carved runnels into their faces. The stone looks as if it were quarried only this morning by a giant’s iron wedges, though the fissure has been here for a generation. It is raw, black, and crystalline, glistening with an oily sheen where the internal moisture of the deep earth seeps out through the microscopic cracks.

Because of this absolute lack of natural soil, the floor of the trench is a chaotic floor of sharp talus—slate-dark, angular fragments ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a hay-cart. Every flat space has had to be made by hand.

And there are almost no flat spaces left.

The density of human presence here is overwhelming, packed into the narrow trench like salt-herring in a cask. Lacking the space to spread out, the settlement has grown upward and inward, clinging to the basalt columns like a crust of barnacles on a wharf pile. Thousands of small, box-like dwellings are stacked on top of one another, utilizing the natural stepped shelves of the basalt. Where the rock is sheer, heavy pine timbers have been driven into the vertical joints, supporting precarious platforms of split slab-wood. 

Over these platforms rise huts of dark, sun-dried clay and unpeeled fir poles. The roofs are flat, shingled with plates of native slate that have been split with simple iron wedges. There are no streets, only a bewildering maze of narrow gangways, swing-bridges made of woven hemp rope, and vertical ladders. One man’s roof is another man’s front porch; a cooking hearth of flat stone sits directly above the shuttered window of a neighbor's sleeping cell. The structures are so tightly packed that from the center of the trench, the black rock of the walls is visible only in narrow, vertical stripes between the gray timber facades.

Everything is damp. The cool air of the trench causes the water vapor rising from several thousand cooking fires to condense against the cold stone of the chasm walls. It runs down the basalt in slow, silent weepings, dripping from the undersides of the timber platforms and gathering in tiny, lens-shaped pools in the hollows of the slate roofs. Occasionally, a brief, fine drizzle falls from the strip of sky above, lasting only a few minutes before the high winds of the upper plateau carry the clouds away. 

Vegetation is a luxury. In the small, shaded crevices where the damp is constant, there are patches of a pale, rubbery liverwort that looks like spilled green wax. Some residents have dragged baskets of acidic forest loam down from the surface, balancing them on their window ledges to grow single stalks of bitter cabbage or small, white-rooted radishes. These tiny pots of dark earth are the only spots of brown in a world of gray wood and black stone.

The silence of this crowded place is striking. Despite the thousands of people living shoulder-to-shoulder, there is no roar of traffic or clamor of heavy machinery. The technology here is manual, tedious, and quiet. Men work with bone-handled adzes, slowly squaring green timbers; women spin dark wool using simple drop-spindles made of soapstone whorls; stone-cutters tap at the basalt with soft iron chisels, the sound a rhythmic, damp *clink-clink-clink* that is swallowed almost instantly by the felt-like dampness of the air. Everyone speaks in a low, flat undertone. To shout in the Rift is considered an act of violence, an intrusion on the precious, crowded privacy of one’s neighbors. 

Even the fauna has adapted to this quiet. There are no dogs. The only animals are small, short-furred mountain goats that are kept tethered on the larger stone terraces, their hooves muffled with wraps of old rag-cloth to prevent them from slipping on the damp stone or making too much noise on the timber bridges. Now and then, a solitary gray kite will drift down from the upper rim, its wings motionless as it circles between the dense rows of hanging laundry, looking for scraps of dried fish or the small, blind cave-crickets that occasionally crawl from the deep cracks.

Yet, despite the quiet, the landscape is not static. It is a place of constant, slow-motion movement. The earth itself is still settling. Every few days, a deep, resonant *thrum* vibrates through the soles of everyone's feet—not a loud bang, but a heavy, low-frequency groan as the basalt columns adjust to the pressure of the plateaus on either side. 

In response to these shifts, the entire city is constantly being rebuilt. A timber that was plumb yesterday is skewed today; a doorframe binds as the rock behind it pinches. Everywhere, men are adjusting wedges, tightening hemp lashings, and moving their slate roofs a few inches to the left or right to accommodate the breathing of the stone. It is a slow, tireless dance between a young, restless earth and a people who have nowhere else to go.
