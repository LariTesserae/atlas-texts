Here, at the roof of the world, the ocean does not sit in a basin of earth; it is cradled in a colossus of stone. 

The water is intensely cold, heavy with a dull, mineral chill that numbs the skin but never freezes. It is a perched sea, held five miles above the lowlands in an endless, artificial plateau. If one swims far enough toward the periphery, the stone floor simply ends, and the cold water plunges over monumentally high, smooth lips into a howling, dry abyss of cloud and gas far below. But here, toward the interior, the horizon is nothing but an endless, dim stretch of water, dark slate-grey and thick with micro-fine masonry silt.

There is no soil. There is no sand. Every square inch of the seafloor is built. 

The ground is an unbroken expanse of colossal, interlocking stone blocks, each one the size of a temple. There are no natural rifts, no volcanic vents, no muddy sea-floors—only the endless, modular joints of an ancient, mortarless architecture that has been worn down by the water over untold millions of years. The edges of the great blocks, once sharp and geometric, are now soft, rounded, and smooth as river stones, polished by the ceaseless friction of the silt and the currents. 

The scale of this place is vast but empty. Monolithic walls, three hundred feet high, rise out of the grey gloom, running for miles before turning at perfect, ancient right angles. They are like the foundations of a city built for giants, but they support nothing. Between these towering dividers lie immense, empty plazas of grey basalt blocks, stretching out into the dim light until they are lost in the haze of the water. There is a profound loneliness here; the structures are so large, and there is so much empty, cold water between them.

Yet, this world is far from still. It is governed by a chaotic, silent turbulence. 

While the depths are quiet, the water is constantly, violently in motion. Great, invisible currents sheet across the stone plazas, driven by the cold upwellings from the edge of the world. They do not rage with the sound of wind or waves; instead, they move with a heavy, terrifying silence. One moment the water is still; the next, a massive thermal plume sweeps through, dragging everything in its path with the force of an avalanche, yet making no more sound than a soft, rhythmic whistle. 

Under the weight of these shifting currents, the ancient architecture itself moves. The blocks were designed without mortar, engineered to slide and shift to relieve the pressure of the water. When a major current hits a plaza, the massive stone slabs slowly, ponderously slide over one another. A wall three miles long might tilt three degrees to the left, its joint-lines yawning open like stone mouths before settling into a new alignment. This movement is felt rather than heard—a deep, infrasonic hum that vibrates through the chest, a bone-deep shuddering that travels miles through the cold liquid, while the ears perceive only silence.

No plants grow in this twilight. There is no kelp, no green algae, no pale sea-grass. The stone is scrubbed clean of vegetation by the constant, silt-laden currents. The only colors are the charcoal-grey of the basalt, the dull iron-blue of the water, and the stark white of the fauna.

And the fauna is everywhere, thriving in the cold, moving gloom.

Because there are no plants, the food chain is entirely animal and mineral. The stone blocks are home to billions of tiny, white, shell-less bivalves that cling to the joints where the masonry shifts. They feed on the microscopic organic dust that falls from the air above the water. 

Through the corridors of the giant walls drift the "gliders"—pale, translucent organisms shaped like wide, ribbed blankets. They have no eyes, only long, feathery sensory filaments that trail behind them, reading the subtle shifts in the water's temperature and vibration. They ride the chaotic currents like paper in a gale, twisting and flattening themselves to slip through the narrow gaps between the sliding stone blocks. 

In the deeper trenches, where the masonry plunges into dark, stepped wells, larger creatures stir. They are slow, blind, and rubbery, with pale skin that has the texture of wet leather. They crawl along the vertical faces of the blocks, using thousands of tiny, suction-tipped limbs to anchor themselves against the drag of the currents. They lick the mineral silt from the stone, their mouths simple, soft-lipped vacuums. When the stone blocks begin to slide, these creatures do not flee; they simply press themselves flat into the seams, surviving the grinding weight of the masonry by virtue of their cartilaginous, compressable bodies.

Apart from the low, occasional thud of a shifting block or the hiss of a fast-moving current, the world is hushed. Light is a rare commodity. It filters down from the high surface as a dim, uniform dawn-glow, never brightening, never fading, just a constant, pale-slate twilight that illuminates the crests of the giant walls while leaving the deeper plazas in near-total shadow.

It is a world that has reached its final, stable state—an ancient, artificial mountaintop ocean, where the ruins of an unknown civilization have become the bedrock of a wild, cold, and restless ecosystem. There are no tools here, no relics of the builders, no active machinery. There is only the dumb, sliding stone, the rushing water, and the silent, pale things that have made the ruins their home.
