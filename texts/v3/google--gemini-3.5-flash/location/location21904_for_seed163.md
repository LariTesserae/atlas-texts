The light is the first thing that reaches you. At this altitude, the atmosphere is thin but saturated with a dense, suspended moisture that scatters the sun into a piercing, shadowless white glare. It is blindingly bright, a flat and silver intensity that reflects off every wet surface. There are no shadows to orient the eye; instead, the world is defined by high-contrast glints, wet sheen, and the deep, absorbing greens of an endless botanical crust. 

Here, the earth is not made of stone. The landscape is a colossal, high-altitude shelf composed entirely of living vegetation. Giant, cushion-like mosses, tough peat-succulents, and fibrous, interlocking root-systems have grown so dense and deep that they form the actual topography of the region. This is a pure-plant substrate, fifty or a hundred feet deep, resting on some unseen basement of young mountain rock. 

The geography is raw, sharp, and young. Because the erosion rate is so low and the landscape is still in an embryonic, emerging stage, the valleys have not yet been smoothed out by wind or water. Instead, the country is broken by clean, vertical splits in the green crust—canyons of compressed peat where the walls are sliced as cleanly as cake. Looking down into these moss-chasms, you can see the geological history of the plants: a top layer of brilliant, wet jade-green, transitioning downward into a dense mustard-gold, then a rich tobacco-brown, and finally, at the dark bottom, a compressed, near-black peat that has never seen the sun.

Water is the dominant medium. It does not flow in defined rivers; the entire landmass acts as a saturated sponge. Clear, cold water—kept at a constant, bracing chill—weeps continuously from the vertical moss-cliffs. Every green wall is a vertical marsh, dripping thousands of thin, silver threads of runoff into the deep chasms below. In the flat basins, the water pools in perfectly still, black tarns that reflect the glaring white sky like mirrors of polished obsidian. 

The silence one might expect from such a lonely, high-altitude bog is entirely absent. The air is filled with a deafening, continuous roar. Part of this is natural—the combined, rushing hiss of millions of water droplets falling from the moss-shelves—but the true source is mechanical. 

Widely scattered across this brilliant green expanse are massive, dark industrial installations. They are few and far between, separated by miles of open, empty marsh-country, but their presence dominates the auditory world. These are water-harvesting stations: heavy, black-iron pump-houses, copper-clad distillation towers, and massive hydraulic rams that look as though they have been bolted directly into the living peat. 

The sound they produce is a low-frequency, bone-shaking rumble, a rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of colossal steam-driven pistons that shakes the wet moss beneath your feet. Huge copper-insulated power lines, greened by oxidation, sag under the weight of condensing fog as they run from the valley-floor stations up toward the high, bright ridges. Every few minutes, a relief valve on a distant tower lets loose a high-pressure shriek of white steam, cutting through the low drone of the pumps before dissolving into the cold, bright mist.

Despite the heavy machinery, there is no bustle here. The system operates on an incredibly slow, patient cycle. There are no workers to be seen; the factories run on automated, gravity-fed hydraulic systems or the sluggish burn of local peat, their gears turning with agonizing slowness. A giant, thirty-foot iron flywheel at the edge of one chasm rotates perhaps once every ten minutes, its movement almost invisible until you watch the spoke slowly cross the horizon. The decay of the plants and the growth of the new moss-walls happen at the same geologic crawl. It is a world of massive, permanent installations working slowly against a landscape that grows and heals its wounds at a pace of inches per century.

Apart from the plants and the clanking iron, the region is functionally empty. Animal life is nearly non-existent; once or twice a day, a heavy-bodied, grease-colored mountain beetle might crawl across the wet, structural iron of a pipeline, or a solitary, silent white bird might circle the rising steam vents before disappearing back into the glare. 

There is a strange, clean order to the place. Though industrial, it is not filthy; the water is too abundant, the rain too frequent, and the moss too hungry for nutrients. Any oil or soot is quickly washed away, filtered through miles of living peat, or swallowed by the fast-growing green carpet that slowly, patiently tries to climb the cold iron legs of the pumping stations. It is a landscape under construction, being built simultaneously by the slow, silent accumulation of cell-upon-cell of moss, and the heavy, deafening heartbeat of the iron pumps.
