At this height, the atmosphere does not flow; it settles like cold, wet wool. 

The mountains are not old. They have no scree, no gravel slopes, no soft collars of dirt. They are obsidian teeth, miles high, thrust straight out of some unseen cradle below. The stone is vitreous, black, and perfectly smooth, still bearing the whorls and ripples of its liquid birth. There are no cracks for frost to wedge into, no rounded edges from centuries of wind. It is an embryonic geology, raw and freshly cast, cold to the touch but smelling faintly of sulfur and the deep, pressurized interior of the world.

Between these sheer black obelisks, suspended over gulfs that drop into blue-gray nothingness, sits the city of Oakhaven-on-the-Spire. 

It is a sparse kind of urbanity. Rather than a dense carpet of roofs, the settlement is a series of isolated, massive vertical wards anchored to different teeth of rock. One needle holds the foundry-complexes, their brickwork blackened by grease; another, three miles away across a silent void, holds the residential tiers, stacked like multi-layered birdcages of iron and green-veined copper. They are connected by sagging, three-inch-thick steel cables. Along these cables, immense, steam-driven transport hulls crawl with agonizing slowness, their boilers sighing into the damp air, taking half a day to make a single crossing.

Everything is wet, but it rarely rains. Instead, the clouds are partners in the architecture. They drag themselves across the iron gantries, leaving a heavy, cold condensation that beads on the raw black stone. Because the rock is so new and lacks any pores, the water does not soak in; it forms perfect, trembling spheres—some as small as peas, others the size of carriage wheels—that cling to the vertical basalt by some sluggish, non-Newtonian surface tension. They slide downward in slow motion, like melting glass, before dropping into the abyss.

Vegetation here is thick, heavy, and opportunistically flat. Massive, liver-colored lichens and fleshy ferns with leaves like wet leather plaster themselves to the iron foundations and the lower levels of the brick towers. They grow in the path of the steam vents, thriving on the warmth and the oily draft of the coal engines. In the spaces between these industrial hearths, where the cold of the high altitudes takes over, the flora turns pale and hair-like, giant mats of silver beard-moss that hang thirty yards off the underside of the suspension bridges, dripping water into the fog.

There is a strange, heavy logic to how things fall here—or rather, how they fail to fall. 

Physics is tired at this altitude. Occasionally, a massive block of unrefined pig iron, dropped from a foundry gantry, will not plunge. It will sink through the air at the speed of an autumn leaf, drifting sideways in the draft until it gently wedges itself between two lower cable lines. The workers do not marvel at this; they simply wait with long gaff-hooks to pull it in. Great fragments of raw, unweathered basalt float in the middle distance between the towers, suspended like frozen debris in a river of syrup. They do not rise, and they do not sink; they rotate once every few weeks on an axis known only to themselves, their sharp edges glinting when the dim, silver sun briefly parts the mist.

This slow-motion drift governs the life of the fauna as well. The air is thick with them. They are not birds, but heavy-bodied, six-winged creatures with skin like wet seals and throat-sacs that whistle with the tone of church organs. They do not flap; they paddle through the dense, cold air, their long, ribbon-like tails trailing behind them. They nest in the iron honeycombs underneath the factory floors, heated by the boiler flues. They are so numerous that their shadows construct a second, moving architecture over the stone pillars. When the city’s shift-whistles blow—deep, steam-powered groans that shake the soot from the chimneys—the creatures respond in unison, thousands of low, resonant whistles that harmonize with the industrial bass, filling the chasm with a deafening, vibrating drone that rattles the teeth in one’s skull.

The light is always late-afternoon silver, filtered through leagues of suspended water vapor. It never grows fully dark, nor does it ever bright-out. The factories operate on a single, endless midday shift. In the workshops, gas lamps burn with a green-tinged flame, fed by coal-gas lines that run alongside the water pipes. The heat from these pipes is the only thing keeping the dampness from freezing into sheets of black ice on the catwalks.

Below the suspended city, there is nothing but the "Well." If one peers over the edge of a residential bracket, past the hanging moss and the white bellies of the nesting air-beasts, there is only a soft, luminous gray floor where the clouds pack tight. No one has seen the bottom of the teeth. Some of the older engineers say the mountains are still growing, rising from the wet dark at the rate of an inch a year, pushing the iron city further into the cold, thin sky. Sometimes, in the quietest hours between the whistle-blasts, one can hear a sound like shattering glass from deep below—the sound of the raw stone cracking as it forces its way upward into the light.
