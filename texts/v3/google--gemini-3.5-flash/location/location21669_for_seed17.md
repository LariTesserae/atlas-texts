The world is not air, but it is not dark. 

A pale, diffused, cerulean light—even and unchanging—saturates the infinite fluid. There is no surface to this ocean, or if there is, it lies millions of fathoms above, beyond the reach of any pressure gradient. This is a high-altitude sea, suspended in a cold, low-gravity envelope around a colossal, silent world. The light does not come from a single sun but is refracted through leagues of water so pure it holds no silt, no dust, and no green decay. 

The temperature is locked at a shivering 1°C. It is a liquid desert, vast beyond comprehension, where the distance between physical objects is measured in continent-spans. 

Out of this empty blue void rise the spires. 

They are basaltic columns of monumental scale, thrust upward by some ancient, violent tectonic event that ceased as abruptly as it began. Because there are no storms, no waves, and no sandy currents to wear them down, the spires are impossibly sharp. They are young, raw, and wicked. Their edges are like black volcanic glass, cleaving the cold water with razor precision. No moss grows on them. No kelp clings to their flanks. There are no plants here, nor any simpler lichen or algae. There is only the obsidian glass of the stone, the endless blue of the water, and the machines.

Every hundred leagues, clamped to the sheer, vertical face of a spire, sits a solitary industrial unit. 

These structures are not ruins, nor are they inhabited. They are gargantuan, blocky engines of iron and green-patinaed copper, bolted directly into the basalt by rivets the size of houses. Their purpose is simple, ancient, and ceaseless. Deep within their chassis, massive turbines—three miles in diameter—spin with agonizing, unvarying rhythm. They do not pump water; they seem to exist only to generate an enormous, localized electromagnetic field. Great, thick copper cables, thick as redwood trees, drape from the turbine housings, looping through the blank water to disappear into the blue distance. Blue-white electrical arcs, thirty feet long, occasionally crawl across the rivets, illuminating the dark metal in silent, brief flickers.

Because of these machines, the world is not quiet. 

The sound is a physical presence. It is a deafening, bone-shaking, low-frequency roar—an eternal, unchanging thrum that vibrates through the teeth and chest of anything that enters the water. It is the sound of thousands of miles of cold, heavy water being sheared by rotating iron plates, overlaid with the high, singing hiss of high-voltage electricity. The sound does not rise or fall. There are no waves to crash, no wind to howl. There is only the hum. It is a stable, frozen equilibrium of noise that has occupied the void for millennia. 

Yet, life is abundant here.

In the vast, empty spaces between the spires, creatures drift. Having none of the soft green food of shallower worlds, they have adapted to the iron and the electricity. They are blind, pale, and constructed of materials that mimic the landscape: their shells are made of translucent silica and organic glass, their muscles of dark, oil-thick fibers. 

They do not have ears, but they are entirely defined by the sound. They use the deafening, omnipresent thrum of the turbines as a permanent acoustic map. By measuring the minute phase shifts in the vibration, they navigate the trackless void with perfect precision. 

Millions of small, needle-like glass-shrimp drift in schools that stretch for miles, glittering like ice shavings in the diffused blue light. They graze on the faint mineral precipitates that peel off the raw basalt spires. 

Larger organisms—cetacean-scale entities with long, segmented bodies like armored trains—glide slowly through the columns. They have no mouths; instead, they have wide, flat induction plates along their underbellies. They gather in silent herds around the energized copper cables of the stations, hovering inches away, absorbing the stray electrical currents to power their slow, cold metabolisms. They do not fight, they do not play, and they do not change their routes. They are as static and invariant as the engines themselves.

Nothing changes here. The light never dims. The water never warms. The spires do not erode. The great turbines spin at the exact same revolutions per minute, century after century, producing the same deafening, protective roar that keeps the glass-herds moving along their invisible, geometric paths through the cold, bright, empty airless sea.
