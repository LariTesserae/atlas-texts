The Sump of Orthogonal Condensation lies in a shallow, low-elevation basin, no larger than a cathedral close or a small hamlet. Its boundaries are sharp: the rising basin walls are composed of a cold, grey, non-porous bedrock that appears entirely embryonic. This is raw geology, un-weathered and un-scuffed, as if the basin were excavated only hours ago by some clean, enormous blade. There are no chips, no gravel, and no dust; the stone is a matte, pre-formative substance that has not yet learned how to crumble.

The air here is cold, holding a sharp, chemical chill that does not circulate. Light is normal—the flat, even brightness of a high-altitude overcast sky—but it behaves strangely as it falls into the basin. It does not scatter. Instead, shadows are cast with double borders, slightly offset from one another, suggesting the light is passing through a space where distance is measured along two slightly different vectors at once.

This small hollow is crowded with an impossibly dense micro-urbanism. Hundreds of slim, tower-like pillars, some no wider than a human thigh and others the width of a small house, rise meters from the grey floor. They are packed together like basalt columns in a giant’s causeway, but they are not natural. They are constructed of a dark, glass-smooth chalcogenide material and an alloy of beryllium that seems to actively repel the air around it. 

These columns are layered with microscopic infrastructure: rows of glowing, hair-thin conduits that carry pulses of lilac light, and tiny, vertical slits that serve as roads or passages for things that move through the third and fourth dimensions. Despite their extreme density—one can barely squeeze between many of these structures—they do not touch. Where two towers should collide, their surfaces appear to bend around one another without deformation, or simply occupy the same space in a state of mutual transparency. If you reach out to touch a column, your fingers may pass through the first two inches of its facade before meeting an unyielding, freezing resistance that feels like polished jade.

Water exists here, but not as flow. Because of the cold, moisture from the upper atmosphere condenses within the basin, but the laws of surface tension have been altered. Heavy, spherical droplets of water—each the size of a plum—hover millimeters above the horizontal surfaces of the columns and the basin floor. They do not run or sink. They sit suspended on cushions of quiet, localized gravity, shivering slightly when the air is disturbed. Occasionally, one of these liquid globes will slide horizontally across a ledge, moving with a slow, mechanical purpose, before merging with another. Underneath these droplets, the raw rock remains perfectly dry, protected by the micro-fields that keep the water aloft.

Vegetation is sparse and pale. It looks less like organic matter and more like an accidental materialization of mathematical curves. Thin, silver-blue threads, reminiscent of dried pine needles but as stiff as copper wire, sprout in tiny, isolated tufts from the narrow gaps where the towers meet the raw ground. They do not seek the light; instead, they curve gently toward the nearest source of logic-bearing current, their ends glowing with a faint, static violet spark. They grow at an imperceptible pace, lengthening by perhaps a millimeter every year.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, but there are presences. Every few hours, a small, hollow sphere of graphite, about the size of an apple, will emerge from one of the vertical slits in a tower. It moves with a heavy, deliberate slowness, drifting three inches above the ground, rotating along an axis that tilts twenty-three degrees every ten seconds. It makes no sound except for a dry, woody *clink* when it encounters another sphere. They seem to be surveyors or caretakers of a town that has no citizens, measuring the distance between the columns to ensure that the impossible density does not collapse into a singular point of mass.

The Sump is mostly quiet. The atmosphere is too thin and cold to carry high frequencies well, and the density of the buildings acts as a massive acoustic dampener. The only regular sound is a deep, sub-audible hum—a slow, patient vibration that rises from the raw bedrock once every twenty minutes. It is the sound of the earth being compiled, a low-gradient rumble that sounds like a massive engine turning over in wet sand, miles below.

In this place, change is an exercise in immense patience. Structures do not decay, because there is no wind to wear them down and no chemical oxidation to rust them. However, they do drift. On a scale of months, a street that was once wide enough to walk through will narrow until it is only a seam, while another gap will open elsewhere, revealing a new, razor-sharp vista of the basin wall. The city, if it is a city, is slowly and silently rearranging its parts, searching for a perfect layout that its geometry has not yet allowed it to find.
