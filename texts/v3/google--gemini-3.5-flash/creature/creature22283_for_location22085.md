To watch this hollow for an hour is to realize that nothing here lives in isolation; the entire basin is a single, wet machine of meat, stone, and grease. The inhabitants do not merely live in the cleft; they are the cleft, grown mobile and strange under the influence of the three black needles.

Under the graphite sand, where the basalt bedrock begins to curdle in the damp heat, live **The Sieve-Worms**. They are the color of pencil lead, thick as a man’s thigh, but they possess no bones and no teeth. Their skin is as slick as grease-wood, allowing them to glide through the silky, iron-dark sand without disturbing its surface. A traveler standing still would never see them, but they would feel them: a rhythmic, subterranean pulsing beneath the arches of their feet, like the heartbeat of a great, buried dog. The Sieve-Worms serve as the basin’s cleaners. Their mouths are wide, soft funnels lined with hair-fine cilia that filter the sand for the tiny, fallen husks of the glass-wing shrimp and the heavy, copper-colored pollen of the trumpet-leaves. They are the reason the basin never smells of decay, despite the frantic pace of life above them; they drink the death before it can rot.

Above them, on the stone floor, the **Violet Lichen** acts as a nervous system for the valley. It is not a plant, nor is it truly fungus; it is a colonial animal made of billions of microscopic, water-filled beads. When a footstep or a falling stone compresses these beads, they do not pop; they shift their internal salinity, releasing a tiny spark of static electricity that causes the neighboring beads to glow. This violet luminescence is a warning system. If one of the emerald reptiles moves too quickly, or if a trumpet-leaf drops a leaf, a wave of cool purple light rolls across the floor of the hollow. The other inhabitants watch these ripples. To the eyeless reptiles, the shifting waves of violet light are like the shadows of clouds passing over a sunny field—they tell them where the movement is, where the danger is, and where the next meal might fall.

The emerald reptiles, known to the inner world as **The Slick-Plates**, are the most active residents of the stone walls. They have six legs, each ending in a broad, soft pad that can stick to the polished basalt like wet leather on glass. Their bodies are flattened, like jade green coins laid overlapping on a string. Because they have no eyes, they live in a world of heat and pressure, mapped out by the vibrating yellow "dishes" on their foreheads. These dishes are actually thin membranes of cartilaginous skin stretched over hollow chambers of warm oil. When a glass-winged shrimp flies nearby, the vibration of its wings strikes the dish like a thumb against a drumhead, and the Slick-Plate darts forward with a sound like a wet leather belt hitting a floor. 

The Slick-Plates do not drink water; they lick the heavy grease-mist from each other’s backs and from the sides of the three black needles. They are social, but cold-bloodedly so. They huddle together in the damp crannies of the basalt arches, forming great, chittering carpets of green-and-yellow light that pulse in time with the deep, jaw-vibrating hum of the needles below.

Then there are **The Glass-Shrimp**, or *The Chimes*, which fill the air like dry snow. They are the currency of the basin’s life. Their bodies are completely transparent, save for a tiny, amber-colored heart that beats three hundred times a minute behind their ribs. They do not fly so much as they swim through the thick, greasy air, using four pairs of feather-like oars that click together at the end of every stroke. This clicking is their language and their defense. When a Slick-Plate strikes at them, a thousand shrimp will click in unison, creating a sharp, metallic *crack* that rattles the sensory dishes of the reptile and leaves it confused and twitching while the cloud drifts safely upward.

But the most mysterious inhabitants of all are those that live in the bent air around the three black needles.

In the localized gravity well of the needles, where the condensation droplets drift sideways, lives **The Weft**. These are not creatures of flesh or leaf, but of surface tension and dust. They look like ribbons of dark, wet silk, three or four feet long but no thicker than a single autumn leaf, floating horizontally in the air. They have no visible mouth, no eyes, and no limbs. Instead, they are composed of a dense, gelatinous membrane that traps the graphite sand as it blows through the cleft, incorporating the iron-dark grains into their own bodies to give themselves weight and structure. 

The Weft are the only creatures that can touch the black needles without dying. They cling to them like wet rags wrapped around a iron bar, sliding slowly up and down the twelve-foot shafts. They seem to feed on the hum itself, or perhaps on the strange, static electricity that pools where the three needles point toward the empty sky. When the Weft are satisfied, they drift away from the needles in slow, looping spirals, their bodies glowing with a faint, greasy sheen that looks like oil on water. 

If one of the glass-shrimp clouds drifts too close to the gravity well, the Weft do not hunt them; the gravity simply pulls the tiny creatures in, where they stick to the Weft’s wet, iron-slick skin. The ribbon-creatures absorb them slowly, over days, leaving only the tiny, glassen wings to drift down onto the violet lichen below like sparks of cold light.

There is a rhythm to this place, a slow breath that takes twelve hours to complete. In the "morning"—though the blue sky above never changes—the black needles hum at a higher pitch, a sharp, metallic ring that causes the trumpet-leaves to open their bronze bells and release their sweet, yellow spores. In the "evening," the hum drops to a chest-vibrating growl; the Slick-Plates retreat to their stone crevices, the glass-shrimp settle into the cups of the trumpet-leaves, and the Sieve-Worms rise to the very surface of the sand, their soft, black heads glistening in the violet light as they drink the day's condensation.

Through it all, the three needles remain dark, silent, and heavy, holding the warmth in and the cold out, like three black fingers holding back the weight of a dead world.
