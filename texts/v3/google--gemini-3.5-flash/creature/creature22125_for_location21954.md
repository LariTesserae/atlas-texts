To walk among the mirror-polished lanes of the basin is to realize that the distinction between a machine, a plant, and an animal has been dissolved by some ancient, non-designing geometry. The things that live here do not possess soft blood, nor do they dream, but they are alive. They are the functions of the valley, and they have the terrible, beautiful persistence of physical laws.

If you look closely at the basalt floor, beneath the shadow of the brass-tinned ferns, you will find the smallest of the basin’s denizens: the **Tallow-Grubs**. They are the only soft-bodied entities in this high, cold place, but their softness is not that of meat. They are the size of a man’s thumb, segmentless cylinders of dense, cold lard, the color of rancid butter. They do not have eyes or mouths; instead, their snout-ends terminate in a small, porous bronze disk that constantly weeps a heavy, mineral grease. 

The Grubs exist in a state of slow, geologic migration, sliding along the edges of the stone avenues at the speed of rising sap. They leave behind them a trail of thick, clear oil that never dries, smelling faintly of sheep suet and cold iron. This oil is the lubricant of the basin. The other creatures—the pewter lizards and the steel birds—deliberately cross these grease trails during their rigid, looping paths. A lizard will pause, drag its rubber-jointed elbows through a Grub’s trail to coat its pins, and then resume its march. Without the Grubs, the valley’s joints would scream, seize, and grind themselves to powder within a century. 

Above the grease trails, inhabiting the copper-wire moss, are the **Tickers**. These are the lizard-like things of pewter and rubber mentioned before, but to call them lizards is to miss the elegance of their interior lives. Their hollow, cast-metal bellies house no organs, only a single, heavy brass flywheel suspended on sapphire bearings. 

The Tickers have no mouths, but their six needle-sharp legs are tipped with tiny shoes of soft, conductive copper. As they walk over the copper moss, the static electricity generated by the wire carpet discharges into their legs in tiny, blue sparks. These sparks travel through internal silver wires to a tiny electromagnet that indexes the flywheel. One footstep, one spark, one click of the wheel. They are literally wound up by the ground they tread. If a Ticker is lifted off the moss, its legs falter, its flywheel spins down to silence, and it freezes into a pewter monument. If returned to the copper wire, the first passing draft that tilts its body will strike a spark, and the loop will begin again, exactly where it was interrupted.

In the geometric grids of suspended water spheres, where the air is wetness itself, live the **Siphon-Stalks**. 

These are the giants of the valley’s open spaces. They resemble three-meter-tall herons, but their bodies are constructed from telescoping tubes of dark, oxidized steel. Their legs are so thin they are nearly invisible against the black basalt, ending in heavy, leaden disks that anchor them to the stone lanes. Their necks are long, segmented coils of copper piping that curve upward with a heavy, hydraulic grace.

The Siphon-Stalks do not eat. Their "heads" are long, needle-sharp glass prisms. Every twelve minutes, a Stalk will extend its neck into the grid of floating grape-sized water spheres. With a dry, sucking *shlick*, its glass bill will pierce a single sphere. The water does not fall; it is drawn up through the transparent tube of the neck, where it is exposed to the static charge of the Stalk's internal copper coils. For three seconds, the water inside the glass neck glows with a pale, violet phosphorescence—a cold, electrostatic reaction that purifies the mineral load of the spray. The Stalk then exhales the water back into the grid as a perfect, dry sphere, slightly smaller and colder than before, which locks back into its geometric coordinates. The Stalk then retracts its neck with a low, metallic hiss and remains motionless for another twelve minutes.

Higher up, in the bronze-collared branches of the ironwood trees, are the **Steam-Whistlers**. 

They resemble flocks of sharp, steel-winged gulls, but they are not visitors from the sky; they are the physical venting valves of the trees themselves. Their feet are not claws, but threaded brass couplings that are permanently screwed into the branch tips. These birds are hollow. When the roots of the ironwood trees draw up the high-pressure water from the boiling deeps of the basalt, the pressure rises through the trunks, turning the self-lubricating bronze collars and driving the organic gears. 

The excess steam from this process is channeled upward, into the hollow branches, and out through the legs of the Whistlers. It escapes through their razor-sharp, steel-leaf plumage with a high, clean whistle that keeps the entire forest in a state of constant, rhythmic vibration. When the steam pressure drops, their wings fold down in unison, their metal feathers clicking like sliding bolts, and they wait in dead, silver silence until the pressure builds once more.

And yet, even in a world of perfect stasis, there is wear. 

A pewter joint will eventually lose a fraction of a millimeter to friction; a brass fern leaf will eventually split under the constant hiss of the steam. To prevent the system from winding down, the basin produces **The Calibrators**.

The Calibrators are flat, hexagonal plates of soft, dull lead, about the size of a shield. They have no limbs, no eyes, and make no sound. They slide over the mirror-polished avenues by the microscopic vibration of the basalt itself, moving like water droplets on a hot skillet. They are the street-sweepers and the machine shops of the basin.

If a leaf of tinned brass breaks off a fern, it falls to the stone lane. It does not rot. It lies there, a discordant note in the geometric perfection. Within hours, three or four lead Calibrators will slide toward the fallen leaf. They will glide over it, covering it completely. Beneath their heavy, leaden bodies, a slow, cold-pressure process begins. Using only the weight of their bodies and the rhythmic vibration of the earth, the Calibrators grind the brass leaf down, atom by atom, turning it back into a fine metallic dust. 

They ingest this dust through porous membranes on their undersides. Then, moving to one of the bare basalt "altars" at the center of the groves, they spit out tiny, perfect replacement washers, rivets, or leaf-joints, leaving them in neat, sorted piles. 

When a Ticker or a Whistler passes these altars on its eternal loop, the missing or worn part is replaced. A Ticker with a worn elbow joint will brush against the altar; the magnetic attraction of the new pewter washer will draw it into place, while the old, paper-thin washer is shed, falling to the ground to be found by the lead Calibrators.

This is the life of the basin. It is a world that has bypassed the messy, wasteful experiments of flesh. There are no mothers here, no childhoods, no corpses, and no graves. There is only the preservation of the loop. If you were to sit on the copper moss, smell the sweet, burnt-sugar static, and watch the pewter lizards perform their ninety-degree turns, you would realize that you are the only thing in this valley that is dying.
