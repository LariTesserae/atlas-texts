To inhabit this high distillation engine, an organism must be more plumbing than flesh. In a world where there is no soil, no seasons, and no sun, the boundaries between the geological, the mechanical, and the biological have dissolved. Life here does not fight the wet, heavy wear of the machine; it has become a part of the cycle, a wet skin grown over the iron bones of the plateau.

The most common inhabitants—if they can be called that—are the **Siphon-galls**. They resemble massive, bulbous barnacles of calcified leather that root themselves directly onto the joints of the iron pipes. These creatures are the size of wine casks, their surfaces the color of mutton fat and wet slate. They possess no eyes, no limbs, and no brains. Instead, they are living valves. Every Siphon-gall has a tough, fibrous proboscis that secretes a mild, warm acid capable of pitting the ancient iron. Once they have bored a tiny pinhole into a steam-pipe, they seal themselves over the leak, using the engine’s internal pressure to pump geothermal fluid through their own translucent, chambered interiors. 

To watch a grove of Siphon-galls is to watch the slow breathing of the landscape itself. When the pressure in the lines peaks, they swell, their pale hides stretching until the network of red, copper-rich veins beneath their skin becomes visible. When the pressure drops, they deflate with a low, whistling sigh, filtering out the heavy metals and sulfur compounds before letting the cleansed water spill down the pipe's flank. They are the kidneys of the towers, self-seeding wherever a weld begins to fail, sealing the breach with their own calcifying bodies.

Beneath the gantries, in the deep shadows where the hot water pools in the basalt channels, live the **Trestle-striders**. These are the only large, mobile creatures of the high plateau, yet they move with such agonized, mechanical deliberation that they are easily mistaken for shifting ironwork. A Trestle-strider is a spindly assembly of jointed, hollow limbs made of dark, resinous cartilage—material that looks like ancient, waterlogged oak but is as tough as vulcanized rubber. 

The striders have no bones. Their movements are entirely hydraulic, powered by the difference in temperature between the blistering iron pipes they cling to and the slightly cooler air of the fog. They have no faces; their forward-facing segment is a flat, circular disc of sensory pits that detect infrared radiation. They climb the vertical lattices of the towers like colossal, three-meter-wide spiders, seeking "hot-spots" where the steam is thickest. They feed on the Siphon-galls and the thick coats of sulfur-crust, scraping them off the iron with three-toothed, scraping plates at the ends of their limbs. When a Trestle-strider dies, its hydraulic pressure fails; it does not fall, but slowly calcifies in place, its limbs rusting into the girder work until it becomes just another bracket supporting the pipe.

Higher up, where the air is a white wall of scalding mist, the **Parchment-gliders** drift. If the striders are the iron of the world, the gliders are its steam. They are incredibly light—large sheets of what looks like scorched, fibrous paper, ten feet across but only a millimeter thick. They are the only dry things in this world. Their upper surfaces are covered in a dense, microscopic velvet of hydrophobic hairs that repel the grease-heavy condensation, causing the falling droplets to bead and roll off them like quicksilver.

The gliders have no wings to flap; they are entirely passive sails. They rely on the powerful, sulfurous updrafts rising from the open cisterns. They drift upward into the dark-grey sky until the air grows too cool and thin, then slide downward through the mist in ten-mile-long, silent arcs. They feed by skimwise contact, draping themselves momentarily over the wet underbellies of the high-altitude bridges to absorb the dense mats of bacterial slime that grow in the grease. A glider’s life is a constant, delicate balancing act: if its hydrophobic coating becomes fouled by too much grease, it lose its buoyancy, grows heavy with water, and tumbles into the black glass valleys below, where it dissolves into a grey, soapy lather.

In the vast, circular cisterns, the water does not boil, but it is hot enough to kill any creature from the lower world. Here, the life is collective. The orange salt-crusts that ring the lakes are not merely mineral deposits; they are the dry margins of the **Slurry-mats**. These are colossal, floating rafts of colonial, silicon-based single-cells that have bound themselves together with a webbing of tough, gelled proteins. 

The Slurry-mats can be miles across, looking like vast, orange and ochre fields of floating peat. They are thick—sometimes six feet deep—and so dense that the heavy, greasy condensation pools on their surfaces in shallow, stagnant lagunes. These mats are the true processors of the plateau's chemistry. They drink the geothermal discharge, extracting silicon, arsenic, and iron, and exhaling tiny, high-pressure bubbles of methane and carbon dioxide. 

If one stands on the edge of a cistern and listens, the Slurry-mat can be heard "working." It is a sound like a wet carpet being wrung out, a wet, tearing creak that moves across the orange expanse in slow waves. The mats are sensitive to the vibrations of the deep earth; hours before the mountain shudders, the mats will begin to sink, drawing themselves down into the boiling depths of the reservoirs to ride out the tremors in the calmer, deeper water, leaving the cisterns looking like empty, black caldrons of bubbling oil.

There are no voices here. No screams, no mating calls, no songs of territorial defense. The inhabitants of this high-altitude greenhouse have no use for sound; the air is too thick and the industrial groan of the pipes too constant. Instead, they communicate through the engine itself. 

When a Trestle-strider climbs a column, its weight and heat change the vibration of the iron. Other striders miles away feel this frequency change through their foot-pads and alter their path to avoid them. When a Siphon-gall dies and dries up, the pitch of the steam whistling through its empty shell changes, signaling to the grease-slugs below that a new feeding ground of raw, mineral-rich condensation has opened up. 

It is a quiet, closed-loop society of consumers and filters, perfectly adapted to a home that is half-factory, half-mountain. They do not know of a world with soil, or blue skies, or cold rain. For them, existence is a warm, grey verticality, defined by the rise of the steam, the drip of the grease, and the eternal, low-frequency hum of the great iron pipes.
