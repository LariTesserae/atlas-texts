Within this vertical engine of glass and water, life does not merely persist; it clings, drifts, and adapts to the law of the perpetual impact. The species here are few, but they are defined by an extreme, specialized geometry—they are either entirely soft, elastic enough to survive being crushed between colliding water-worlds, or they are as hard and sharp as the volcanic glass they inhabit.

### The Core-Marrow (The Glacial Algae)

The water entering from the high fissure is not sterile. It bears the microscopic spores of a deep-glacier cyanobacteria known colloquially as *Thread-Marrow*. 

Inside the drifting water-spheres, this algae does not remain scattered. Because the spheres are in a state of constant, chaotic rotation—spun by the opposing thermal drafts like liquid tops—a mild centrifugal force is established within each floating planet. This force drives the heavier, mineral-rich silt and the green algae inward, toward the absolute center of each sphere. 

Over hours of drifting, the algae aggregates into a dense, emerald yolk at the heart of the water-globes. This "marrow" is a thick, gelatinous knot of oxygenating weed, humming with active photosynthesis whenever the brilliant chimney-light strikes it. To look through a floating carriage-sized sphere is to see a pale green, brain-like core suspended in perfect, crystal-clear sapphire, fizzing softly with tiny, trapped bubbles of pure oxygen.

### The Mist-Gliders

These are the primary residents of the void’s open air, acting as the animal link between the stone walls and the floating water. 

A mist-glider’s body is a marvel of evolutionary concession. They are completely ribless and contain no solid bone; their skeletal structure consists of hydraulic, fluid-filled tubes that flex rather than snap. Their outer hide is a pale, greasy leather, resembling the skin of a boiled pig but coated in a thick, hydrophobic slime. This slime keeps them from becoming waterlogged, but it also serves a defensive purpose: when two water-globes collide with a concussive roar, any glider caught in the impact is not crushed. Its soft, putty-like body simply deforms, flattening like bread dough before its internal hydraulic pressure snaps it back into shape.

Lacking eyes, which would be shattered by the flying, needle-like spray, the gliders navigate through a series of "pressure-pits" grooved into their snout and flanks. These pits can detect the massive, localized drop in barometric pressure that occurs when a haystack-sized water-globe is about to pass close to their perch. 

When a globe nears, the glider releases its sticky suction-discs from the basalt and launches itself into the void. It does not fly; it parachutes, flattening its body and using its wriggling, flat tail to steer into the slipstream. It plunges directly through the surface tension of the floating globe. Once inside, it is in a world of eerie, muffled silence, insulated from the roar of the chamber. The glider swims toward the center, gobbles mouthfuls of the sweet, emerald *Thread-Marrow* yolk with a small, circular rasp, and then waits. When the sphere inevitably collides with another or brushes against a basalt column, the glider uses the disruption to leap back onto the rock or drift to a new globe.

### The Gush-Vanes

While the mist-gliders occupy the interior of the water, the upper reaches of the chimney—where the air meets the open sky—are home to the *Gush-Vanes*. These are the apex organisms of the pocket, living in the narrow band of space where the volcanic steam meets the cold stratosphere.

A Gush-Vane is shaped like a triangular, three-foot-wide kite, constructed from a light, cartilage frame-work over which a dark, oily membrane is stretched. They have no legs, only four long, hinged spurs of blackened bone at the corners of their wings. 

These creatures do not flap. Instead, they hang-glide eternally on the wild thermal updrafts rising from the steam vents. They are highly sensitive to heat and draft; their bellies are lined with pits that map the thermal currents of the chamber in real-time. 

When a Gush-Vane hunts, it hovers above the path of the drifting water-globes. Spotting the pale silhouette of a mist-glider inside a translucent sphere, the Vane folds its wing-spars, plunging like a heavy stone plumb-line. It cuts through the water-globe, its sharp, wax-coated wingtips slicing the water like a knife. It snatches the glider from the core of the sphere with its prehensile, beak-like tail-claws, and then pops out the other side of the globe, instantly snapping its wings wide to catch the rising volcanic air before it hits the wet floor far below.

### The Vitreous Basalt-Lice

On the obsidian walls, where the stone is so sharp it can cut air, lives a creature that has matched its environment’s hardness. These are the *Vitreous Basalt-Lice*. 

They are small—no larger than a coin—and look like moving chips of the rock itself. Their outer shells are made of a dense, silica-heavy chitin that is black, glossy, and perfectly matches the basalt columns. They crawl sideways using dozens of tiny, glass-tipped legs that find purchase in the microscopic pores of the cooling stone.

The lice are incredibly heavy for their size, weighted down by their mineral carapaces. This weight prevents them from being blown off the walls by the concussive wind-blasts of the water-globes. 

They do not eat plant matter. Instead, they survive on the discarded skins of the mist-gliders. The gliders must shed their mucus-coated outer skin every few days to prevent the sulfur gases of the vents from burning them. These shed skins drift through the spray and stick to the raw rock faces. The basalt-lice gather on these wet rags of skin, using razor-sharp mouthparts to rasp them down to the bare stone. 

### The Life of the Human Relic

Even the rotting pine platform and the ancient grass-gut rope have become their own tiny, isolate ecosystem. To the organisms of the chamber, this small dump of wood and fiber is a mountain of rare, organic carbon—something that does not exist anywhere else in the vertical void.

*   **Tallow-Grubs:** The pine logs are inhabited by the descendants of a high-altitude beetle that has long since vanished from the region. The grubs—fat, sightless, ivory-colored things—chew through the damp wood. Over centuries, they have developed a unique survival mechanism: they process the wood's resin and secrete a thick, waterproof tallow. They smear this wax along their bore-holes and over the exterior of the platform. This wax rind is the only reason the platform still exists; it has preserved the rotting pine from the dissolving power of the wet air, encasing the crumbling structure in a protective, greasy shell.
*   **The Splay-Mold:** The grass-and-gut rope is covered in a pale, hair-like fungus that thrives on the traces of animal fat left in the dried intestine. This mold does not grow in flat patches; it hangs in long, feathery tassels that dangle two feet below the sagging line. These tassels are perpetually saturated with water. When a water-globe drifts past, the wind of its passage causes These tassels to lift, spread, and weave in the air like a fringe of pale, ghostly fingers reaching out to touch the wet planets as they roll by. Under the bright, prismatic light from above, these sodden white tassels catch the refraction, glowing momentarily in brilliant, spectral hues of violet and gold, before falling back into the wet, roaring shadow.
