The people of Alto-Vand are so few that they do not have a plural name for themselves; to those in the valleys below, on the rare occasions they are spoken of, they are simply called the Vandish, though they refer to one another only by the specific tower they inhabit. 

There are never more than nine of them. At present, there are eight.

They are small-statured, rarely exceeding four and a half feet in height, but they do not appear stunted or thickset. Instead, they are drawn with the same austere, vertical lines as their dwellings. Their collarbones are high and prominent, their chests narrow and deep—an adaptation for drawing the thin, cool air deep into their lungs without effort. Under their skin, which is the dull, light-absorbent grey of dry lichen, the transition from bone to muscle is nearly seamless. They do not possess the soft, protective pads of fat on their fingers and heels that lowland humans do; their palms are dry and crosshatched with fine, leathery creases, and their feet are long and flat, perfectly suited for distributing their light weight across the split slate plazas.

In the hard, shadowless light of the saddle, their eyes are their most striking feature. They have no whites to speak of; the sclera is a pale, wet-stone grey, and the irises are so large and dark a blue that they appear black except when the sun is low and strikes them from the side. They blink with a slow, deliberate drop of the lid that mimics the rhythm of the dripping condensation. 

Movement in Alto-Vand is an exercise in conservation. A Vandish person does not walk so much as they drift from one point of support to another. They move with their shoulders slightly hunched against the wind, their arms kept close to their ribs, tucked into the heavy folds of their ochre wool wraps. When they cross the two-square-meter plazas, they do not step in the center of the stones but along the joints, their soft-soled shoes of cured mountain-goat hide making a dry, whispering *shuck-shuck* that is barely louder than the rasp of the raven’s wing.

They live, for the most part, in absolute solitude. Each of the seven towers belongs to a single inhabitant, save for the double-tower at the southern cliff-boundary, where two sisters, both ancient and dry as pressed flowers, have lived together for sixty years without speaking more than a dozen words a month to one another. 

Their life within the towers is organized around the maintenance of the equilibrium. In the mornings, when the sun first touches the hand-hewn bronze pins of the larch doors, the inhabitants emerge to perform the "clearing." They do not use brooms, which would wear down the flagstones over centuries; instead, they use flat, wedge-shaped scrapers made of horn to gently lift the damp moss that has crawled too far out of the joints during the night, returning it to the deeper crevices where it belongs. 

Their diet is as sparse as the landscape. Twice a year, the great copper windlass at the southern cliff is greased with tallow. Using a system of massive, stone-weighted counterweights that hang into the empty abyss, they lower a wide wicker basket three thousand feet down to a ledge where the valley people leave parched barley, dried mutton, and blocks of salt. In return, the Vandish place in the basket small, hand-loomed squares of wool, woven so tightly on their hearth-side looms that the cloth can hold water without leaking. This wool, dyed with the grey-green moss of the saddle, is their only export, prized by the lowlanders for its near-indestructible quality.

They do not cook with fire in the way valley dwellers do. There is no wood to burn, and the smoke would soil the pale limestone of the towers. Instead, in the dark ground floors of their towers, they keep small, deep stone basins filled with oil rendered from sheep fat, brought up from below. A single, thick braided wool wick burns in these basins, producing a tiny, smokeless blue flame that provides neither light nor heat, but keeps a small iron pot of well-water just below the point of simmering. Into this water they drop three or four grains of parched barley, which they chew slowly throughout the day. 

Because of the thinness of the air and the constant, low-frequency whistle of the wind through the tower slits, their voices have lost all guttural and plosive sounds. When they speak—which is only when a chimney-joint begins to draft or the well-rope shows signs of wear—their language consists entirely of soft, sibilant whispers and low, hummed vowels. To an outsider, it would sound like the wind changing direction around a corner. 

They do not seem to age in the manner of other people. They do not grow soft or stooped; they simply become more dry, their skin tightening over their cheekbones until the fine, pale down that covers their temples looks like frost on the granite. When one of them dies, there is no funeral. The remaining inhabitants wait until the night when the clouds roll through the saddle, thick and white. Under the cover of this wet shroud, they place the body in the wicker basket at the southern cliff and release the counterweights, letting the mountain take back what was borrowed, down into the green, heavy air of the valley where they have never set foot.

By morning, the basket is back, empty and dry, and the tower is vacant. It will remain empty for years, perhaps decades, its larch door swollen shut, until a child is born in one of the other towers—an event so rare and quiet that it is marked only by the extra cup of water drawn from the public well once a day. The child will grow up in the silence of their parent's tower, learning to walk on the joints of the slate, until the day they are deemed old enough to cross the yard, open the door of an empty tower, and take up the long, slow work of waiting.
