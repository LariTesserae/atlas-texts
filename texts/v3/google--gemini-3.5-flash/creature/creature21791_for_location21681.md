### The High Ceiling and the Wet Dome

High above the wet, polished valley floors, the underside of the dolomite shelf is not a dead crust but an upside-down marsh of leather, gas, and bone. Here, where the dampness first condenses from the rising heat of the biological churn below, the **Siphon-lungs** hang like the wet, swinging udders of some impossible mountain. 

These bus-sized filtration sacs do not live alone; they are small, hanging biomes. The grey-white hide of a Siphon-lung is deeply wrinkled, encrusted with salt-blooms and colonies of tiny, blind **Lard-Mites**. These mites, no larger than apple seeds, are translucent and soft-bodied; they spend their entire lives crawling through the deep skin-folds of the giant bladders, grazing on the yeast-like yeasts and sulfur-eating molds that grow in the warmth of the host’s respiration. 

Within the exhaling vents of the Siphon-lungs, where the filtered air is squeezed out in a rhythmic, whistling hiss, live the **Pipe-Weavers**. These are long, thread-thin arachnids with legs like horsehair. They do not spin webs to catch insects, for there are no flying bugs in the Sough; instead, they spin dense, felted soot-funnels across the vents to catch the fine, grey drift-spores and skin-flakes exhaled by the lung. The air-whistle through these funnels is tuned by the spiders’ silk, producing a thin, piping scream that rises and falls with the creature's slow breath—a high, silver thread of sound that stitches through the heavy cello-drone of the Harp-mouths below.

Cluster by cluster, the **Lantern-galls** share the ceiling, suspended by thick, fibrous cords of yellow fat. These galls are essentially floating stomachs. They have no mouth; they absorb the lime-heavy ceiling water directly through their porous upper hides. Inside their bellies, calcium carbonate is perpetually digested by highly volatile bacterial soups, producing methane that keeps them bulbously inflated. 

Because they are light and filled with gas, their skin is stretched thin as parchment. Through this membrane, their internal bioluminescence leaks out—a cold, marshy green that reveals the slow, rhythmic curdling of their innards. Sometimes, when a Siphon-lung exhales too violently, the hot, wet draft causes a cluster of Lantern-galls to sway and bump into one another with the soft *thud* of heavy melons. This friction can rupture a weak gall; when one pops, it does not explode, but rather tears open with a wet *shluck*, releasing a slow-falling trail of burning green grease that sizzles as it drops through the dark, burning the backs of whatever crawls beneath it.

### The Great Churn of the Slopes

On the middle slopes, where the bare dolomite is polished to a greasy, grey mirror, life is a matter of traction. Here, the **Scritch-carpets** flow in their endless grey rivers. Because they crawl over one another three or four deep, the lower layers are constantly being ground into the rock. This is not a loss; it is part of their lifecycle. 

The **Marrow-Drums** wait for them. These are massive, sphere-like beasts of dense fat and muscle, the size of wine casks. They have no legs, ears, or eyes. Their outer hides are composed of overlapping rings of horn-like hide, resembling a closed pinecone. To move, a Marrow-Drum uses internal muscular contractions to roll itself down the slopes, crushing thousands of Scritch-carpets beneath its immense, rolling weight. 

As it rolls, the sticky, resinous oil that coats its plates collects the pulverized paste of the crushed bugs. When the Drum reaches the bottom of a slope, it lies stationary for hours, secrete-absorbing the protein-rich paste through the seams in its outer plates. 

Inside the head-space of the Marrow-Drum sits a pair of grapefruit-sized **otoliths**—dense balls of solid limestone that float in chambers of thick, greasy fluid. These stones rattle against nerve-endings with every tilt of the ground, telling the Drum exactly where "down" is, allowing it to navigate the blind, slippery slopes through gravity alone.

Moving with a slow, hydraulic grace that seems almost mechanical among the grease-slicked hollows are the **Stilt-walkers**. Their twelve-foot legs are not bone, but hollow tubes of pressurized amber-colored fluid, flexing and contracting through water-pressure rather than muscle. Because their feet are needle-thin, they do not step on the Scritch-carpets; instead, they feel for the minute gaps between the crawling shells, placing their tips directly onto the stone. 

The Stilt-walkers are entirely silent, but they are not deaf. Their long joints are hung with delicate, feathery antennae that catch the vibrations of the Harp-mouths' drone. They use this drone as a sonar grid; by analyzing how the sound bounces off the mounds and the ceiling, a Stilt-walker can build a perfect three-dimensional map of the dark basin in its tiny, knot-like brain. 

When a Stilt-walker finishes feeding on a Grease-slug, it leaves behind a "slough"—a deflated, yellow skin-bag containing nothing but acid-burned cartilage and grease. This debris does not last. Within minutes, the **Tallow-Slicers** arrive. These are flat, spade-shaped crabs with claws like iron shears. They do not walk; they slide on their bellies over the wet stone, their three pairs of legs modified into small, flat oars. They live to slice the grease-skins into ribbons, dragging the fat down into the silt-floors where it can be stored in cold, underwater caches.

### The Depths of the Silt

Below the slopes, in the lowest valleys of the Sough, lies the grease-silt. This is a blackish-grey, lukewarm sludge, sometimes six feet deep, composed of everything that has died, defecated, or been ground down in the basin. It is thick, smelling of ammonia and cold fat, and it is far from dead.

Buried deep within this muck are the **Silt-Spars**. These are segmented, worm-like creatures that can grow to the length of a log. They have no skin; instead, their bodies are covered in a thick, overlapping armor of grey slate tiles that they secrete from their own sweat. The Spars "swim" through the silt by expanding and contracting their segments, making a sound like a deck of dry cards being shuffled underground. 

The Silt-Spars are the great digesters of the Sough. They scoop up the silt with wide, shovel-like lower jaws, passing it through a hundred-foot-long, triple-looped gut that extracts every trace of organic tallow and ground chitin. What they excrete from their rear is a clean, white, calcified sand. Over centuries, this sand is pressed by the weight of the basin into new dolomite, forming the very floor upon which the Sough crawls.

Sharing the silt are the **Sponge-Wells**. These look like calcified hummocks, some ten feet wide, that sit level with the mud. They are colonial animals, cousins to the stone-finger lithotrophs, but they do not pump water; they pump silt. They draw the cold, thick mud into their central wells, filter out the microscopic lime-dust and bacteria, and then spout the cleaned water out in low, bubbling fountains that smell of wet slate. Around these wells, the Scritch-carpets gather in dense, writhing nests to lick the salt-crusts that form on the rim.

### The Bone-Clickers (The Lithic Sodalists)

There is a cognitive presence in the Lithic Sough, though "human" is a word that has lost its meaning here. They are known to themselves—if they have a name for their kind—by their vibrations, but to an outsider, they might be called the **Bone-Clickers** or the **Sodalists**.

They are pale, hairless hominids, their skin the color of lard that has sat in a dark cellar. Their bodies have adapted entirely to a life of touch, grease, and absolute, deafening noise:

*   **The Head:** Their skulls are long and low, with no nose or ears, only smooth, scar-like flesh where these features once were. Their eyes have completely vanished; the sockets are filled with solid, protective plugs of fat. Their lower jaw is massive, braced against the collarbone by a thick band of cartilage.
*   **The Chest:** Their rib cages are enormous, barrel-shaped, and hollow. This is not for lung-capacity, but to act as a resonator. Their chests are acoustic drums that absorb the deep, vibrating hum of the Harp-mouths, preventing their inner organs from being torn apart by the constant sound-pressure.
*   **The Limbs:** Their arms are long, ending in three-toed hands with flat, spade-like nails of solid grey horn. They do not walk upright; they crawl on all fours, their knees and elbows protected by thick, leathery pads of yellow tallow-callus.

The Sodalists do not use vocal cords; the Sough would swallow any voice before it could cross a yard of air. Instead, they communication through **osteophonics**—bone-speech. 

To speak, a Sodalist presses their flat, broad sternum against the wet dolomite floor and clicks their teeth together in complex, rhythmic patterns. The vibration travels down their jawbone, through their collarbone, and into the solid rock. A mile away, another Sodalist, lying flat with their rib cage pressed against the stone, can "hear" the message through the bones of their chest. It is a slow, heavy language—a talk of stone, tide, and oil.

They live in the "Crinkles"—the narrow, dry crevices high up on the dolomite walls where the ceiling drops so low that the Stilt-walkers cannot reach them. Here, they build flat nests of dried grease-skins and the needle-like legs of dead walkers, bound together with cement made of calcite-paste and their own sticky saliva.

They are hunters and herders, though their methods are slow and patient. They do not use spears; they use **Sound-Staves**. A Sound-Stave is a long, hollow bone of a Siphon-lung, capped with a dried Harp-mouth cartilage. By scraping the stave against the polished stone, a Sodalist can mimic the deep, attractive thrum of a feeding spot, drawing a slow-rolling Marrow-Drum or a herd of Grease-slugs directly into their waiting pit-traps.

They worship the **Siphon-lungs**. To the Sodalists, these swinging ceiling-giants are the "Great Breathers." Every few weeks, when the CO2 tide rises and the air in the lower basins turns to a heavy, invisible poison, the Sodalists do not run to the high ridges like the beasts. Instead, they gather beneath the largest Siphon-lungs, climbing up the stone-corals to reach the hanging bladders. They slide their arms into the skin-folds of the lung, clinging to the giant beast like ticks. 

As the lung pumps, the Sodalists breathe the warm, filtered oxygen that escapes from its seams, surviving the dead tide in a state of suspended, shivering hibernation, their chests thrumming in perfect unison with the great, wet heart of the ceiling.
