The people who live in the Sieve are small-boned and spare, their bodies shaped by a lifetime of squeezing through gaps and climbing vertical handholds. They have none of the broad, heavy-striding bulk of the plateau-dwellers above. Instead, they move with a low, light-footed grace that suggests a lizard’s familiarity with gravity. 

To look at them is to look at the stone itself. Their skin is unusually pale, almost translucent in the brief silver noon, but it is not soft; it has been toughened by the constant abrasive kiss of the limestone. A fine, white dust of dry calcium often settles in the small creases of their knuckles and around their fingernails, giving them the look of stonemasons who have forgotten to wash. 

They do not let their hair grow long. A single wet strand falling into a calcifying drip could stiffen into a needle and break off at the scalp, so both men and women keep their heads shorn close with bone-handled bronze razors. To protect their scalps from the cold, wet weeping of the gorge, they wear skullcaps of thick, boiled-wool felt, lacquered with tallow to shed the water like a duck’s back.

Indeed, their clothing is their shield. They wear heavy, sleeveless jerkins of hide or multi-layered wool, dyed the dark charcoal-gray of wet slate so that when they stand still against the cliffs, they seem to disappear entirely. Every seam is sealed with a grease made from boiled sheep-fat and the yellow liverwort that clings to the rocks. It smells of cellar-cold and mutton, a scent that hangs over the entire population like a second skin. On their feet, they wear no hard leather shoes—which would slip on the wet, mossy shelves—but rather thick socks woven from coarse dog-hair and hemp, which they dip into the calcium-water for a single day to form a stiff, grippy sole that is worn for a week and then discarded when it becomes too brittle.

In the Sieve, survival is a matter of discipline and absolute space-etiquette. Because the pathways are too narrow for two people to pass shoulder-to-shoulder, they have developed a movement known as "the turn." When two inhabitants meet on a cliffside ledge, they do not push past; instead, they step into each other's space, chest-to-chest, and rotate like the interlocking gears of a clock, their bodies brushing in a silence so complete that the only sound is the slide of wool against wool.

This silence is their greatest law. To speak in a normal tone of voice is considered a grotesque display of selfishness, an intrusion that strikes the ears of fifty neighbors simultaneously. For generations, they have developed a language that is almost entirely lacking in hard plosives or sharp, clicking consonants. They do not say "t," "k," or "p." Their tongue is a fluid, rushing murmur of sibilants, low hums, and soft dental fricatives—a language that sounds like the sigh of water through dry-stone walls. When a mother speaks to her child, she places her lips directly against the child’s forehead, allowing her voice to vibrate through the bone rather than carrying through the air.

Because of the sheer cliffs and the ever-present danger of a fifty-foot drop, a child’s life in the Sieve begins under the law of "the Tether." From the moment they can crawl, children are fitted with a harness of braided hemp rope, the other end of which is pegged deep into the dry-stone wall of the family’s cabin. For the first six or seven years of life, a child’s world is a radius of twelve paces. They learn to walk on vertical stone before they ever see a flat field. Their hands become thick and strong early; a four-year-old in the Sieve can hang from a dry-stone cedar rung by their fingertips for minutes without crying out.

As they grow, the inhabitants specialize in the management of the water that would otherwise swallow them. There are the *Scrapers*, mostly the young and the very old whose hands are still nimble or no longer suited for heavy carrying. Every morning at dawn, before the pale smoke of the turf-fires begins to rise, the gorge is filled with a dry, rhythmic *chrr-chrr-chrr*. This is the sound of thousands of slate scrapers cleaning the doors, the ladders, and the public channels. To miss a morning’s scrape is to invite the stone to take your home.

Then there are the *Weavers of the Drip*, the engineers of this vertical city. They are the ones who understand how to guide the petrifying waters. Armed with small, hollowed Elder-wood troughs and clay dams, they redirect the heavy calcium drips toward things they wish to harden. They place bundles of dry willow branches beneath the heavy flows, transforming them in a matter of months into stone-hard lattices used to reinforce the foundations of new cabins. They petrify their baskets, their storage chests, and even the heavy wool blankets they use for roof-coverings, turning soft, rot-prone organic materials into everlasting, bone-white slate.

Because no grain can grow in the perpetual shade of the cleft, the Sieve is tied to the plateau above by long, swinging hemp ropes. The people of the Sieve are master carvers; they trade petrified stone-fittings, indestructible travertine bowls, and dried shelf-fungus (which burns with a slow, smokeless heat highly prized by the plateau-farmers) in exchange for salt, barley, and hard lard. They rarely go up to the Rim themselves. The blinding light of the flat earth hurts their wide, dark-adapted pupils, and the vast, open sky above the plateau gives them a sickness of the inner ear, a terrifying feeling that they are about to fall upward into the empty blue.

Death in the Sieve is not a departure from the city, but a permanent integration into it. 

When an inhabitant dies, their family does not seek a grave in the hard limestone of the plateau, nor do they burn the body, for wood is too scarce. Instead, the deceased is wrapped in a shroud of open-weave hemp and carried down to the *Sough*, the deep, lightless troughs at the very bottom of the gorge where the water is thickest with lime. Here, in the cold, weeping dark, they lay the body upon a bed of river-silt and direct the heavy petrifying drips over it. 

Within three seasons, the soft tissues are gone, replaced atom by atom with the white, glittering travertine of the gorge. The body becomes solid stone—a heavy, smooth, white sculpture that is entirely fused to the floor of the Sieve. Once the process is complete, the *Weavers of the Drip* retrieve the calcified ancestor. They carry the heavy, marble-like form back up into the residential tiers, where it is laid with great ceremony as a foundation stone for a new cabin, or as a step in a public staircase. 

Every person in the Sieve knows exactly which steps were once their grandparents. They tread upon them with bare, calloused feet, grateful for the familiar, steady grip of their ancestors' shoulders, holding the city together against the wet, cold slide of the cliff.
