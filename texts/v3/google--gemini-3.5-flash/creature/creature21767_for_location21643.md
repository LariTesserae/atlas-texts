The people who inhabit Oakhaven-on-the-Spire are defined by weight, or rather, by their obsession with retaining it.

### The Spire-Born (The Leaden-Footed)

Because the air is like wet wool and the local gravity behaves with a sluggish, viscous hesitation, a human body here is structurally different from those of the lowlands. The Spire-born are long-limbed and thin-ribbed, with pale, slightly translucent skin that never burns under the silver sun but instead develops a gray, slate-colored grain. Their voices are low—high frequencies do not travel well through the dense, water-logged atmosphere, so they speak in deep, gravelly hums and sibilant murmurs that carry across the open gaps between the iron gantries.

They do not run. To run on the smooth, vitreous surface of the obsidian is to invite disaster; one’s feet lose purchase too easily, and once airborne, a person might drift forty feet before touching down again, helpless against the cold updrafts. Instead, they move with a heavy, sliding gait known as the *silt-shuffle*. 

Their dress is industrial and protective. They wear long coats of cured seal-skin, heavily greased with coal-tar to shed the constant condensation. The most distinctive feature of their attire is the *ballast-belt*—a thick sash of woven copper wire filled with lead shot or scrap casting-iron. A Spire-born citizen feels naked without their weights. To go "un-burdened" is considered a sign of madness or a desire for suicide. Children are fitted with small, pewter-soled boots as soon as they learn to walk, to keep them from drifting off the residential balconies during a sudden steam-vent surge.

### The Gaffer Crews

Among the human population, the most respected and feared are the Gaffers. These are the workers who tend the great transit cables and salvage the things—and people—who have slipped from the wards.

Because a falling object descends at the speed of a dropping leaf, a "fall" in Oakhaven is not an instant death, but a long, terrifying ordeal. If a coal-shoveler slips from a foundry platform, they do not plunge into the abyss. They sink through the gray air, drifting sideways on the thermal currents of the boilers. 

The Gaffers watch for these occurrences from the lower gantries. They are equipped with *harpoon-lines*—coiled ropes tipped with rubberized suction cups or soft-iron hooks—and sixty-foot poles made of hollow, light-weight nickel alloy. When a worker or a crate of rivets goes over, the Gaffers deploy their hooks. It is a slow, methodical rescue; they must calculate the drift of the cold wool-air, casting their lines with the deliberate patience of deep-sea fishermen. 

There is a tragic caste among them known as the *Drift-watchers*. Sometimes, an individual falls too far from the cables, into the dead spaces between the towers where the thermal currents do not reach. These souls enter the "Long Sinking." Because they are still wearing their ballast-belts, they do not float indefinitely; they sink toward the gray cotton of the Well at a rate of perhaps five miles an hour. If the Gaffers cannot reach them within the first mile, they are abandoned. Through telescopes, these "sinking stars" can sometimes be seen for hours, small, dark shapes slowly turning in the silver mist, waving their arms in silent, agonizing farewell until they are swallowed by the cloud-floor.

### The Glass-Listeners

At the lowest points of the residential towers, where the iron girders are bolted directly into the young black stone, live the *Litho-Scholars*, commonly called the Glass-Listeners. 

They are a deaf or semi-deaf order, their eardrums scarred by years of pressing their ears to long copper speaking-tubes driven deep into the heart of the obsidian teeth. They do not work the foundries or weave the moss. Their sole purpose is to monitor the growth of the mountains.

To the Listeners, the stone is alive, but its life is measured in the screech of vitreous friction. They interpret the sounds of the deep—the glass-cracking growls that reverberate up from the Well. They carry slates upon which they scratch the "temper" of the spires. When the cracks are high-pitched and frequent, like the breaking of thin ice, they warn the ward-masters to loosen the tension on the connecting cables; if the stone is silent, it means the needle has stalled, and the cold of the high altitudes is beginning to freeze the deep joints of the rock.

The Listeners are easily recognized by their calloused, black-stained ears and their habit of touching everything—walls, tables, other people—with their fingertips first, sensing the vibrations of the city rather than listening to its sounds.

### The Vesper-Bellows (The Air-Beasts)

The six-winged, seal-skinned creatures that nest in the iron ironworks are called *Vesper-bellows* or simply *Soughs*. They are as much a part of the city’s population as the humans.

The relationship between the Spiresmen and the Soughs is one of uneasy utility. The beasts are not domesticated in the traditional sense, but they are habituated to the warmth of the coal-ovens. They are scavengers, their wide, toothless mouths designed to scoop up the giant, pale woodlice and silver scale-worms that live in the hanging moss. 

During the night-shifts, when the gas lamps flicker low, the Soughs will glide close to the foundry chimneys, their long, ribbon-like tails trailing through the soot. The workers harvest them for their *bellows-fat*—a thick, yellow grease that does not freeze even at the highest altitudes. This grease is rubbed onto the sliding joints of the cable-cars and the hinges of the great ward-doors.

The Soughs also serve as the city’s organic barometers. When a cold-front is rising from the Well, the creatures will abandon their nests in the lower iron honeycombs and fly upward, settling on the high, bare tips of the obsidian teeth. When they do this, their throat-sacs—which usually whistle in harmony with the industry—emit a low, clicking rattle. To the Spire-born, this is the "frost-cough," and it means the water-spheres on the rock will freeze within the hour, turning the smooth paths into slides of black glass.

### The Pale-Worm and the Slick-Gliders

Down where the iron meets the stone, in the places where the steam pipes leak their sulfurous condensation, a different kind of life exists.

These are the *Slick-gliders*. They are flat, translucent organisms, six to eight feet long, that look like sheets of wet parchment. They have no limbs; instead, their undersides are covered in millions of microscopic cilia that allow them to adhere to the perfectly smooth obsidian. They crawl in silence, feeding on the liver-colored lichens and the mineral salts that bloom where the sulfurous water dries.

The Spire-born do not kill them; they are the city’s cleaners. A Slick-glider can slide over an iron anchor-plate and leave it completely free of rust and coal-soot within a night. However, they are a hazard to foot traffic; their bodies are so slippery that stepping on one is like stepping on wet lard. 

Alongside them are the *Cold-Worms*—blind, thread-like giants that live in the cracks of the older brickwork and the spaces between the copper shingles of the residential wards. They are bioluminescent, glowing with a faint, cold-green light that mirrors the gas lamps. When the coal-gas lines fail, the poorer residents of the lower tiers will collect these worms in glass jars, using them to light their damp, windowless cabins. The light is weak and smells faintly of rotting fern-leaves, but it does not require fuel, and in Oakhaven, fuel is life.
