To understand what lives on the Pedestals, one must first discard the concept of the cell. 

Here, sixty thousand meters above the heavy-gas floors, life does not keep its secrets in wet membranes or warm, soft sacks of water. Such things would boil instantly into the violet-black sky or freeze into lumps of stone-hard ice before the first drop could split. 

Instead, the inhabitants of this high shelf are creatures of tension, glass, and mineral memory. They are as silent as the stone they inhabit, operating on a clock that tikks not in heartbeats, but in the slow accumulation of static charge and the centuries-long decay of magnetic fields.

### The Shiver-Mites (The Core of the Moss)

The pale-blue glass-moss that clings to the basalt dips is not actually a plant, nor is it entirely inanimate. Its hollow, silica-glass fibers are merely the shells—like Caddisfly cases—of the true inhabitants: the **Shiver-Mites**.

To the naked eye, a Shiver-Mite looks like a single drop of liquid mercury, no larger than a grain of titanium dust. They are composed of a eutectic gallium-indium alloy, liquid even in the profound cold of the high altitudes, held together by an incredibly strong electro-static meniscus. They live inside the hollow chambers of the silica fibers, protected from the direct teeth of the solar wind.

When a gravity-shear strikes a patch of glass-moss and shatters it with that dry, metallic click, the Shiver-Mites are cast out onto the bare basalt. Exposed to the raw vacuum and the unblinking sun, their surface tension begins to fail; they are at risk of spreading so thin they would vaporize. 

Their response is a frantic, coordinated dance of electromagnetism. 

Using the natural charge of the basalt, these liquid droplets roll toward one another. When they contact a shard of shattered glass, they run along its length like solder on a copper wire. They carry the shards with them, pulling the pieces together through capillary attraction. Within five minutes of a disaster, hundreds of thousands of these silver droplets have dragged the broken glass fibers back into their original, interlocking hexagonal patterns. They weld the joints back together with tiny, localized arcs of static electricity. To an observer, the moss seems to heal itself; in reality, it is a city of liquid iron-smiths rebuilding their glass towers before the sun can evaporate their bodies.

### The Chime-Parasites of the Loom-Strider

If you were to stand beneath a forty-foot **Loom-Strider** as it stood motionless on the satin edge of a basalt cliff, you would hear nothing. But if you were to press your ear directly against one of its hollow, pitch-black polymer legs, you would hear a faint, irregular, crystalline ringing.

This is the music of the **Chime-Parasites**.

The Loom-Strider’s skeleton is made of a dense, light-absorbing polymer that is incredibly slick and friction-free. However, at the joints—where the great leg-rods pivot to allow the creature to adjust its balance against the gravity-storms—the physical forces are immense. To prevent these joints from grinding into dust, they are colonised by thousands of microscopic, disk-shaped organisms made of natural pyrite.

These disks sit inside the joints like self-perpetuating ball bearings. They do not eat organic tissue, for the Strider has none. Instead, they feed on the immense static electricity produced by the friction of the Strider’s movements. When the Strider steps, the joints warp, compressing the pyrite disks. The disks absorb this kinetic energy and release it as a tiny, harmless spark of light inside the dark joint-sockets, accompanied by a high-frequency vibration. 

This vibration—the *chime*—keeps the joint from seizing. Without these tiny, metallic "lubricants," the Loom-Strider’s joints would weld together under the cold-press of the vacuum within a few dozen miles of travel. The Strider provides the movement; the parasites provide the grease of light and sound that keeps the great beast limber.

### The Weft-Makers and the Great Bridges

The name *Loom-Strider* is not a metaphor. It is a literal description of their function.

As a Strider steps off the edge of a Pedestal and commits its weightless, forty-foot frame to the thirty-mile abyss, it does not merely drift. From a small, specialized pore at the base of its central joint-cluster, it begins to spin.

It extrudes a single thread of pure, vitrified silica, no thicker than a single strand of DNA, but reinforced by molecular alignment. As the Strider floats on the magnetic updrafts, drifting slowly through the purple void toward the next grey island, it drags this thread behind it. The thread is so light that it does not fall; it is suspended by the same magnetic rivers that keep the Strider aloft.

When the Strider finally touches down on the distant pedestal miles away, it anchors the other end of the thread into the basalt. It does this by standing over a patch of Shiver-Mites; the mites, attracted by the charge of the thread, instantly climb it, welding it to the stone with their metallic secretions.

Over three billion years, millions of Striders have crossed these chasms. The space between the Pedestals is not empty. It is threaded with trillions of these invisible glass cables. 

Under the direct, glaring light of the white sun, if you look at a angle of thirty degrees, these cables become visible—not as individual threads, but as a vast, shimmering highway of horizontal light. They form the **Weft**: a planetary network of glass bridges. 

The Weft has its own ecosystem. It serves as the primary hunting ground for the most elusive shape on this world.

### The Sliver-Gliders

The **Sliver-Gliders** are the apex dwellers of the high air, though they possess neither mouths, claws, nor eyes.

A Sliver-Glider is a flat, rectangular sheet of polarized silicate glass, measuring roughly twelve feet long by six feet wide, and only a single molecule thick. They are completely invisible when viewed from the front or side; they can only be seen when they turn, caught in the sun, flashing like a mirror dropped from the heavens.

The Gliders spend their entire existence sliding along the glass bridges spun by the Loom-Striders. They have no limbs; instead, they clamp onto the invisible glass threads using electrostatic attraction, like a monorail car on its track. They slide along these cables at speeds of up to eighty miles per hour, propelled by the solar wind pushing against their broad, flat surfaces.

They are cleaners. As they travel the lines, they sweep them of the titanium dust that constantly threatens to weigh the cables down and snap them. The Glider’s underbelly is highly reactive; it chemically bonds with the dust, "eating" the titanium and incorporating it into its own frame to repair the micro-fractures caused by solar radiation. 

When two Gliders meet on the same thread, they do not fight. They slide through one another. Because they are only a single molecule thick, their atomic lattices simply interlock for a fraction of a second, exchanging heat and structural data, before sliding apart to continue their endless, clean-up run across the abyss.

### The Iron-Drimes (The Anchored Minds)

The absolute mysteries of this world are the **Iron-Drimes** that live within the protective circles of the ancient obsidian needles.

In the dead, warm pockets of space where light does not warp and the titanium dust cannot enter, there are no Loom-Striders or Shiver-Mites. These spaces are too quiet for them. Instead, floating around the black needles like a halo of dark iron smoke, are the Drimes.

They are small, needle-sharp crystals of magnetite, hovering in the stable magnetic field of the ancient anchors. They do not move of their own volition; they are held in place by the silent, active forces of the needles. 

Yet, they are not dead. 

If you watch them for hours, you will see that they are constantly shifting. A single grain of iron-sand will fall from the top of the needle, drift down through the warm air, and join a cluster at the base. Another will rise. This motion is not chaotic; it is a slow, mechanical computation.

The Drimes are a physical, three-dimensional neural network. The magnetic field of the needle acts as the "brain," and the iron-sand grains are the "synapses." The speed of their thought is glacial—it may take three weeks for a single "thought-wave" to ripple from the top of an obsidian needle to the ground—but it is continuous. 

They are the archivists. They remember the names of the mountains before they were worn down into flat basalt tables. They remember the seas of cold methane that once filled the chasms. They gather the vibrations of the gravity-storms, translation-waves from the Weft-bridges, and the slow, rhythmic steps of the Loom-Striders, and they turn them into stone data, writing and rewriting the history of a dead planet in the geometry of floating iron dust.

They are the only things that know what this world was. They are the only things that care that it is gone.
