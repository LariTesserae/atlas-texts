To enter the Oubliette is to enter a throat of leather and stone, where every breath is borrowed from the exhalations of ten thousand bodies. 

The undisputed masters of the basalt—the foundation upon which all other life in the chimney is built—are the **Plinths**. 

Seen from a distance, if there were light to see them, they would look like a coat of heavy, dark-grey mail armor protecting the mountain’s throat. Closer, they are individual giants of patience. A Plinth is a creature of simple, heavy architecture. Its upper surface is a thick dome of charcoal skin, covered in a fine, greasy velvet that repels the constant, dripping condensation but traps the warmth rising from the dark below. Underneath this dome lies no skeleton, only a dense, concentric web of muscle—muscles like the cured meat of an ancient bull, tough enough to withstand the immense pressure of their neighbors. 

The Plinth’s underside is a single, circular organ of attachment: the *sole*. This sole does not merely stick to the basalt; it has spent generations digesting it. Through microscopic pores, the sole secretes a weak, vinegar-sharp acid that slowly softens the rock, allowing the Plinth to sink its flesh into the microscopic pores of the stone. Over centuries, these creatures have literally eaten their way an inch or two into the mountaintop, creating the very "scoops" and hollows that define the wall. They are the sculptors of the chimney, turning jagged volcanic vents into a smooth, biological canal.

A Plinth has no head, no eyes, and no limbs. It has only two bodily functions visible to the outer world: the *chasm* and the *pore*. The chasm is a deep, horizontal fold across its upper rim, lined with stiff, yellow bristles. This is its lung and its mouth. Every few minutes, the chasm peels open with a sound like wet leather tearing apart, draws in a long, rattling draft of the warm, sulfurous steam, filters out the microscopic fungal spores and mineral dust floating in the draft, and then slaps shut. The pore, located at the lower edge, is a small, muscular valve that occasionally releases a thick, paste-like deposit—the digest of rock-minerals and mountain moisture—which slide down the wall to feed the lower orders.

Living on, between, and through the Plinths are the **Scrapers**. 

They are the chimney’s citizens, its tenders, and its only thinkers. A Scraper is roughly the size of a human child, but its proportions are stretched and distorted by the verticality of its world. Its skin is the color of lard, translucent enough that in the faint violet light of the liverwort, one can see the sluggish pulse of its blue, copper-based blood moving through its long, spindly limbs. It has four arms, each identical, terminating in three long, multi-jointed fingers. These fingers do not have nails; instead, the tips are covered in a hard, black substance resembling horn, worn into sharp, chisel-like edges.

The Scrapers have no eyes. Where a face should be, there is only a smooth, pale wedge of skin, highly sensitive to drafts and temperature changes, and a wide, lipless mouth filled with flat, grinding plates of bone. Above the mouth sits a pair of deep pits—organs of vibration. When a Scraper wants to "see" its world, it taps its stone tool against the basalt. The sound waves travel through the air and the stone, bouncing off the flesh of the Plinths and the bodies of other Scrapers, returning to those facial pits as a perfect, three-dimensional map of their immediate surroundings. 

Their culture is one of custody and extreme preservation. Because they cannot build, they must inherit. The most precious possession a Scraper can hold is its *core-stone*—a pebble of green river-jasper or red porphyry. These stones do not belong to the chimney; they were brought here aeons ago from the valleys below, perhaps by ancestors who still remembered the sky, or perhaps carried up in the gizzards of long-dead mountain birds. These stones are passed down from parent to child. When a Scraper dies, its companions do not let it fall into the Sump; they harvest its stone tools, its vine-cords, and even its bones, which are scraped clean and wedged into rock crevices to serve as anchor-pegs for the living.

The relation between Scraper and Plinth is mutual, though grim. The Plinths are prone to **Grit-Barnacles**—small, conical parasites that drill into the Plinth’s velvet skin to reach the hot, rich blood beneath. If left unchecked, the barnacles will multiply until the Plinth dies and drops from the wall, leaving a cold, bare patch of basalt. The Scrapers prevent this. With their stone tools, they climb across the leathery backs of the Plinths, searching for the hard, grey cones of the barnacles. When they find one, they begin the slow, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of excavation, chipping away the parasite’s shell until they can reach the soft, fat grub inside. This grub is the Scrapers’ sweetmeat, their primary source of fat and salt. 

In return for this dental-like service, the Plinths tolerate the Scrapers. When a Scraper’s fingers rest on a Plinth’s hide, the Plinth does not slide away or contract its lung. It remains still, a massive, warm island of flesh for the little blind keeper to cling to.

There is another, rarer thing that lives in the high, cold throat where the chimney begins to leak its heat into the sky. These are the **Wind-Catchers**, or *Vanes*. They are degenerate Scrapers who have been cast out, or who have willingly climbed up into the "Freezer"—the zone where the basalt turns cold and the water freezes into rime. 

The Vanes have lost the use of their limbs; their fingers have fused into long, stiff rib-bones that support thin sails of skin. They do not climb; they hang from the very lip of the chimney by a single, calcified hook-toe, dangling out over the freezing, cloud-swept abyss of the outer peak. There, they catch the high-altitude winds, their skin sails turning blue and stiff with frost. They serve as the chimney’s sentinels. When a storm approaches, or when the outside air pressure drops, the Vanes feel the change first. They begin to whistle—not with their mouths, but by forcing the freezing air through the hollow, marrowless bones of their sail-limbs. 

That whistle, a high, piercing keen that cuts through the warm hum of the chimney below, is the signal for the **Great Sealing**. 

Upon hearing the Vanes' whistle, the Plinths begin to shift. In a movement that takes six or seven hours, the thousands of flat, leathery plates slide together, overlapping their edges more tightly, closing the gaps of liverwort, and compressing their bodies until the walls of the chimney are a solid, seamless tube of living hide. The Scrapers retreated into the deep, wet wrinkles between the Plinths, tying themselves tight with their vine-cords. For a day, or a week, while the storm rages outside, the chimney becomes airtight. No heat escapes; no cold enters. The air inside turns thick, foul, and sweet as rot, and the ten thousand hearts beat together in a single, suffocating dark.

At the very bottom of the world, below the Plinths, where the chimney drops into absolute water, lives the **Sump-Life**. 

Here, in a pool of black, tepid water that has filtered through three miles of basalt, there are no Plinths, for the stone is too wet and smooth for their acid-soles to find purchase. Instead, the pool is home to the **Flensing-Worms**. They are long, white, ribbon-like creatures, up to ten feet in length, with no mouthparts but a skin that is entirely absorptive. They live on the rain of waste that falls from the crowded walls above—the shed skin-velvet of the Plinths, the droppings of the Scrapers, and the occasional body of a Scraper whose vine snapped in the dark. 

When something falls into the Sump, the water does not splash so much as it parts to receive it. Within seconds, the white ribbons lazily entwine the fallen object. There is no sound of tearing or biting; there is only a slow, silent dissolving. Within a week, even the teeth of a fallen Scraper have been turned to soup and drunk by the water, leaving nothing but the green or red core-stone resting on the pool's floor, waiting for some incredibly brave, deep-diving Scraper to descend on a long cord and retrieve the stone of their ancestors.

This is the cycle of the chimney: a closed loop of basalt, blood, and stone. Nothing is lost. Every scrap of heat is recycled; every drop of water is licked from the rock; every bone is kept to serve as a ladder for those who are still holding on.
