If you leave a machine running in the dark for a thousand years, the water does not remain empty. It does not breed fish, for there are no eggs to hatch and no oxygen to sustain them, but it breeds an ecology of stasis. 

The primary inhabitant of the vault lives inside the turbine itself. It has no name, but if it had to be categorized, it would be called a *Saponum*—a creature born of chemical transformation and extreme, unmoving time.

When the machine was first submerged and abandoned, its interior chambers were packed with heavy lubricants: tallow, whale grease, mineral oils, and ancient petroleum compounds designed to seal the massive gears and pistons against the water. Over ten centuries, as the minerals from the surrounding basalt seeped through the micro-cracks in the vault dome, they reacted with these fats. They saponified. The lubricants did not wash away; instead, they turned into a hard, waxen, lard-gray mass that slowly expanded to fill every internal void of the twenty-meter turbine.

This mass is alive, though not in any way a biologist would recognize. It is a slow, calcified mind of grease and copper-sulfate. 

The Saponum has no organs. Its "flesh" is the color of old candles, cold and soap-like to the touch, but dense as oak. Because it has been pressed into the interior of the machine for centuries, its body is cast in the exact shape of the turbine's inner workings. Its outer skin is stamped with the negative impressions of cogs, the spiral grooves of screw-threads, and the smooth, curved bore of the hydraulic cylinders. It is a living mold of the technology that contains it.

It feeds upon the sixty-hertz hum. The electrical current that runs through the iron coils generates a faint, micro-thermal footprint—a fraction of a degree warmer than the sterile water around it. To the Saponum, this warmth is a sun. It draws the current into itself through a network of "nerves" that were not grown, but precipitated: paper-thin veins of green copper-scale and lead-bismuth dendrites that have naturally formed along the boundaries of its waxen segments. 

The ribbon-like "water weeds" that cling to the copper piping are actually the Saponum’s sensory organs. They are not biological plants. They are thin, hollow tubes of silica and grease that have squeezed out of the machine's joints and wept toward the light. Having no eyes, the Saponum uses these stiff, glass-like threads to sense the state of the vault. They do not drift because they are perfectly rigid, engineered by their own slow growth to act as tuning forks. They are attuned to the 60Hz vibration of the iron. If a single weed were to be touched or bent, the frequency would shift, and the Saponum would experience it as a sudden, blinding flash of pressure.

The Saponum’s consciousness is entirely static. It does not remember a past, and it does not hope for a future; it exists in a massive, heavy, eternal *Now*. It "imagines" the cathedral vault not as a visual space, but as a map of physical resistances—the cold basalt floor, the absolute weight of the water column, and the distant, burning points of the floodlamps, which it perceives only as faint, prickling needles of heat against its external feelers.

It is not alone in the vault, though its companions are smaller and even more foreign to the concept of life.

Clinging to the outer plates of the turbine, and occasionally to the iron housings of the floodlamps, are the *Nodules*. 

They look like hexagonal scabs of tarnished steel and green carbonate, each about the size of a human palm. To a casual observer, they would appear to be nothing more than unusual patterns of corrosion. But they move.

Their movement is so slow that it can only be measured over decades. They have no legs or cilia; instead, they are "current-walkers." They are electrical precipitates, held together and animated solely by the stray voltage that leaks from the ancient wiring into the chemically pure water. By slowly reorganizing their internal magnetic alignment against the iron hull of the machine, they lift and slide across the surface, migrating toward the points of highest electrical potential.

A Nodule will spend fifty years crawling up a copper conduit to reach a junction box where the insulation is particularly thin. Once there, it will settle down, its mineral body locking into place. It "feeds" on the voltage, glowing with a microscopic, cold, blue luminescence—a tiny, flickering spark of violet light that is the only deviation from the flat, yellow glare of the lamps. 

If the current in the chamber were to be cut, the Nodules would not die; they would simply cease to exist. Without the magnetic fields to hold their mineral lattices together, their structures would collapse, and they would slide off the machine, dissolving into fine, orange rust on the basalt floor within a few hours.

In this flooded cathedral, there is no death because there is no birth. There is only the preservation of form. The Saponum sleeps inside its iron shell, listening to the eternal hum of its cradle, while the Nodules crawl their glacial miles across the rust-brown plates, all of them locked in a perfect, frozen equilibrium with the dead water and the unblinking lights.
