### The Lichen-Wool and the Shingle-Backs

To understand what lives in the Sump-Well of Section Nine, one must first understand that here, the boundary between the organic and the mechanical has dissolved, not through any sudden miracle of technology, but through two centuries of slow, damp intimacy. The inhabitants of this well do not merely occupy the infrastructure; they are its crust, its lubricants, and its ears.

#### The White Wool (The Tallow-Moss)
In the high crevices where the mortar has turned to a soft, alkaline paste, the **White Wool** grows. It is the lowest tier of life in the well, though "growth" is perhaps too active a word for its progress. It behaves more like a mineral deposit that has learned to breathe. 

The Wool is composed of hair-thin, hollow threads of a substance resembling soft, wet nylon. It produces no chlorophyll and requires no light; instead, it is a chemical scavenger. Its root-hairs secrete a mild, vinegar-like acid that slowly dissolves the lime of the blue brick, while its upper filaments trap the microscopic droplets of vaporized tallow and machine-grease that float in the heavy air. 

If you touch a patch of the Wool, it does not crush like green moss; it yields with the dry, springy resistance of sheep's fleece. It has no scent of its own, but it acts as a filter, absorbing the sharp copper tang of the walls until the patches nearest the brass valves smell faintly of old pennies. Because it is saturated with tallow, it is highly hydrophobic; water-drips do not sink into it but roll off its white tufts like globules of mercury, falling into the black pools below without wetting the plant itself.

In the complete darkness, the Wool is a dull, chalky white. But when a lantern-light or a spark from a wire touches it, its filaments absorb the energy and store it. For several minutes afterward, the patch will glow with a cold, phosphor-green luminescence—a slow, silent emission that looks like green breath rising from the brickwork.

#### The Grease-Skimmers (The Slate-Lice)
The middle tier of the Sump-Well’s brickwork, where the water-level rises and falls along the walls, is covered by a secondary skin of **Grease-Skimmers**. 

These are flat, oval creatures about the size of an engineer’s palm. Their backs are protected by five overlapping plates of dark, soot-colored chitin that have the texture and matte finish of wet roofing slate. Underneath this armor, they are nearly flat, possessing twelve pairs of short, jointed legs tipped with tiny, rubbery pads that can grip wet magnetite or slimy brick with equal tenacity.

The Skimmers do not swim; they are too heavy, and their bodies are designed for two-dimensional existence. They crawl in slow, interlocking herds across the lower three feet of the walls, moving with a collective, shifting slide that resembles the motion of shingles on an old roof during a gale. When two Skimmers meet, they do not fight; they slide over one another with a faint, dry *click-click* of their plates, forming double and triple layers over the richest oil-seeps.

Their mouths are specialized scraping tools—two curved, horn-like blades that move horizontally, like garden shears. They spend their lives shaving the microscopic film of tallow-moss and diesel-soot from the brick. A brick that has been grazed by a herd of Skimmers is left perfectly clean, showing the raw, dark blue glaze of the original Victorian clay beneath the grime. 

The Skimmers are the true timekeepers of the well. Every three hours, when the deep siphon below the floor-plates begins to draw the pools down, the Skimmers feel the drop in pressure. In unison, they tilt their hindplates upward, allowing the retreating water to drain from beneath their bodies. The sound of ten thousand slate-like shells tilting at once is a dry, rustling rattle—like a hand being run through a bucket of dry river stones—that echoes off the boilerplates just before the water begins to move.

#### The Steam-Crickets
On the horizontal iron mains, where the steam-leaks keep the black magnetite pipes at a constant, lukewarm temperature, live the **Steam-Crickets**. 

These are dry, brittle creatures, straw-white and entirely blind. Their bodies are small—no larger than a pine nut—but their antennae are extraordinary, stretching four or five inches from their heads in thin, glass-like whips that are constantly in motion, tracing the currents of warm air.

Unlike their surface cousins, the Steam-Crickets have lost the ability to jump; their hind legs have withered into short, stout anchors that they use to clamp themselves to the rusted seams of the pipes. They do not chirp. To make sound in this wet, echoing chamber would draw the attention of the eels in the pools below. Instead, they communicate through vibration. 

A cricket will tap its abdomen against the iron pipe—*tap-tap... tap*—sending a tiny, metallic click through the metal. A second cricket, fifty feet away down the same pipe, will receive the vibration through its front legs and click back. Throughout the day, the iron mains act as a telegraph wire for thousands of these silent conversations, a subtle hum of iron-born Morse code that is completely inaudible to anyone standing in the center of the chamber, but which can be felt if one lays a bare palm against the warm magnetite.

They survive on the shed skins of the Grease-Skimmers and the drift of dead Wool-moss that floats up on the warm updrafts from the pipes. They are creatures of pure thrift, living for years on a diet of dry grease and iron-dust.

#### The Siphon-Eels
In the black, circular pools, where the water is thick with oil and dissolved minerals, the **Siphon-Eels** rule the dark. 

These are pale, muscular creatures, three to four feet long, with skin so thin and translucent that their internal workings are visible under a strong light. They have no skeleton; their bodies are supported by a thick, cartilege-like rod that runs from their snout to their tail, and their flesh has the consistency of cold lard. Through their clear sides, one can see the slow, rhythmic pulsing of their three hearts—soft, dark-purple sacks that beat in a staggering, three-part rhythm—and the long, grey tube of their digestive tract, which is often packed with the round, black shells of dead Grease-Skimmers.

The Siphon-Eels have no eyes or teeth. Their heads terminate in a wide, circular disc of ribbed, pink flesh—a mouth that acts as a powerful suction cup. They survive by seeking out the iron grates where the small drainage siphons empty into the deeper sumps. An eel will mount itself against a grate, sealing its disc around the rusted bars, and use its powerful muscular throat to suck at the debris—dead crickets, clumps of moss, and grease-crust—that has become trapped there.

When they breathe, they draw water through their nasal slits and expel it through five pair of gills set deep behind their heads. The sound is a wet, mechanical *shluck-shluck*, identical to the sound of a leather bilge pump with a worn valve. When three or four eels are feeding at separate grates around the chamber, the room sounds as though it is being slowly drained by a team of invisible, tired workers.

The eels are long-lived and slow-growing. A specimen thirty inches long may be fifty years old, its gut containing nothing but polished brass rivet-heads it has sucked from the pipes and is unable to pass. When they die, they do not float; they sink to the bottom of the deep sumps, where the grease in their tissues slowly soapifies, turning them into hard, white lumps of tallow that the next generation of eels will eventually scrape and dissolve.

### The Lead-Sleevers

If you remain in the Sump-Well long enough—past the three-hour mark, when the siphon has completed its long, wet breath and the water begins to rise again—you may see the **Lead-Sleevers**.

They are not animals, nor are they human, though they possess the general silhouette of a man who has spent his life bent double in a narrow crawlspace. There are never more than two or three of them in Section Nine at any time. They are the descendants of some long-forgotten class of sewer-tender or boiler-cleaner, though they have lived in the dark so long that their very tissues have been rewritten by the lime and the lead.

A Lead-Sleever is about four feet tall when standing, though they rarely stand; they move in a low, three-legged crawl, using their long arms to lift their heavy, trailing lower bodies over the wet brickwork. Their limbs are long and thin, like iron rods wrapped in grey felt. They have no skin in the human sense; their surface is a thick, self-healing layer of grey, waxy tallow that smells of lard and wet slate. This "skin" is completely impervious to the acidic water of the pools and the sting of the oxidizing copper carbonate.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. They have only three fingers on each hand, each finger as long as a chisel and tipped with a heavy, spade-like nail of dark, hardened keratin. These fingers are used to scrape the lime-scale from the small, vital brass vents that feed the steam-piston. They do this without tools, using their nails to peel the crust of copper carbonate from the metal as though they were peeling an orange.

The head of a Lead-Sleever is small, smooth, and neckless, sitting directly between their high, muscular shoulders like a wet stone. They have no nose or external ears, and where their eyes should be, there are only two deep, vertical slits that run from the forehead to the cheek. These slits do not contain eyeballs; instead, they are lined with fine, nerve-rich hairs that can detect the slightest change in air pressure or temperature. If a door is opened in Section Eight, three levels up, the shift in the draft will cause the Lead-Sleevers to halt instantly, their long fingers freezing against the iron pipes as they "listen" to the weight of the air.

They do not speak. They have no vocal cords, and their mouths are small, horizontal slits that they use only to draw in the cool, wet air of the well. Their internal temperature is low—only a few degrees warmer than the well-kept cellar temperature of the water—and their movements are so slow and deliberate that they do not disturb the Steam-Crickets on the pipes they pass.

The Lead-Sleevers and the machinery exist in a state of mutual necessity. The Sleevers do not understand the purpose of the bronze piston or the carbon-filament bulbs; they do not know what lies beyond the brick walls of Section Nine. To them, the piston is a giant, iron heart that must be kept free of lime, and the copper mains are great, warm roots that must be cleared of the choking Wool-moss so they may continue to breathe. 

In return, the machine provides them with their only source of heat and food. The Sleevers feed on the **tallow-crust**—the thick, yellow grease that accumulates around the glands of the bronze piston and the packing-guts of the larger valves. Once every few hours, a Sleever will approach the piston-recess, wait for the rod to draw back with its long, wet *sigh*, and then reach into the dark chamber with three long fingers to scoop out a lump of the warm, mineral-scented grease. They eat it slowly, their waxy mouths moving with a silent, rhythmic grinding, before turning back to the dark walls to continue their endless, scraping patrol.

### The Cycle of the Well

In this place, nothing is wasted. 

The Wool-moss feeds on the steam-grease; 
The Skimmers scrape the moss; 
The crickets eat the Skimmers' shed skins; 
The eels clean the grates that keep the water moving; 
The Lead-Sleevers eat the grease of the piston and clear the lime that would otherwise choke the siphons; 
And the siphons, breathing their long, three-hour sighs, lift the water back to the upper streets, carrying with it the microscopic salt and iron-dust of the well’s slow decay.

It is a world that has traded light for survival, and speed for duration. It is cold, it is wet, and it has all the time in the world.
