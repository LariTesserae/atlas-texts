To understand the life within Segment Eighty-Two, one must first understand that to everything living here, the world has no horizontal axis.

There is no "beside" or "across" that is not also a "clinging to." To move is to ascend or descend; to rest is to wedge oneself into a seam of iron or to hang by a line. There is no concept of a floor, only of a fall that has no end, and thus the creatures here do not have feet meant for walking. They have hooks, pads, clamps, and prehensile tails. They do not have ears, for to have ears in a place where the air is a solid, vibrating block of four-hundred-decibel machine-thrum would be to live in constant, bleeding agony. 

They are a people and an ecology of silence, existing in the heart of an eternal roar.

### The Tap-Lizards (The Iron-Drummers)

They are the most common visible tenants of the plates, scuttling over the emerald-green moss like pale, five-inch fingers. Their skin is so thin and transparent that you can see the rhythmic pulsing of their two hearts—one to pump their cold, grease-sweetened blood, and another small, auxiliary heart near the tail that seems to exist solely to balance the pressure of the vertical drafts.

Their eyes are large, black, and pupil-less, covered by a tough, clear scale that protects them from the stinging steam-spray. But they do not use sight to find their way. 

Instead, their front claws are tipped with hard, carbonized nodules of bone, like little drill-bits. As they move, they tap. They do not tap at random. Each skink is a telegraphist. By striking the cast-iron plates, they send a sharp click through the metal. Because the plates are bolted together into a single, continuous vertical drum, these vibrations travel for miles. 

A skink on Segment Eighty-Two can tell, by the tone of the ring, whether a plate three hundred meters down is dry or wet, whether the lichen is thick or sparse, and whether another skink is proposing a meeting or warning of a grease-leak. To a human, the iron wall is dead metal; to the skinks, it is an instrument of immense complexity, humming with a thousand overlapping family histories, territory disputes, and weather reports from the hot deep.

### The Cable-Saddles

The three great black cables that drop through the center of the void are not dead iron rope. They are an island-chain, occupied by a species of creature that never touches the walls. 

These are the Cable-Saddles. They are segmented, slug-like organisms, three to four feet long, that have evolved to clasp the thick, greasy steel of the cables. Their bellies are grooved to match the twist of the cable’s wire strands, like a nut on a bolt. They have no limbs; instead, they have rows of small, muscular valves on their undersides that squeeze the cable, allowing them to slide down the grease-film like slow, heavy drops of oil.

They feed exclusively on the tallow, grease, and carbon dust that accumulate on the cables. They are the cleaners of the lines. They move in only one direction: down. They slide, millimeter by millimeter, over weeks and months, scraping the steel clean with broad, radula-like tongues that smell faintly of sulfur.

When a Cable-Saddle reaches the bottom of its run—far down where the heat becomes dry and the steam begins to burn—it does not turn back. It cannot climb. Instead, it swells with gas, its body cavity filling with light, heated vapors from the boiler-exhausts. It detaches. 

In the open void of the conduit, the creature becomes a small, dark balloon, drifting upward on the heavy updrafts. It floats up through the pearlescent light of Segment Eighty-Two, a silent, bloated sausage of skin, until it reaches the cold, wet air of the upper segments. There, the gas condenses, the balloon collapses, and the creature—now thin and hungry again—re-establishes its grip on one of the three cables, ready to begin the long, slow downward slide once more.

### The Steam-Owls (The Gliders)

In the dark, hollow maws of the defunct steam pipes—ancient bronze vents that have gone cold and no longer blow—live the only things that fly. 

They are called owls, though they have more of the bat or the leather-pouch about them. They are entirely featherless. Their skin is the color of wet coal, tough and greasy to the touch, and their wings are long, narrow sails of ribbed membrane that they stretch out to catch the chimney’s constant thermal rise. 

They do not flap. To flap in this narrow shaft would be to strike the walls or the cables. They glider-hunt. They sit in the dark of their pipe-mouths, their long, sensitive nose-whiskers twitching. These whiskers do not feel the wind; they feel *pressure*. They can feel the slight swell of air that occurs when a glider-bird several hundred meters away drops from its porch, or the tiny eddy created by a tap-lizard leaping across a seam.

When they hunt, they do not drop; they launch themselves into the updraft, letting the hot wind carry them up. They slip silently along the wall, their dark wings nearly scraping the moss, and with a single, rubbery foot, they pluck a lizard or a cluster of copper-winged moths from the iron. They do not chew; they have no teeth, only a throat lined with hard, grinding ridges that process the chitin and the bone, spitting out small, dense pellets of compressed rust-scales and lichen-fiber that fall down into the bottomless dark like gray hail.

### The Stranded (The Silt-Skins)

There are people here, too. Or, at least, there are the descendants of those who were once people. 

To the Silt-Skins, the Great Conduit is not a structure; it is the entire universe. They have no word for "world" other than *The Chimney*. They have no word for "earth" or "ground." 

They are small, rarely exceeding four feet in height, but their shoulders and chests are immensely broad, developed from a lifetime of pulling themselves along the catwalks and the vertical bronze ribs. Their legs are short, bowed, and remarkably flexible at the hip, allowing them to turn their feet almost completely backward to grip the round heads of the rivets. Their skin is a dull, glossy gray-green, the result of a diet rich in sulfurous moss and the coal-ash that coats every surface. They resemble the statues of saints in a flooded cathedral, worn smooth by centuries of dripping water.

They do not wear clothes, for cloth rots in thirty days in this humid, hot-swamp atmosphere. Instead, they paint their skin with a paste made from the parakeet-oil lichen. This paste dries into a rubbery, waterproof lacquer that protects their flesh from the constant, acidic drip of the steam-rust and keeps their body heat from escaping into the vertical drafts.

They live in the "shadows" of the great machines—in the spaces behind the five-story gears, or in the dry niches where the massive bronze ribs curve out from the iron walls. Here, they suspend their homes: three-dimensional webs of braided vine-silk, hung like larval cocoons under the iron galleries. 

Their social life is a complex, noiseless dance. Because they cannot speak—their voices having been drowned out by the thunder of the lower engines generations ago—they use a sign language of extraordinary speed and economy, executed with their long, grease-stained fingers. For longer distances, they use **The Iron-Tongue**. Each Silt-Skin carries a "tapper"—a short, heavy rod of cold-worked brass. By striking the bronze ribs of the conduit in specific rhythmic patterns, they can send messages that travel through the skeleton of the chimney.

A Silt-Skin mother, resting in her vine-cradle three segment levels up, can press her teeth against the brass bolt of her home and "hear" her husband tapping his progress as he climbs the wet maintenance ladder six hundred meters below. The vibration travels through the iron, through the bronze, through the anchor-bolts of her cradle, and directly into her jawbone.

They are not survivors of a shipwreck; they are keepers of an equilibrium. They do not wish to leave the conduit, for the thought of a world without a wall to hold onto—a world where there is only "air" in all directions—is to them a madness, a nightmare of falling upward into an empty sea. 

When a Silt-Skin dies, there is no grave. Their family carries the body to one of the giant, slow-turning gears that drive the pumps of the lower levels. They lay the body tenderly upon the teeth of the gear, tucked among the thick green lichens. As the gear turns, slowly, imperceptibly, it carries the body into the dark housing. There is no crunching, no violence; there is only the slow, heavy, five-hundred-ton press of the machine, folding the flesh back into the iron, greasing the wheels of the world with the oil of those who lived upon them. Highly reverent, the family stands on the cat-walk, their fingers pressed to the vibrating metal, feeling the final, low thud that means their kin has become part of the great, turning silence of the Conduit.
