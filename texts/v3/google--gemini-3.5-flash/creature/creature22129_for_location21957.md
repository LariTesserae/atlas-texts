### The Splicers of the Green Joint

The humans who inhabit this lattice do not know themselves as men or women of a nation, but as threads within a seam. To look at a Splicer is to look at someone who has been slowly, generation by generation, modified by the texture of the ironwood they tend.

They are long-limbed and soft-boned. Their joints do not click or snap; they bend with a cartilaginous silence, allowing them to cling to the undersides of three-cord plaits like sleeping bats. They do not have fingernails. Instead, the tips of their fingers and long, prehensile toes are capped with thick, yellowed pads of horn, scarred by the sliding grit of hempen rope and the scraping of flint adzes. They have no hair on their bodies—hair is a liability in a world of wet resin and sticky bark-tar—but their scalps and shoulders are covered in a fine, silver-grey down. This down behaves like the moss on the cables; it absorbs the drifting cloud-mist, keeping their skin perpetually cool and damp, shielded from the rare, scorching shafts of direct light that break through the high canopy.

Their language is not spoken in the open air, where the wet mist would swallow their voices. Instead, it is a language of touch and vibration. When two Splicers meet on a five-mile void-rope, they do not shout; they press their foreheads together, or touch their palms to the same taut strand of guide-line. Through the tension of the cord, they send low, rhythmic thrumming sounds produced by clicking their tongues against their teeth or tapping their chests with their hollow horn-fingers. A Splicer can "hear" a cousin three miles away through the wood, telling them of a weakening knot or a cluster of ripe cup-fruit.

They live in the "Burls"—vast, bulbous swellings in the vertical timbers that have been hollowed out with fire and bone chisels over a thousand years. Inside these homes, the air is warm, thick, and smells of yeasty dough and fermenting sap. The Splicers do not burn wood for light; wood is too precious to destroy, and the smoke would choke them in the enclosed cells. Instead, they cultivate "Lanthorn-Rot"—a pale, bioluminescent fungus that grows in wet logs inside their dwellings, casting a soft, breathing green glow that never goes out.

In the deepest, most ancient Burls, one might encounter the *Drowsers*. These are Splicers who have reached the end of their active lives. They do not die as those on the ground might; instead, they crawl into the dampest, most resin-choked corners of the inner bark and lie still. Over decades, their skin hardens into the likeness of wet green marble; their veins match the capillary systems of the host tree, and their blood slows to a syrupy crawl. They become living nodes of the Loom, their consciousness dissolved into the vast, slow thoughts of the ironwood. The younger Splicers visit them, pressing their palms to the old ones' mossy cheeks to ask where the wood is rotting or where the deep cisterns are dry.

### The Steam-Sows of the Hanging Cradles

Inside the massive, willow-woven spheres that hang in the center of the five-mile voids lives the Loom’s primary domestic beast: the *Steam-Sow*. 

A Steam-Sow is a creature of immense weight and absolute gentleness. It has no legs to speak of—only four stout, leathery flippers tipped with curved bone hooks, which it uses to adjust its position inside its suspended basket. Its body is pig-like but covered in a thick layer of grey blubber that smells of tanner's oil and boiled pine-needles. It has no teeth; its snout is a wide, soft tube lined with whalebone-like baleen, which it uses to filter the millions of tiny moths, lichen spores, and seed-down that drift through the high chambers.

The Sows are kept for two reasons: heat and seed.

The Sills are drafty and damp, but a single Steam-Sow’s internal engine runs at a constant, feverish temperature. Their leather hides are always hot to the touch, and they exhale a thick, sweet steam that rises through the vents of their baskets. The Splicers build their communal nurseries around these hanging cradles, using the warmth of the beast to hatch their own hairless young and dry their harvest of hemp fiber.

Furthermore, the Sow’s digestive system is the only place where the seeds of the *Heart-Vine* can germinate. The seeds must be swallowed, fermented in the warm acidity of the Sow's first stomach, and then coughed up in wet, fibrous cakes. The Splicers collect these cakes—treasuring them like gold—and graft them into the fresh cambium of the older cables, starting the long process of a new fiber-cord.

### The Rib-Tappers (The Bark-Clicks)

If the Splicers are the caretakers of the Loom’s exterior, the *Rib-Tappers* are the guardians of its health. 

These are six-legged lizards, each about the size of a human forearm, with skin that mimics the speckled slate of rotten lichen. They have no lungs; they breathe through tiny, crimson slits along their flanks that pulse with the rhythm of the wind. Their eyes are milky and blind, covered by thick scales, but they do not need to see.

A Rib-Tapper spend its entire life clinging to the undersides of the cables. They move with an erratic, mechanical jerkiness, tapping their long, hollow rib-cages against the wood. The sound—a sharp *click-click-click*—reverberates through the interior of the timber. By listening to the pitch of the return vibration through their sensitive toe-pads, the lizards can locate the hollow tunnels made by wood-boring grubs or the silent, wet rot of internal fungus.

The Splicers treat the Rib-Tappers with a reverence that borders on worship. To harm a lizard is to invite the collapse of the very branch one stands on. Splicer children are taught to feed the lizards with fat, grease-gland moths, and when a Splicer hears a lizard tapping in rapid, frantic intervals, they know it is time to bring their bone-drills and adzes to that spot, cutting out the infected wood before the whole cable dies and drops its weight into the void.

### The Pearl-Snouts and the Drifting Droplets

In the corners where the cables cross and the giant gravity-wells hover, lives the *Pearl-Snout*. 

These are fish, but they do not look like the fish of the world’s deep oceans. Their bodies are transparent, revealing a spine that looks like a thread of spun glass and a heart that pulses with a pale, cold gold. Their gills are not hidden; they fan out around their heads like ruffs of pink feathers, absorbing oxygen from the warm, stagnant water of the floating spheres.

The Pearl-Snout’s world is small but fluid. They are not confined to their water-globes; when the humidity is high and the clouds are thick, they can "walk" through the wet air, sliding from one floating reservoir to another. They look like streaks of silver light moving through the leaden fog, their fins rowing through the oily mist as easily as they do through water.

They feed on the *Sap-Weep*—the rich, sugary syrup that exudes from the joints where the cables are grafted. They are the only creatures that can cross the voids without ropes, and the Splicers watch them to navigate the unstable currents of gravity. If the Pearl-Snouts are swimming high, the Splicers know the local gravity is leaning toward the ceiling-thatch; if they drop into the lower steam, the path below is wet and safe to travel.

### The Great Sail-Moths (The Lace-Wings)

The largest living things in the voids, save for the timber itself, are the *Lace-Wings*. 

These moths are so vast that their wingspans can block out the dim twilight of an entire five-mile chamber. Their wings are not covered in scales but in a thick, velvety wool that smells of dry leaves and cinnamon. They have no mouths, no legs, and no stomachs. They are born from the rotting heart of the great vertical columns, where their massive, worm-like larvae spend a century chewing through the ancient, dead peat of the interior.

When they emerge into the air, they do so only to breed and die. They do not fly so much as they drift, their sails catching the slow, hot drafts that rise from the lower cisterns. They are silent, beautiful, and tragic. Their flight is a series of slow, rhythmic undulations that make the mist curl behind them like the wake of a ship.

When a Lace-Wing dies, it is a day of feast and harvest for the Splicers. The giant body does not fall quickly; it drifts through the misty cells for days, caught in the soft gravity of the nearest branches. The Splicers descend upon it with their bone knives, harvesting the thick, cinnamon-scented wool of its wings to make blankets and winter shirts, and collecting its tallow-rich fat to grease their hemp-ropes against the endless rot of the rain. What remains is left to the moss and the lizards, its skeleton of soft cartilage dissolving into the green turf within a single season.
