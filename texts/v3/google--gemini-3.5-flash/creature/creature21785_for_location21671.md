To understand what lives on the plateau of Silt-and-Iron, one must first discard the notion of the breath. Nothing here inspires or expires. The air, tasting of cold iron and gypsum, is not a medium for gas exchange but a weight that holds the congregation down, pinning each form to its specific coordinate on the white salt-pan.

The inhabitants do not have names for themselves, for they have no language, but a traveler of patient mind might categorize them by their posture and their relationship to gravity.

Lowest of all are **the Shingle-Tongues**. These are the flat, segmented tiles of grey flesh that form the literal carpet of the waste. To the eye, they resemble large, hexagonal plates of slate-grey leather, overlapping so perfectly that not a grain of the salt beneath them is visible. They are the substrate of the plateau’s life. If one were to slip a iron spade beneath a Shingle-Tongue and pry it upward—a task requiring immense leverage, for their suction to the salt is absolute—one would find no moist underbelly, no crawling legs, and no mouth. Instead, the underside is a pale, calcified surface lined with thousands of microscopic, tooth-like ridges fused to the salt. They do not eat the salt; they "know" it. They are a collective organ of touch, registering the dry, atomic vibrations of the deep iron beds miles below. They have no eyes, but they are aware of the light by the way it slightly warms their grey hides, a slow, greasy heat that never quite reaches the level of a summer day.

Above them, standing upon their flat backs, are **the Boat-Shells**. These are several-legged creatures whose carapaces are formed of a dry, translucent horn, yellowed like the toenail of an old beast. They are shaped like upturned dories, about three feet long, their ribs visible through the thin horn like the struts of a paper kite. They have six legs, but these limbs are jointless, dry pegs of bone that end in blunt, rounded stumps. The Boat-Shells do not walk. They are the ballast of the plateau. Within their hollow shells, there are no intestines or lungs; there is only a suspended mass of dry, black resin that smells faintly of peat and old oil. This resin is their heart, or their brain—a single, solid lump of organic pitch that reacts to the shift in the plateau’s gravity. When the weight of the air alters, the resin inside a thousand Boat-Shells shifts its tilt by a fraction of a hair, causing their dry legs to click against the Shingle-Tongues beneath them. This click is the pulse of the plateau.

Higher still are **the Reed-Walkers**. These six-foot-tall structures of dry reed are the most delicate of the inhabitants, yet they are the most numerous. Their legs are no thicker than straws, hollow and segmented like bamboo, but made of a grey, fibrous horn that does not snap. At the apex of these legs sits the amber globe—a perfect, solid sphere of fossilized resin, free of bubbles or debris. The globe behaves like a burning glass. It gathers the tilt of the eternal sun, focusing the yellow light downward into the hollow tube of the central leg, where a small, dry core of iron-ore rests. This iron core is the "furnace." It is always warm. If you rest your cheek against the stem of a Reed-Walker, you can feel that slow, non-combustive heat, like a brick that has sat all day in an oven. The Reed-Walkers do not think, but they *focus*. Each amber head is a lens turned toward the north-northwest, not because they are looking at a destination, but because the light at that angle is steady enough to maintain the heat in their iron cores. 

Among these three dominant orders dwell **the Gray-Stitchers**, the creatures responsible for the dry-stone walls and the clay cups. 

To call them "builders" implies an intent and a design that they do not possess. The Gray-Stitchers are small, heavy, and shaped like bundles of dry, salt-crusted roots. They are the only creatures on the plateau that move with any regularity, though "regularity" here must be measured in centuries. A Gray-Stitcher moves by the process of thermal expansion. When the sun—fixed as it is—warms their left side over fifty years, the dry fibers of that side expand, causing the creature to lean slowly to the right. When the long, slow shadow of a Reed-Walker falls across them, they contract, pulling themselves forward by a fraction of an inch.

In this manner, they encounter loose flakes of slate. Their root-like limbs, coated in a sticky, mineral grease that exudes from their pores, adhere to the stone. When they contract, the stone is dragged with them. Over a millennium, a single Gray-Stitcher, sliding back and forth in a path determined entirely by the passage of shadows and the tilt of the light, will draw twenty or thirty pieces of slate into a curve. They do not build walls to keep things out or in; they build them because the slate is there, and their bodies cannot help but gather it.

Their hands—if the clustered, hardened tips of their root-limbs can be called hands—sometimes press into the soft, talcum-like clay that lies in the dry gullies of the low shale humps. They do not "model" the clay vessels with artistic intent. Rather, their weight presses into the mud, and when they slowly pull away, they leave behind hollow depressions that dry in the crisp air, hardening into the small, cup-like shapes. The seeds and yellowed vine-scraps within these cups are the remains of the old world—the world before the plateau rose to the roof of the sky. These fragments do not rot, because there is no moisture to feed a fungus, and no oxygen to rust their cells. They sit in the clay cups like letters in a mailbox that has been welded shut.

Finally, there are **the Intersectors**. These are not a separate species, but rather those individuals of all types that have lived so long in one place that the laws of matter have grown tired around them. 

You will see two Reed-Walkers whose amber heads are lodged in the same space, their colors blending into a deep, greasy orange. They do not struggle for room; they do not even perceive each other. The physical universe on the Silt-and-Iron plateau has begun to simplify its calculations. It no longer insists that two things cannot occupy the same point. 

Beneath them, a **Pewter-Beave**—a massive, slab-sided entity like a three-ton seed-pod—lies half-merged with a block of basalt. The basalt has not grown over the beast, nor has the beast burrowed. They have simply exchanged coordinates. If you were to crack the stone with a hammer, you would find that the grain of the basalt gradually becomes fibrous, then resinous, then pewter-colored, until it is indistinguishable from the creature’s hide.

This is the life of the plateau: a slow, cold, molecular consensus. There is no hunger here, because there is nothing to lose. There is no fear, because there is nothing to break. There is only the long, dry weight of the afternoon, the smell of gypsum, and the silent, crowded staring toward the north-northwest, where the sky is always the color of a bruised plum, and where nothing ever happens for the first time.
