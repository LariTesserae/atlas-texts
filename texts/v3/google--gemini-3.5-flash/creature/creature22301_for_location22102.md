In the gaps of this silent, shifting industry—nested among the vibrating conduits and the sudden, crumbling towers—lives a fauna made of the exact same materials as the floor they walk upon. Here, the boundary between vegetable structure, mechanical device, and animal life has been rubbed smooth by the cold. 

There is no bone in this world. There is no red blood, no soft marrow, and no wet, salt-filled eyes. The things that walk, drift, or cling are constructed of laminate heartwood, cured pitch, dry reeds, and pressurized sap. They are clean, bloodless automatons that have run for eons on cold chemistry and static charge.

### The Suture-Spiders (*Platestitchers*)

Where the high-pressure rubber conduits snake across the gray cambium canyons, one will find the **Platestitchers**. 

They are the shape of carriage wheels, though flat and hexagonal, composed of thick, layered plates of orange cork. They have no distinct heads; instead, a ring of six dull, green light-receptor patches circles their upper rim, sensing only the stark difference between the white sun and the blue-black shadows of the towers. Beneath their cork bodies are twelve legs made of dense, lathe-turned ironwood, tipped with sharp, resin-coated pegs.

The Platestitchers are the self-appointed maintenance crew of the great botanical grid, though they do so without intelligence. They are drawn to pressure drops. When a conduit is torn by a falling balsa tower or ruptured by its own internal turgor, it begins to leak its thick, sweet starch-sap. This fluid is under such immense pressure that it does not pool; it sprays out in silent, viscous fans that freeze almost instantly into brittle, amber glass.

The Platestitchers gather at these leaks, guided by the drop in the conduit’s low-frequency vibration. Moving with a stiff, rhythmic *click-tap* that is entirely swallowed by the floor-moss, they align themselves over the rupture. From their underbellies, they secrete a thick, gray latex—a natural vulcanized rubber that they knead into the tear using their front pegs. To hold the patch against the immense hydraulic pressure, they spin cords of tough, fibrous bark-thread from spinnerets in their rear plates, wrapping the conduit over and over like a splint.

They do not eat the sap; they are powered by the temperature differential between the warm, fermenting fluid inside the conduit and the freezing air outside. Once the leak is sealed and the conduit grows warm and tense again, the Platestitchers remain clamped to the patch for days, their flat wooden bodies absorbing the heat, before slowly creeping away in search of the next vibration of failure.

### The Siphon-Pipes

Deep within the narrow crevices of the petrified canals, where the sap pools freeze into thick, dark jellies, live the **Siphon-Pipes**. 

These are long, segmented creatures, resembling giant wind instruments left to rot in the cold. A single Siphon-Pipe can grow to fifteen feet in length, yet its jointed, yellow-cane body is no wider than a child’s wrist. They are entirely hollow. At their forward end is a heavy, dark snout tipped with a drill-like whorl of hardened, resin-impregnated wood, tough enough to pierce the iron-hard peat of the bedrock.

The Siphon-Pipe behaves like a living siphon. It drills into the active, rubbery conduits that carry the pressurized starches from the deep underground reservoirs. Once it punctures a line, the high-pressure fluid rushes through its hollow interior. 

To prevent itself from bursting like an over-inflated bladder, the Siphon-Pipe is ringed with dozens of small, paper-thin reed-valves along its flanks. As the sap surges through it, these valves flutter rapidly to release the excess pressure. In a world with a thicker atmosphere, this would produce a deafening, discordant whistling—like a calliope gone mad. Here, in the thin, dead air, the venting is silent. The only sign of their feeding is a faint, rhythmic pulsing along their yellow segments and the occasional puff of pale, vinegar-scented vapor that cardings off their sides and immediately freezes into white dust on the moss.

### The Spring-Bundles

In a world without sound, hunting is an art of pure tension. The apex predator of the balsa-forests is the **Spring-Bundle**, an creature that looks so much like a heap of discarded, frost-rimed twigs that even a careful eye cannot distinguish it from the forest floor.

The Spring-Bundle’s body is a masterpiece of mechanical energy storage. It is built around a single, massive piece of recurved heartwood—dense, black, and incredibly flexible, like a longbow. This bow is held under immense tension by a cord of dried, braided root-sinew that runs along the creature’s "belly." The rest of the creature consists of dozens of light, hollow balsa-wood splinters that fan out from this central spine like the ribs of a broken umbrella.

The Spring-Bundle cannot run. It moves by a slow, agonizing process of winding itself up. Over several hours, using small, internal hydraulic pistons filled with slow-moving resin, it bends its own spine back until the wood is screaming with stored energy, locked in place by a simple, tooth-and-groove joint near its tail.

It then lays flat across a bio-electric conduit, completely still. 

It does not hunt by sight or smell, but by static reception. Its outer splinters are coated in a fine, dry fuzz of conductive cellulose. When a *strutter*—sluggish and heavy with static electricity harvested from the conduit—steps onto or near the Spring-Bundle, the tiny electrical field of the prey triggers a chemical reaction in the lock-joint. 

The joint slips. 

With a silent, instantaneous snap of unimaginable force, the bow-spine straightens. The fan of balsa splinters collapses inward like a jaws-of-life, driven by hundreds of pounds of spring-tension. The prey is not bitten or crushed; it is instantly impaled and pinned to the conduit by dozens of needle-sharp wooden spikes. 

Because the air cannot carry the sound of the snap, nearby strutters do not even look up. They continue to graze on the static sparks, completely unaware that three feet away, one of their kind has been pinned to the bark, its dry resins being slowly absorbed by the Spring-Bundle’s parasitic root-feet.

### The Vellum-Drifters

High above the frantic, grinding cycle of the forest floor, where the air is so thin that the sky shows a deep, twilight indigo even at noon, live the **Vellum-Drifters**. 

These are the largest organisms in the valley, though they weigh almost nothing. A mature Vellum-Drifter is a flat, kite-like sail of translucent, paper-thin xylem membrane, stretched over a delicate, hollow frame of elder-pith. They can be forty feet across, yet they are so light that a sudden updraft from a warm, fermenting root-furnace can send them soaring thousands of feet into the freezing blue.

The Drifters have no means of active propulsion. They are entirely at the mercy of the high, dry glacial winds. They survive on the invisible mist of waxy esters and ether vapors that rise from the valley’s chemical chimneys. Their wide sheets of skin are damp with a thin layer of sticky, dark grease that catches these airborne chemicals as they drift through the plumes.

They are beautiful, ghostly things. When the flat white sun shines through them, they glow with the color of aged parchment and dried honey. From their undersides trail long, hair-like rootlets of black thread, sometimes sixty feet long, that sweep through the tops of the growing balsa towers like the anchors of a fleet of silent airships. 

Sometimes, one of these trailing threads will snag in a rapidly growing spire just as it loses pressure and collapses. The sudden downward jerk pulls the Vellum-Drifter out of the sky, dragging it down into the dark, silent moss of the canyon floor. It does not fight. It cannot. Its fragile paper wings buckle and rip without a sound, and within minutes, the hungry capillary mosses have dissolved its frame, returning its cellulose back into the great, looping factory of the soil.
