To survive on the face of the vertical miles, the small inhabitants of the splits have abandoned all use for soft, heavy flesh. 

They are the *Ancre*: creatures who look less like animals and more like dry, articulated husks of gray-green pith. They are light—scarcely heavier than a dry bundle of reeds—and their bodies are flattened, designed to hug the bark so closely that the screaming draft of the gulf passes over them without ripping them from their holds. Their skin is not skin but a tough, fibrous rind that does not sweat, but rather exudes a bitter, waxy grease that keeps the biting frost from sticking to their limbs. 

They have no feet. Instead, their lower limbs terminate in three-pronged, scissor-like pincers of hardened, thorn-like cartilage. With these, they grip the long, longitudinal fibers of the heartwood, sliding them along the grain like tiny, vertical cable-cars. Their hands are similar, though more delicate, tipped with three long, jointed digits that can strip the inner bark or weave the stiff hair-grass with mechanical precision.

Because of the blinding, shadowless glare of the phosphorescent cambium, their eyes are not white and dark, but solid, polished spheres of dark amber. These biological lenses are naturally polarized, filtering the winter-noon glare down to a manageable, deep-forest violet. They do not have eyelids; instead, they possess double-thick, translucent nictitating membranes that sweep horizontally across the eye, clearing away ice-needles and drifting wood-dust without ever closing off their sight.

They make no sound with their mouths, which are simple, vertical slits lined with hard plates for crushing frozen moss. In this cavernous void, where the thin air swallows the voice, they communicate through the wood itself. They are players of the Great Trunk. Each Ancre carries a small, fire-hardened wooden mallet slung at their thigh. By striking the resonant fibers of the bark, they send sharp, rhythmic *clacks* and *taps* vibrating through the wood. To an Ancre, the world is a vast, humming telegraph. They can "hear" a neighbor three miles away through the soles of their claw-joints, recognizing the specific, resonant "fingerprint" of a cousin’s split, or the warning vibration of a trunk fifteen miles distant beginning its terminal buckle.

They live in a state of quiet, perpetual migration. Their basket-like nests, wedged into the amber-filled hollows, are temporary things. The aggressive, unstable growth of the world makes permanent settlement impossible; a split that is five yards wide today may be squeezed down to a tight, rib-cracking vice by next month as the cellulose expands. When the *tink-tink-tink* of the wood rises to a frantic, high-pitched pitch, the Ancre know the wall is tightening. They pack their few belongings—their beetle-shell scrapers, their mats of dried hair-grass—and climb.

They are incredibly skilled climbers, but when they must cross the open, five-mile gulfs between the giant trunks, they rely on the wind. 

An Ancre traveler will stand on a ledge of raw green wood, waiting for a thermal draft to rise from the unimaginable floor of the rift. Behind them, they trail a long, incredibly strong cord woven from the silken abdominal glands of the *ring-lice*—the only other common residents of the splits. When the wind-shear reaches a certain whistling pitch, the traveler unfurls a "kite-wing" made of thin, cured inner-bark, stretched over a frame of springy wood-splinters. They step off.

For a terrifying, silent minute, they do not fly so much as drift like a falling leaf across the white-blue nothingness, suspended over five miles of empty air. If the wind is true, they slam into the distant, frozen moss of the opposite trunk, immediately driving their hand-thorns deep into the fiber to anchor themselves. If the wind fails, they are saved only by their drag-lines, which their kin slowly haul back in, reeling the traveler out of the void like a drowned spider.

Below them, in the wider vacuum of the rift, other things drift that have forgotten the wood entirely.

The three-meter *drift-gliders* are not the only denizens of the sky. In the deeper, colder layers where the circular winds are strongest, there are the *Scythe-Moths*. These are giant, blind insects with bodies like papery kites, colored the exact blue-white of the empty air. They have no wings that flap; instead, their limbs are long, rigid spars of hollow chitin, between which are stretched thin, transparent membranes that catch the thermal currents. They spend their entire lives in the air, never touching the trunks. They sleep on the wing, drifting in slow circles, filtering the spore-clouds with comb-like antennae.

Only when they die do they interact with the wood. Their light, hollow carcasses are caught in the drafts and plastered against the windward sides of the giant pillars. There, the Ancre find them. The Scythe-Moth’s wing-membranes are prized as waterproof roofing for the basket-nests, and their sharp, serrated mandibles are used as saws to cut the stubborn hair-grass.

Deep within the dark, wet cracks where the sap does not freeze, lives the *ring-louse*. These are slow, heavy creatures, the size of sheep, but flattened like ticks. They are the cattle of this world. They have no eyes or legs; they are merely giant, segmented wedges that push themselves through the soft, growing cambium of the tree, digesting the starchy pulp and leaving behind tunnels of clean, white wood. The Ancre follow these tunnels, harvesting the sweet, sticky excrement of the lice, which they dry into hard, energy-rich cakes.

The most solemn aspect of Ancre life is their relationship with death. Because there is no earth to dig, and no fire to burn, a dead Ancre cannot be buried. Instead, when an elder’s vibrations finally stop, their kin carry the body to a fresh, yellow-green scar where the wood has recently split. 

They place the deceased deep within the crack, arranging their limbs so they clasp the raw heartwood. Within days, the tree’s aggressive, healing growth begins to take hold of them. The pale, cold moss grows over their limbs; the fast-moving cambium flows around their torso. Within a year, the body is completely absorbed into the trunk, sealed forever in a transparent pocket of amber resin or encased within the rings of the giant pillar. 

To look into the translucent walls of the great pathways is to see them: centuries of ancestors, preserved as pale, quiet statues within the glowing wood, their amber eyes staring out into the silent glare, forever part of the structure that supports their children.
