To understand what lives in this high, wet amphitheater, one must understand that here, the line between the stone and the flesh has worn thin. The creatures of the ledger are not guests upon the mountain; they are extensions of its verticality, its weeping water, and its rushing air.

Within the dry, circular ruins of the stone shelter, the most substantial of these tenants makes its home. The builders of the wall called them **the Liths**, though today there is no one left to speak the name. They are mammals, about the size of a mountain hare, but their bodies have been flattened by evolutionary necessity until they resemble thick, organic tiles. Their backs are covered in overlapping, overlapping plates of hardened, gray-blue keratin that perfectly match the texture and color of the local shale. When a Lith tucks its nose and limbs beneath its shell, it becomes indistinguishable from a fallen stone. 

The Liths have no claws; instead, the undersides of their broad, five-toed feet are lined with thousands of microscopic, hook-like hairs and fleshy, self-wetting suction pads. They do not walk so much as they adhere. They move across the vertical wet limestone with a slow, rippling glide, browsing upon the black lichens and the tender, lime-green tips of the liverworts. 

Inside the shelter, where the floor is dusty and dry, two or three Liths are almost always found curled together. They are social, communicating in low, dry clicks that mimic the sound of pebbles rattling down a chute. They have a strange relationship with the ancient granite mortar left behind by the humans. The granite, hauled from some forgotten valley far below, contains mineral salts absent in the pure, washed limestone of the cliff. Over generations, the Liths have licked the inside of the mortar to obtain these essential salts. Their small, rasping tongues have worn the dark granite basin smooth and shiny as polished obsidian, leaving a shallow, spiraling groove in the bottom that grows a fraction of a millimeter deeper each century.

Hovering over the Liths in the hierarchy of the ledge are the **Scythe-Wings**—the white-bellied swifts. They do not belong to the stone, but to the empty mile of air beneath it, yet they are tethered to the amphitheater by the necessity of sleep and birth. They nest in the deep, narrow cracks where the limestone has split away from the main wall. Their nests are marvels of vertical engineering: hemispherical pockets constructed from dried moss, wind-blown grass, and the shed, copper-colored under-fur of the Liths, all bound together by the birds’ own thick, fast-drying saliva. 

The Scythe-Wings are creatures of pure velocity. Their legs are tiny, clawed stumps, useless for walking but perfectly designed for clamping onto the vertical rock face. To see them sleep is to see a dozen dark, arrow-shaped shadows hanging side-by-side in a wet crevice, their heads tucked beneath their wings, vibrating in unison with the deep drone of the wind-pipes. They are the clean-up crew of the air, clearing the amphitheater of the dancing mists of midges that rise from the wet moss during the sunny intervals.

But the most mysterious inhabitants of the ledge are those that occupy the three smooth-bored "wind-pipes" that pierce the rocky lip. These are the **Chime-Snails**. 

They are not like the sluggish snails of the lowlands. The Chime-Snails are large—the size of a man’s fist—with pale, translucent shells that are thin as eggshells and possess the pearlescent sheen of wet ice. They live inside the howling dark of the flutes, where the wind speeds can easily strip the flesh from a lesser creature. To survive, they have developed a highly specialized, rubbery foot that secretes a tenacious, non-drying mucus that acts as a powerful adhesive and shock absorber. 

The Chime-Snails are the true musicians of the cliff. They do not stay still; they slide slowly up and down the interior walls of the stone pipes, responding to the subtle shifts in barometric pressure that precede a change in the weather. By repositioning their bulky, liquid-filled bodies within the narrow stone throats, they unconsciously act as organic valves. They alter the volume of air passing through the holes, tuning the mountain. When a heavy storm approaches, the snails migrate to the deepest, sheltered curves of the pipes, allowing the wind to pass unobstructed and causing the flutes to scream in a high, terrified register. In the calm, sunny afternoons, they spread themselves open across the openings, reducing the wind’s voice to a sleepy, rhythmic hum.

Below the snails, in the constantly weeping gardens of moss and liverwort, live the **Glass-Frogs**. They are the keepers of the wet. Their translucent green skin is so thin that one can watch the dark, thread-like veins of their circulatory system pulsing with life, and their tiny, golden eyes are the only part of them that does not blend into the wet vegetation. 

The Glass-Frogs do not experience the world through sight or scent; they live in a universe of vibration. Because they live directly on the sounding-board of the amphitheater—where the wind-pipes drone and the water drips—their entire bodies have adapted to register frequencies. Their large, flat toe-pads contain highly sensitive mechanoreceptors that can tell the difference between the impact of a raindrop and the footstep of a Lith. 

When the great, blind mists roll up from the valley and swallow the ledge, the Glass-Frogs begin their chorus. It is not a call for mates, but a collective sonar. Each frog emits a dry, wooden *tclck-tclck* at a slightly different pitch. The sound bounces off the wet, vertical walls and the stone floor, and by listening to the microscopic delays in the echoes, the frogs construct a perfect, three-dimensional map of their shifting, watery world. Even in a fog so thick that a man could not see his own hand, the frogs know exactly where every drop of water is pooling and where the dry shelter begins.

Finally, there are the silent partners of this high world: **the Thread-Weavers**. These are tiny, blind spiders that live in the deep, dry crevices of the stone circle. They do not spin webs to catch flies; instead, they spin long, single lines of incredibly strong, non-sticky silk that they cast out into the furious updrafts of the abyss. These lines, sometimes thirty feet long, trail out into the empty air like the tail of a kite. As the wind rushes past, the threads collect the microscopic organic dust, pollen, and spores that are carried up from the forests miles below. Every few hours, the spiders reel in their threads, swallowing the accumulated debris before casting the lines back out into the nothingness.

In this place, nothing is wasted. The shed feathers of the swift become the nest of the frog; the droppings of the Lith fertilize the moss that filters the water; the mucus of the Chime-Snail provides a foothold for the lichens. They live in a state of precarious, violent balance, suspended over a mile of empty air, bound together by the cold wet stone and the eternal, roaring wind.
