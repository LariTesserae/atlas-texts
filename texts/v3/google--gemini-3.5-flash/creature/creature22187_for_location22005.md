To understand what lives in this basin, one must first dismantle the common division between the organic and the mineral. Here, life does not merely inhabit the stone; it is made of the stone, feeds upon the stone, and, in dying, returns to stone within a matter of weeks. 

The primary inhabitant—the true master of this sunken space—is the liver-colored, rubbery skin that blankets the flowstone. This is not a passive carpet of scum, but a complex, highly organized consortium of cyanobacteria, iron-oxidizing microbes, and ancient archaea. Locally, across the basin's floor, this mat is known as *the skin*. It is several inches thick in the deeper crevices, possessing the cool, dense weight of raw liver or wet chamois. It does not grow toward a sun it has never known; instead, it grows downward and inward, digesting the calcium and sulfur seeping from the limestone bluff. 

If you watch the skin for many hours, you will see that it is not still. Under the steady, rhythmic *clop... clop... plip* of the falling water, the mat performs a slow, peristaltic breath. Where a drop falls, the rubbery surface does not merely splash; it dimples. The impact triggers a localized contraction, a tiny pucker that opens a microscopic pore to swallow the mineral wealth of the droplet before closing again. Over centuries, these mats have learned the topography of the drip. Directly beneath each major drip-point on the ceiling, the bacterial skin has grown into a raised, soft cup, a biological funnel designed to catch and hold the falling water, preventing it from draining away too quickly.

Moving within and upon this living skin are the *thread-gliders*—the pale, glass-shelled snails. No larger than rice grains, their shells are so thin they are virtually transparent, revealing the slate-blue organs and the faint, amber pulse of a heart beneath. These snails have no eyes, nor do they have the dark pigmentation that protects surface creatures from the sun. They possess only two swollen, sensitive tentacles that sweep the mat before them like white hairs. 

The thread-gliders do not seek food in the active sense; they simply drift across the rubbery ochre skin, grazing on the microscopic film of water that slides over it. Their lives are measured in decades of unbroken, slow-motion travel. A snail may take three days to cross a single hand-carved sandstone groove left by the ancient builders. When they die, their shells do not rot. Because the water is so heavily saturated with calcium, the dead shell is immediately coated in a micro-thin layer of limestone. Within a year, the shell is fused to the floor; within ten, it is a tiny, bulbous node on a travertine mound. The very floor underfoot is a cemetery of a billion glass shells, now turned to fat-like stone.

Deep in the narrow, water-filled crevices between the flowstone mounds, where the water is stillest and warmest, lives a creature that represents the apex of this quiet world: the *tallow-siren*. 

These are tiny, neotenic salamanders, rarely growing longer than a human pinky finger. They are entirely blind, their eyes reduced to dark, useless pinpricks beneath a layer of pale, translucent skin. Their bodies are the color of lard or melted beeswax, so soft and fat-rich that they seem to have no bones at all—only a flexible rod of cartilage. They have no lungs; instead, they breathe through three pairs of external gills that fan out from their necks like tufts of fine, crimson silk. These gills are the only bright color in the entire basin, drifting in the warm pools like tiny, biological flames.

The tallow-siren is an ambush predator of the absolute lowest energy. It does not swim; it lacks the muscle for it. Instead, it anchors itself to the wet clay at the bottom of a pool using its four tiny, weakly clawed legs and waits. It can remain completely motionless for months at a time, its heartbeat slowing to a single, lazy thrum every three minutes. 

It does not hunt by sight or scent, but by the lateral line along its flanks, which detects the minuscule pressure waves of the water. It has memorized the rhythm of the basin. It knows the exact vibration of a falling water drop, the slow slide of a snail, and the hair-thin ripple of the ribbon-worms that inhabit the wet rock-face. When a snail drifts within reach, the tallow-siren does not strike with lightning speed. It merely opens its wide, pale mouth with a sudden, soft vacuum action—a silent *gulp*—and swallows the snail whole. Its stomach acids are incredibly potent, capable of dissolving even the mineralized shell of the thread-glider over a period of weeks.

But the most striking relationship in the basin is between these living things and the ancient human ruins. 

The shallow sandstone basin hollowed out by primitive hand-tools has become the exclusive nursery of the tallow-sirens. Because the ancient builders carved this basin to catch the purest, warmest spring-water, the water here is free of the heavy clay silt that chokes the lower crevices. Every few years, when the water temperature rises by a fraction of a degree, the sirens crawl from their dark crevices and gather in this hand-carved stone bowl. Here they lay their eggs: clusters of clear, gelatinous spheres that look precisely like the calcified ooliths that strew the floor. 

The narrow, chiseled finger-channels that the humans cut to divert the springs have become the expressways of the basin’s micro-fauna. Because the channels are smooth and clean, free of the bulbous obstacles of the natural travertine, the flow of mineral-rich water is swifter here. The liver-colored bacterial mats have grown thickest along these artificial gutters, forming long, parallel ribbons of deep ochre that trace the exact path the ancient stone-cutters laid down. 

In this way, the work of the long-dead humans has been kept alive, not as history, but as plumbing. The channels still run, the basin still fills, and the warm water still spills, directed not by the iron tools of a modern world, but by the quiet, wet, living things that have made this tomb their cathedral.
