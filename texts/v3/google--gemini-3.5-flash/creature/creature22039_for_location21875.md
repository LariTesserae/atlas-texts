To live in the Hollow-of-the-Loom is to be a part of its plumbing.

The things that live here—the *Kneaders*—are not guests of this wet pocket; they are its maintenance. They are short, low-slung creatures, barely four feet from snout to rump, though they rarely stretch to their full length. Their bodies have the heavy, bulbous shape of wet clay sacks, and they seem to have been put together with the same unpolished haste as the walls around them. 

Their skin is the color of scalded lard or boiled tripe, turnip-pale and permanently slick with a greasy film that smells faintly of mutton-tallow and old yeast. It is a thick, porous hide, completely hairless except for a sparse, stiff down along their spine that looks like the coarse, hairy fibers of the binding-vines. They have no skeletons in the traditional sense; instead, their limbs are stiffened by rods of cartilage as flexible as green wicker, allowing them to squeeze and fold their bodies into the wet crevices of the floor without breaking.

They have no proper feet. Their four limbs end in identical, blunt extremities: two massive, opposing thumbs, thick as parsnips, with no other fingers between them. These double-thumbs are raw and calloused, stained a deep, indelible iron-red from their constant work in the clay. 

Because of the unstable gravity and the narrowness of the chamber, the Kneaders do not walk. They are slightly buoyant, their thick bodies pocketed with internal air-sacs that expand and contract with the changing steam pressure of the room. They travel by a wet, swinging crawl, pinching the split-log floors and the woven-vine walls with their powerful thumbs, dragging their heavy bellies through the air like seals navigating a flooded cave. When they cross the center of the room, they do not jump; they simply let go of the walls and drift through the hovering globule of brown water, their pale limbs paddling sluggishly through the warm liquid before they grip the copper ceiling on the other side.

Their heads are simple, wedge-shaped extensions of their thick necks, devoid of any external ears or noses. In the center of their face is a single, vertical slit—a pale, wet mouth-gasp that is constantly drawing in the pressurized steam. They do not breathe to oxygenate blood so much as to filter the mineral-pulp from the vapor. Inside this slit, instead of teeth, they have rows of black, horn-like ridges used to scrape the gray velvet fuzz off the undersides of the tongue-ferns and to crack the slate-colored shells of the valve-lice, which they eat whole and live.

Above the mouth-slit sit two small, unblinking eyes, milky and yellowed like soft-boiled bird eggs. They are covered by a thick, transparent membrane that keeps out the stinging, mineral-rich condensation. Because the light in the chamber is so dim and bent by the floating water-globule, the Kneaders' sight is poor, limited to recognizing shapes within a few feet. 

Instead, they navigate through a profound sensitivity to vibration. Their flanks are lined with deep, horizontal skin-folds that act as acoustic sensors. By pressing their bellies flat against the split-timber floor or the birch water-pipes, they "hear" the structural health of the Loom. They can feel the microscopic shift of a split log three layers down, or the thinning of a clay seal on the far side of the dome, long before the steam begins to whistle.

This is where their frantic, clumsy labor begins. 

A Kneader’s life is an endless, desperate cycle of patching and squeezing. When they feel a vibration change, they scramble toward it, their thumbs scooping up double-handfuls of the raw, red clay from the floor-crevices. With wet, slapping-sounds, they slam the mud into the leaks, dragging their heavy thumbs through it to carve the deep, double-handed grooves that seal the steam inside. They are always wet, always panting, their lungs whistling in unison with the squealing valve-lice on the pipes.

But it is their relationship with the spatial paradox of the bladder-ports that reveals the true purpose of their "clumsy" anatomy. 

To build or repair the outside of their five-meter wicker shell in a freezing, vertical void would be impossible. Thus, they weave from the inside out, utilizing the fold in space. 

A Kneader will take a long, bleeding vine of green timber, shove it out through the eastern bladder-port into the swirling steam, and then—with a wet, heavy lurch—scramble across the room to the western port. Three seconds later, the Kneader’s own hands, reaching out from the "other" sideways room, will grasp the vine as it emerges from the fog. They pull it through, twist it, and feed it back into the ceiling-seam, cooperative-partners with their own past and future selves. 

Watch them long enough through the steam, and the rhythm becomes clear: they are a single, continuous thread of labor, passing the materials of their home through the holes in reality, stitching themselves into existence.

They do not seem to die of old age, nor do they reproduce sexually. Instead, they are born of the Loom’s own excess. When the floor-clay becomes too saturated with the bitter white sap of the binding-vines and the mineral-crust of the steam, a mound of the red mud will begin to thrum. It absorbs the grease of the tongue-ferns dripping from the ceiling; it listens to the relentless roar of the birch conduits. Over several weeks, the mound swells, pulses, and hardens into a pale, fatty caul. 

Eventually, a pair of thick, red-stained thumbs will punch through the wet clay envelope from the inside, and a new Kneader will drag itself out onto the green logs, immediately reaching down to scoop up the scraps of its own birth-sac to patch the nearest leaking seam.
