Those who dwell in the vertical spaces do not have a word for the earth. Under their tongues, the concept of a flat, immovable limit of the world is a non-thing, a myth of the blind. For them, existence is an endless *along* and an unthinkable *down*. 

### The People of the Shells

The human inhabitants call themselves nothing that translates to "mankind" or "the tribe"; they refer to themselves simply as *The Hand-Strong*, or more intimately, those who *keep the grip*. 

Physically, they have been remade by the pillars. They are long-limbed and slight, their bones light and slightly flexible, like the bamboo-ferns they use for shelter. Their hands are their most remarkable feature: the palms are wide, thick-skinned, and covered in a dense pattern of deep, whorled ridges that never sweat but are naturally tacky with a sebum that smells of cedar resin. Their toes have grown long and distinct, the big toe opposable enough to clamp around a vine as thick as a man’s wrist. They do not walk so much as they crouch and flow, moving across the vertical bark of the ironwood with a lateral, four-limbed stride that looks, from a distance, like the shadow of a hunting lizard.

Their skin is a uniform, dull olive-gray, the color of wet moss under a golden sun. It is thick, resistant to the constant abrasion of rough bark and the stinging hairs of the pillar-vines. They wear no fabric in the lowland sense. Instead, they drape themselves in "cloud-sheets"—sheets of beaten inner-bark from the giant ferns, soaked in the oil of the six-winged gliders to make them waterproof. 

Because space is a vertical ribbon, their architecture is an art of suspension. Their huts, clustered under the massive, woody eaves of the shelf-fungi, do not have floors in the traditional sense. The floor is a net. It is woven from the high-tensile silk of the ballooning spiders, stretched tight across a frame of curved ironwood ribs. When a family gathers to sleep, they do not lie down; they slot themselves into individual silk pockets that hang from the ceiling-beams, swaying slightly with the pulse of the wind through the columns. To sleep loose is to risk the night-slide.

Their domestic life is silent. In a world where the background drone of the wind-flutes is as constant as the beating of one’s own heart, human voices are kept low, melodic, and soft. They communicate across the gulfs between dwellings using "pitch-pipes"—hollow bone reed-flutes that mimic the wind-tones of the pillars, sending coded whistles across miles of blue air where a human scream would be shredded by the drafts.

### The Great Loomers (The Wind-Spiders)

Sharing the high shelves with the humans are the *Weavers of the Gap*. These are not the small, skittering arachnids of the forest floor, but massive, slow-moving creatures the size of goats, with soft, bulbous bodies covered in a pelt of silver fur. 

They are effectively blind, navigating by the tension of the massive cables they spin across the empty chasms. A single wind-spider will spend its forty-year life tending to three or four primary lines that span the three-mile gaps between pillars. They do not hunt with webs; instead, they launch long, sticky drift-threads into the updrafts, letting the wind carry the line until it snags on a neighboring tower. Once anchored, the spider works back and forth, reinforcing the line with a silk that is stronger than steel and cures in the damp air to a dull, translucent green.

The humans live in a state of symbiotic tolerance with these creatures. The spiders are docility itself, consumed entirely by the great work of their spans. The human weavers use long, notched bone hooks to "steer" the spiders' drift-lines when they are being cast, helping to direct them to specific shelves to establish new bridges. In return, the humans clean the spiders of parasite-ticks—flat, hard-shelled mites that the people boil for their sweet, fatty grease.

### The Six-Winged Gliders (The Gale-Skins)

Where the air is thinnest and the light is brightest, the sky belong to the *Gale-Skins*. These are the apex predators of the open blue, though they are more like leather-winged fish than birds. 

They have no feathers. Their bodies are long, hydrodynamic tubes of pale, cartilage-ribbed hide, powered by three pairs of wings that do not flap but twist and tilt to harvest the thermal currents rising from the deep. Their eyes are massive, gold-rimmed globes positioned on the sides of their heads, allowing them to look simultaneously up into the sun and down into the gray void. They hunt the small leaf-mimic cicadas and the drift-lice that are blown off the pillars by the afternoon squalls.

To watch a Gale-Skin hunt is to watch an exercise in falling. They do not pursue; they drop. Spotting a swarm of cicadas a mile below, a glider will tuck its six wings flat against its flanks and plunge, reaching terminal velocity in the silent blue before snapping its wings open with a sound like a whip-crack, leveling out to scoop the insects into its wide, skin-fringed maw. 

Their nests are found in the transition zone—the damp, rotting undersides of the shelf-fungi where the wood has gone soft and chambered. Here, they lay leathery, balloon-like eggs that inflate with the warm, rising gasses of the forest’s respiration, floating within the nesting chambers like clusters of pearlescent grapes.

### The Sighers of the Under-Mist

Deep below the human habitations, where the golden light begins to dissolve into a warm, gray twilight that never changes, lives a class of beast that the humans know only by sound and the occasional, drifting corpse.

These are the *Sighers*. 

They are mountain-sized bladders of thin, vascular membrane, filled with light, warm hydrogen generated by the fermentation of the colossal drifts of leaf-litter that rot in the unseen bottoms of the world. They have no skeleton, no face, and no limbs. They are floating stomachs, drifting up and down the vertical faces of the ironwood pillars like elevator-cars of raw flesh. 

Their outer skin is covered in a carpet of symbiotic ferns and pale, blind fungi that feed on their waste. As they drift, they graze on the thick moss of the lower pillars, scraping it away with a wide, ring-shaped mouth located on their flat underbellies. 

The humans hear them long before they see them. To regulate their altitude, the Sighers must constantly vent gas through wet, muscular sphincters along their sides. The sound is a low, mournful *hiss-shiver* that rumbles through the core of the pillars, a sound the people associate with the coming of the thick, wet evening mists. Occasionally, a Sigher will die of old age or are punctured by a falling branch from the high canopy; their deflation is a slow, week-long descent, their vast, pale skins folding in upon themselves like dying stars as they sink back into the gray soup below.

### The Lichen-Stalkers

Closer to the bark, inside the thick green carpet that coats every inch of the ironwood, is the micro-world. 

The most prominent of these inhabitants is the *Lichen-Stalker*, a lizard whose body is so perfectly adapted to its environment that it has lost the classic reptile shape. Its torso is flat and wide, resembling a large, heart-shaped leaf of silver-green lichen. Its legs are short and set wide apart, ending in circular adhesive pads that can grip even the slickest slime-mould. 

The Stalker has a long, thread-like neck, often twice the length of its body, which it keeps coiled beneath its chest like a spring. When a drift-louse or an aphid passes, the neck strikes out with the speed of a snapping twig, the small, toothless mouth vacuuming up the prey before retracting back into the safety of the lichen-carpet. Their eyes are tiny, black beads set on the ends of short, fleshy tentacles that can peek out from beneath their leaf-camouflaged bodies while the rest of the animal remains utterly motionless for days at a time.

### The Law of the Drift

In this world, life is not defined by where you stand, but by how you cling. 

If a human child drops a cup, they do not watch it hit the ground. They watch it get smaller. They watch it change from a brown speck to a gray speck, until the silver mists of the mid-depths swallow it whole. 

The dead are treated with the same necessity. There are no graves; there is only the *Release*. When someone dies, they are wrapped in their own sleeping-net, weighted with a few heavy, water-logged pieces of ironwood, and tipped from the edge of the shelf-fungi. The family stands at the edge, their toes locked into the moss, and they do not weep; they whistle. They blow their pitch-pipes into the great flutes of the pillars, sending a long, descending chord down into the gulf, matching the pitch of the falling body until the sound of the wind carries it away. 

Below them, the gray mist receives everything, digesting the dead as it digested the stone of the old world, returning it all as the warm, wet breath that keeps the green pillars growing toward the light.
