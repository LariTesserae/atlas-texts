### The Vanes: Dwellers of the Upper Gale

The Vanes are not birds, nor are they insects; they are something more skeletal and refined, creatures designed solely as armatures for the wind. 

To look closely at a Vane is to see a creature stripped of all biological luxury. Their bodies are long, narrow spindles of cartilage and pale, dry fiber, smelling faintly of old tallow and mineral salt. They possess no warm blood; instead, a sluggish, amber-colored lymph rises through their narrow veins, driven not by a quick heart but by the constant, rhythmic flexing of their wings as they ride the air.

Their wings are their primary organs. stretching nearly twenty feet from tip to tip, they are composed of a dry, leathery membrane that is almost entirely transparent. In the blinding sun, one can see the shadow of their delicate, hollow bones cast through their own wings onto the white stone below. Each wing terminates in three long, jointed fingers, tipped with hard claws that are the exact grey-white of the dolomite.

Because they cannot flap their wings—their chests are flat and smooth, devoid of the heavy muscle required for active flight—their relationship with the gale is absolute. They do not fly so much as they are held by the air. When a Vane wishes to descend, it does not dive; it allows its wing-fingers to curl slightly, spilling the wind and sinking like a falling leaf. When it wishes to rise, it extends its fingers, locking the joints with a dry, wooden click, and lets the rising thermal currents lift it toward the dark indigo sky.

#### The Language of Flashes
In a world drowned by the bone-shaking roar of the mountain organ, sound is useless for communication. The Vanes have no vocal cords, and their ears—internal chambers buried deep within their skulls—are tuned only to the low-frequency vibrations of the rock shelves. They do not hear voices; they feel the mountain.

Instead, they speak in silence and light. 

The underside of each Vane’s flight membrane is not white, but a deep, velvet black. By tilting their wings relative to the sun, they can flash this dark underside against the glaring white face of the terraces. These flashes are quick, geometric, and highly coordinated. 

A single Vane, hanging motionless three thousand feet above a terrace, will glimpse a dark nodule of obsidian in a newly eroded fissure. It tilts its left wing, a brief tick of black against the white. Three miles away, another Vane catches the flash and repeats it. Within seconds, a wave of black-and-white semaphore ripples down the length of the twenty-mile shelf. The colony moves as a single, distributed mind, guided by these brief, silent Morse-like pulses of shadow.

They do not have names. Instead, each Vane is identified by the "tone" of the shelf where it was hatched. Because each terrace has its own height, width, and number of blowholes, each shelf vibrates at a slightly different pitch. A Vane hatched on the Ninth Shelf carries the specific vibration of that shelf in its marrow; when it roosts with others, they recognize its place of origin by the subtle frequency of its resting limbs.

### The Marrow-Grubs: The Wealth of the Crevices

If the Vanes are creatures of the dry wind, then the *Marrow-grubs* are the keepers of the deep moisture. They are the only soft things in this hard world.

Living deep within the narrow, frost-rimed fissures of the dolomite, these grubs are segmentless, translucent tubes of cold-bodied fat, some the length of a man’s forearm. They are blind and legless, navigating the dark seams of the rock by the slow, muscular contraction of their entire bodies.

The grubs exist to process the mountain itself. They feed on the black, iron-like wire moss, but their primary nourishment comes from the fine mineral dust and lime-silt that trickles down the cracks during the morning melt. They are geological filters. As they crawl through the dark, they consume the wet stone-dust, digesting the minerals with highly acidic stomach fluids and leaving behind a clear, dense grease that coats the paths behind them.

This grease is vital to the mountain's ecology. It prevents the moisture in the cracks from evaporating too quickly when the sun passes overhead, keeping the fissures damp long after the morning frost has vanished. 

To the Vanes, the grubs are ice and fuel. Because the Vanes cannot drink from open pools—which do not exist—they rely entirely on the grubs for moisture. A Vane will use its obsidian scraper to wedge open a tight seam of rock, reach in with its long, parrot-like beak, and retrieve a single grub. The grub is crushed and consumed, providing the Vane with both the fat necessary to maintain its wing-membranes and the pure, mineral-rich water stored in the grub's translucent flesh.

### The Chinks: The Keepers of the Binary Light

Because the sun creates a landscape of absolute white and ink-black shadow, there exists a specialized class of inhabitants that live only in the geometry of the dark. These are the *Chinks*.

No larger than a human hand, the Chinks are flat, many-legged arthropods with shells of a black so deep they appear as moving holes in the light. They are physically incapable of enduring the direct rays of the sun; their thin, chitinous carapaces would cook and split within seconds under the sharp, parallel beams of the white coin-sun.

Consequently, the Chinks are masters of shadow-navigation. 

As the sun moves across the dark sky and the ink-black shadows of the boulders creep across the flat terraces, the Chinks move with them. They huddle in the thousands on the eastern sides of the rocks in the morning, and as the day progresses, they migrate around the stone, staying strictly within the shifting margins of the dark. 

From above, they look like a boiling, black liquid clinging to the edges of the limestone. They are incredibly agile, their clawed legs designed to grip the greasy bone-like surface of the dolomite even when the gale is blowing at its highest velocity.

The Chinks are the scavengers of the terraces. They follow the Vanes to the knapping-grounds, waiting in the shadows of the low ridges. When the Vanes depart, the Chinks rush out into the newly cast shadows to feed on what has been left behind: the tiny, discarded scraps of moss, the oily drippings of the Marrow-grubs, and the paper-thin sheets of shed skin that the Vanes cast off during their seasonal molts.

### The Chiming Reeds: The Living Pipes

Deep within the vertical blowholes—where the air is compressed into screaming, high-velocity jets—lives an organism that is neither plant nor animal, but a calcified colony known as *The Chiming Reeds*.

These are long, hollow tubes made of a light, porous lime-carbonate, grown by microscopic, colonial polyps that secrete stone instead of shell. They cling to the interior walls of the vertical shafts, growing downward into the dark like pipes within pipes.

The Chiming Reeds require the wind to live. They are filter-feeders, their hollow interiors lined with millions of microscopic, sticky hairs that catch the organic dust carried down from the flat terraces by the gales. 

But their growth is not random. The polyps are sensitive to vibration; they grow only in the patterns dictated by the air currents. Where the wind shears hardest against the rock, the reeds grow thin and dense as steel; where the air eddying in the shaft is slow, they grow thick and soft.

This selective growth turns every blowhole into an acoustic chamber. The Chiming Reeds are responsible for the high, metallic whistling that overlays the mountain’s low rumble. They do not merely inhabit the sound; they are the strings of the instrument. 

When the wind shifts even a fraction of a degree, the air pressure within the shafts changes, and the Chiming Reeds heat up from the friction of the air, expanding or contracting to change their pitch. The Vanes, floating miles above, listen to this rising and falling whistle; to them, it is a perfect meteorological report. A sharp rise in the pitch of the Fourth Shelf’s blowholes tells them that the northern gale is hardening, and it is time to drop into the deep crevasses where the dead-air lies.
