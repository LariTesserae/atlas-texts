They are not visitors here, nor were they brought. They are what happens when sixty years of animal fat, lime-dust, and coal-soot are left to simmer together in a permanent, warm drizzle. They are the *Condensates*.

To call them animals would be to ignore their lack of bones, blood, or anything resembling a heart; to call them machines would be to ignore the soft, moist sighing of their flanks. They are a third thing—a low-grade, heavy flesh that has precipitated directly from the damp air and the grease-slicked water.

The largest of them are the *Tallow-Glands*. They look like nothing so much as discarded sacks of wet suet or raw, salted lard, greyish-white and marbled with veins of black coal-dust. They have no limbs, heads, or eyes; instead, they exist as slow, shifting masses, three or four feet across, that cling to the lower brickwork where the water is deepest. They move at the speed of rising damp, perhaps three inches a day, their underbellies secretes a pale, alkaline slime that reacts with the acidic run-off. They are the filters of the basin. They do not eat; they breathe the hot tallow-mist through wide, puckered spiracles that look like rubbery gaskets, and they scrape their bellies against the iron floor-plates. Where a Tallow-Gland has rested, the basalt is left clean and dark, stripped of its green grease and iron scale, which the Glands digest and turn into more of their own heavy, lardaceous bulk.

Within the brickwork itself, in the mortar lines that the lime-leach has hollowed out, live the *Sleeve-Worms*. These are long, fibrous cords, the thickness of a steam-shroud, made of compressed horsehair and black oil-slime. They are the children of the ancient pipe-insulation. They have no sensory organs other than a extreme sensitivity to the rhythmic *clack-hiss* of the great cast-iron wheel. When a joint in the copper conduits begins to fail, leaking its hot, bitter water, the Sleeve-Worms are drawn to the wet heat. They crawl toward the leak with a blind, hydraulic pressure, winding their hair-thick bodies around the joint until they form a dense, wet plug. Over years, their bodies calcify from the mineral-heavy water, turning into hard, stony bandages that seal the pipe forever.

Around the single orange lightbulb, the one wrapped in the green vine, live the *Glass-Gills*. They are small, translucent discs, like fleshy barnacles or large, amber drops of resin, that grow directly upon the hot glass of the jar. They are the only things here that seem to care about the light. Their interiors are filled with a greasy, yellow ichor that glows with the same low, dull orange of the filament. They do not move at all once they have settled. They drink the heat, their thin, paper-like skins vibrating in sympathy with the low hum of the wet-wound dynamo. When one dies, it does not rot; it simply cools, hardens into a small lump of amber-colored wax, and is slowly overgrown by the leaves of the heating vine.

There is no competition among them, no hunger as we understand it, and no anger. They are as much a part of the alley’s plumbing as the copper siphons. 

If you were to sit on the copper gantry and watch them through the amber dusk, you would see no movement that did not look like the sagging of wet cloth or the dripping of oil. Yet they are aware of one another. When the 9-foot drive-wheel slows—as it sometimes does when a cistern far above runs low—the Tallow-Glands down in the basin will stop their slow scraping. They will swell slightly, drawing the warm, wet soot into their spiracles, and emit a collective, low-frequency hiss, a sound like steam escaping from a woolen blanket. It is not an alarm; it is simply the way they register the rhythm of their world.

They are completely blind to the world outside the four walls. If a man were to fall into the basin from the grey seam above, they would not flee him, nor would they touch him with any purpose. He would simply be a dry, cold thing in their wet world. Within days, the warm drizzle would soak his clothes; the liverwort would take root in his boots; the Sleeve-Worms would explore his pockets for coal-ash; and the Tallow-Glands would slowly, very slowly, slide their heavy, warm bulk over his feet, sealing him into the basalt floor with their clean, protective wax, making him just another calcified terrace in the quiet, damp liturgy of the machine.
