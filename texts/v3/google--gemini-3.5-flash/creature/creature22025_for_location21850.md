In the Sinking Rift, life does not fight the cold or the noise; it has settled into them, matching their grand, terrible pace. To survive here, an organism must be three things: deaf, slow, and insulated by either fat or down. 

At the bottom of this food chain are the grease-eaters and the vibration-drinkers.

Wherever the copper utility cables run, they exude a faint, chemical warmth and a low, electrical hum. Around these lines, and clustered thickest on the bolt-heads of the zinc-roofed generator shacks, grows **Tallow-Mould**. It is not a true fungus, but a dense, waxy lichen that looks like congealed lard colored a toxic, sulfurous yellow. It feeds directly on the petroleum-based grease of the generators and the microscopic copper-dust shed by the vibrating cables. It is completely dry to the touch, feeling like cold candle wax, and it smells faintly of fat and hot solder.

Living within the Tallow-Mould are the **Hum-Gnats**. These are tiny, wingless flies whose thoracic muscles have adapted to vibrate at exactly sixty cycles per second—the same frequency as the low-voltage current in the cables. They do not fly—there is no room for flight in the heavy, static air—but they cling to the insulation in drifts of thousands, looking like patches of grey soot. When a generator falters, the gnats die instantly, their tiny bodies freezing into hard, black peppercorns that drop into the abysses below.

The **White Wood-Boring Beetles**—the "downy giants" of the bark—are the true lords of the timber. Because the ironwood is as hard as cast iron, these beetles do not bite through the wood; they dissolve it. Their mouthparts secrete a highly acidic, tannin-rich saliva that softens the petrified bark into a dark, fibrous pulp, which they then scrape up with heavy, spade-like mandibles. This process is so slow that a single beetle may spend forty years excavating a trench no longer than a man’s arm. 

Their shaggy, white down is not hair, but modified, hollow chitin fibers that trap the tiny pocket of warmth generated by their slow digestions. If you place a hand near a sleeping beetle, you can feel a faint, throbbing heat, like a brick that has spent the day in a weak sun. They are entirely blind. In place of eyes, they have two long, feathery antennae that they press flat against the bark to monitor the seismic groaning of the Rift. They know when a structural column is about to crack hours before it happens; when they feel the specific, high-frequency "shearing" vibration in their legs, they will begin their agonizingly slow migration to a safer trunk, moving perhaps three inches an hour.

The beetles are hunted by **The Peelers**. 

These are the largest native predators of the Rift, though "predator" is a generous term for an animal that moves with the glacial deliberation of an hourglass. A Peeler looks like a cross between a star-nosed mole and a three-toed sloth, about the size of a hound, but flattened as if it had been pressed between two floorboards. Their skin is the color of the dark-grey ironwood, wet-looking and hairless except for a thick, greasy ruff of white wool around their necks to protect their jugulars from the cold. 

The Peeler has no eyes and no external ears—its ear-canals are fused shut with thick plugs of wax to prevent the turbine scream from destroying its brain. Instead, it "hears" through its chest. It presses its flat, cartilaginous sternum directly against the ironwood trunks, receiving the vibrations of the wood-boring beetles below the surface. When it locates a beetle, it uses its two massive, chisel-like foreclaws to peel back the frozen bark in long, dry splinters that ring like slate when they hit the floor. The Peeler’s metabolism is so low that a single hand-sized beetle will sustain it for three months. Between meals, it enters a state of torpor, hanging from the underside of a horizontal trunk by its curved claws, indistinguishable from a burl of knotwood.

But there are also humans here, or what remains of them.

They are called **The Thrummers**. They are the descendants of the maintenance crews left behind when the industrial concerns above abandoned the Sinking Rift a century ago. They have not survived by conquering the Rift, but by submitting to it.

A Thrummer is short, barrel-chested, and thick-necked, built to draw down the dense, turbine-scrubbed air. Their skin is the pale, curdled white of skimmed milk, and their eyes are small, watery, and useless, covered by a thick, protective nictitating membrane that shields them from the flying frost-needles. 

They are universally, profoundly deaf. The physical pressure of the ventilation turbines blew out their ancestors' eardrums generations ago; modern Thrummers are born with fused middle-ear bones. They do not know what silence is, but they know what *movement* is. They communicate through **"Cable-Talk."** By pressing their throats or fingertips against the long copper lines or the ironwood trunk-roads, they can send and receive complex patterns of vibrations. They can "talk" across three miles of empty void by striking a zinc roof with a lead-weighted mallet, the patterns traveling through the structural timber to be read by the soles of another Thrummer’s feet miles away.

The Thrummers live in the zinc-roofed generator shacks, which they treat as sacred hearths. Their entire culture is built around **The Maintenance**. They do not understand the engineering behind the automated generators or the great eight-bladed turbines at the northern bottleneck, but they know that if the humming stops, the "Cold-Rot" will come, the wood will soften, and their world will collapse into the black deep.

They tend the machines with religious devotion. They climb the rusted suspension bridges with wide, bare feet—the soles of their feet are as thick and calloused as boot-leather, split into deep, self-healing rills that grip the frosty bark. They carry skin-pouches of tallow harvested from dead Peelers, using it to grease the massive, groaning bearings of the condensation collectors. 

They eat the white beetles, roasting them on the hot, vibrating casings of the generators until the downy coat singes off with a smell like burning hair, leaving behind a rich, oily meat that tastes of iron and turpentine. They dye their heavy wool garments with the yellow Tallow-Mould, so that in the dim, yellow glare of the utility bulbs, they look like flickering, mechanical ghosts moving through the frozen columns of the world.

When a Thrummer dies, they do not bury them in the stone or the wood. They carry the body to one of the great voids, tie a heavy iron gear-tooth to the ankles, and cast them out into the dark. The fall is silent, lost in the permanent, thundering roar of the turbines and the groaning of the ice, until the body vanishes into the black air three miles below, where the ironwood finally ends and the true deep begins.
