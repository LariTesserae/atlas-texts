The people of Oru-Val do not look like creatures of the open sea, for they are daughters and sons of the mountains, built for gravity and rock, even if they must navigate both through a medium of liquid diamond. 

To look upon a weaver of the high basin is to look at someone made of grey birch bark and mountain fat. They are thick-bodied and short-limbed, their torsos barrel-shaped to house the massive, double-valved bellows of their chests. They are entirely hairless, their skin a smooth, rubbery hide of pale slate-grey, insulated beneath by a dense, clean layer of white blubber that smells faintly of river-mint and wet stone. 

Because the water is so pristine, their eyes have adapted to see through great distances of light. They are immense, round, and ringed with a double margin of gold-colored muscle. They do not have pupils like land-dwellers; instead, their irises are webbed, contracting to a series of vertical pinpricks when they look up at the blinding silver ceiling, and widening into dark, lusterless pools that swallow every scrap of blue-green dusk when the sun dips behind the hidden peaks. They have no eyelashes, but a thick, oily membrane—the *glaze-lid*—glides horizontally across the eye every few minutes, clearing away microscopic spores of moss and the tiny, biting water-fleas that drift through the willow colonnades.

They are slow. To move quickly in Oru-Val is considered a sign of madness, or a dangerous waste of heat. A weaver moves through the water with the deliberate, heavy grace of an owl flying through pine branches. When they travel between the floating wefts, they do not swim with frantic kicks; they reach out with long, five-jointed fingers, catching the coarse, hairy hand-lines of grey moss and pulling themselves forward in long, rhythmic glides, their broad, flat ankles twisting together behind them like a single rudder.

Beneath their armpits are two narrow, muscular slits—the *breathing-slots*. When a weaver rests in the warm, mossy belly of a weft, these slots pulse with a slow, sleeping rhythm, drawing in the cold, oxygen-saturated water and expelling it in tiny, low-frequency rings that wobble toward the ceiling.

***

In the absence of spoken words—which under this high pressure would sound like the deafening clatter of stones in a landslide—the weavers speak in a language of clicks, thrums, and skin-touch. 

They have a small, bean-sized nodule of hard bone situated just below the tongue. By striking this against the back of their lower teeth, they can produce a sharp *tck-tck* that sounds identical to the snapping of the blue bivalves. This represents their "high speech"—used to coordinate the complex, multi-family work of grafting the spine-willows or tightening the spruce-root cables. For intimate matters, they use the *thrum*. By vibrating the air-bladders behind their collarbones, they can project a warm, low hum through the water that can only be felt, rather than heard, by someone pressing their forehead or hands against the speaker's chest. To a stranger, a family circle in Oru-Val would seem utterly silent, yet the water between them is thick with public work-calls and private, vibrating lullabies.

Their children—the *elvers*—are born in the deep, warm nests of the wefts. Unencompassed by the heavy currents of the outer valley, they are tiny, slippery creatures, more pink than grey, with skin so thin that one can see the tiny, orange threads of their hearts beating against their ribs. For their first three years, they do not leave the sphere. They cling to the moss-walls like caterpillars, feeding on *creme-spores*, a fatty, wax-like secretion that the weavers scrape from the undersides of the giant willow ribbons and ferment in hollowed basalt bowls.

***

Beside the weavers, other things find their place in this suspended garden.

The *glass-skates* are their domestic companions and their curse. These flat, triangular creatures—the size of a woven shield—are almost entirely transparent, save for a central mass of bright orange jelly that constitutes their stomach and liver. They are blind, finding their way by tasting the water for the scent of rot or old tallow. The weavers keep them much like goats; they tether them to the outside of the wefts, where the skates spend their days scraping the green slimes and algae off the willow-wood with their hard, horn-like underbellies. The sound of their grazing—*shrr-shrr-shrr*—is the domestic music of the hamlet, reassurances to those inside that the walls are being kept clean and the wood is not rotting. 

Yet, if a skate escapes its tether, it will slide into the willow-groves and begin to eat the tender, young grafts that a family has spent ten years training. When this happens, the young men of the village hunt them with long, tipped bone-spears, tracking the faint, shimmering distortions of their transparent bodies against the blue-green dark.

Deep down, where the spruce-root cables meet the black granite of the mountain summits, lives the *grey-moss crone*. This is not a single beast, but a sprawling, symbiotic crust of ancient, blind crabs—each no larger than an acorn—that live inside the calcified lime-deposits of the anchor stakes. They do not move, save to reach their feathery antennae into the lazy gyre to catch falling debris. Over centuries, their shells fuse together, forming great, pale blankets of living stone that lock the anchor lines into the mountain. The weavers treat these encrusted cables with immense respect; to damage one is to risk a weft breaking free and bobbing uselessly up to the silver ceiling, where the water is too thin to breathe and the cold sun burns the skin to ash.

***

And then, there are the *great ones*—the basin-carp. 

They are the true owners of the deep valley. Four meters of slate-colored muscle and slow, cold intelligence, they do not care for the weavers, nor do they fear them. They drift through the vertical avenues of the spine-willows like low-flying clouds, their heavy, stone-hard scales scraping against the trunks with a sound like iron filings shifting in a pan. 

The weavers hunt them only once in a generation, when a weft has grown too heavy with moss and needs the buoyancy of the carp’s oil-filled swim-bladders to stay afloat, or when a family’s tools have worn down to nubs. The hunt is a quiet, terrifying affair that takes place in absolute silence. It does not involve hooks or nets, which would be snapped instantly by the beast's weight, but rather a slow, communal poisoning. For weeks, the weavers will insert small plugs of toxic, white *death-moss* into the hollow stems of the willows where the carp are known to graze. 

When the great fish finally slows, its gill-slots stiffening with white mucus, it does not thrash. It simply sinks toward the basalt floor. The weavers swim down to meet it, their slate-knives ready, their golden eyes wide in the gloom. They apologize to the beast by holding their hands to its massive, plated head and singing a long, low thrum—a vibration of thanks that travels through the bones of the fish, through the granite of the mountain, and out into the endless, silent silver above.
