The life that occupies this shelf of high-altitude moss is as patient, damp, and slow-moving as the land itself. It has developed in the shelter of the constant, deafening roar, adapting to a world where light is a blinding, shadowless blade and the earth underfoot is elastic and alive.

### The Silt-Walkers

The primary residents of the high bog are the Silt-Walkers. It is difficult to say if they are human, or if they are simply a strain of humanity that has been pressed and cured by the high-altitude pressure like the peat beneath them. 

To survive the flat, silver glare of the sky, they have lost their pupils. Their eyes are flat, silvery discs, covered by a tough, translucent membrane—not unlike the nictitating membrane of a bird—that filters the intense light. To look at a Silt-Walker is to meet a reflection of the sky. 

They are extraordinarily tall and light, their skeletons made of a flexible, dense cartilage rather than brittle calcium. This makes them supple; if they fall down a vertical peat-chasm, they do not break, but bounce and slide through the wet, slick moss. Their feet are their most remarkable feature: wide, flat, and splayed, with skin like tough, black leather. From the arches of their feet grow coarse, hair-like fibers that mimic the root systems of the peat-succulents. This webbed, fibrous structure distributes their weight so perfectly that they do not sink into the saturated sponge of the terrain; they skim across the jade-green crust with a silent, sliding stride, leaving footprints that fill with clear water and vanish in minutes as the moss bounces back.

Because the atmosphere is a loud, ringing chamber of rushing water and steam-shrieks, the Silt-Walkers have no use for voices. They are mute. Instead, they communicate through the ground. They carry long, copper-tipped rods made of hollow reed-wood. By striking these rods against the wet iron pipelines or plunging them deep into the green substrate, they send rhythmic, vibrating pulses through the wet earth. They can "hear" a companion three miles away through the soles of their feet, reading the vibrations like a language of taps, hums, and long, low slides.

They dress in garments sewn from the leathery sheets of dried tobacco-brown peat, sealed with a thick grease they harvest from the gears of the water-pumps. This grease—black, heavy, and smelling of mineral oil and rancid vegetation—keeps them perfectly dry. They move with the same agonizing slowness as the thirty-foot iron flywheels. A Silt-Walker might stand on the lip of a wet chasm for three hours, motionless as an iron valve, simply observing the slow, vertical weep of water before taking a single, gliding step forward.

### The Steam-Suckers

Near the massive, dark pumping stations, where the air is thick with hot, pressurized vapor, a strange, parasitic ecology has taken root. Here live the Steam-Suckers.

These are colonial organisms that look like a cross between a giant barnacle and a pale, fleshy bladder. They anchor themselves by the hundreds to the hot copper-clad distillation towers and the steam release pipes. Their shells are made of a dense, calcified grit they secrete from the minerals dissolved in the mountain runoff. 

When a relief valve shrieks and lets loose its column of superheated steam, the Steam-Suckers open. They expand their pale, ribbed bladders to catch the moisture, swelling to the size of wine casks. They do not eat plants or animals; they live entirely on the heat of the machines and the trace minerals carried in the industrial condensation. 

In the brief, quiet intervals between the steam releases, these bladders contract with a wet, sighing whistle, squeezing the captured heat into their core to survive the biting, high-altitude chill. To the Silt-Walkers, they are a vital resource: when a Walker travels far from the warmth of the pumping stations, they will harvest a swollen, hot Steam-Sucker, carrying it under their peat-coats like a living, radiating hot-water bottle.

### The Gristle-Roots of the Chasm-Deeps

Down in the clean, vertical cuts of the canyons, where the peat turns from mustard-gold to the black of ancient, compressed history, there is no light at all. Here, in the cold, high-pressure dark, live the Gristle-Roots.

These are the true giants of the bog. They are soft-bodied, blind, and segmented, resembling massive earthworms but composed of a dense, fibrous tissue that is nearly identical to the roots of the giant cushion-mosses above them. Some are as thick as steam boilers and stretch for hundreds of feet through the subterranean peat.

The Gristle-Roots do not dig tunnels. Instead, they secrete a highly acidic, cold slime that dissolves the ancient, half-decayed plants before them. They slowly swallow this sterile, organic slurry, filtering out nutrients and depositing a highly dense, black, coal-like loam behind them. 

They move with a geological patience—perhaps three feet a month. Their presence is only felt on the surface when their massive, slow-turning bodies displace the surrounding peat, causing the green crust above to groan, buckle, and occasionally split into a brand-new, vertical canyon. The Silt-Walkers know when a Gristle-Root is active; they can feel the deep, wet *shudder* in the soles of their feet, a frequency even lower than the thrum of the steam pistons.

### The Glass-Spoons

In the perfectly still, black tarns that dot the flat basins, life is delicate and geometric. These obsidian pools are sterile of algae, too cold and nutrient-poor to support standard aquatic life. Instead, they are inhabited by the Glass-Spoons.

These are transparent, delicate arthropods, about the size of a human hand. Their bodies are made of a clear, silica-based chitin that is virtually indistinguishable from water. When they sit motionless in the tarns, they are completely invisible, save for the way they warp the reflection of the glaring white sky.

The Glass-Spoons feed on the micro-spores of the mosses that fall constantly from the air, drifting across the surface of the black pools. They move across the water tension on long, hair-thin legs that end in tiny, paddle-like spoons. They do not swim; they walk on the clouds reflected in the water, their movements so light they do not even create a ripple to disturb the perfect mirror of the sky. 

Once in a long while, the white birds that circle the steam vents will dive toward a tarn, but their beaks find only empty water. The Glass-Spoons are too quick, slipping beneath the surface tension with a dry, glass-click, leaving behind only the cold, unyielding reflection of the white sun.
