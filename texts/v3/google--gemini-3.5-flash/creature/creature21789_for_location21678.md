### The Skin of the Vahri

To see a resident of the Shelf—a *Vahri*—is to see a creature that has been varnished. 

Because the high salt-springs weep an water so caustic it would crack bare skin to the bone within days, and because the absolute light of the mock-suns incinerates unprotected tissue, the Vahri do not leave their skin bare. They are lacquered. From infancy, their bodies are rubbed daily with the cool, heavy fat of the lichen-snakes, mixed with the fine soot of burnt mountain-hare bones. This paste cures on their skin under the punishing UV glare, forming a thick, violet-grey rind. It is smooth, slightly hydrophobic, and smells faintly of rancid tallow and cold iron. Water does not wet them; it beads on their shoulders and runs off in oily droplets, leaving them dry beneath their second skin.

They are short, densely muscled, and broad-chested, built with a center of gravity that lies low in the hips. Their knees and elbows are capped with thick, horn-like calluses from a lifetime of crawling through the low, stone-lined tunnels of the stack-huts. They do not walk with the upright, swaying gate of lowland peoples; they climb even when they are on flat ground. Their feet are wide, with short, highly prehensile toes that have never known shoes, save for the flat, plate-like *clack-pads* carved from the ribs of mountain goats. These pads are strapped to their soles with gut-string, allowing them to drag their feet across the wet stone paths without crushing the millions of glass-shield isopods underfoot. To walk through Vahre is to hear a constant, wet, rhythmic *clack-shove, clack-shove* as the residents slide their feet, clearing a path through the clicking carpets of translucent crustaceans.

The most striking adaptation is in their eyes. The Vahri do not squint; they have no need to. Their eyelids are lined with double rows of stiff, interlacing white lashes that filter the glare like tiny bone combs. Beneath these lashes lies a second, semi-transparent nictitating membrane—the *saffron-veil*—which is a deep, amber-yellow. When the mock-suns are at their peak and the basalt walls are vibrating with white flare, the Vahri draw this yellow filter across their eyes. To them, the world does not look white and blinding; it is rendered in shades of deep, high-contrast gold and charcoal, allowing them to see the subtle, shifting fractures in the ice-lenses before they burst.


### The Architecture of the Cord

Because a stone room may exist for only twelve hours before an ice-bulge shears it in half, the Vahri do not live in "families" defined by blood or property. They live in *Cords*. 

A Cord is a group of four to nine individuals who are literally bound together. Around their waists, each member wears a braided belt of hare-gut and dried kelp-fiber, which is hooked to a central pewter ring. They sleep, eat, and work in this cluster. If a wall collapses in the middle of the frozen twilight, or if a step gives way over the two-thousand-foot drop of the Shelf, the Cord catches itself. There is no panic; the outer members brace against the wet basalt while the fallen are hauled back up into the crawlways, smooth and silent as seals being pulled onto a shelf of ice.

Their domestic life is a triumph of small, warm spaces. Inside the corbelled slate huts, there are no chairs or tables; the floor is a communal nest. To insulate these tiny chambers against the sub-zero drafts that hiss through the dry-stone seams, the Vahri share their beds with the giant woolly silt-moths. 

These moths, which are as large as flatbreads and covered in three inches of dense, grease-white pile, are treated with a quiet, maternal tenderness. They are slow, blind, and warm-blooded, their flightless bodies constantly vibrating at a high frequency to maintain their internal temperature. A Vahri family will sleep beneath a living blanket of thirty or forty of these moths, the shivering of the insects’ wings creating a soft, mechanical hum that is one of the only sounds to survive the thin, dead air of the cleft. The moths feed on the salt-sweat of the residents’ skin and the grease of the hare pelts; in return, they keep the temperature inside the stone nests just above freezing.


### The Silent Speech

In the high, thin air of Vahre, the human voice is a useless thing. It does not carry; the sound waves are swallowed by the damp, heavy mist and the constant grease-slicked weeping of the walls. 

Consequently, the Vahri are almost entirely mute. Their throat-noises are reserved for warnings—a sharp, bird-like click of the tongue that signifies structural failure, or a low, rolling growl produced in the chest to indicate that a salt-spring is about to burst its channel. 

For all other communication, they use a language of touch and stone. 

When two Cords pass each other in the dark, narrow tunnels between the huts, they do not speak; they rub shoulders. The texture of the snake-grease on their skins, the warmth of their bodies, and the rhythmic tapping of their fingers against each other’s collarbones convey everything from lineage to mood. 

More complex information is sent through the rock itself. The Vahri are masters of the *Heel-Tap*. By striking their bone-soled clack-pads against the basalt foundation plates of the village in specific rhythms, they can send vibrations telegraphing through the entire stack of huts. A mother three levels down can "feel" the arrival of her partner at the upper springs by the specific, low-frequency hum vibrating through her slate hearth. The village is never really quiet to those who live there; it is a constant, subtle drumming, a conversation of bones against stone that the residents read through the soles of their feet and the palms of their greasy hands.


### The Great Re-stacking

The Vahri do not have builders; everyone is a mason. 

Every evening, when the white glare of the sun fades into the deep violet darkness of the high-altitude dusk, the "Weeping" slows. The gelatinous sheets of mineral water on the basalt walls begin to congeal into those fat, blue-white rime-knuckles. As the water freezes, it expands with terrifying force, groaning inside the rock fissures and behind the slate back-walls of the huts.

Instead of hiding from this transformation, the Vahri move with it. They emerge from their tunnels in the purple half-light, their oiled bodies gleaming, working in silence. 

They dismantle the walls that are being pressured by the ice. With flat wooden wedges and bone mallets, they tap the slate tiles apart, stacking them in neat, small piles. They watch the ice-lens grow. If a three-ton tongue of blue ice forces its way out of a crevice, they do not try to melt it or chip it away; they use it. They lay new slate shingles directly over the ice-tongue, using it as a lintel or the bench for a new communal bed. 

Because of this, the geography of the town is entirely fluid. A Vahri child does not learn the way to the water-source by landmarks; they learn the *principles* of the cleft. They know that the water always flows down, and that the dry-stone stack must always lean into the wind. If a dwelling that existed yesterday has been ground into gravel by the mountain's shifting ribs, they simply take the pieces and build a new, smaller chamber five feet to the left, using the cleft's new contours as their guide.


### The End of the Cord

The lintels of the Vahri huts are not made of wood, for no tree has ever grown at this altitude. They are made of whale ribs dragged up from the coast in some long-forgotten epoch, or the heavy horns of the great mountain goats. But when these materials fail, the Vahri turn to their own.

When a Vahri dies, they are not buried; there is no soil to receive them, and the cold is too great for decay. Nor are they burned, for fuel is far too precious to be wasted on the dead. Instead, they are *un-woven*. 

The dead person’s Cord prepares the body. They scrape the violet grease-rind from the skin, returning it to the collective pots. They then lay the body in one of the deep, water-worn runnels at the edge of the shelf where the saline spring-water runs fastest. Within a few weeks, the highly mineralized, oily water has petrified the flesh, turning the limb-bones and torso into a hard, green-grey stone that is heavier than basalt and impervious to the frost.

These petrified ancestors are then brought back into the stacks. Their long, mineral-hardened femurs and ribcages are used to reinforce the roofs of the newborn chambers. A child sleeping in a low-hanging bunk will often look up to see the yellow, salt-crusted arm-bones of their great-grandmother serving as the joists that keep the slate roof from collapsing under the weight of the winter snow. 

In Vahre, the dead do not leave. They simply stop moving, their bones joining the slow, silent work of holding the village together against the wind and the white, incinerating sun.
