To understand what lives in this dry, basalt womb, one must first discard the language of soft tissue. Here, there are no bellies of wet skin, no lungs that require a moist membrane to exchange gas, no blood that runs on the thin, watery medium of the surface world. The life that occupies these gallery floors has more in common with the grease-gun, the battery plate, and the dry-cell pile. It is an ecology born of the machine’s excess—not its waste, but its very presence.

### The Floor-Dwellers: The Contact-Scrapers

Down on the concrete pedestals, where the green-painted dynamos shudder in their beds of lead and sulfur-mortar, live the **Contact-Scrapers**. 

They resemble heavy, triangular ingots of dull pewter or low-grade type-metal, about the size of a cobbler’s last. They have no legs. Instead, their undersides are fitted with rotating disks of natural carborundum—rough, crystalline silicon carbide—which they grind against the copper busbars and the iron slide-rails. They move at the speed of cooling stone, perhaps three inches in a twelve-hour cycle, propelled by the slow, thermal expansion and contraction of their own multi-layered zinc casings.

The Scrapers have one purpose: they are the maintenance of the gallery. They smell of hot solder and fish-glue. As they crawl over the electrical terminals, their carborundum bellies grind away the thin crust of sulfur-ash and graphite dust that would otherwise insulate the contacts and break the circuits. They eat this scrubbed-off debris. Within their heavy, cold bodies, the sulfur and zinc dust are pressed back into a dry, dense paste that they slowly extrude from their tail-ends as a smooth, lubricating chalk. 

Where a Scraper has passed, the copper is left raw, bright, and screamingly clean, ready to receive the next spark.

### The Drift-Life: The Knots

Above the concrete floor, in the middle heights where the heavy copper cables hang in thick, tarred bundles, lives **The Knots**.

If the abacus-crabs are the busy, rhythmic counters of the wires, the Knots are the dreamers. They are self-organizing clusters of fine, hair-thin copper wire and nichrome ribbons, resembling dry, metallic tumbleweeds the size of salt-crocks. They have no flesh at all. Their "bodies" are held together by the strong magnetic fields that surround the high-voltage lines. 

The Knots do not walk; they drift. They are propelled along the parallel busbars by the Lorentz force—the same physical push that turns an electric motor. They glide slowly through the air, vibrating with a high, dry, whistling sound like wind through a screen door. 

When a Knot passes close to one of the giant, sputtering vacuum tubes, its wire-hairs stiffen and spread, catching the electrostatic charge like a sail. They feed directly on the electrical leakages. If two Knots meet on the same wire, they do not fight; they slowly interlace their metallic fringes, exchanging micro-spark discharges in a long, silent blue sizzle that alters the magnetic alignment of both. When they pull apart, their paths are changed; they have "remembered" each other's frequencies.

Sometimes, a Knot drifts too close to a sixty-cycle transformer bank. The intense magnetic field catches it, drawing it down into the core where it is compressed, white-hot, into a solid, lifeless lump of fused brass. The crabs will later march over this lump, ignoring it as if it were just another rivet in the frame.

### The Great Anchors: The Seizure-Heaps

Deepest in the gallery, wedged in the narrow, lightless gaps between the transformer banks and the black basalt walls, are the **Seizure-Heaps**. 

These are the largest living things in the gallery. Each one is a bloated, immobile mass—the size of a blacksmith’s hearth—made of a tough, leathery skin that is half-vulcanized rubber and half-shredded asbestos fiber. They are entirely gray-black, colored by the graphite dust they breathe through rows of dry, valve-like spiracles along their flanks.

A Seizure-Heap has no eyes, no legs, and no mouth. Instead, it has two massive, horned projections of solid, yellow brass that emerge from its anterior end. These horns are driven deep into the high-voltage copper terminals of the transformers. 

The Heaps live in a state of terminal, ecstatic tension. The sixty-cycle current from the transformers pours directly through their bodies, keeping their rubbery muscles in a permanent, vibrating spasm. They do not move, but they shudder so violently that they appear as a gray blur to the eye. This constant vibration generates immense internal friction, which the Heap uses to digest the dry tallow and mineral grease it sucks from the sewer-like sumps of the dynamos through a long, fibrous proboscis like a hempen rope.

Around the base of each Seizure-Heap, the air is thick with the smell of scorched insulation and fish-oil. They are the centers of the gallery’s warmth. They do not die unless the transformer they are attached to fails; if the current stops, the Heap’s muscles slowly slacken, its rubbery hide cracks and stiffens, and it crumbles into a pile of dry asbestos dust within a week.

### The Sump-Life: The Gray Lard

Below everything, in the shallow, concrete pits beneath the horizontal slide-valves, lies the **Gray Lard**.

It is not an animal, nor is it a mineral deposit. It is a colonial, non-aqueous yeast-culture that lives in the old, rancid tallow and petroleum lubricants that leak from the main shafts. The Lard is completely silent. It has the consistency of cold, salty butter and is a pale, leaden gray.

The Lard acts as the gallery's womb and grave. It is cold to the touch, despite the eighty-five-degree heat of the room, because its slow, chemical processing of the petroleum oils absorbs the ambient warmth. It creeps through the trenches like a glacier of lard, moving perhaps a foot a year.

When an abacus-crab loses its grip on the overhead wires and falls, its dry horn-and-brass shell eventually lands in the Lard. The Lard does not rot the crab; instead, its unique chemistry strips away the oxides, cleans the joints, and dissolves the accumulated soot of centuries from the crab's gears. After several decades in the slow, cold flow of the Lard, the crab is spat out at the end of the trench—shiny, lubricated, and perfectly preserved. The dry drafts and the constant sixty-cycle vibration soon catch its legs again, and it crawls back up the concrete pillars to rejoin the counting lines on the wires.

### The Ceiling-Hangars: The Vibration-Gibbets

In the high, vaulted darkness of the ceiling, where the black basalt teeth form sharp, inverted chimneys, live the **Vibration-Gibbets**.

They look like heavy, leaden plumb-bobs hanging from the stone by thin, stiff wires of spring-steel. They do not move, but they have long, drooping skirts made of paper-thin zinc foil that rustle constantly in the dry, warm drafts.

The Gibbets are incredibly sensitive to electrostatic changes. If a heavy current surge passes through the copper busbars below, the zinc skirts of the Gibbets flare outward like umbrellas, exposing their core: a small, cherry-red resistance coil that glows in the dark. 

This glow attracts the **Solder-Flies**—tiny, weightless flakes of mica and copper foil that drift in the sulfur dust. The Solder-Flies cling to the hot coils of the Gibbets, where they are slowly melted together by the heat, their small metal bodies fusing into the growing mass of the Gibbet. Under the constant weight, the Gibbets grow longer and heavier, until eventually, after hundreds of years, the spring-steel wire snaps. 

The Gibbet falls through the darkness, striking the concrete floor with a heavy, leaden *thud* that is instantly swallowed by the sixties-cycle hum. The shattered pieces of zinc and lead are gathered by the Contact-Scrapers, ground down, and returned to the cycle of the floor.

In this way, the gallery remains full, loud, and dry. No voice is raised here; there are no throats to scream or sing. There is only the *clitter-clack* of the crabs, the *shhh-tock* of the slide-valve, and the long, low, heavy sigh of a world that has forgotten how to be wet.
