To understand the things that dwell within the Great Vault of the Silt Basin, one must first discard the concept of the wet machine. 

There is no blood here. There are no tears, no mucous membranes, no yielding, damp bellies. In a world where liquid is chemically absent, life has abandoned the sack of water in favor of the bellows, the jointed rod, the dry slide of graphite, and the dense, waxy cell. The biology of the Basin is a triumph of solid-state chemistry and dust-lubrication. 

### The Silt-Walkers

Along the deep, polished trenches that snake between the colossal towers move the *Silt-Walkers*. These are the "travelers" whose soft, padded feet have spent three billion years wearing the salt floor into smooth-bottomed canals.

A Silt-Walker is a creature of long, arching lines, built like a tall, three-meter tripod of dry, fibrous horn. Its skin is not leather, but a substance resembling dense, grey felt or pressurized suede—impermeable to the dry nitrogen air, preventing the loss of the precious, oily lipids that serve as its internal medium. It has no head in the traditional sense; instead, its uppermost segment tapers into a heavy, downward-curving prow of polished bone, like the neck of a cello. 

The Silt-Walker does not possess eyes. It navigates entirely by the *Song of the Spires*. Its hollow chest cavity contains a series of dry, vibrating diaphragms made of parchment-thin skin. As it walks, the shifting wind-notes from the surrounding towers play through its body. It knows where it is by the chords the city sings: if the B-flat of the high southern obelisks begins to sharpen, it knows it has veered too close to the salt-flats; if the low, organ-like thrum of the three-hundred-meter spires grows deafening, it is home.

They move with a slow, pendulous stride, their wide, circular feet—padded with thick, self-renewing layers of dry, velvet-like wool—muffling what would otherwise be a heavy stride. They do not eat; they filter. Their lower undersides are carpeted in sticky, electrostatic hairs that attract the drifting, microscopic spores of the violet velvet-moss. Every few miles, they draw these hairs inward through a series of dry, scraping combs, swallowing the nutrient-rich dust to be processed by a digestive tract that is essentially a slow-burning, dry chemical furnace.

### The Great Segmented Grazers

Below the spires, where the velvet-moss grows thickest, move the massive, multi-segmented herds of *Pumice-Backs*. 

These creatures are the size of houses, but they weigh very little. Their carapaces are composed of a highly aerated, calcified foam—like biological pumice—light enough that their massive bulk does not sink into the fine grey silt of the basin floor. They resemble enormous, high-backed caterpillars made of dry, white plaster, their segmented bodies shifting with a slow, Accordion-like contraction.

At the front of a Pumice-Back is a massive, downward-pointing jaw apparatus: two flat, circular plates of yellowed, horn-like material that spin against one another like millstones. They do not bite; they grind. As the herd slowly traverses the plains of paper-moss, the sound is like a thousand mason’s files working on dry slate. They shave the ancient, slow-growing fans down to the rock, digesting the tough cellulose and mineral crusts. 

Because they have no liquid feces, their waste is deposited as dry, tightly compressed bricks of sterile lime, which are immediately targeted by smaller denizens of the basin. The Pumice-Backs are the earth-movers of this world; their slow, crushing passage breaks down the aging, brittle towers that have begun to lean, returning the calcified resin to the dust from which it will eventually be re-absorbed by the flora.

### The Lithocoursers

If the Silt-Walkers are the quiet pilgrims of the basin, the *Lithocoursers* are its frantic heart. These are the dog-sized, multi-legged arthropods that inhabit the honeycomb interiors of the spires.

Their bodies are masterpieces of dry engineering. Their exoskeletons are not made of chitin, which requires moisture to form, but of a dense, glass-hard silicate compound that rings like fine porcelain when struck. They have ten or twelve jointed legs, each tipped with a tiny, needle-sharp peg of solid quartz. As they scuttle through the hollow galleries of the towers, their legs click and clatter against the resinous walls in a ceaseless, dry rain of sound.

The Lithocoursers are entirely color-blind but highly sensitive to infrared radiation and vibration. They can feel the heat of a decaying radioactive isotope deep within a limestone vein from miles away. They feed on the *Resin-Gnashers*—smaller, grub-like creatures that bore into the structural spires. In doing so, the Lithocoursers act as the guardians of the city. When a spire’s internal passages become blocked by dust or debris, the Lithocoursers clear them, maintaining the precise aerodynamic shapes of the vents so that the wind may continue to whistle through them unimpeded.

They do not mate with liquid exchange. Instead, they produce dry, seed-like capsules containing their genetic material, which they deposit into the high-velocity vents of the spires. The wind carries these "seeds" through the city, where they are caught in the electrostatic webs of other towers, ensuring the survival of the species across the vastness of the basin.

### The Slate-Wings

High above, in the violet twilight where the spires lose themselves in the cavern roof, live the *Slate-Wings*. These are the giant, moth-like gliders with wingspans that can reach five meters.

A Slate-Wing is almost entirely wing. Its body is a small, pencil-thin spindle of hollow, wicker-like bone, weighing less than a kilogram. Its wings are made of a dry, leathery membrane that has the texture of ancient, lipid-treated parchment. They do not flap their wings—there is no energy for such waste in this dry world. Instead, they are masters of the thermal drafts.

The Slate-Wings spend centuries in the air, never touching the ground. They ride the hot, dry nitrogen geysers that rise from the mantle-fissures, circling the giant towers like flakes of burnt paper. Their wings are edged with tiny, comb-like fringes that hiss as they slice through the wind. 

When a Slate-Wing dies, it does not fall or rot. Its body is so light and dry that the wind keeps it aloft long after its life has departed. It drifts through the upper vault for decades, its leathery wings slowly fraying and tattering in the drafts, until it finally disintegrates into a fine, grey powder that settles over the velvet-moss below.

### The Un-Rotting Dead

The most striking inhabitant of the Great Vault is, perhaps, the dead themselves.

Because there is no moisture, there are no bacteria of decay. When any creature of the Silt Basin ceases to function, it does not decompose. It does not smell of rot; it smells only of dry bone and old wax. 

A Silt-Walker that dies along the trail remains standing for centuries, propped up by its tripod legs, slowly turning to stone as the wind coats its grey, felt-like skin with deposit after deposit of fine salt and mineral dust. Over the millennia, these dead travelers are polished by the silt-laden drafts. Their edges round out. Their prows become smooth. 

Eventually, they are indistinguishable from the ancient, biological architecture that surrounds them. The young walk past the stone-hard, wind-polished monuments of their ancestors, their soft pads clicking faintly on the trenches that guide them home, while above them, the dead continue to sing in the wind.
