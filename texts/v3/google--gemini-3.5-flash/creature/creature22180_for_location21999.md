Deep within this dry plate-world, where there is no water to rot a bone or green a stone, life exists not as a damp, brief spark, but as a slow, friction-free continuation. Every creature is a child of the dry silt and the static, born from the patient assembly of the wind or the blind, rhythmic stamp of the high-mesa foundries.

### The Small and the Constant

In the joints of the great machinery, where the fifty-yard flywheels meet their stone-set collars, live the **Greasers**. They are about the size of a man’s hand, shaped like flat, oblong bellows made of thick, sulphur-cured hide that smells of old lard. They do not have mouths, but rather a broad, porous pad on their underbellies. They spend their decades crawling along the massive iron axles, feeding on the dry dustings of iron filings and saltpetre that shed from the moving parts. In return, they secrete a heavy, black, self-lubricating oil—a distillation of the graphite they swallow—directly into the bearings. It is a perfect, silent partnership: if the wheel stops, the friction heat dies, and the Greasers freeze into hard, brittle disks of resin; if the Greasers die, the wheel screeches, heats to red ruin, and shears its mounts.

Higher up, along the rounded cornices of the purple siltstone cliffs, the **Glass-Flits** make their homes. These are small, fragile things, no heavier than dried leaves, whose bodies are spun from hair-thin filaments of volcanic silica. They are entirely transparent, save for a small, dark nodule of magnetite in their centers that serves as both heart and compass. They do not flap their wings; instead, their sails are frozen in permanent, cupped curves that catch the continuous dry thermals rising from the deep basins. When the wind rises, thousands of Glass-Flits are lifted from the stone like a cloud of salt. As they drift through the canyon hazes, the passage of the air through their hollow, lead-glass bones produces a endless, pale whistling—a dry, multi-tonal chord that rises and falls with the temperature of the day. They sustain themselves by drifting through the smoke plumes of the square chimneys, their static-charged bodies attracting the microscopic flakes of zinc and sulfur that escape the sieves.

### The Dwellers of the Deep Basins

Below the plateaus, down in the blue-haze plains where the graphite dust lies miles deep, the scale of life swells. Here, where there is no solid ground to stand on, the **Basin-Gliders** rule the silent depths. 

These are massive, hollow cylinders of jointed shale and horn-dry hornplate, some reaching the length of a railway carriage. They have no eyes—light does not penetrate the deep, dark drifts of the graphite sea—but their sides are lined with long, spring-tempered steel whiskers that carry the slightest vibration through the dust. They "swim" through the dry, slippery graphite with a slow, undulating compression of their body segments, like a piston moving in an oiled chamber. 

The Basin-Gliders are the cleansers of the deep. They draw the dry, mineral-laden dust into their immense, bellows-like mouths, filtering out the heavy nodules of native copper and lead that have rolled down from the mesa slopes over the millennia. Their internal organs are a series of hard, grinding gizzards lined with diamond-hard corundum. Once a century, a Glider will rise to the edge of a plate-slope and disgorge a neat, compressed mound of pure, sorted metal ore—which is eventually gathered by the long cables of the high stations—before sinking back into the silent, black silk of the dusty sea.

### The Silt-Scribes

The closest thing to a thinking population in this dry world are the **Silt-Scribes**, or *The Gnomons*. 

They are tall, slender, tripod-like figures constructed of dark violet siltstone and jointed brass, standing twelve to fifteen feet high. A human observer would at first mistake them for surveyor's instruments or old, abandoned gears left behind by some long-forgotten industry. They move so slowly that their passage across a mesa can only be measured by the long, shallow grooves their lead-weighted feet engrave into the stone over the course of years. 

The Scribes have no faces. Their heads are heavy, polished blocks of lead-glass, ground into many-faceted prisms that turn slowly on brass collars. They do not speak, nor do they hear. They communicate across the vast, blue-shifted canyons by catch-and-release signaling: they tilt their faceted heads to catch the yellow light of the high sun, throwing bright, brief lances of amber glare to their kin situated ten or twenty leagues away on the opposite cliffs. 

A conversation between two Silt-Scribes is an event of vast, geological patience:
* A flash is sent.
* Six hours pass as the recipient tilts its heavy slate neck to register the light.
* A day passes as its internal clockwork gears click through the calculation.
* On the third morning, the reply flash is returned.

They are the keepers of the balance. They do not build the machines, nor do they repair them in the sense of a mechanic. Rather, they are the living regulators of the world’s friction. If they observe a giant cable vibrating at a frequency that suggests it is wearing thin, a Scribe will travel for three seasons to reach the base of that cable’s tower. There, it will stand for a decade, slowly adjusting a single, massive turnbuckle by the weight of its own suspended ballast-stones until the cable's song returns to its proper, low-frequency hum.

### The Great Nursery

In this world, life does not begin with an egg or a seed. There is no birth that involves the shedding of fluid or the tearing of membrane. 

On the highest mesas, inside the great yellow-brick findries that never sleep, the birthing is mechanical and clean. Here, the automated hammers beat out the bronze carapaces of the *wheel-backs*, and the gravity-slides deposit the lead-glass prisms into the frames of new *Scribes*. 

A newborn creature does not cry. It is stamped from a sheet of warm alloy, cooled in a bath of dry graphite dust, and set upon an iron ramp. When it reaches the bottom of the slide, the warm, dry wind catches its ribs, or the magnetic pull of the iron veins beneath the stone wakes its copper heart. It begins its first, slow crawl toward the edge of the plateau, already possessing the perfect, instinctive knowledge of its place in the great, dry, and beautiful machine.
