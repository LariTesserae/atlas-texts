In this heavy, unclosing basin, life is not organized into families or kingdoms; it is a single, warm, continuous digestion. The boundary between the creature, the vegetation, and the stone is soft and easily blurred. 

At the base of everything is the **Clay-Born**. These are the blind, pale amphibians that lie in the shallow, sulfurous pools. They are three to four feet long, but their bodies have no hard structure; they are made of a dense, gristly cartilage that bends like soft wood, and their skin is so thin and translucent that one can watch the dark, sluggish pumping of their double-chambered hearts. They have no necks. Their heads are mere wedges of skin-covered bone that taper into wide, chinless slits. They do not hunt or chase; indeed, they have no legs for running, only four stubby, paddleless limbs that they use to drag themselves through the warm, grey silt. They live in a state of perpetual, semi-conscious waiting. Red, feathery tufts of external gills float around their heads like halos of raw silk, swaying to the slow, subterranean currents of the pools. To touch one is to touch the grey clay itself—cool, slick, and slightly sticky, yielding to the finger without bouncing back.

Navigating the bulbous basalt mounds are the **Glass-Spines**, the giant millipedes. They are the only creatures of the basin that possess a true shape, wrapped in segments of wet, grease-glossed obsidian that click together with the dry sound of falling pebbles as they bend. They are four feet of heavy, armored muscle, yet they are entirely without malice. They have no eyes, only long, twitching antennae that whip the air, testing the thickness of the steam. They feed upon the liverwort paths, their thousands of needle-thin, yellow legs drumming a rhythmic, hissing march across the slick stone. Where they travel, they leave a wide trail of thick, clear mucus that never dries. Over hours, this mucus is colonized by microscopic yeasts, turning into a pale, glowing ribbon that other Glass-Spines follow. In this way, they move in endless, circular loops around the basalt loaves, following the scent of their own previous passages, living and dying on the same five hundred yards of stone for generations.

Higher up, in the green-jointed forests of sixty-foot horsetails, live the **Stridulators**. These are the six-winged insects, each the size of a human forearm, with heavy, segmented bodies the color of wet liver. They do not fly so much as drop. Their wings—stiff, amber-colored membranes—are too heavy for sustained flight in this dense air, and so they use them primarily as resonators. They cling to the hollow stems of the giant ferns with thorn-like tarsi, their bodies trembling in a constant, feverish palsy. This vibration is their life; it is how they shed the weight of the water that condenses on their backs in greasing layers. When millions of them vibrate at once, the horsetail forest acts as a sounding board, amplifying their collective thrum into a deep, physical hum that rattles the teeth of any creature that enters. They feed by boring their sharp, three-sided proboscises into the fleshy joints of the plants, drinking the hot, starchy sap until their abdomens swell to the point of splitting.

But the most true occupant of the basin belongs to the deeper, reed-choked hollows where the water is too thick to run. The "lung" of the basin is not a metaphor; it is the **Sighing Sacks**. 

These are great, stationary mounds of animate flesh, half-buried in the clay, looking like nothing more than grey, wet boulders covered in veined skin. They are massive—some the size of haystacks, others as large as small cottages. They have no faces, no limbs, and no brains. They are simple, hollow chambers of thick, muscular fiber that have taken root in the earth. Their only movement is the slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction of their vaults. Through wide, ringed orifices that face the sky like toothless mouths, they draw in the heavy, yellow-white steam, filter it for nutrients, and then exhale it back into the basin in long, tepid sighs. 

Around these Sighing Sacks, the other life of the basin gathers. The pale amphibians crawl into the folds of their warm, pulsing skin to escape the heavy drip of the canopy. The Glass-Spines graze on the grey lichens that grow on their flanks. When a Sack dies, it does not rot; instead, it slowly loses its tension, collapsing inward like a wet leather tent. Within days, the liverworts and mosses have climbed its damp sides, and the clay-dwellers have burrowed through its softening walls, turning its carcass into a new, warm hilloc which the next generation will inhabit.

In this place, nothing is born with a struggle, and nothing dies with a cry. The creatures do not possess the lungs for screaming or the teeth for tearing. They are all soft edges, mucus, and slow, metabolic heat. They are the tenants of a world that was paused before the invention of hunger as we know it—surviving instead on a mutual, wet consumption, drifting through the warm soup of their eternal afternoon.
