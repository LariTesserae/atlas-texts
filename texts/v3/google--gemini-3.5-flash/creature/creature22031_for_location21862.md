To remain in this room for more than an hour is to realize that the rhythm of your own breathing is too fast, too violent, and too loud. Your lungs are a tempest of dry air in a chamber that demands the slow, wet suction of a tide. 

If you hold your breath until your ears throb, you will find that the darkness is not silent. Beneath the dripping *tock* of the water, there is a second pulse. It is a dual rhythm: a low, thick bellows-breath, and a dry, sliding friction. 

Something is shifting in the cracks between the clay cylinders.

There are no "animals" here in any sense that a naturalist would recognize. There are no eyes to reflect a light that never comes, no legs meant for running, and no lungs designed to chest the thin, cold air of the mountain peaks. What lives here is of the room; it is the room’s soft tissue.

Let your hand slide down from the razor-sharp gypsum shelves, past the salt-crusted edges of the clay pots, into the narrowest, warmest crevice near the floor where the geothermal steam rises. 

Your fingers will not find fur, or scales, or the cold chitin of an insect. Instead, you will touch something that feels exactly like the wet, inner lining of a cheek. It is warm—precisely thirty-eight degrees Celsius, the exact temperature of the mineral broth weeping from the walls. 

This is the *Kneader*.

It has no name for itself, for it has no voice with which to speak and no ears to hear a name. It is a creature of pure surface. It has no head. Where a head might be on a beast of the sunlit world, there is only a broad, circular disk of muscle—a giant, tactile palm that occupies the entire front of its body. This disk is covered in millions of microscopic, finger-like papillae, so sensitive they can detect the temperature of a falling water drop before it lands.

The Kneader's body is flat and segmented, like a thick, leathery tongue or a heavy strip of salt-cured beef. It is perfectly adapted to the razor-edged geography of the tufa. Its skin is not fragile; it is incredibly thick, lubricated by a dense, yellowish grease that smells of old yeast and copper. This grease is its shield. It can slide over the needle-sharp basalt crystals and the glass-like edges of obsidian without drawing its own pale, copper-rich blood. The sharpest gypsum teeth simply sink into its fat, rubbery hide and are held there, harmlessly, until the creature moves on.

In this tiny chamber, there are perhaps five or six of them, though they are so densely packed and so slow-moving that they often fuse together in the dark. For decades at a time, two Kneaders might press their flanks against one another in a crack behind the pots, their skin secreting the same biofilm until they are joined by a single, shared crust of sunless lichen. They do not fight for space; they share the same physical volume, breathing in alternating rhythms so that the small room never runs out of its thin, greasy air.

Their hands are their only limbs. 

They do not have legs. At the sides of their flat, muscular bodies are three pairs of short, stubby appendages that end in broad, double-jointed fingers. These fingers are remarkably human in their proportions, though they lack nails. Instead, the tips are capped with thick, mineralized pads—tools worn hard by centuries of scraping the mountain's bones.

These are the hands that made the pots.

The Kneaders are the potters, but their work is not a conscious craft; it is a metabolic necessity. The river-silt that forms the pots is not found in this mountain room. It was brought up long ago, perhaps carried in the gullets of their ancestors, or perhaps it is the residue of their own digested waste, ground down from the limestone they scrape from the walls. 

A Kneader’s life is an endless, agonizingly slow choreography of maintenance. Using their fingers, they scrape the fresh, wet tufa from the walls, mixing the soft, raw calcium carbonate with the greasy biofilm they lick from the backs of the old pots. They knead this paste between their double-jointed fingers for weeks, warming it with their breath until it reaches the consistency of thick lard. 

Then, they shape it. They press it against the wet basalt, smoothing the inner walls of the cylinders with their broad, facial disks until a new cell-chamber is formed. 

They do this to survive the water.

The water of this mountain is a slow, calcifying hemlock. If it were allowed to pool on their skin, it would encrust them in stone within a year. They would become statues, locked in their own private graves of spar. To prevent this, the Kneaders build the clay pots directly beneath the major drips. The pots are drainage basins, diversion channels, and armor. They collect the heavy, mineral-thick tears of the ceiling, turning the killer water into solid plugs of harmless calcite inside the clay.

When a clay cylinder is completely filled with the hard, translucent plug of spar, it is no longer useful. A Kneader will eventually crawl over it, find the solid stone core with its sensitive face-palm, and begin the work of reclaiming it. With their hard, fingernail-less digits, they chip away the old clay, break the calcite plug into tiny, brittle shards, and push the debris into the deep, steaming fissures where the geothermal vents dissolve it back into the heart of the mountain. Then, they begin to build the pot again.

This is the cycle of the room. It is a slow, closed-circuit economy of stone, water, and flesh.

If you were to remain here, wedged between the cold limestone and the warm, breathing leather of these entities, you would eventually feel one of their hands touch you. 

It would not be a grab or a strike. It would be a touch of infinite caution. The three-finger hand would find your arm, or your cheek, or your neck. The blunt, warm pads would move across your skin with the slow, searching drag of someone trying to read a map in a language they have not spoken for ten thousand years.

The Kneader would find your skin too dry. It would find your pulse too fast, like the fluttering of a dying bird. It would detect the strange, saltless evaporation of your sweat, so different from the heavy, alkaline grease of the mountain.

It would not hurt you. But it would begin, with a patience that has no end, to knead you. 

Its fingers would press into your flesh, attempting to smooth out your sharp angles, to mold you into the cracks, to seal you with its yellow grease so that the sharp crystals of the basalt would not cut your thin, strange hide. It would try to make you fit. It would try to turn you into another cell of the hive, another container for the water, until the mountain's warm, mineral breath became your own, and the dark caught you, and did not let you go.
