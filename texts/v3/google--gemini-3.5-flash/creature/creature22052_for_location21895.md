Within this silent, biological fortress, life does not simply inhabit the architecture; it is the currency, the maintenance crew, and the heating system of the city itself. Every creature is a gear in a cold, hydraulic clock, adapted to a world where the line between plant, animal, and ice is perpetually blurred.

### The Hearth-Bearers: *Pith-Pikas*
The heavy, white-furred rodents that slip through the circular portals of the moss-walls are known to the silence of the basin as *Pith-Pikas*. No larger than a closed fist, they are shaped like smooth, woolen eggs, completely devoid of tails and possessing only tiny, wool-filled slits where their ears should be. Their fur is not hair but a dense, felted down, saturated with a greasy, resinous lanolin that repels the constant drizzle of condensation.

The pikas are the warm-blooded engines of the city. While the vegetable towers are cold, the interior of a pika’s nest can reach a sweltering ten degrees above freezing—a tropical luxury in this basin. They do not live alone; they live in "huddles" of thirty or forty inside the hollow, multi-tiered bark towers. 

Their relationship with the city’s structure is deeply symbiotic. They nest exclusively on the horizontal shelves of bracket-fungi that divide the towers. In exchange for shelter, the pikas’ intense collective body heat warms the fungus. This warmth triggers the bracket-fungi to secrete a thick, yellowish, starch-rich milk from their pores, which the pikas lick from the shelves. Furthermore, the pikas’ nitrogen-rich droppings are the only soil-substitute in the entire basin; they are instantly reclaimed by the roots of the dwarf-willows that wrap around the towers, feeding the high-climbing vines that support their homes.

### The Stitchers: *Spindle-Grubs*
Along the suspended root-bridges and the cellular streets of *megasphagnum*, one can occasionally see what appears to be a slow, rhythmic pulsing of the vegetation itself. These are the *Spindle-Grubs*—flat, multi-segmented arthropods the size of pine needles, covered in a shaggy coat of green-and-white lichen mimicry.

The grubs are the city’s repairmen. They possess leg-joints that act like tiny, mechanical ratchets; when the temperature drops at dusk, they lock their joints and freeze in place, conserving all their energy without using a single muscle. 

The grubs are sensitive to the slightest change in air pressure. When a fierce wind-gust from the peaks cracks a moss-brick or threatens to tear a root-bridge from its mooring, the drop in pressure and the whistle of cold air alerts the grubs. Hundreds of them will crawl to the fracture, guided by the vibrations. From glands near their tail-segments, they spin a tough, elastic, frost-resistant silk that does not snap when frozen. They stitch the green masonry back together, weaving living moss-fibers into the wound until the plant can grow over the seam. Without the grubs, the winter winds would reduce the basin’s delicate terraces to green shreds in a single season.

### The Lanterns of the Deep: *Amber-Snails*
Deep within the water conduits and the pitch-black, resin-scented cisterns at the base of the towers, where the water remains fluid at just a fraction of a degree above freezing, lives the *Amber-Snail*.

These snails do not have shells of calcium, which is rare in this young basalt basin. Instead, they scrape dried spruce and willow resin from the inner walls of the conduits and fashion it into deep, translucent, amber-colored cups that protect their soft parts. Their bodies are completely transparent; in the dim light of the cisterns, one can see the slow, pale beat of their two- chambered hearts and the delicate, feather-like gills that extract oxygen from the mineral-heavy water.

The Amber-Snail’s tissue hosts a dense colony of cold-adapted, bioluminescent dinoflagellates. They glow with a faint, milky, greenish-blue light—a soft phosphorescence that illuminates the dark water-pipes like wet jade. This light is not for sight, as the snails are blind. Instead, it acts as a beacon for the translucent flatworms that navigate the currents. The flatworms are drawn to the glow; they crawl over the snails' skin, grazing on the film of cold-tolerant bacteria that would otherwise suffocate the snails, while the snails crawl along the conduit floors, scraping away the thick peat-silt that threatens to clog the city’s water supply.

### The Gray Sentinels: *Needle-Gauzers*
Beneath the shingled roof of horizontal conifers, where the air is eternally gray and damp, lives the basin’s quietest predator: the *Needle-Gauzer*. 

These are stick-like mantises, nearly half a foot long but no thicker than a conifer twig. Their bodies are segmented in shades of dull spruce-green and bark-brown, and their backs are lined with tiny, cup-like structures that catch and hold droplets of frozen dew, giving them the appearance of rime-crusted wood.

The Needle-Gauzer’s life is lived in units of weeks, not seconds. They do not hunt by speed; they are so slow that their movements are indistinguishable from the swaying of the branches in the thin mountain wind. They will sit on a hemlock branch for an entire month, their large, pale-violet eyes tracking the slow journeys of the spindle-grubs below. When a grub eventually crawls within reach, the Gauzer’s forelimbs—which operate on a slow, hydraulic pressure system rather than fast muscle fibers—reach out with an agonizingly slow, deliberate grace, taking several minutes to close around its prey. 

### The Importers: *Lithic-Ticks*
At the very edges of the basin, where the green metropolis meets the raw, black ribs of basalt and the howling mountain winds, a different kind of life exists. Here, on the freezing rime-ice combs, live the *Lithic-Ticks*.

These are heavy, round-bodied mites, the color of wet slate, their carapaces heavily mineralized with incorporated basalt dust. They are the only inhabitants of the bowl that regularly cross the border into the lifeless, frozen world outside.

The Lithic-Ticks climb out onto the naked, windy crags of the young mountains. They do not eat plants; they feed on the sulfurous dust, iron oxides, and wind-blown pollen that accumulate in the cracks of the high peaks. Their bodies are specialized chemical processors, converting these raw minerals into soluble compounds. 

When their multi-year life cycle near the peaks is complete, the ticks, heavy with minerals and slow with age, crawl back down into the sheltered basin. They seek out the wet outer edges of the *megasphagnum* walls, where they settle into the rime ice and die. As the spring sun briefly melts the ice, the mineral-rich bodies of the dead ticks dissolve, providing the nitrogen, sulfur, and iron that the root-vines and mosses need to continue their slow, architectural growth. They are the city’s miners, importing the raw chemistry of the dead mountain to sustain the living green paradise below.
