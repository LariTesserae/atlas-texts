To understand the citizens of the Notch, one must first understand that to them, water is not the source of life, but a solvent of terrifying, corrosive violence. In their language—which is not spoken, but patted and smeared upon the skin—there is no word for *wet*, only a word for *the peeling sickness*. They are creatures of grease, tallow, and dry mineral meal.

They are known, to the extent that they can be named, as the **Lard-keepers**, though closer inspection reveals them to be more like living, segmented candles wrapped in dense, moth-like wool. 

### The Physicality of the Keepers

A Keeper’s body is divided into seven distinct, barrel-shaped segments, each about the size of a small melon, linked by tough, flexible bands of yellow gristle. They do not possess bones; instead, their interiors are pressurized vaults of dense, white fat, structural lard, and slow, churning systems of oily lymph. 

Their outer surface is entirely covered in a pelt of extraordinary density. It is not hair, but a forest of microscopic, branched scales—a velvet pile so thick that it feels to the touch like a plush cushion that has been lightly dusted with starch. This pile is a pale, sulfurous yellow, matching the color of the butter-shrubs perfectly. It serves three purposes:
1. It insulates their cool, greasy interiors from the baking sun of the ridge.
2. It acts as a silent buffer; when two Keepers bump into each other in the narrow ledge-streets, there is no sound of impact, only a soft, deadened *thup* that is swallowed instantly by the dry air.
3. It collects the volatile resins of the cork-trees, turning each Keeper into a walking sponge of dry perfume.

They do not have eyes. In the brilliant, white glare of the gypsum cliffs, sight would be an agony of over-exposure. Instead, their first segment is dominated by four horizontal slits—pit-organs lined with sensitive, dark membranes. These pits do not see light; they see heat. To a Keeper, the Notch is a landscape of glowing, amber-hot plant veins, cool obsidian shadows, and the warm, radiating chimneys of their neighbors’ homes. Above these pits wave a pair of feathery, triple-jointed antennae that are constantly damp with a thin, non-evaporating oil. With these, they smell the world, reading the shifting clouds of turpentine vapor like a daily bulletin.

Their limbs are short, broad, and end in three blunt digits wrapped in thick, horn-like sheaths of yellow keratin. These sheaths are self-healing; if chipped by the razor-sharp obsidian plates of the ridge, they exude a small bead of quick-hardening resin from a duct at the nail-bed, sealing the wound within minutes.

### The City-Makers and Their Art

The Keepers do not build their homes with hands; they build them with their bellies.

Along the underside of their middle three segments run the *tallow-glands* that give the Notch its name. These are rows of pale, puckered pores that exude a specialized, heavy lipid—a structural stearin that is solid at body temperature but becomes malleable under the steady, warm pressure of their limbs. 

When a Keeper wishes to add a room to the vertical terrace, they first harvest the dry, corky fiber of the organ-pipe trees, chewing it into a fine, grey lint. They then crawl over the building site, pressing their belly-glands against the rock, depositing sheets of raw, golden wax. Using their flat forelimbs and their broad, blunt chins, they knead the lint into the wax, working the mixture until it becomes a stiff, leathery mortar. 

Because they move so slowly—perhaps three meters an hour when fully active—the construction of a single, bowl-shaped room is a matter of months. A nest-tower is a collaborative work of three generations, and the older chambers at the bottom of the stack are often several centuries old, their walls polished to a glassy, dark gloss by the passage of millions of velvety bodies.

### The Silent Society

In the Notch, there is no shouting, no song, no rattle of tools. The Keepers communicate through the **Secretion-Tongue**. 

When two Keepers meet on the narrow, resinous spiral ramps, they do not step aside. Such a movement would be too sudden, too risky on the high ledges. Instead, they perform the *soft-slide*. They press their flanks together and slowly glide past one another. During this contact, the specialized scent-glands on their shoulders exchange tiny smears of fat-soluble esters. 

In a single, three-minute rub, a Keeper can learn:
* The health and thickness of their neighbor's fat-stores.
* The location of a newly cracked butter-shrub down-slope.
* The emotional state of the neighbor (which ranges from *dry-stilled* to *warm-soft*).
* A record of who the neighbor has touched in the last several sun-cycles.

In this way, news travels through the vertical city like a slow, greasy ripple through a pond. A disturbance at the lower gates is felt at the highest nest-tower by evening, simply by the transmission of molecules from body to body.

### The Co-Inhabitants

The Keepers are not alone. Their high-density towers are home to two other distinct forms of life, which live in a quiet, unquestioned triad with the builders.

* **The Paper-Bugs:** These are flat, segment-less creatures the size of dried bay leaves, with thirty tiny, hair-like legs that move in a synchronized wave. Their bodies are made of a dry, stiff chitin that resembles grey cardboard. They do not eat the plants; they eat the old, flaky wax of the Keepers’ homes. As the sun bakes the high terraces, the outer layer of the leather-carton homes becomes brittle and chips. The paper-bugs patrol these walls, scraping away the dead wax with their serrated mandibles and leaving behind a smooth, polished coat of dry glaze. They are the maintenance crew of the Notch, sliding soundlessly over the walls like living shingles.
* **The Snail-Discs:** These are heavy, circular grazers that live on the paddle-leaves of the butter-shrubs. They have no heads; they are simply dome-shaped shells of solid, dark-red resin that protect a soft, yellow foot. They move with such glacial slowness that a single leaf can support one for its entire three-year life. They scrape the white, powdery dust and the golden fats from the leaves, condensing them into a dense, oily paste. The Keepers occasionally harvest these snail-discs, scraping the paste from their hulls to use as a soothing salve for their joint-segments.

### The Arc of a Life in Wax

The lifecycle of a Keeper is a gradual transition from fluid to solid.

They begin as eggs—pale, hard spheres of solid fat, buried deep within the insulating floor-layers of the inner nest-rooms. When they hatch, they are **oil-grubs**: small, thin, and entirely devoid of the velvet pile. They are highly active, sliding through the internal tunnels of the towers like droplets of warm butter, feeding on the softest, most highly refined oils brought to them by the nurse-adults.

As they grow, they develop their segments and their velvet coats, gradually slowing down as their bodies accumulate the dense structural fats necessary for adulthood.

The end of a Keeper’s life is not marked by rot or decay—there is no moisture to support the bacteria of decomposition. Instead, it is a process of **Stiffening**. 

When a Keeper reaches their second century, their oil-flow begins to slow. The joints of their limbs lose their flexibility; the velvet pile grows brittle and begins to flake away, revealing the hard, yellow-white skin beneath. Knowing their time of movement is ending, the elder Keeper makes a final, deliberate climb. 

They do not leave the city. Instead, they find a gap in the masonry—a crumbling archway, a weak point in a terrace floor, or an unfinished window-frame. They wedge their segmented body into the space, pulling their limbs tight against their chest. 

Over the next few months, their oily blood ceases to circulate, drying into a solid, resinous amber. Their skin hardens, fusing with the surrounding carton of the walls. Their segment-creases are filled in by the paper-bugs with fresh wax. 

They do not die so much as they become architecture. The arches that support the high towers of the Notch are not made of stone; they are the rib-curves of five-hundred-year-old ancestors, preserved forever in the dry, turpentine-scented air, holding up the homes of their children.
