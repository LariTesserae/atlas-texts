To live in the cleft of Saint-under-Sough is to be salted by wet iron and preserved in grease. 

The people who occupy these vertical tiers—the Soughers, as they are known to the few lowlanders who trade with them—are physically distinct from any race on the plains. At five thousand meters, the human form has had to make its peace with the thinness of the air and the weight of the water. 

They are short, heavy-boned people with ribcages that are wide and barrel-shaped, built like the iron boilers they tend. This extra capacity allows them to draw enough oxygen from the cold, soot-choked mist without panting. Their center of gravity is low; they walk with a characteristic wide-set, shuffling gait known as "the cleft-plat." They do not lift their feet high—to do so on the wet, moss-slick cedar of the walkways is to invite a five-hundred-meter slide—but instead slide their boots forward with a rhythmic, scraping confidence, their soles heavy with square-headed iron hobs that bite through the lichen to the wood beneath.

### The Tallow-Skin and the Hair-Plait

A Sougher’s skin has the waxy, water-repellent quality of lard that has been left to set in a cold pantry. Because the air is a perpetual, dripping sponge, they do not wash with water; to do so would strip the natural oils from the skin and leave it open to the "white-web," a parasitic mold that feasts on damp epidermis. Instead, they "grit" themselves. Once a day, from infancy to old age, they rub their hands, cheeks, and necks with a paste made of sheep-tallow, wood-ash, and pulverized slate. 

This paste creates a gray, polished barrier. Under the dim, orange glow of the steam-pipe vents, a Sougher’s face shines like a wet cobblestone. They do not chap, they do not blister, and they rarely show wrinkles; the tallow fills the creases of their lives before they can deepen.

Their hair is another matter. Left to itself, hair in Saint-under-Sough would rot within a season. Thus, both men and women wear it in "the lead-braid." They comb their hair with coarse combs dipped in linseed oil and soot, then braid it tight with fine copper or lead wire. These braids are heavy and stiff; they hang straight down their backs or around their neck-collars like grease-hardened ropes, swinging with a metallic *clack-clack* against their coats as they move. A Sougher with loose hair is considered either mad, dying, or dangerously unclean.

### The Language of the Low-Voice

Because the thick moss and the heavy, moisture-laden air swallow all mid-to-high frequency sounds, the Soughers do not speak as lowlanders do. They have no use for sibilants or high-pitched vowels; those sounds are lost twenty feet from the mouth, devoured by the purple-black carpets that drape the basalt.

Instead, their language—Sough-speech—is a series of low chest-tones, clicks, and rhythmic thrums. It sounds to an outsider like a wet gravel slide or the gut-vibrations of a large animal. They speak from the diaphragm, using the resonance of their barrel-chests to push the sound through the fog. 

For distances greater than fifty yards, they abandon speech entirely for "the strike." Every Sougher carries a "clapper-stick"—a short cylinder of heavy, tallow-soaked larch. By striking this against the iron cable-ways, the copper cistern chimneys, or the basalt walls themselves, they can send vibrations through the very bones of the town. A double-strike on the copper walkway-rim means *fresh coal arriving*; a slow, three-beat roll on the basalt means *the shroud is coming; stay where you are*. The children learn to read these vibrations through the soles of their boots long before they learn to write.

### The Sough-Ewes and the Ledge-Beasts

Though there are no horses or cattle, the Soughers are not without livestock. Clinging to the sheer, fluted basins below the town are the *Sough-ewes*—a breed of dwarf sheep that look more like multi-legged boulders than wool-bearing beasts.

The Sough-ewe has no eyes. In the black mists of the cleft, eyes are only targets for the sharp pine-needles and the acidic soot. Instead, they have two broad, muscular facial discs that are covered in fine, oily whiskers sensitive to the slightest change in air pressure or the scent of wet liverwort. Their hooves are not hard flint, but spongy and split, acting like suction cups on the wet basalt. They spend their lives on sixty-degree slopes, their heavy, yellow fleeces so thick with mutton grease that the rain does not even penetrate the outer inch of wool.

The Soughers harvest them with extreme care. A "sheerer" is let down on a coal-cable, suspended over the abyss with a pair of spring-shears. They do not take all the wool; they leave a thick "spine-ridge" of fleece along the sheep's back to keep the animal from dying of the high-altitude cold. 

From these ewes comes the fat that runs the town. The tallow is boiled down in the third-tier sumps, mixed with coal-tar to grease the creakers, and stuffed into the leather boots of the miners. The meat is stringy, black, and tastes of sulfur and wet pine; the Soughers cure it by hanging it directly over the active chimney-stacks until it is as hard and dark as obsidian. They call this *stack-strip*, and they chew it for hours as they work the cable-plugs.

### The Castes of the Cleft

Human society in Saint-under-Sough is divided not by gold or bloodline, but by tier and steam.

*   **The Boiler-Keepers (The Dry Ones):** These are the elite of the cleft, though their privilege is a strange one. They live and work inside the massive vault of the third tier, where the peat-fired boilers hiss and groan. Because they are the only people who are constantly dry, their skin is different—it is baked brown, cracked, and smells of lignite smoke. They do not grease their skin; they do not need to. However, this dryness is a trap. After ten years in the boiler-vaults, a Keeper’s lungs become "caked"; if they spend more than a few hours on the wet, external walkways, the cold mist causes their dry lungs to spasm and fill with fluid. They are kings who cannot leave their hearth.
*   **The Moss-Scrapers (The Green-Fingered):** Mostly children and those too light to work the heavy cable-ways. Their sole job is to prevent the town from being eaten alive. They carry long brass spuds—brass because it will not spark against the basalt and ignite the coal-dust—and continuously scrape the purple moss away from the hinges, the copper gutters, and the wooden window-latches. Their fingers are permanently stained an oxidized, copper-green that no amount of tallow can remove. They are the fast-moving spiders of the town, sliding down the ropes with their spuds, singing low, wordless hums to keep the chimney-ravens from stealing their lunch-bags.
*   **The Cable-Turners:** Huge, one-shouldered men and women who tend the "creakers." They are the ones who understand gravity. They do not speak; they have been deafened by the constant *thrum-click* of the iron-bound axles. They communicate entirely by foot-taps and the smell of the grease on the pulleys. If a cable-wheel is running hot, a Turner can smell the scorching mutton-fat from fifty paces and will douse it with a bucket of wet moss-water before the cable can snap.

### The Return to the Sump

In a place where there is no soil, death is handled with the same hard utility as life. There are no graves in Saint-under-Sough; one cannot dig into three-hundred-million-year-old basalt with a spade.

When a Sougher dies, they are not dropped into the void—the wind is too unpredictable, and the elders say that a body dropped into the lower clouds will only find its way back up in the next spring's mist, clinging to the underside of the walkways as a gray, rotted shape.

Instead, the body is stripped of its copper wire and leather boots, wrapped in grease-saturated wool sacking, and weighted with lead slag. It is then lowered into the *Boiler-Sump*—the deep, lightless lime-cistern that sits directly beneath the fire-boxes of the third tier. Here, in the perpetually boiling, sulfurous water, the dead are rendered down over the course of three weeks. 

The fat that rises to the top of the sump is skimmed off by the Boiler-Keepers. It is refined, mixed with low-grade fish oil, and used to lubricate the great cable-cars that carry the living up and down the spires. 

To the Soughers, this is the highest form of respect. A child riding the creaker to the fourth tier does not feel fear; they feel the vibration of the wire and know that the smooth, greasy run of the pulley is being made easy by the bones of their grandfathers. It is a closed loop, tight and heavy, where nothing—not even the breath of a dead man—is allowed to escape the cleft.
