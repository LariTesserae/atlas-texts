The flat, gray light from the overhead wells reveals a world that is not merely inhabited, but occupied to the very limits of its volume. Here, life does not sprout or bloom; it is ground out of the stone and the turn of the iron, dry-molded and self-assembling. 

The dominant keepers of the conduit are the gray, ceramic shield-creatures—the **Scutes**. 

To look closely at a single Scute is to see a thing made of bisque-fired clay and powdered lead. They have no organs as soft-bodied things understand them. If one is split open by the weight of its neighbors, it does not leak grease or fluid, but a fine, dark meal of bismuth and antimony. At their center lies their only "soft" part: a small, dark pellet of magnetite, suspended in a pocket of dry, fibrous hair. This pellet is their compass and their heart. It vibrates in sympathy with the deep, 50-cycle hum of the generator-vault below, keeping them in a state of perpetual, low-voltage agitation.

The Scutes have no legs in the traditional sense. Beneath their hard, rimmed margins are dozens of small, overlapping scales made of vulcanized rubber-stone. By rippling these scales in rhythmic waves, they slide across the graphite-slick basalt like heavy, self-propelled tiles. Because the floor is so slick, they cannot climb well individually; instead, they move by compaction. They stack and wedge themselves together, using the mass of those behind them to shove the front-runners up the slopes of the stone channels.

Their lifecycle is a closed, sterile loop. They do not mate. Instead, when a Scute reaches the end of its friction-life—when its bottom scales are worn flat and smooth as glass by the basalt—its magnetic heart loses its charge. It halts. Within minutes, the ceaseless tide of its brothers climbs over it, their hard mandibles shaving its ceramic shell down into fine, gray grit. 

From this accumulated grit, new life is pressed. In the deep, stagnant corners of the trench where the wind of the machinery does not reach, the graphite dust and the ground-up powder of the dead pack into dense, static drifts. The sheer weight of the crawling swarm above compresses these drifts, while the stray electrical currents from the iron beams weld the particles together. Tiny, flat wafers form in the dark of the silt. Over decades, polished by the sliding bellies of the adults, these wafers harden, acquire a static charge from the dust-friction, and begin to twitch. They emerge from the drifts already hard, already hungry, and immediately join the slide.

Wading through this gray carpet are the **Pestles**—the pale, chalk-white giants of the trench. 

A Pestle is nearly the size of a roofing tile, supported on six thin, needle-like stilts of calcified bone. Because they are light and their legs are so thin, they do not sink into the shifting mass of Scutes; instead, they walk *through* them like waders through loose shingles. They have no eyes and no antennae. Their heads are blunt, downward-pointing cones of heavy, yellowed lime. 

The Pestles do not hunt by sight or scent, but by the resonance of the floor. When the brass rods overhead drop a flake of copper, the sudden, frantic pile-up of Scutes creates a localized, high-frequency clattering. The Pestle detects this through its needle-legs. It stalks toward the sound, its long limbs clicking like knitting needles against the basalt. 

Once over the pile, the Pestle does not bite. It simply drops its heavy, cone-shaped head with the force of a small hammer, crushing three or four Scutes instantly into powder. It then lowers a soft, gray, bellows-like tongue from its underside. This tongue, moist only with a tacky, paraffin-like grease, is pressed into the dust, picking up the rich, mineral meal of the crushed shells and the copper flake before rising back into the Pestle's dry gut.

Higher up, where the copper drive-rods turn in their iron brackets, lives a different class of inhabitant: the **Verdigris-Lice**.

These are tiny, stationary lumps, looking like nothing more than bubbles of green corrosion on the brass. They are, however, alive. They are ancient, slow-breathing mollusks of metal. They have no shells of their own; instead, they secrete a hard crust of copper carbonate around their soft, sulfurous bodies, cementing themselves directly to the rotating rods. 

The Verdigris-Lice live on the friction-heat of the machinery. As the rods turn, the friction at the iron brackets generates a faint, constant warmth. The Lice drink this heat through their flat, copper-plated bellies. To feed, they extend thin, feathery fans of black iron wire into the air, catching the dry graphite dust kicked up by the Scutes below. When the dust touches the wire-fans, a tiny static charge pulls it down into the Louse’s mouth-pore. 

They are the only things in the trench that make a sound of their own: when the rods slow down or change speed, the Lice emit a high-pitched, metallic hiss, like cold water hitting a hot stove, as they adjust their grip on the brass.

Finally, in the deep crevices where the ancient cast-iron beams join the basalt bedrock, lie the **Tallow-Slabs**.

The Tallow-Slabs are the source of the cold lard smell that fills the dry air of the conduit. They look like thick, irregular sheets of dirty yellow wax, sometimes three feet across, wedged into the cold gaps between the metal and the stone. They do not move. They have no limbs, no mouths, and no apparent sensory organs. They are colonial masses of a specialized grease-fungee.

The Slabs feed on the very age of the vault. They draw their sustenance from the slow, chemical decay of the iron beams above them—eating the microscopic flakes of rust and decomposing zinc that fall from the ancient brackets, and combining them with the trace hydrocarbons carried on the attic-cool drafts. 

As they digest the metal, they slowly exude a thick, heavy, semi-solid fat. This fat does not run; it is too cold. Instead, it accumulates on their surface like the rind of an old cheese. This cold grease is the grease of life for the conduit. The Scutes slide against the edges of the Tallow-Slabs, coating their bottom scales in the wax to ease their journey over the basalt. The Pestles use it to seal their joints against the grinding slate-dust. 

Without the Slabs, the friction of the trench would eventually grind everything to static, unmoving dust. The Slabs keep the dry world oiled, sacrificing their own waxy bodies to the endless, clicking passage of the ceramic herds.

Beneath the flat, gray light-wells, the cycle continues without pause. A cam drops a shaving of copper; the Scutes pile into a clicking pyramid; a white Pestle stalks over them like a tripod, its heavy head falling with a dull *thump-crack*; the Verdigris-Lice hiss on their turning rods; and the cold, gray dust rises and falls, settling back onto the polished stone to be scraped and trodden once again.
