### The Spire-Dwellers (The Gantry-Born)

The people who inhabit the suspended shelves of the Spire-Cities do not walk with the heel-strike of flat-grounders. To walk normally here is to invite the lazy, heavy hand of the air to pluck you from the mesh. Instead, they have developed the *heel-hook*—a shuffling, sliding glide. Their boots are specialized tools: heavy, double-soled things clad in soft lead at the toes to give them weight, with a curved brass notch in the instep that perfectly matches the thickness of the iron-mesh gantries. 

In the high cold, their skin has taken on the slate-grey pallor of the petrified wood they cling to. They are a lean, long-limbed stock, with fingers that seem slightly too long, scarred by the yellow oil of the sap-heaters and the rough, hair-like fibers of the air-moss they spin. Because the wind is a constant, throat-drying engine, they do not speak in the liquid, vowel-heavy languages of the lost lowlands. Their tongue is a series of low, clicking sibilants and sharp, dry glottals—sounds that can be thrown between brackets of iron without being torn to pieces by the five-mile gale.

To protect their eyes from the grit-laden wind that constantly polishes the Spire walls, they wear "fog-cups"—thick goggles hand-carved from the amber-like resin found in the deep burls of the heartwood. These lenses are kept smeared with a thin film of scutter-beetle grease, which traps the flying charcoal-dust before it can blind them. 

Inside their birch-veneer homes, there are no chairs. To sit is to waste space in a dwelling that must be bolted to a vertical cliff. Instead, they sleep and rest in heavy, grease-treated canvas hammocks suspended from the ceiling-beams by iron turnbuckles. A family’s wealth is measured not by land, but by the number of iron spikes they have driven into the ancient heartwood to anchor their floors, and the quality of their "drip-lines"—the copper wires that provide their daily cup of bitter, condensation-harvested water.

### The Slack-Men

Among the Spire-dwellers, the Slack-Men are a distinct and insular caste. They are the ones who manage the transition between the dead, petrified iron-wood and the living, creeping growth of the Spire-tips. They live in the "under-belly"—the damp, dark timber-spaces beneath the city platforms where the wind is loudest and the shadow of the Spire is never broken.

The Slack-Men do not wear wool or woven air-moss; they dress in garments of raw, boiled beetle-hide, thick enough to deflect the sparks of a snapping cable and entirely waterproof. They oil their entire bodies with *beetle-tallow*, giving them a rancid, vinegar-sweet smell that distinguishes them even in the crowded market-gantries. 

Their job is one of constant, rhythmic measurement and adjustment. They communicate with one another across the three-mile spans not by voice, but by striking the great iron cables with heavy copper-tipped clubs—a language of metallic pings and deep, bell-like thrums that tell them which way the tower is leaning, which bolts are buckling under the slow, ten-year inch of the wood’s growth, and where the tension must be let out.

Because they spend their lives in the harness, suspended over the three-thousand-foot drop, their legs are often withered and thin, while their shoulders and hands are abnormally broad. They have a saying: *"The wood never sleeps, and the iron never forgets."*

### The Ground-Gliders (The Peat-Harpooners)

Down on the charcoal-grey plateau, five thousand feet below the hanging cities, lives a different breed of human: those who have been "grounded." These are the miners, the peat-cutters, and the salvage-hunters who brave the vast, cold emptiness of the lichen-plains.

Unlike the Spire-dwellers, who are light and quick, the Ground-Gliders are squat, dense, and carry immense weight. To survive on the horizontal plain—where the lazy gravity makes it easy for a sudden gust of wind to lift a man and roll him five miles across the hard wood-crust like a dry leaf—they wear "anchor-harnesses." These are heavy leather belts connected to iron-shod sledges that they drag behind them. The sledges are laden with blocks of mined peat, acting as deep-sea sinkers to keep their boots pressed firmly to the ground.

They do not walk; they "plow." They use long, iron-tipped poles to shove themselves along the hard wood-crust, navigating the five-mile stretches between the towers in small, sail-rigged land-boats. These boats have runners made of polished fossil-lignin and are steered by massive brass tillers. 

The Ground-Gliders are quiet, superstitious people. They will not look up at the cities suspended above them; they believe that to look into the sky is to invite the lazy gravity to "loosen" its grip on your boots. When they must sleep, they do not hang in hammocks; they crawl into shallow trenches they have cut into the hard peat-crust, covering themselves with heavy, oil-soaked lichen-felts that smell of grease and vinegar.

### The Drift-Lace and the Void-Weavers

In the five-mile voids between the Spires, where there is only blue-grey air and drifting mist, lives the *Drift-Lace*. 

These are not plants, nor are they animals in any traditional sense. They are colonial organisms that look like immense, semi-transparent sheets of grey lace, some as small as a hand, others wide enough to drape over an entire city block. They have no weight; their tissues are filled with a gas that is slightly lighter than the cold air of the plateau, allowing them to float indefinitely in the horizontal thermals.

The Drift-Lace survives by catching the water-fog and the yellow grease-steam vented by the Spire-cities. Their bodies are covered in millions of microscopic copper-colored cilia that move in slow, rhythmic waves, straining the soot and coal-dust from the air. When a cloud of steam passes through a colony of Drift-Lace, the organism glows with a faint, phosphor-green phosphorescence—a cold, chemical light that the city-dwellers use to navigate the gantries during the long, misty nights.

Living among the Drift-Lace are the *Void-Weavers*—blind, long-legged arachnids with shells of grey scale that match the lichen of the plains. They are the size of a dog, but their hollow, straw-like legs make them weigh almost nothing. They weave no webs; instead, they spin single, mile-long threads of incredibly tough, sticky silk that they cast into the wind. When the thread catches on a passing Drift-Lace or the ironwork of an adjacent Spire, the spider uses its legs to pull itself through the open air, "sailing" the gap with a jerky, slow-motion grace that looks like swimming in grease.

The city-dwellers hunt the Void-Weavers for their silk, which is the only material strong and light enough to be used as guide-cables for the small, hand-cranked cable cars that run between the towers.

### The Burl-Mites and the Wood-Eaters

Deep within the hollow, dark interiors of the three-mile-wide Spires, where the air is warm with the internal heat of the earth and the smell of ancient wintergreen resin is thick enough to choke a man, lives the *Burl-Mite*.

These are blind, soft-bodied organisms the size of a sheep, with skin like wet parchment. They have no limbs; instead, they have six pairs of heavy, circular rasping-discs around their mouths. They spend their entire lives burrowing through the fossilized rings of the heartwood, moving at a speed of perhaps three feet a year. 

As they tunnel, they secrete a thick, black oil that prevents the dry, cold air of the exterior from rotting the tunnels behind them. This oil, known to the Spire-dwellers as "Burl-Tar," is highly valued. It is harvested by daring "Interiorists" who climb down into the pitch-black hollows of the Spires with brass lanterns. The Interiorists must be careful: the Burl-Mites are slow, but their rasping-discs can cut through iron bolts if they are left in one place too long, and their bodies are filled with a pressurized, volatile grease that will explode into yellow flame if exposed to an open spark.

### The Smear-Folk

Because of the stubborn light of the high plateau, which leaves shadows that cling to the walls long after the object has moved, the older inhabitants of the Spire-Cities undergo a strange, aesthetic transformation.

Those who work the same posts for decades—such as the boiler-tenders, the gantry-gatekeepers, and the water-cistern watchmen—develop what is known as "The Wash." Over forty or fifty years of standing in the same light-cast, their own flesh begins to absorb the slow shadow-lag of the place. Their skin, once pink or grey, becomes smudged and indistinct at the edges. 

An old watchman sitting in his iron chair does not look like a solid man; he looks like a pencil-sketch that has been rubbed with a wet thumb. When he rises to check his gauges, a soft, grey "after-image" remains in his chair for nearly ten minutes, sitting with its head bowed, fading only when the cold wind grows strong enough to scour the grease-light from the ironwork.

These "Smear-folk" are highly respected, but they are also feared. It is said that when a Smear-man dies, he does not leave a corpse that can be hung from the Dead-Gantry. Instead, his body simply grows thinner and less distinct over several days, until one morning his family finds only his woollen work-coat and a dark, grey stain on the birch wall that smells of wintergreen and coal-gas, and which nothing—not even the strongest vinegar-muck—can ever scrub away.
