To understand the inhabitants of this pocket-world, you must first discard the idea of individual lives. 

Nothing here exists in isolation; there is no solitary explorer, no citizen who does not bear the literal weight of another’s survival on its back. The dwellers of this freezing hollow are part of a slow, cold-locked machine—a confederacy of distinct species so tightly bound by necessity that they function as a single, sprawling entity. Some call them the *Thrum-workers*, though they have no name for themselves but the shivering of the grass-blade bridges.

The most numerous are the **Glass-Mites**, the *Vitreous Craftsmen*. Close up, they do not look like insects so much as tiny, animated fragments of salt. Their bodies are completely transparent, save for a central, thread-like gut of dark-brown peat-paste and two tiny, black-pigmented pressure-sensors where eyes should be. They have no red blood; their limbs are moved by a clear, syrupy hemolymph that is rich in natural glycols—a biological antifreeze that keeps them limber at temperatures that would turn other micro-fauna into brittle needles. 

The Glass-Mites are the weavers. They move with a dry, mechanical *clack-slip-clack*, their eight needle-sharp legs clicking against the moss-pith. From glands near their spinnerets, they secrete a specialized, tallow-thick silk. This silk does not harden through drying—the air is too humid for that—but through *chilling*. It is a substance that only gains its steel-like tensile strength when it drops below two degrees Celsius. If a warm hand were to touch a suspension bridge, the structure would instantly liquefy, collapsing into a greasy smear of fat. 

Cooperating with them in perfect, silent synchronicity are the **Ivory Ants**, the *Silt-Menders*. These are the giants of the chamber—nearly two millimeters long—though they are entirely blind. Their bodies are the color of old lard, soft and slightly damp, covered in microscopic, water-repellent hairs that keep them from drowning when a condensation drop breaks over them. 

The Ivory Ants are the engineers of weight and leverage. While the mites spin the silk, the ants drag the heavy materials: the pine-needle beams, the slate-flakes for the terraced streets, and the dried seed-cases of bog-pimpernels which they use as communal cisterns. They work in chains of ten or twenty, their jaw-parts locked together, moving with a rhythmic, swaying gait. They communicate through *stridulation*—not a sound that travels through the air, but a scratching of their abdominal plates against the grass-blades. To the inhabitants, the town is a massive stringed instrument. A vibration at the eastern wall is not noise; it is an announcement that a pine-needle has slipped, or that a cistern is full.

Below them, navigating the vertical walls with agonizing, geological slowness, are the **Velvet Snails**. Their shells are not lime-white but a dark, resinous brown, spiraled like the horns of rams and no larger than mustard seeds. Their skin is a deep, plush black that seems to absorb what little blue light the ceiling-mold provides. 

The snails are the pathfinders and the lamp-bearers. They carry upon their backs small, thriving colonies of the pale-blue frost-mold, which feeds on the mucus the snails secrete. Wherever a snail goes, it leaves a faint, luminescent track and a trail of hardened slime that acts as a highway for the mites. Without the snails, the cities would drift into darkness; they are the mobile lampposts of the terraced streets, parking themselves for days at a time at busy bridge-junctions, glowing silently as the traffic of mites and ants crawls over and around their shells.

But there is a heart to this city, and it is not mobile. 

Deep within the rubbery floor of the chamber, half-submerged in the largest pool of black water, lies the **Silt-Marrow**. It is a bulbous, tuberous growth—neither plant nor animal, but a symbiotic fungus-gall that has grown into the very flesh of the tussock. It is the size of a wild cranberry, dull orange and wrinkled like a knuckle. 

The Silt-Marrow is the anchor of the colony's consciousness. It slowly pulses—perhaps once every ten minutes—a movement so subtle it barely ripples the water around it. This pulse is a physical pressure wave that travels through the rootlets of the ceiling and the suspension lines of the towers. It is the metronome of the pocket-world. In rhythm with this pulse, the ants take their steps, the mites pull their silk, and the snails turn their heavy shells. It is a slow, cold dream of survival, whispered through the peat.

If you watch the city long enough, you realize there is no waste here. The dead of the Ivory Ants are not buried; they are stripped of their soft parts by the mites, and their hollow, ivory exoskeletons are used as structural joints—cornerstones for the hanging wicker towers, or vessels for storing the precious, blue-glowing mold-spores. Even the predatory **Thread-Legged Huntsmen**—the long, spindly bugs that wait in the cold shadows of the root-stalks—are part of the order. They do not hunt with the frantic hunger of warm-blooded things. They move like branches in a draft, their front limbs raised like scythes. They strike only the slow, the old, or those workers who have begun to "stiffen"—individuals whose antifreeze hemolymph has begun to fail, turning them to useless glass. The hunters are the pruners of the garden, keeping the gears of the city free of dead weight.

This is a world that has made peace with the absolute minimum. Its inhabitants do not seek warmth; warmth is the end of their world. Warmth is the rot of their silk, the melting of their slate-streets, the evaporation of their cisterns, and the death of their glowing mold. 

To them, your breath—drifting through the fissure in the tussock—is a terrifying, suffocating summer. As the white steam curls around the hanging towers, the city goes utterly, breathlessly still. The tiny, glass-bodied mites cling to their bridges; the ivory ants lock their jaws into the pine-needle rungs; the velvet snails withdraw into their horn-dark shells, dousing their blue lamps. 

They wait, suspended in the dark, shivering slightly as the alien heat passes over them, praying in their silent, vibrating language for the return of the saving, merciful frost.
