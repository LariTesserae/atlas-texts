To live on the High Loom is to be an extension of its circuitry. Nothing here exists in isolation; every creature is a node, a ground-wire, a valve, or a spark-gap in a colossal, self-regulating engine of green and copper.

### The Ground-Dwellers: Collectors of the Spark

At the lowest level, deep within the four-mile mattress of olive-black moss, life is slow, wet, and heavy. 

Here dwell the **Plumbeons**, or *lead-strands*. They are not worms in the biological sense, but miles-long, tendon-thick cables of white, rubbery fat and zinc-laced nerves that thread through the compressed peat. They have no heads, no eyes, and no limbs. Instead, they are the sensory web of the plateau. They are tuned entirely to vibration and voltage. When a Spire’s valve slams three miles away, the shockwave ripples through the water-logged peat, and the Plumbeons twitch in unison, a silent, subterranean string section responding to the *clank-thud* of the towers. They spend their centuries-long lives slowly digesting the petrified moss-fibers of the deep strata, chemical-burning the ancient peat to produce a slick, oily tallow that lubricating-beetles harvest from the surface vents.

Above them, on the freezing rime-crust, run the **Tread-Beetles**. Close up, these dinner-plate-sized creatures resemble clockwork toys made of dark, tarnished brass and green tortoise-shell. They have no legs; instead, their undersides are fitted with rows of tiny, interlocking zinc rollers that spin on organic axles, driving them forward over the slippery frost with a dry, zipping hiss like a zipper being drawn across canvas. 

The Tread-Beetles are the cleaners of the lightning. When a Spire discharges its blinding arc into the plain, these creatures do not flee; they race toward the flash. They gather around the glass-fused craters of molten peat, their long, copper-plated antennae twitching in the ionized air. They plunge their needle-like snouts into the cooling, vitrified slag, drinking the super-heated, mineral-rich brine that wells up from the wound. As they feed, their bodies act as organic capacitors. They store the static charge in specialized, multi-layered shells that expand and contract like bellows. A fully charged Tread-Beetle glows with a dim, internal yellow light, its roller-tracks emitting tiny, green sparks as it zips back toward the absolute dark of the expanses.

### The Drifters of the Middle Air

Sixty feet above the moss, in the zone where the warm steam of the Spires meets the freezing iron-slate air, lives a population that has forgotten the concept of dry land.

These are the **Loom-Orbs**, or *glass-bellies*. They look like translucent, blue-veined spheres of tough fluid, ranging from the size of a walnut to the span of a carriage. They are not solid, nor are they mere water; they are living, membrane-bounded jellies whose internal salt-density is perfectly balanced against the micro-gravitational warps of the Spires' electrical fields. They drift, weightless, in the cold air.

Inside these floating globes, swimming through the lukewarm, nutrient-rich water of their own bodies, are the **Spindle-Fry**. They are tiny, needle-thin ribbon-fish with skeletons made of flexible, crystalline silica. They have no eyes—there is nothing to see in the gray twilight but the blue flash of St. Elmo’s fire—but they possess delicate, feathery gills that extract both oxygen and dissolved Spire-ash from the water. 

The life of a Spindle-Fry is a series of aerial shipwrecks. When two floating water-globes drift into one another, they do not bounce; they merge with a heavy, wet *shlump*. The Spindle-Fry of both globes instantly mingle, engaging in a silent, frantic flurry of mating that lasts only minutes. If a globe drifts too close to a Spire’s intake-root, it is drawn in by the suction, and the fry are swept down into the boiling, mechanical heart of the tower to be sterilized and reconstituted as sap. 

But if a globe loses its charge and begins to fall, dropping toward the freezing peat like a heavy, liquid tear, the Spindle-Fry undergo a desperate metamorphosis. They secrete a thick, waxy grease that hardens around their bodies, turning them into tiny, aerodynamic darts. When the globe shatters on the cold moss, these darts pierce the crust, burying themselves in the wet peat where they remain dormant, waiting for the next lightning strike to boil the ground, release them, and lift them back into the sky on plumes of hot steam.

### The Parasites of the Steam and Valve

The Spires themselves are not merely trees; they are cities for creatures that feed on pressure, heat, and speed.

Deep inside the 300-foot hollow trunks, where the giant, three-story cellulose valves slam shut every twelve seconds, lives the most specialized organism of the High Loom: the **Piston-Louse**. These are flat, leathery creatures the size of sheepdogs, with shells made of overlapping plates of iron-hard lignin. They possess twelve short, powerful legs ending in curved, copper claws that they hammer deep into the Spire’s inner walls. 

The Piston-Lice live in the "dead zones" just behind the valve-hinges, where the turbulent, scalding sap rushes past. Every time the valve slams (*thunk-shudder*), the Piston-Lice are subjected to forces that would crush a conventional animal to paste. They survive by flexing their segmented shells, using the immense hydraulic pressure of the sap-flow to pump oxygen through their own respiratory chambers. They are the maintenance crew of the Spires; they scrape the mineral scale and calcium deposits from the valve-seats, keeping the seals watertight. Without them, the Spires’ pumps would fail within weeks, clogging with lime and salt.

High above, near the whistling steam-vents at the summit, nest the **Ozone-Skimmers**. These are the leather-winged, blind fliers that fill the twilight with their tin-horn cries. Their wings are not made of feathers or skin, but of a dry, parchment-like bark that is impervious to the scalding water-vapor. 

They do not fly by flapping; they are gliders, launching themselves from the steam-plumes and riding the thermal updrafts generated by the Spires' metabolic heat. They navigate by sensing the magnetic lines of the plateau, their long, copper-wire antennae vibrating like tuning forks as they approach the high-voltage zones. They feed on the sulfur-bearing spores released by the boiling tubers of the plain, skimming the surface of the peat for a fraction of a second before soaring back into the safe, warm shelter of the steam-clouds.

### The Great Girders: The Walking Knolls

Every few weeks, the flat monotony of the horizon is broken by the slow, monumental passage of the **Silt-Wethers**.

These are the giants of the High Loom. A mature Silt-Wether is the size of a three-story warehouse, looking less like an animal and more like a low hill of moss and rotted wood that has taken to its feet. They are six-legged, moving on thick, stump-like pillars of compressed peat-husk and lignin plates. 

The Silt-Wethers have no heads, no eyes, and no mouths. They feed through a massive, undulating skirt of orange, fibrous cilia that trails beneath them, dredging the moss like a combine harvester. They creep across the plateau at a speed of perhaps half a mile a day, leaving a wide, dark, rototilled trench in the rime-frost behind them. 

The Silt-Wethers are the living grounding-rods of the plateau. Because of their immense bulk and high concentration of copper-bearing fluids, they are prime targets for the Spires' lightning arcs. A single Silt-Wether may be struck thirty or forty times a day. When the lightning hits, the beast does not scream—it has no voice—but it stops walking. It shudders. Its entire mossy flank-coat glows with a beautiful, incandescent green-gold light, and steam rises from its joints in a hissing shroud. 

The electricity does not kill the Silt-Wether; it is its lifeblood. The current drives their slow, electrochemical metabolism, cracking the complex carbon sugars they dredge from the peat into simple, digestible starches. The heat of the strike also bakes the thousands of seeds embedded in the creature's back. These seeds—which can only germinate after being subjected to both five thousand degrees of electrical heat and the freezing cold of the rime-frost—fall into the warm, muddy trench behind the walking giant, where they immediately begin to sprout into the orange, boiling tubers that feed the next generation of Tread-Beetles.

### The Static Weaver

When a Spire dies—either through an explosion of trapped gas or because the deep taproots have exhausted the local copper deposit—the landscape does not leave its carcass to rot. 

Before the shrapnel of the explosion has even cooled, the **Loom-Weavers** arrive. These are not individual animals, but a gray, mold-like colonial organism that grows in shimmering, felt-like sheets over the ruins. They work with a silent, terrifying efficiency. 

The Loom-Weavers do not eat the wood for sustenance. Instead, they disassemble it. They pull the copper and zinc veins out of the shattered cellulose ribs, spinning them into neat, tight coils that look like the copper windings of an electric motor. They clear the clogged intake-wells, digging out the calcified peat and lining the pit with a smooth, waterproof layer of black, waxy resin. 

Within forty-eight hours, the site of the dead Spire is cleared. The Loom-Weavers have laid the "foundations" for the next project: a perfectly circular, copper-threaded nest in the wet moss, ready to receive the three new saplings that will sprout from the ruins. 

Thus, the High Loom continues. It is a world that has discarded the soft, the delicate, and the quiet. Its inhabitants are made of copper, sap, leather, and steam, and they live in service to a grand, unthinking, perpetual design—a cold machine that sings its clattering, whistling song to an empty sky, forever rebuilding its own steel-hard heart.
