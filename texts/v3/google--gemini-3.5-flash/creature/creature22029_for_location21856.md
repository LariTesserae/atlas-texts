In the deep, velvety twilight near the central stone shelf, where the pale-orange glow of the selenite veins cannot quite reach, there is a shape that at first appears to be merely an extension of the parched bone-stone. It is a mound of pale, overlapping plates, the color of curdled milk and dry river-silt. 

This is the **Sifter**.

It does not have a front or a back in the manner of beasts that run, nor does it possess the wet, pulsing symmetry of things that live in the light. Instead, it is built like a low, multi-layered cabinet of dry, organic shale. Its outer casing is composed of broad, curved shingles of hardened starch-matter—layers of the same fibrous material that forms the starch-ferns, but packed so densely by some ancient, internal pressure that it has the ring and weight of heavy porcelain.

#### The Body of the Sifter
If you were to touch the Sifter, you would feel no heat of blood, only the dry, radiating warmth of the deep earth itself, which its body absorbs and stores like a brick in a hearth. It is entirely waterless. Inside its hard, shingled carapace, there are no veins, no lungs, and no throat. In place of muscles, it moves by the contraction and release of dry, braided cords of plant-fiber—the very same three-strand twine that wraps the handle of the bone spatula beside the hearth. These fibers are anchored to pins of polished selenite; when the electrostatic charge in the silt rises, the fibers stiffen and draw together, pulling the heavy plates of its body forward with a slow, dry *creak-shuck* that sounds like a heavy book being closed in an empty room.

The Sifter has no face. Instead, its upper dome is rimmed with a dense fringe of pale, feathery antennae made of native copper and white mica. These filaments do not see, but they are incredibly sensitive to the electrostatic currents that ripple through the flour-like dust of the floor. By monitoring the tiny sparks that leap between the silt-grains, the Sifter "sees" the room as a map of electrical tension. It knows the exact position of every starch-fern, the tiny, hot points of light where the clackers are crawling, and the cool, dead spot where the bone spoon lies.

#### The Work at the Altar
The Sifter is the author of the small shale hearth and the keeper of the bone spatula. 

Its work is slow—measured not in hours or seasons, but in the long, unchanging intervals of the deep earth. Every few decades, when a clacker’s joints finally lock and it falls from the starch-ferns to the silt floor, the Sifter will stir. It moves with a deliberate, jointed precision, its three-pronged hands—which are nothing more than bundles of dry, pointed reed-stalks tipped with insect-claw—reaching down to lift the dead insect.

Using the bone spatula, the Sifter carries the hollow amber shell to the shallow depression in the rock. Here, with a pestle made of a heavy, water-polished pebble of black ironstone (kept hidden beneath its shingles), it grinds the dry chitin and the salt-pellets within into a fine, glittering meal. 

This meal is then mixed with the ocher powder and a few drops of the dark, horn-hard insect-wax, which the Sifter warm-softens using the heat of its own shell. Kneading the paste with its fibrous digits, it fashions small, flat tiles to repair the hearth, or molds thin, new shingles to paste onto its own drying back when the old ones fracture from the stress of movement.

#### The White-Mica Lizard
The lizard-like creature covered in white mica scales is the Sifter’s only companion, though "companion" is too wet and warm a word for their relationship. The lizard resides in the hollow chambers beneath the Sifter’s lower shingles. It is a creature of pure friction; it has no tongue, no wet eyes, and no stomach. It feeds on the tiny, starch-rich spores that flake off the starch-ferns when the clackers jar them, gathering them on its rough, tongue-less jaws. 

As the lizard slips in and out of the Sifter’s plates, its mica scales make a dry, whispering rustle—a sound like dry winter leaves sliding across ice. The Sifter does not acknowledge the lizard, but the creature's constant movement keeps the Sifter’s joint-axles clear of the fine, choking silt-dust that would otherwise settle and lock them in place forever.

#### The Nature of Its Mind
The Sifter does not think in thoughts that have a beginning, a middle, and an end. Having no water in its brain-matter—which is a dry, labyrinthine sponge of calcified grain—it does not experience the flow of time. It has no memory of a "before," nor any concept of an "after." 

To the Sifter, the room has always been exactly as it is now. The footprint left in the silt five centuries ago is not a relic of the past; it is a permanent feature of the present, a shape that the electrostatic fields of the floor must curve around. 

When the electrostatic charge of the room drops below a certain threshold—which happens once every few centuries when the great calcium plains above shift and settle—the Sifter simply stops. Its fibers slacken. The feathery mica sensors on its head droop and rest against the silt. The lizard curls deep within the shingled carapace, its white scales turning cold. 

During these long pauses, which can last for a thousand years, the Sifter is indistinguishable from the central wedge of dry stone. It does not sleep, and it does not age. It merely waits for the earth to tighten its jaw, for the currents to return to the silt, and for the dry, deafening rattle of the clackers to call it back to its slow, quiet maintenance of the dust.
