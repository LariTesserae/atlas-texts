If you were to slide your hand through the weeping curtains of moss and let your fingers drift down to the miniature shale terraces, you would not find them empty. 

At first, your fingertips would register only what felt like more of the wet, felted turf—loose clumps of organic matter, perhaps, or rounded nodules of basalt. But if you held your hand still against the cold, heavy water, waiting until the skin-numbing chill gave way to a dull ache, you would begin to discern a difference in weight and resistance. 

Resting in the niches of the green shale stairs, half-buried in the pale, thread-like algae, are the builders.

To look at one is to look at something that occupies the borderland between a liquid and a stone. They are small—none larger than a goose egg—and they possess the dull, greasy sheen of wet graphite. They have no faces, no limbs, no eyes, and no gills. In the ghostly violet light that clings to the sharp basalt edges, they look like heavy, dark drops of mercury that have somehow been frozen, yet remain soft to the touch. 

Their skin is an extraordinary thing: it is thick, hairless, and cold, with the texture of a thoroughly soaked chamois or a turnip that has spent the winter in frozen mud. It is entirely immune to the razor-sharp fracture-points of the basalt. Where the stone would slice human skin to the bone, these creatures slide with a slow, lubricating ease, their bodies secreting a heavy, mucus-like oil that never mixes with the water, but hangs around them in a faint, insulating shroud.

If you should try to lift one, the first thing that would strike you is its impossible weight. A creature the size of a walnut weighs as much as an iron bolt. They are built for the immense hydrostatic pressure of the deep sinkhole; their interiors are not hollowed by lungs or blood vessels, but are composed of an incredibly dense, gelatinous marble of mineral-saturated proteins. If they were brought to the surface, they would not explode, but they would lose their tension, sagging into a shapeless, oily gray puddle as the atmosphere failed to hold them together. Here, in the dark, they are as solid and turgid as ripe plums.

They do not swim. Indeed, they do not "travel" in any sense that a land-dwelling creature would recognize. They move by *welling*. When one wishes to ascend a shale step, it does not crawl; it simply allows its lower half to liquefy slightly, flowing forward like grease over wood, while its upper half remains anchored. 

It is these creatures that gathered the green shale. The stone is foreign to this basalt pocket, belonging to a stratum hundreds of feet above, where the sinkhole meets the upper world. Over centuries, these slow, heavy things have labored to bring the tiles down. They do not have hands, but they have a single, deep, muscular fold running along the underside of their bodies—a clean, horizontal cleft that can clamp onto a hand-sized shale block with a hydraulic force so absolute that the stone becomes part of them. 

They did not build the terraces for shelter. They built them as an ear. 

The green shale is softer than the basalt, less crystalline, and it vibrates at a different pitch when the earth groans. When those low, planetary rumbles travel through the stone shelf, the green shale stairs act as resonators, transforming the dull, tooth-rattling thrum into a physical harmony. The creatures line the steps like silent, dark cushions, pressing their flat bellies against the green stone. The vibrations pass directly into their dense, gelatinous interiors, shaking the iron and sulfur salts in their flesh. This is how they "hear." It is also how they think, and perhaps how they feed. The seismic groaning of the earth is their sun; it is the energy that stirs their cold, slow blood and keeps the ice from forming inside their skins.

In the warped perspective of the pocket, where the tiny moss stems look like ancient banyans and the distances stretch into impossible depths, these creatures are the giants of the forest. To them, a journey from the bottom step of the shale ruins to the top is an epochal pilgrimage. A individual may sit upon a single shale tile for thirty years, its body slowly secreting the carbonate cement that pins the stone in place, perfectly content to do nothing but listen to the slow, heavy breathing of the tectonic plates below.

They do not mate, nor do they lay eggs. Their reproduction is as geological as their environment. Every few decades, when the mineral saturation of the water reaches a certain density, one of the graphite-colored nodules will begin to crease down its center. The process of division is so slow that the creeping white liverworts have time to grow across the seam before it is finished. 

For half a century, the two halves remain joined, sharing the same vibrations, tasting the same sulfur currents, until a particularly strong groan from the basalt shelf finally shears them apart. They do not drift away from one another. They simply settle side by side on their green stone step, two dark, silent weights in the crowded moss, waiting for the next vibration to pass through the floor of the world.
