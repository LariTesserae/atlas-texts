Those who occupy the tenements of the Silt-Close do not call themselves by any name, for they have no language that requires a name for the self. To the forgotten world above, they might be called the tenants, the grease-keepers, or simply the weight-bearers. 

They are human, but only in the way that an anchor is still an iron bar. Under the three-mile columns of brine, their bodies have grown short, dense, and barrel-ribbed. Their bones are no longer brittle calcium; they have softened into a tough, cartilaginous marrow, like the ribs of sturgeons, allowing their chests to expand and contract with the agonizing, twenty-minute respiration of the Silt-Close’s great accumulator piston. 

To see one emerge from a sixth-story window is to watch a transition of pure, oily grace. They do not open doors; they slide through heavy, silt-weighted flaps of mineral-tanned hide. They do not swim—for the grease-like brine is too thick for kicking—but rather crawl-drift. Their hands and feet are wide, spade-like, and tipped with thick, spade-shaped nails that have grown green and hard from a lifetime of scraping copper-rust from the tenement plates. Using these fingers, they hook themselves into the glass-sponge frills on the cables, pulling their heavy bodies along the copper lines with the slow, hand-over-hand rhythm of sloths in a metal canopy.

Their skin is the color of tallow—a pale, yellowish-white that has never seen a ray of light stronger than the dull orange of a carbon filament. It is covered in a permanent glaze of hydrophobic grease which they secrete from glands beneath their arms and thighs. This grease keeps the freezing, mineral-saturated brine from leaching the salt from their blood. They smell of cold whale-fat and kerosene.

They have eyes, but they are not like ours. They are great, glassy hemispheres, completely white, without pupils or irises, resembling peeled grapes. These globes do not focus; they merely drink. They can detect the low-frequency glow of the carbon lanterns from three yards away, but they are otherwise blind to form. 

Instead, they navigate by a profound, whole-body sense of vibration. Along their temples, jawlines, and the lengths of their collarbones run rows of tiny, pitted pores—lateral lines, like those of deep-sea fish. Through these, they "hear" the Silt-Close. They feel the approach of a neighbor forty yards away by the tiny, oily displacement waves their body pushes through the brine. They know when a valve in the central dome is beginning to pit because the pitch of its twelve-hour rotation shifts by a fraction of a vibration. 

Because they cannot speak—the vocal cords being useless in a medium three hundred times thicker than air—their social life is one of silent, physical resonance. They communicate by pluck. A tenant clinging to a copper cable will strike it with a heavy brass ring worn on the thumb. *Tock. Tock-tock.* The vibration travels along the lead-jacketed conduit, through the tenement walls, and into the soles of another tenant sleeping three buildings away. It is an alphabet of tension, frequency, and weight. 

For more intimate thoughts, they touch. When two meet on a narrow wrought-iron balcony, they do not look at each other. They step close, leaning until their foreheads press against one another's collarbones. In this position, the small, rapid thumping of their hearts can be felt directly through the bone. They transfer heat and rhythm, sharing their warmth in the freezing dark until their temperatures equalize, before releasing each other and drifting back into the gloom.

Their diet is as slow and mineralized as their home. They eat the "marine snow"—the endless, silent drift of organic grey flakes that falls from the surface. Between the tenements, they string vast, sagging hammocks of copper wire. Every few days, a tenant will crawl out onto the wires with a flat iron scraper, clearing the accumulated white silt into clay jars. They mix this cold, biological dust with the sulfurous, white algae that grows near the leaking hot-water pipes, mashing it into a thick, grey paste that they ferment in the warmth of the accumulator’s hydraulic lines. The resulting food is salty, bitter, and rich in lipids; they suck it from narrow copper straws while suspended in their rooms.

They sleep in the upper rooms of the tenements, but they do not use beds. In this dense water, there is no comfort in lying down; instead, they sleep upright, suspended in harness-slings made of old hemp rope and lead weights, tethered to the steam-pipes that run across their ceilings. In these harness-slings, they drift in the slow, dragging drafts of the courtyard, their limbs hanging loose, their white eyes wide and unblinking, rising and falling slightly with the tide-pulses of the seabed.

Beneath them, in the crevices of the iron facades, live their companions. 

There are the "lump-dogs"—not dogs at all, but fat, warty lumpfish that have adapted to the oil-slicked metal. They have massive, disc-shaped suction cups on their bellies with which they anchor themselves to the iron plates near the hot-water leaks. They are completely blind and possess no teeth, only hard, bony ridges with which they scrape the velvet-violet rust-bacteria from the rivets. The tenants keep them as we might keep chickens or goats. The lump-dogs are slow-moving and docile; when a tenant needs oil for a pocket-chronometer or a valve-gasket, they will gently pry a lump-dog from the wall, squeeze its soft, fatty flanks to express a thick, clear lipid from its oil-glands, and place it back on its rust-pasture unharmed.

And inside the steam-pipes, deep within the dark, knocking hollows of the iron tenements, live the "clinkers." These are giant, segmented isopods, as long as a man’s forearm, with shells of overlapping, iron-plated chitin that they have reinforced by consuming the scrapings of the accumulator. They are the garbage-eaters of the Silt-Close, crawling through the drainage pipes and grease-traps to feed on the waste of both machines and tenants. You can hear them moving through the walls at night—a dry, rhythmic *click-shirr, click-shirr* as their dozens of copper-tipped legs grip the interiors of the pipes. The tenants do not hunt them; the clinkers keep the narrow conduit lines from clogging with silt, and their presence is a sign that a pipe is still warm and whole.

Occasionally, a death occurs in the Silt-Close. It is a quiet event. When a tenant’s slow heart finally stops—perhaps after a hundred and fifty years of ticking—their body does not float, nor does it sink. In this grease-dense water, their body is perfectly buoyant. 

Their neighbors do not bury them in the silt, for the silt is shifting and cold. Instead, they wrap the body in heavy, lead-weighted copper wire until it is stiff and dark, like a cocoon. Then, with great care, they haul the bundle to the central dome and lash it to the great accumulator. Over months and years, the rising and falling of the giant piston draws the body into its mechanism. The cold, violet bacteria claims the flesh; the iron-eating crust seals the wrappings; the grease of the piston coats the bones. 

In this way, the dead do not leave the courtyard. Indeed, they never can. They are pressed into the joint-grease and the packing-gaskets, their mineralized remainders becoming part of the silent, heavy machinery that keeps the Silt-Close turning, twenty minutes at a time, beneath the dark and frozen weight of the forgetting sea.
