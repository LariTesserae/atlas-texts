Beneath the heavy, overhanging brim of the limestone shelf, where the air is a warm and motionless weight, life has not departed. It has merely slowed its pulse to match the rate at which the limestone decays. 

To a traveler standing in the twilight of the basin's edge, the cavern would appear dead—a gallery of dry stone and preserved dust. But if one were to remain perfectly still for a week, or a year, the static quality of the chamber would begin to peel away, revealing a strange, dry biology that has adapted not to survive the drought, but to inhabit it as a permanent state of grace.

The true master of this basin does not walk, nor does it have eyes. It lives beneath the grey crust of the dust-flats, occupying the entire fifty-yard bowl like a single, flat sheet of lace. This is the **Lithophyte-Net**, though it has no common name. It is a dry, fibrous mycelium, but it contains no water. Its threads are made of hollow, vitreous silica and calcified salt, woven so tightly through the compacted dust that they act as a grout, holding the floor of the basin in its smooth, unbroken shape. 

The wire-like, ash-grey tufts that rise between the basalt boulders are the "leaves" of this net, though they do not photosynthesize. They are sensory masts. In the absolute, heavy silence of the chamber, these stiff stalks are tuned to the earth. When a micro-grain of salt falls from the ceiling and strikes the dust with that rare, dry *tick*, the vibration travels through the crust. The Net registers this impact. Over the course of several years, the silica threads will slowly, invisibly migrate toward the spot where the grain fell, to slowly dissolve and absorb its trace minerals. To the Net, the fall of a salt grain is a harvest; a tiny event that feeds its geologic hunger for another century.

***

Beside the Net, inhabiting the thermal gaps around the basalt boulders, are **The Sisters**. 

The boulders are not entirely inert. Because they are dense with iron and sit deep within the earth’s crust, they retain a fraction of the deep geothermal warmth of the planet’s mantle. They are always precisely two degrees warmer than the surrounding air. This tiny difference in temperature creates the only "weather" the basin has ever known: a series of miniature, closed-loop thermal currents. Above each boulder, a column of warm air rises, barely a few inches, before cooling, sliding down the smooth, dark basalt, and pooling in the dust at its base.

In these microscopic draft-chimneys live the **Mica-Drifters**. 

They are organisms so small and light they are nearly indistinguishable from the grey dust itself. They are shaped like minute, multi-pointed stars, formed of thin, translucent plates of mica and dry chitin. They do not fly, nor do they crawl; they spend their entire multi-century lifespans suspended in the weak, rising heat of the basalt stones. They rise an inch a decade, then drift back down to the stone's base to begin the loop again. They feed on the invisible, parched organic dust that occasionally drifts down through fissures in the limestone roof—the dried-out pollen of plants that died on the surface during the last ice age, preserved in the cold upper cracks until gravity finally brings it down.

***

But the most delicate life in the basin is found in the graveyard of the tools.

On the flat slab of basalt, the three chert scrapers rest in their timeless row. To the naked eye, they are clean, their glassy black edges sharp and stark against the stone. But if one could lift them—and to do so would destroy an empire—one would find their undersides are the foundation of a tiny, hyper-specialized existence.

When the toolmaker laid these stones down thousands of years ago, they did so with bare hands. Their fingers, warm and damp from labor, left behind microscopic traces of skin-oil, sweat, and salt. In the dry, sterile air of the basin, these lipids did not decompose. They became a fixed, fossilized layer of nourishment.

Living on these hand-prints is a colony of **The Oil-Gleaners**. 

They are creatures the size of a pollen grain, with bodies of pale, amber-colored wax and legs like the fine hairs of a moth. They are blind, and their movements are so slow that a human observer would have to watch for a lifetime to see one turn around. They do not consume the chert; they cultivate it. They graze upon the ancient thumbprints of the toolmaker, eating the molecules of oil so slowly, and with such infinite conservation, that they have not yet finished the first print. 

They live in the micro-shadow under the blade, where the air is a fraction of a millimeter deeper and cooler. To them, the chert tools are not weapons or scrapers; they are a great, dark sky of glass that protects them from the weight of the basin's silence. They are the keepers of the toolmaker's touch, translating the warmth of a hand that died five thousand years ago into a slow, rhythmic dance of survival that will continue until the limestone roof finally collapses, or the earth itself grows cold.
