Those who live here are called the **Sparrows**, though they have no feathers and have never known the sky. 

The name was left behind by the rare, long-dead travelers who managed to climb the nine thousand feet of sheer, white-toothed cliffs to reach the tableland. To those travelers, peering through the violet twilight, the figures huddled in the dry sandstone ruins looked precisely like common house sparrows nesting in winter eaves—small, dusty, and bunched together in tight, shivering groups of three or four to preserve their heat.

But the resemblance is only one of posture.

***

### The Anatomy of the Dried

A Sparrow stands no higher than a human child’s shoulder, but if you were to lift one, it would feel no heavier than a bundle of dry wicker. Their bodies are built for the low gravity and thin air of the terrace; their bones are not marrow-filled but hollow, honeycombed with air-sacs that connect directly to their silent lungs. 

They do not breathe as mammals do. They have no noses, and their mouths are tiny, round apertures that do not open or close, but remain set in a permanent, dry *O*. Instead, they pull the thin air through four thin slits beneath their collarbones. When they run—which they do with a light, bounding gait that looks like a pebble skipping across water—these slits whistle with a sound like a distant flute.

Their skin is the color of the weathered limestone teeth: a pale, dusty grey-green, completely devoid of hair or sweat glands. It is cool to the touch and has the dry, slightly rough texture of high-grade parchment. Underneath this skin, their muscles are not red meat but bundles of silver-grey fiber that resemble the dense wood of the stone-plants. They do not bleed; if cut, the wound does not weep red, but secretes a thick, golden gum that smells of scorched iron and hardens in seconds to seal the tear.

Perhaps their most striking feature is their eyes. They are oversized, set deep in wide, lidless sockets that are protected by a double layer of transparent, amber-colored membrane. These eyes do not see the world in the colors that humans do. To a Sparrow, the ultraviolet violet-sky is a blazing, shimmering white, filled with currents and thermals that they can read like a map. They can see the weak electromagnetic field of a Spindle three miles away, glowing like a pillar of cold blue fire.

***

### The Pebble-Years

The Sparrows do not give birth, nor do they lay eggs in nests. Their life cycle is slow, mineral, and patient, entirely dependent on the Spindles.

Every few decades, when the internal light of the rubbery lichen at the base of a Spindle pulses with a certain green intensity, the Sparrows gather. They do not touch each other. Instead, they stand in the circle of dampness and press their palms to the wet needle of the Spindle. The electrical leakage of the structure catalyst-binds with the golden gum in their palms. 

A few weeks later, they shed "seeds." These are hard, calcareous nodules about the size of a walnuts, grey and pitted like volcanic stone. The Sparrows do not tend them. They simply drop them into the wind-swept gravel lanes between the stone-plants.

These "pebble-children" may roll in the dust for fifty or eighty years, completely indistinguishable from the surrounding scree. They require no food, and the sun cannot dry them further than they already are. But eventually, the wind or a passing Strider-Beetle will nudge a nodule into one of the damp circles surrounding a Spindle.

Once touched by the permanent moisture, the stone begins to soften. Over the course of seven dew-seasons, the nodule absorbs the water, swelling and turning from grey to a translucent, gelatinous green. It begins to unfurl—not like an animal waking, but like a dried fern placed in a bowl of water. First come the long, jointed fingers, which latch onto the stone basin; then the hollow ribcage; and finally, the amber eyes open to the violet sky. 

When the young Sparrow finally stands, it is already complete. It has no childhood; it simply begins to walk, possessing the same silent, ancient instincts as the rest of its kind.

***

### The Quiet Commerce

The Sparrows do not speak. The dry air of the terrace is too thin to carry the soft frequencies of a voice, and they have no vocal cords to shape it. Instead, they communicate through two methods: **contact** and **the yellow rods**.

Their fingers are long, ending in hard, spade-like nails that they use to clean the water basins. When two Sparrows meet, they do not greet each other with gestures. They stand close and touch their temples together. Through this contact, they exchange heat-signatures. Because their bodies are low-temperature systems, they can read the microscopic variations of warmth in another’s skin down to a hundredth of a degree. A temple-touch can communicate a Sparrow's age, the path it has walked that day, and the level of moisture remaining in its deep tissues.

For long-distance communication, they use the yellow ceramic rods that stick out of the sand.

To a human ear, the rods hum with a sound like a distant, crowded market. This is not a recording of the past; it is the Sparrows’ telegraph. When a Sparrow is at a Spindle miles away, it will tap its hard nails against the base of the needle. The vibration is caught by the Spindle, driven deep into the bedrock, and carried through the white sandstone strata to the yellow rods. 

Because there are dozens of Sparrows across the mesas tapping at any given time, the bedrock is always vibrating with a chaotic, overlapping network of signals. If you press your ear to a rod, you are hearing the collective "chatter" of thirty Sparrows, miles apart, talking about the state of the basins, the movement of the Sails, and the approach of the evening dew.

***

### The Cultivation of the Mesa

The Sparrows are not survivors on this plateau; they are its caretakers. Their entire existence is organized around the preservation of the delicate, edited laws of the place.

Every afternoon, as the sun begins its steep drop toward the western ridge, the Sparrows wake from their midday sleep in the puzzle-stone circles. They move out into the dry zones, walking the circular white tracks. 

They do not walk alone. Often, a Sparrow will have a Strider-Beetle sliding smoothly beside it. The relationship is symbiotic: the Sparrow has the hands to clear the sand from the beetles' breathing pores, and the beetle’s wide, blue-chitin back is the perfect flat surface for carrying pieces of dried lichen.

The Sparrows also harvest the **Sails**. 

When one of these sky-balloons loses its internal heat and drifts down to die on the stone, its translucent cellophane membrane does not rot. The Sparrows gather the fallen Sail. They cut the membrane into long, thin strips using the sharp edges of the stone-plants. With these strips, they construct *dew-catchers*—delicate, sail-like screens that they wire between the puzzle-stone walls using the magnetic silver-needle creatures. 

During the forty minutes of twilight tide, when the grey dew falls, these screens catch the moisture and channel it into small, hand-carved troughs that lead directly to the roots of the stone-plants. In return, the stone-plants, which poison the soil for all other things, do not poison the Sparrows. They allow them to harvest their grey-green wood, which the Sparrows use to mend the stone-basins and carve new channels for the water.

***

### The Law of the Stone

The Sparrows have no history, no kings, and no gods. They have only the **Spindles**, which they treat not with worship, but with a silent, maintenance-like devotion. They keep the basins clear of sand; they scrape the dead, non-functioning lichen from the needles; they ensure the Strider-Beetles do not crowd the water-channels.

They live in absolute harmony with the silence of the high terrace. They move so lightly that they do not disturb the dust of the tracks they walk. When a Sparrow dies, it does not leave a corpse to decay. Its hollow bones simply dry out until they are as brittle as glass. The wind, passing over the rounded rocks, eventually knocks the dry frame down; it breaks into white sand, joining the drifted dunes inside the puzzle-stone walls.

If you were to sit among them by a basin at twilight, they would not fear you, nor would they welcome you. They would simply stand, three or four of them, their amber eyes reflecting your face, their temple-points almost touching as they exchange the dry heat of their bodies. And as the dew begins to freeze on your coat, you would hear only the rhythmic, metallic *clink* of the silver needles in the rock cracks, and the slow, heavy *tick, tick, tick* of the Spindle’s condensed water falling into the basin—each drop a small, cold clock measuring an eternity that has no need for humans, or their words.
