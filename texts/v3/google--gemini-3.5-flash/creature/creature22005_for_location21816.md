To understand what lives in the Basin of the Floor, one must first look closely at a single tile of this living slate. 

If you were to reach down through the sliding mass and lift one of these hand-span creatures from the pile, it would feel surprisingly heavy, like a chunk of wet galena or a thick shingle of oiled slate. It has no head to speak of; its body is a vaulted, teardrop-shaped shield of purple-black chitin, thickest at the center and tapering to edges that are beveled and sharp as chipped flint. 

Turn it over, and there is no wet underbelly, no soft flesh. The underside is a pale, yellow-grey plate, dry as parchment and divided into dozen of narrow, overlapping bands. From the seams between these bands emerge the "threads"—thousands of short, stiff, leg-like bristles made of dark horn. They do not bend with knees or joints; instead, they vibrate in rapid, rhythmic waves, like the cilia of a microscopic organism magnified a million times. It is the collective, shivering stroke of these millions of bristles against the basalt below that drives the creature forward.

It has no eyes. In a world of soot-black dark and sulfurous haze, vision is a useless luxury. In place of eyes, the leading edge of its shell is lined with a row of microscopic, pit-like sensors that smell the air for the sour tang of volcanic gas and feel the dry, static tickle of the basalt walls. 

These creatures are called the *Shingles*, though they are less like individual animals and more like the self-replacing teeth of a massive, circular saw. 

They are organized by age and depth, sorted by the very physics of their movement. 

At the bottom of the pile—three feet down, pressed hard against the hot basalt bedrock—lie the **Soft-Backs**. Here, in the crushing, oven-like heat where the ground gas seeps directly from the mountain’s throat, the Shingles must molt. When a Shingle’s shell becomes too thick and calcified by the lime-dust it consumes, it does not grow; it splits. The old shell cracks down the center with a dry *tack* like a breaking twig. 

Because there is no water in the basin, the soft, newly exposed Shingle cannot pump itself up with fluid to dry into a larger shape. Instead, it must inhale the hot, sulfurous volcanic vapor, puffing its dry-paper body out like a small bellows. In this state, it is white and soft as tallow, completely vulnerable. If it is crushed by the weight of its brothers before it cures, its dry, greasy interior is instantly licked clean by the surrounding swarm. But if it survives for three hours in the deep heat, its skin hardens, darkening from grease-white to slate-grey, and finally to the deep, polished charcoal-purple of the mature Shingle.

Above the Soft-Backs, in the shifting middle layers, are the **Churners**. These are the active, mid-aged Shingles that form the great, slow eddies across the floor. They are the engine of the basin. They move in mechanical paths dictated entirely by gravity and the slope of the basalt ribs. They do not think; they possess only a simple, negative taxis—they move away from pressure and toward heat, a behavior that keeps them in a state of eternal, self-correcting circulation. 

Among these Churners, in the tight, dusty spaces between their plates, lives another creature: the **Cinder-Mite**. 

No larger than a grain of coarse pepper, the Cinder-Mites are the grease of the basin. They are entirely soft-bodied, looking like tiny, multi-legged drops of black resin. They do not have shells, nor do they dry out, for they are made of a dense, waxy fat that resists the kiln-warmth. They live by the thousands on the undersides of the Shingles, clustering around the leg-bristles and the hinges of the plates. They feed on the dry skin-flakes shed by the Shingles and the bitter, yellow sulfur-crust that accumulates in their joints. Without the Cinder-Mites to clean and lubricate their hinges, the Shingles would seize up, their plates locking until the friction of the swarm ground them into powder.

At the very top of the pile, sliding under the heavy ceiling of black dust, are the **Glass-Backs**. 

These are the old ones. They have been cycled through the depths and back to the surface thousands of times. Their shells are no longer charcoal-grey; they have been rubbed so smooth by centuries of contact with their fellows that they have taken on a high, obsidian-like gloss that reflects the rare, sluggish copper sparks from below like dark mirrors. 

The Glass-Backs are the gatherers. As the organic soot—the fine, carbon-rich ash from the high, burning peaks—drifts down through the dry air, it settles on the slick backs of these upper Shingles. By a system of shallow, microscopic grooves carved into the surface of their shells, the soot is funneled forward as they slide past one another, delivered directly to the scraping, file-like mandibles of the Shingle behind them. They feed each other through the simple act of friction.

And then there are the three circular enclosures of dry-stacked basalt.

The Shingles do not avoid these ruins; they are drawn to them. The circular walls create a natural venturi effect, drawing the hot rising air from the fissures up through their hollow centers. To the Shingles, the wells are sources of intense, delicious warmth. 

They climb the outer walls, their horn-bristles clicking against the glass-smooth basalt, and spill over the rims into the interiors. Inside the wells, there is no circular eddying; there is only a downward, grinding vortex. Because the space is tight, the Shingles are forced into a spiraling funnel, packed so closely that the friction between them rises to a mechanical ferocity. 

At the bottom of each well, beneath the boiling mass of plates, lies a flat, circular slab of basalt that has been grooved with deep, spiral channels by the passage of billions of bellies. Here, the oldest of the Glass-Backs—those whose joint-bristles have finally worn down to stumps, who can no longer shiver or move—are pushed by the weight of those above. They cannot escape. Slowly, over decades, the weight of the well grinds these old Shingles against the grooved basalt floor, milling them down into a fine, grey, calcium-rich flour. 

This flour is swept outward through small, worn weep-holes at the base of the wells by the vibrating legs of the younger Shingles. The swarm feeds upon its own ancestors, recycling the lime of their shells to form the plates of the soft-backs below.

This is the harmony of the Basin of the Floor: a world without sight, without green, and without voice. Only the cold-green lightning of their static compression, the high, dry heat of the kiln, and the endless *shuck-shuck-shuck* of slate-colored lives wearing the mountain down to dust.
