To understand what lives here, one must first dismiss the concept of the individual. In a world where the dark is absolute and the air is a thick, oiled steam, life does not exist in isolation. It is a single, continental tissue of wet flesh, rubbery fiber, and hot iron, wound so tightly together that the boundary between creature, plant, and machine has dissolved over the millennia. 

If you were to shine a light—an impossibility in this damp soot, but as a thought experiment—you would find that nothing here has a face as we understand it. Because there are no eyes, there are no expressions. There are only portals for intake, vents for exhaust, and surfaces for vibration.

### The Floor-Weavers and the Sward

The foundation of all life on the basalt terraces is the *Sward*—the massive, crawling carpet of chemosynthetic vegetation that forms the false forest floor. 

This is not grass; it is a system of thick, braided cables, some as wide as a human torso, that pulse with a slow, hydraulic rhythm. The vines are covered in a pale, violet velvet that feels like damp suede. This velvet is a specialized predatory mold. It does not wait for prey to die; it is highly electro-receptive. When the high-voltage lines overhead hiss and crackle with blue static, the velvet swells, its microscopic cilia standing on end to drink the ionized air. 

Interspersed among the vines are the *Cistern-Leaves*: massive, basin-like structures made of a material like vulcanized rubber. They do not photosynthesize; they are heat-sinks. They collect the boiling runoff from the steam-vents, cooling it within their thick, insulated chambers to a temperature where the micro-organisms they harbor can digest the heavy metal carbonates dissolved in the water. The water inside these basins is thick and dark, containing a soup of single-celled life that smells of yeast and hot vinegar.

### The Cleaners and Solder-Strays

Beneath and within this false floor crawls the most abundant class of fauna: the *Scrapers*. 

These are blind, multi-legged organisms ranging from the size of a finger to the size of a dog. They resemble a cross between a woodlouse and a wet leather pouch. They have no skeleton; instead, they are packed with high-pressure fluid, keeping them rigid enough to resist the crushing weight of the wet bogs. 

The Scrapers are the maintenance crew of the basalt. Their mouths are flat, horizontal wedges of hard, copper-like chitin. They spend their entire lives scraping the soot, the black fungi, and the thick, yellow grease that drips from the overhead machine-girders off the rock and iron surfaces. Without them, the ancient basalt would become so slick with organic fat that the larger creatures would slide into the chasms, and the drainage grates of the great pumps would clog within a week.

The Scrapers have a strange relationship with the ironworks. They are drawn to the heat of the geothermal pipes. When a pipe develops a pinhole leak of high-pressure steam, the Scrapers gather around it in a desperate, heap-like knot. The steam cooks the outer layers of their bodies into a dense, gristly tallow, but they do not flee; instead, their dying bodies are pressed by the others into the crevice, effectively plugging the leak with a plug of self-vulcanizing flesh. The machine continues to run, insulated by a scab of its own parasites.

### The Great Thrummers

Moving through the deep, root-choked canyons are the giants of the basalt: the *Thrummers*. 

These six-legged beasts are the size of draft horses, but their anatomy is horizontal, built low to the stone to keep their center of gravity below the driving downslope winds. Their skin is not hair or scales, but a thick, gray, oil-secreting hide that looks like weathered neoprene. 

The most remarkable feature of the Thrummer is its chest. The rib cage is not a cage at all, but a deep, hollow sounding-box made of thick, resonant cartilage. As they walk, they draw the dense, hot air into their lungs and force it out through specialized vocal reeds in their breathing spiracles. This produces a constant, low-frequency *thrum-thrum-thrum* that vibrates through the soles of their broad, suction-padded feet. 

By measuring the return of these vibrations through the wet stone and the roots, the Thrummers map their world. They can "see" a cliff-edge five hundred yards away through the pitch black; they can feel the thickness of the iron hull of a boiler shack and know whether the water inside is boiling or cold. 

The Thrummers do not hunt. They are grease-grazers. They use their long, prehensile tongues—which are coated in a sticky, alkaline saliva—to lick the heavy industrial lubricants from the bearings of the ancient, slow-turning turbines. The machines provide them with fat; the Thrummers, in turn, clear away the rusting scale that would otherwise freeze the gears.

### The Sky-Skimmers

Above, in the heavy soot-clouds that serve as a ceiling, live the *Soot-Skimmers*. 

These are enormous, kite-like creatures with wingspans of twenty feet or more. Their wings are made of a semi-translucent, oiled membrane that is so thin you can see the dark, slow-pulsing veins within. They have no legs to speak of—only dry, hook-like claws at the joints of their wings, which they use to hang from the high-voltage lines like rusted iron chandeliers.

The Skimmers are the only creatures here that possess anything resembling a face, though it is a terrifying one. The front of their head is a single, giant acoustic dish made of pale skin stretched over a skeletal ring of bone. This dish captures the sonar shrieks they emit from their throat-sacks—needles of high-pitched sound that can pierce even the roar of the waterfalls.

They feed on the *Grease-Midges*—clouds of tiny, oil-drinking insects that rise from the vents—and on the blind tree-frogs that cling to the wet canyon walls. A Skimmer hunts by dropping from its wire, falling through the absolute dark like a wet rag, silent and invisible, until its acoustic dish registers the heat-pulsation of a frog on the moss. It snatches the prey with a long, whip-like tail that ends in a group of prehensile, finger-like tendrils, then uses the hot thermal updrafts from the automated smelters to rise back into the soot-clouds without ever having to flap its wings.

### The Sessile Ones (The Heart-Suckers)

Perhaps the strangest inhabitants of this place are those that have given up movement entirely. 

On the sides of the miles-wide iron shacks, directly over the seams where the hot geothermal steam enters the piston-chambers, live the *Heart-Suckers*. They look less like animals and more like giant, pulsating galls or tumors of dark orange flesh. 

When young, they are small, free-swimming larvae in the boiling basins. But once they find a high-pressure iron casing, they attach themselves using a ring of acid-excreting teeth that eat through the outer layers of rust. They do not penetrate the interior of the pipe—the steam pressure would kill them—but they grow their flat, bone-hard base plate directly into the grain of the iron.

Once attached, they become part of the machine. The rhythmic vibration of the piston inside—that slow, four-second *thung... thung... thung*—is the engine of their own existence. The Heart-Sucker has no heart of its own; instead, it utilizes the mechanical shockwaves of the pump to drive its own circulatory system. Every time the iron piston strikes, the physical jar forces the creature’s hemolymph through its rubbery body, oxygenating it via the hot, wet air outside. 

If the machine shuts down, the Heart-Sucker dies of suffocation within minutes. It is a parasite that has gambled its entire existence on the mechanical reliability of an architecture created by someone else, centuries ago.

### The Unified Mind

There is no language here, no culture, and no history. But there is a memory.

The memory does not live in a brain. It lives in the chemical composition of the water that cycles through the landscape. When a Thrummer dies, its body is dissolved by the warm, acidic rain on the basalt terraces. The minerals of its bones, the grease it consumed from the turbines, and the complex enzymes of its sounding-box are washed down into the crevices, back into the deep subterranean aquifers. 

When the giant pumps pull that water back up from the volcanic belly of the earth and spray it out as steam, those chemical signatures are distributed back into the air. The Sward drinks them; the velvet mold absorbs them; the young larvae in the basins taste them.

Every creature that is born here already knows the path of the vertical rivers, the frequency of the nearest piston, and the smell of the grease that sustains them. They do not need to see. They are home in the heavy, roaring, dark wetness, breathing the breath of the iron, and they are entirely complete.
