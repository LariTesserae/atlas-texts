To live in this place is to have feet like hands. 

The people of the Rift have wide, flat feet, the toes splayed from a lifetime of gripping wet birch rungs and the slick octagonal edges of the basalt. They do not wear boots. Leather boots are stiff; they do not feel the stone; they slip. Instead, they wrap their feet in "clouts"—bandages of coarse, grease-stiffened wool lined with dried liverwort to absorb the sweat. Those who work the high ladders go barefoot even in the frost, their soles thick and yellowed, scarred by the crystalline edges of the rock until they are as tough as horn.

Because there is no room to pass another person on the eighteen-inch-wide gangways without touching, the people have developed a physical suppleness that looks like a dance but is only survival. When two meet on a high timber shelf, they do not turn their backs to the drop. They face each other, chest to chest, and roll round one another with a sliding, hip-to-hip motion, their hands lightly touching the other’s shoulders for leverage. They call this "the hinge." 

In the houses, which are small and square like old chests, the families live in a state of folded density. They have no chairs; they sit on their heels or on low three-legged stools of green fir that have been wedged into the floor-slabs. A family of seven will sleep in a space no larger than a farmer's cart, their limbs overlapping in the dark, kept warm by their collective breath and the wool blankets that are always slightly damp to the touch, smelling faintly of sheep-grease and wood-smoke.

This closeness has made them a people of singular privacy. They have learned the art of looking without seeing. If a neighbor is washing himself from a stone basin on a platform three feet from your window, you look through him, your eyes focusing on some distant chimney or the gray kites in the gap above. To look directly at another’s nakedness or their grief is considered as rude as stealing their firewood.

And they are silent. Their speech is a dry, sibilant whisper—all breath and teeth, with very few harsh consonants. They do not use names like those of the upper plateau. Their names are short, flat sounds that can be spoken without lifting the voice above the hum of a spindle: *Mnn, Tey, Sol, Fenn, Kesh.* 

If a man must call his son from three levels down, he does not shout. He takes a small hardwood mallet and taps a specific rhythm onto the spruce joist of his floor. The sound travels down the pine uprights, vibrating through the timber frame of the lower houses until the boy feels the tap in his toes. *Three short, one long.* The boy knows his father wants him, and he climbs.

Because of this constant vibration, the children are tethered from the day they learn to crawl. 

A child in the Rift does not run. From its third month, it wears a harness of plaited hemp around its chest. The harness is attached to a "cradle-cord" six feet long, which is pinned with an iron spike into the family’s structural pine post. The child plays within this semi-circle, its toys the smooth, heavy hexagonal biscuits of basalt that have fallen from the cliffs, or small dolls made of dried corn-husks traded from the top. They learn the limit of the cord before they learn to speak. If a child falls from a platform—and they do, occasionally—there is no scream. There is only the sudden, heavy *twang* of the rope, the creak of the timber post, and the mother reaching down with practiced, muscular arms to haul the dangling bundle back up, her face expressionless, her hands already returning to her spinning.

The only ones who do not live by the tethers are the *Plumb-Keepers*. 

These are the old men and women who have lived past the age of ladder-work. They are the doctors of the timber. Each is responsible for a "reach"—a section of some fifty stacked houses. They carry no tools but a hickory mallet and a pocketful of dried birch shims. All day, they walk the gangways, tapping the joints where the logs meet the basalt. They listen to the ring of the wood. If the wood sounds dry and high, like a tight wire, the rock is pressing; if it sounds hollow and wet, the wall has drawn back. 

A Plumb-Keeper is always dirty. Their skin is gray with coal-dust and grease, and their ears are remarkably large, tilted forward as if they are trying to catch a whisper from the heart of the stone. They are the only ones allowed to make noise. When a Plumb-Keeper taps a beam—*clink, clink, clink*—everyone nearby stops and holds their breath. The spinner pauses her wheel; the goat-herd holds his animal's muzzle. For those few seconds, the only sound in the trench is the iron on the wood, and the deep, wet dripping of the walls behind them.

But there is another class of people here: the *Sky-goers*. 

These are the young men and women who climb the eighty meters of birch ladders every morning at dawn to reach the pine barrens above. They are different from the rest. They have sunburned noses and eyes that are always watering from the dry plateau wind. They smell of wild thyme, turpentine, and dry horse-manure—smells that the deep-dwellers find sharp, greasy, and unpleasant. 

The Sky-goers are the providers. They go up to cut the green spruce logs, to hunt the dry-land hares, and to swap salt-fish and bone needles with the caravan-folk for baskets of sour mountain soil. But they are not trusted. They have "the wide eye." When they return down the ladders at dusk, their legs are shaky from the lack of walls, and they speak too loudly, their voices clattering against the basalt like dropped plates. They are kept on the topmost shelves of the settlement, nearest the drafts, where the air is still thin and dry.

Below them, in the dark grease of the trench-bottom, live the goats.

These are the *Clout-hooves*. They are small, about the size of a large dog, with long, yellow hair that hangs down in greasy ropes, protecting their bellies from the sharp talus. They have no horns; their owners grind them down with flat river-stones when the kids are small to prevent them from wedging themselves into the basalt cracks. 

The goats have an eerie, human patience. They stand on shelves of rock no wider than a man’s palm, their legs tucked under them, their horizontal yellow eyes fixed on the gray mist above. They do not bleat. When they are hungry, they make a low, rattling sound in their throats, like dry beans shaken in a bladder. They are fed on the potato-skins and bitter cabbage leaves from the window-baskets, and on the pale, waxen liverwort that the children scrape from the wet joints of the columns. 

Their milk is yellow and thick as cream, tasting of wet stone and iron. It is the blood of the Rift. Without it, the children’s bones would grow soft and bend like willow-hoops in the damp.

And beneath the houses, in the spaces where the timber platforms do not reach and the basalt columns have split into deep, vertical chimneys that go down into the black water of the deep earth, lives the *hiss*.

If you hang a piece of salt-grease on a string down one of these cracks, it will be gone by morning, the string cut as cleanly as if with a stone knife. 

The children believe that the *hiss* is what keeps the basalt warm. They say that if you put your ear to the black columns in the dead of winter, you can hear it licking the stone from the inside, keeping the frost from splitting the hearths. The Plumb-Keepers do not contradict them. They simply tap their wedges a little tighter, grease their hemp ropes, and listen to the slow, heavy groaning of the columns as they settle another inch into the dark.
