Beneath the shadowless grey light of the basin, the stillness is not the absence of life, but life that has reached its final, mineral state. Nothing here moves fast enough to disturb a grain of red dust, yet the basin is occupied. It has been occupied for so long that the inhabitants and the stone have traded names, textures, and temperatures.

The most visible of these residents—if one knows how to look—lie within the three circular slate enclosures in the center of the floor.

They are the ones who built the circles. Once, they were upright, soft-skinned, and jointed, but that was during an age when the plateau above was still green and the basin held a shallow, moss-greened pool. As the water retreated into the cracks of the earth and the wind died away, they realized that to survive, they had to match the basin’s slow surrender to the stone. 

They did not leave. Instead, they lay down within their small hearth-circles, shed their height, and let their bones flatten. 

There are three of them left, one in each enclosure, though they no longer look like people. They are known only as the Settled. They look like thick, overlapping plates of dark red slate, about five paces long and only two inches thick, mimicry so perfect that they appear to be nothing more than the collapsed flagstones of the sleeping-benches. Their surfaces are dry and velvet to the touch, carrying the texture of a moth’s wing but the weight of solid ironstone. They have no faces, no limbs, and no eyes; such things are useless in a world where nothing changes and there is nothing new to see.

Instead, their bodies are composed of slate-like scales that overlap like shingles. Beneath these hard, protective plates lies a core of dry, pale pith, similar to the interior of a dried gourd. This pith is their flesh, their memory, and their brain. It does not pulse with blood; instead, it is dry and porous, absorbing the warm twilight air through microscopic vents in their stone skins and releasing it so slowly that their breath is indistinguishable from the natural sigh of the baking rocks.

They are connected to the rest of the basin by a web of hair-thin, metallic-grey fibers that run beneath the red silt floor. These fibers, which look like weathered brass wires, extend outward from the enclosures like nerves, spreading across the fifty paces of bare stone until they reach the western curve of the basin.

There, beneath the cool limestone overhangs where the grey water droplets form, lives the second part of this slow entity: the Welts.

The Welts are small, inverted cups of grey, rubbery leather, no larger than a child’s palm, growing directly out of the dark, damp clay beneath the drip-points. They do not look like plants, nor do they look like animals; they are more like pockets of old skin turned inside out. They have no wood in them, nor any leaf, but their centers are hollow, lined with a fine, silver peach-fuzz that is incredibly sensitive to moisture. 

For minutes at a time, the Welts lie perfectly flat and dry against the red soil, looking like wrinkled discardings. But when a single drop of water finally loses its hold on the limestone overhead and falls, the nearest Welt rises slightly. It does not crawl; it simply inflates with a slow, hydraulic pressure. 

When the drop lands—*tink*—the Welt catches it perfectly in its hollow center. There is no splash. The grey leather absorbs the water instantly, turning a dark, bruised plum color. For a single second, the Welt shivers—the only rapid movement in the entire basin. 

This tiny vibration is translated instantly into the brass-like wires beneath the silt. A faint, cool ripple of pressure travels across the floor of the bowl, passing through the dirt, and enters the plates of the Settled in their circular enclosures.

The Settled receive the ripple. Inside their dry pith, the sensation of the water-drop is felt not as a sound or a wetness, but as a microscopic shift in temperature, a tiny, cool note in their infinite warm sleep. 

In response, one of the slate scales on the middle plate will slide over another by the width of a single pollen grain. This movement is so slow that if sand had settled on the plate’s back, the grain would not fall off; it would merely shift its slope. As the scale moves, it squeezes a tiny pocket of air from the dry pith beneath, releasing a faint, ghostly breath into the twilight—the scent of dry sage and ancient stone that lingers for hours in the motionless air.

This is how they speak, and this is how they mark the passage of centuries. One drop falls in the west; a Welt shivers; a plate in the center shifts its weight; a faint scent of sage is given to the warm dusk. 

They do not eat. They do not hunt. They require no sustenance other than the heat stored in the flat rocks from an eternity of sun, and the microscopic trace of iron mineral carried in the rare, grey water drops. Their metabolism is so close to absolute zero that a single drop of water can sustain one of the Settled for three decades. 

They are has-beens and remaining-things. They have outlived the birds, the beetles, and the grass, and they have done so by becoming as durable as the cliffs that shelter them. They have reached their own angle of repose. They are not waiting for the rain to return, nor are they waiting for the world above to change. They are simply content to be the memory of the stone, holding their breath in the warm, shadowless twilight, listening to the slow, hollow *tuck* of the water, until the basin itself finally crumbles into dust.
