They are called, if they have a name that can be translated from the dry snapping of their language, the *Chapped*. 

To see one is first to see nothing at all, for they spend hours perfectly still, wedged into the vertical flutes of the limestone cliff like discarded husks of giant cicadas. They do not have skin as soft-bodied things do; their outer envelope is a complex, articulation of dry, overlapping scales—not of fish-horn, but of a substance resembling gray mica or hardened, unglazed porcelain. These plates are slightly flexible, curved to shed the biting copper dust that would otherwise grind them to powder. 

They are tall, unnaturally thin, and progress with a slow, deliberate folding and unfolding of limbs that seems more architectural than animal. A Chapped has four long, triple-jointed fingers on each hand, the tips of which are capped not with nails but with hard nodules of black volcanic glass. 

They do not possess eyes that weep or require moisture. Instead, their orbital sockets contain dry, polished spheres of deep amber chitin. They do not blink. Instead, with every fifth or sixth movement of their heads, a pair of tiny, brush-like appendages—made of stiff, hair-like bristles—sweeps horizontally across the amber domes, flicking away the red silt before it can scratch the lenses. 

They do not breathe through mouths or noses. To do so in this air would be to fill their interiors with a suffocating paste of dry dust. Instead, their chests are ribbed with broad, horizontal vents lined with fine, dry membranes of vegetable vellum, identical to the ribbons of the shingle-palms. As they move, or as the wind passes through them, these bellows expand and contract with a sound like dry parchment being crumpled in a closed room. They do not extract oxygen; they filter the dry nitrogen and absorb the static potential of the rushing air.

Indeed, they are living, walking capacitors. They have no red blood; their interior is a system of dry, carbonaceous fibers and dense, non-aqueous tallow that acts as an insulator. They run on the voltage of the High Shelf. 

At the middle of the day, when the thermal drafts from the abyss are at their most violent, the Chapped descend from their cliff-niches to forage. They do not eat for calories, but for charge. They gather around the clusters of spark-gorse, their movement a series of quick, jerky hops to avoid the boiling whirlpools of copper sand. They stretch out their long, glass-tipped fingers and touch the black needles of the gorse. One can hear the sharp, rhythmic *clack-crack* of the discharge as the violet brush-glowing static travels up their arms, illuminating the translucent joints of their limbs from the inside with a faint, violet trace. This charge is their grease, their pulse, their thought.

Their communication is not vocal, for they have no wet lungs to push air through a larynx. Instead, they are stridulators of extraordinary complexity. On the inner surfaces of their forearms run ridges of notched horn, and on their ribplates are matching scraper-edges. By rubbing their limbs against their bodies—a gesture that looks to a stranger like an agony of self-scratching—they produce a vast array of dry sounds: the high, dry hiss of a snake in sand, the rapid clicking of dry seeds in a gourd, or a deep, hollow drumming that mimics the wind in the wind-scooped boulders. 

When they sit together in the low, circular stone enclosures, they do not speak in sentences but in rhythmic loops. A single Chapped will begin a pattern—two clicks and a long, whistling rasp—and the others will join, one by one, until the stone circle is vibrating with a dry, mechanical choir that rises and falls with the gusting of the draft. 

They are the ones who made the scrapers of petrified wood and obsidian. They use them to harvest the shingle-palms. They do not cut the trunks, which are too hard, but they shave the long vellum ribbons from the crowns. They use these ribbons to patch their own worn joints, gluing the papery strips to their limbs with a resin harvested from the roots of the spark-gorse—a resin that dries instantly into a hard, amber-colored glaze.

Most beautiful and strange is how they bring new life into this kiln. They do not give birth, nor do they lay eggs. When the population of the Shelf grows sparse—when the wind has carried too many of them over the edge into the copper haze of the abyss, or when some have simply run out of charge and grown still, turning into gray stone statues that the wind slowly grinds away—the remaining Chapped gather to assemble a "sheaf."

Using the obsidian scrapers, they harvest the toughest, most conductive vellum from the oldest shingle-palms. Inside the roofless stone circles, shielded from the worst of the grit, they weave these ribbons into a tight, hollow, man-sized basket. They pack the interior with fine, black manganese dust—which they mine from the deep cracks in the limestone—and thread it through with long, fibrous roots of the spark-gorse.

Once the figure is complete, they carry it out to the edge of the Shelf, where the copper-sand whirlpools boil most violently. They stake the woven basket into the center of a rising dust-devil, securing it with ropes of braided palm-vein. 

Then they wait. 

For three or four days, the dry storm whips the basket, polishing its vellum skin with copper dust until it shines like brass. The static of the spinning sand accumulates within the manganese core, building a massive, silent electrical potential. 

At last, during a night when the air is thick with lavender brush-fire, a single, blinding blue bolt of lightning—drawn down from the heights by the iron-rich cliffs—strikes the staked figure. 

When the dust clears, the basket is no longer a basket. The heat of the strike has fused the manganese and the root-fibers into a dark, humming nervous system. The vellum has stiffened into plates of horn. The figure rises, its joints clicking with their first, stiff movements, its amber eyes opening to receive the dry, roaring friction of its home. It takes its place among the others, shaking the red dust from its limbs, already listening to the great, dry organ-song of the canyon.
