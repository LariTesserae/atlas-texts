To inhabit the High Basin is to be an organism of the hinge, the flue, and the wet brick. 

In this landscape of iron and moss, life does not burrow into soil; it clings, slips, and wedges itself into the seams where the mechanical and the organic have fused over centuries of damp twilight.

### The Lowly and the Cleaners

At the smallest scale, beneath the giants of the Grid, are the **Lard-Snails** and the **Verdigris-Drills**. 

The palm-sized iron-snails of the copper conduits are joined by others of their kind that have adapted specifically to the industrial fluids. The Lard-Snails are heavy, flat-shelled gastropods that do not climb the plants but instead harvest the black grease that weeps from the bronze hinges of the water-gates. Their shells are not calcium but a dense, layered composite of iron-scale and paraffin wax, which they secrete to protect their soft bodies from the acidic mountain rain. They trails they leave behind are clean, shiny tracks of bare, scraped metal, preventing the pivots from seizing under their own weight.

Beside them in the warm, mineral-rich runoff are the **Rust-Lice**—crustaceans the size of walnut shells with segmented brass-colored plates. They are the garbage collectors of the copper flumes. They gather in the filter-baskets where the heavy cloud-harvest deposits mountain grit and fragments of dead ivy. With pincers shaped like tiny wire-cutters, they snip away the choking tangles of fibrous roots that threaten to plug the copper grates. They are blind, finding their way by the changing temperature of the pipe walls; they know that where the metal is hottest, the current is strongest, and the food most abundant.

### The Denizens of the Flumes

Deep inside the warm, interior dark of the brick reservoirs and the submerged locks, where the mountain water is temporarily stilled and heated by the massive steam-chests, lives the **Weir-Eel**.

These are long, muscle-dense creatures, pale as lard and entirely without eyes. Their skin is covered in a thick, protective slime that smells of sulfur and coal tar. They possess no bones, only a cartilaginous spine that allows them to squeeze through the high-pressure narrowness of three-inch copper flumes. They feed on the thick curtains of white bacteria that grow in the darkness of the heat-exchangers. When a water-gate swings shut, the change in pressure sends a shiver through the water; the eels respond by flattening themselves against the brick floors, their suction-cup mouths anchoring them to the masonry so they are not swept down the steps of the basin when the locks drain.

### The Muffled Aviary

Above the waterlines, where the wet ivy climbs the iron lattices, the silence is kept by the **Soot-Swifts**.

These birds have lost the need for song; in a world where sound is swallowed by five-inch-thick moss, a mating call would die before it reached the next gantry. Instead, they communicate through rhythmic clapping of their wings—short, sharp snaps that sound like the popping of dry twigs, barely carrying twenty yards. 

They are small and torpedo-shaped, with feathers of a greasy, charcoal-black that never waterlogs. They do not perch on branches; they have no feet to speak of, only four-toed clamps that allow them to hang vertically from the rivet-heads of the steam pipes, warming their bellies against the hot iron. 

Their nests are marvels of the Basin: they weave them from discarded copper wire, strands of tough ivy fiber, and fragments of coal slag, cementing the structure to the undersides of the active ore-till suspension rails with a thick, quick-drying saliva. When the automated tills rumble overhead, the nests vibrate, keeping the chicks warm even when the parent is away hunting the blind midges in the fog.

### The Feral Till-Beasts

On the high suspension lines, not all the traffic is purely mechanical. 

Some of the automated ore-tills have been running their loops for so long that their original programming has warped into something resembling animal behavior. These are the **Feral Tills**—massive, iron-wheeled structures that once carried ballast but have long since been derailed or marooned on dead-end sidings. 

Now, they are traveling islands of peat. Decades of bird droppings, wind-blown moss spores, and decaying fern fronds have piled three feet deep on their flatbeds. They move only when the weight of the rainwater on their moss-beds triggers their ancient pneumatic weight-compensators. 

When a Feral Till moves, it is a slow, majestic event: a block of green peat, twenty feet long, crawling at three inches an hour along an iron rail, its rusted wheels squealing a wet, low note that sounds like a distant horn in the mist. Smaller creatures travel with them—entire colonies of iron-snails and blind midges live, breed, and die on these slow-moving islands, never touching the stationary metal of the Grid below.

### The Silt-Watchers

Though the Grid has no citizens, it is not entirely empty of humanity. There are the **Silt-Watchers**, though whether they can still be called men is a question the lower world has forgotten.

They are few—perhaps only three or four dozen across the entire three-thousand-meter pass—and they live solitary lives in the "Dry-Pockets." These are the hollow, vaulted chambers beneath the largest heat-exchangers, where the leaking steam keeps the air at a constant, stifling seventy degrees, and the smell is of hot grease and toasted brick.

A Silt-Watcher is clad in layers of thick, boiled leather and canvas that has been rubbed with mutton tallow and coal tar until it is as stiff and black as the iron itself. They wear clogs made of carved leadwood with soles of ribbed copper; when they walk along the wet maintenance paths, their boots do not slip, matching the texture of the Grid beneath them.

They do not speak. Their vocal cords, long unused in the universal silence, have withered to a dry click. If two Watchers meet on a narrow gantry, they do not exchange words. Instead, they tap their heavy brass grease-wrenches against the handrails—a code of rhythmic vibrations that travels through the iron, signaling which flumes are clogged, which water-gates are sticking, and where the moss is growing too thick for the gears to clear.

The Watchers do not maintain the machine out of duty, but out of a symbiotic necessity. They eat the starchy piths of the shovel-leafed ivy, which they boil in the hot discharge of the steam-vents, and they trap the blind crayfish that grow in the sediment tanks. In return, they keep the machine from choking on its own wild growth. With their long, curved scrapers, they peel the heavy mats of sphagnum moss away from the delicate bronze pressure-gauges, and they grease the massive thrust-bearings that the automated oil-pumps cannot reach.

They are the tenders of a garden that is made of iron and water. They move through the perpetual drizzle like shadows, their skin stained with coal-soot and rust-red, indistinguishable from the girders they walk upon—the slow, quiet heartbeats of a world that runs on steam, water, and silence.
