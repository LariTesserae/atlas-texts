The creatures that populate these vertical spikes of slate and basalt are defined by gravity, grease, and the search for heat. In a land where the earth is constantly shearing away in clean, razor-sharp planes, to be soft is to be torn to ribbons. Everything that lives here operates on a principle of armored dampness.

### The Six-Legged Grazers (*The Slate-Walkers*)

They have no names that anyone speaks, but they are the undisputed masters of the vertical. They are heavy, low-slung things, shaped like wet, salt-crusted boulders, with six short, thick legs that bend at strange, hydraulic angles. Their wool is not soft; it is a dense, matted thatch of charcoal-colored fiber, soaked in a lanolin so thick and yellow it smells of tallow and cold peat. This waxen grease makes them entirely waterproof; the constant, weeping mist of the peaks runs off their flanks in round, silver beads, never touching the black skin beneath.

Their feet are masterpieces of adaptation to glassy, new-cleaved stone. Each of their six hooves is splayed and split down the center, lined with a leathery, suctioning pad that secretes a sticky, cold-resistant mucus. When a slate-walker steps, its hoof spreads wide to cup the sharp edges of the basalt. They do not run; they move with the agonizing, mechanical deliberation of a clockwork toy, placing one hoof, testing the grip, and then moving the next, their joints making a wet *clack-pop* with every stride.

Because there is no dirt to speak of, only the black, frozen silt of the saddles, their teeth are not like those of lowland grass-eater. They have no incisors; instead, their mouths are lined with hard, file-like plates of bone that grow continuously, like a rodent’s. They press their flat, rubbery snouts against the wet basalt and scrape. They eat the orange lichen down to the bare, white stone, leaving pale, oval "mouth-scars" on the mountain-faces that look, from a distance, like some ancient, forgotten script.

When the cold wind rises to a howl, they do not seek caves—for there are few deep caves in this young rock—but instead gather in tight, steaming circles. They press their heavy flanks together, heads tucked inward, until they resemble a single, undulating mound of gray wool, rising and falling in the mist, smelling of lard and wet turnip.

### The Grease-Birds (*The Clickers*)

Living in the fissures that the slate-walkers cannot reach are the pale-winged clickers. They are fat, breast-heavy birds, the size of grouse, but with the short, muscular wings of burrowing sea-birds. Their feathers are not built for long, soaring flights; there is no thermal lift in these cold, damp chimneys of stone. Instead, they glide heavily from one wet ledge to another, their pale, grease-stained wings flapping with a heavy, wet *slap-slap-slap* that sounds like wet laundry in the wind.

The clickers are entirely silent in their throats. They have no syrinx, no call. Instead, they communicate through their massive, yellow-white beaks, which are thick and heavy enough to crack walnut shells. They rattle their mandibles together in complex, rhythmic patterns—a dry, wood-on-wood clatter that the mountain walls echo and distort. A flock of five hundred clickers roosting in a deep, sulfurous crevice sounds like a printing press running in a distant basement.

They survive on the life of the seams. In the weeping cracks where the water seeps, there are small, blind, white millipedes that feed on the mineral silt. The clickers pry these out with the curved tips of their beaks. 

They have developed an obsessive relationship with the crawling machines. When a crawler is stationary, its giant boilers radiating a thick, oily heat, the clickers descend in hundreds. They sit on the hot iron pipes, their pale breasts turning black from the coal-soot, their eyes closed in a daze of warmth. They are so indifferent to the machinery that they will remain perched on a piston as it slides back and forth, turning their bodies rhythmically to keep their bellies facing the warm copper cylinders.

### The Parasitic Life of the Rigs

The twelve-legged crawlers are not alive, but they have been in the mountains so long that they have developed their own specialized ecology. They are moving islands of warmth and organic waste.

Living in the greasy hinges of the crawlers’ leg-joints are the *oil-lice*. These are flat, hard-shelled crustaceans, the size of a human palm, that look like prehistoric trilobites. They do not eat flesh; they eat the thick, black grease that seeps from the machine’s bronze bushings. They scrape the old, soot-caked lubricant with their front legs, processing the heavy hydrocarbons through primitive, chemical-tolerant digestive tracts. Where there is a leak in a hydraulic line, one will find dozens of oil-lice clustered together, their shells gleaming like wet coal, drinking the amber mineral oil as it drips.

Along the boiler casings, where the iron is constantly hot but not quite hot enough to burn, a strange, rubbery moss has taken root. It is not green, but a strange, liver-brown color, dry and crusty to the touch. It feeds on the sulfurous soot of the exhaust chimneys and the condensation that pools on the iron plates. This "boiler-scab" acts as a form of insulation for the machines, growing several inches thick in the dead spaces between the pistons, smelling faintly of baked yeast and hot rubber.

### The Crevice-Drifters

Deep in the vertical fractures where the water never freezes—where the earth’s internal heat meets the icy run-off from the surface—lives a creature that rarely sees the sky. 

These are the *crevice-drifters*. They are soft-bodied, pale, and rib-thin, like giant, five-foot-long caterpillars made of wet parchment. They have no eyes, but their skins are covered in thousands of tiny, hair-like cilia that are incredibly sensitive to changes in water pressure and steam. They move through the narrow, water-logged fractures of the basalt by expansion and contraction, squeezing their jelly-like bodies through cracks no wider than a finger.

They are the clean-up crew of the heights. When a slate-walker loses its footing on a wet ridge and tumbles into a dark ravine, its body is soon found by the drifters. They emerge from the wet cracks like pale fingers, dissolving the soft tissues of the carcass with highly acidic secretions before retreating back into the safety of the dark, weeping stone.

### The Silent Balance

There are no predators here in the traditional sense. Nothing has the energy to hunt in this thin, cold air. The slate-walkers do not fear the grease-birds, and the birds do not fear the slow, blind drifters in the cracks. 

The struggle is entirely against the landscape itself—against the razor-sharp stone that can slice a hoof to the bone, against the black ice that sneaks over the ledges in the night, and against the sulfur-choked fog that can fill a beast’s lungs with fluid.

Under the endless, zinc-colored sky, this strange assembly of grease, wool, iron, and steam continues its slow, rhythmic existence. The machines dig; the beasts scrape the stone; the birds click in the warmth of the boilers; and the young mountains continue to rise, sharp and indifferent, into the freezing wet air.
