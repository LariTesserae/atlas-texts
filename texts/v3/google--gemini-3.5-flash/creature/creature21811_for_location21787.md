In the shadow of the five-mile-high basalt needles, where the only light is the dull, internal rust-glow of the crust, the things that live here do not walk. They do not slide, crawl, or fly. They *stress*.

If you were to watch a single pane of the black volcanic glass for several hours, you might notice that some of the sharpest angles are not static. There are forms here that look like three-dimensional iron-frame drawings, or clusters of dark needles that have fallen together by chance. These are the **Cleavers**. 

They are small—the size of a human hand, or perhaps a wolf’s skull—but they have no flesh, no organs, and no blood. Their bodies are skeletal lattices of black obsidian and iron-rich basalt, held together not by ligaments, but by powerful, short-range electrostatic fields. They have no eyes. Instead, their "heads" are heavy, six-sided prisms of smoky quartz that glow with a faint, internal piezoelectric light when the ground beneath them shifts.

The Cleavers move through a process of controlled fracturing. They do not have muscles to bend their limbs; instead, they allow the dry heat and the rising tectonic tension to build up within their crystalline joints until the stress reaches a critical limit. Then, with a sharp, high-pitched *tink* that sounds like a pistol shot, a joint will snap into a new angle, flinging the creature forward across the razor-bright slides of glass. To an outside observer, their movement is a terrifying, jerky dance of sudden, violent explosions and long, dead stillnesses. 

They are drawn to the sound of breaking stone. When a thirty-foot basalt column shatters and falls in a dry, ringing roar, the Cleavers descend upon the debris. They do not eat the stone; they eat the *rupture*. They cling to the freshly exposed surfaces of the glass shards, absorbing the immense electrostatic charges and the heat generated by the sudden breaking of atomic bonds. For hours after a landslide, the pile of glass will be alive with hundreds of small, dark frames, their quartz heads flickering with blue and violet sparks as they drink the dying energy of the mountain's birth.

Farther out, in the wide, flat basins where the dark ash has never known a footprint, there are larger, slower presences. These are the **Sift-Lattices**. 

They are monolithic, resembling the ruins of rib-vaulted cathedrals made of raw, unpolished ironstone. A single Sift-Lattice can be eighty feet tall, yet it weighs very little, for its structure is almost entirely hollow—a delicate, geometric cage of hexagonal basalt bars that allows the warm, sulfurous updrafts to pass through it without resistance.

The Sift-Lattices do not travel by snapping or leaping. They move at the speed of the growing mountains, drifting across the ash basins over the course of centuries. They grow on their forward edge, drawing up the fine, dry iron-dust and volcanic ash through electrostatic attraction, sintering the mineral grains into solid stone using the heat of their own internal currents. As they grow forward, their trailing edge slowly succumbs to the dry, intense vibrations of the earth, cracking and crumbling back into the ash basin. They are perpetual, slow-motion waves of stone, leaving behind them a flat, perfectly sorted trail of dark, hexagonal pebbles.

They are the singers of this empty world. When the warm, aromatic plumes of sulfur and iron rise from the deep fissures, they pass through the hollow bones of the Sift-Lattices. The wind here is weak, but the heat is constant, and as the rising plumes escape through the stone cages, they produce a low, sub-octave vibration—a dry, rasping hum that shakes the nearby obsidian needles and causes the loose glass shards to dance like water on a drum. 

***

And then, there are the builders of the slate rings. 

The rings—those massive, tilting wells of dry-stone blocks—are not empty. They are the resting places of the **Deep-Strikers**, the oldest and heaviest things in this warm night. 

A Deep-Striker does not have a separate body. It is a massive, segmented column of dark slate, identical to the stone of the rings, that extends miles down into the boiling mantle beneath the crust. The dry-stone wells we see on the surface are not structures built *by* them; they are the "heads" of the Strikers, the tips of their massive, stony needles that have been pushed up through the ash plates.

The slate blocks are fitted together with such heavy precision because they are the vertebrae of a single, continuous organism of compressed shale. They are cold compared to the mantle below, but they are highly conductive. 

The Deep-Strikers are the thinkers of the world. They do not possess minds that recognize moments, or hours, or years. Their thoughts are written in the slow, agonizing grind of the tectonic plates. When a mile-long ridge of black flint tilts, rising three inches in an hour, that is a single synapse firing in the deep-earth consciousness of a Striker. 

The slate rings are their sensory organs. By matching the flat, parallel cleavage planes of the slate blocks against the rising basalt needles, the Strikers "feel" the shape of the world above. They feel the weight of the shadow-dome, the temperature of the sulfur plumes, and the tiny, frantic *tink-tink* of the Cleavers leaping across the glass slopes. 

When the rising earth catches one of these slate rings and slowly snaps it in half, it is not a tragedy for the Striker. It is an expression of growth. The broken blocks, tumbling down the slopes in silent, heavy segments, are like dry leaves falling from an oak. Below, in the warmth of the dark fissures, new slate is already being compressed, new vertebrae are being stacked, and the giant, blind needle of the Striker is already prepareing to push another ring up into the dry, terminal twilight.

Here, in this world of pure geometry, nothing is waiting for the rain. Rain would be a pollutant, a dissolver of the beautiful, sharp edges, a short-circuit in the great, dry, electrostatic machine. The life that resides here is perfectly content with its eternal, warm night—a silent, grinding family of glass, basalt, and slate, singing their low-frequency songs to a sky that is nothing but a heavy, protective shadow.
