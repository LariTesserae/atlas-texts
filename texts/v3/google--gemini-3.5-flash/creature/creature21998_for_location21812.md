### The People of the Grip

To know the folk of the Weave, one must first look at their feet. 

An infant here does not learn to walk on flat ground; they learn to clamp. Their feet are wide, with a fan of extraordinarily long, prehensile toes that can grip a wet liana or the slick, rounded back of a pipe-reed with the confidence of a hand. The skin on their soles is yellow and thick as raw hide, textured with fine, concentric ridges like the underside of a mushroom cap. They do not wear shoes—to cover the sole is to go blind to the world—but they occasionally wrap their ankles in strips of oiled nettle-cloth to keep the biting water-midges from their veins.

They are small people, rarely standing higher than five feet, but their torsos are barrel-shaped, housing lungs that are massive for their stature. This is a necessity of the altitude, but also of their tongue: they do not speak in the flat, vowels-first murmurs of the lowlanders. Their language is a system of high-pitched clicks, throat-whistles, and sharp, barking plosives designed to cut through the eternal, heavy music of the falling water. A mother on a high bridge can whistle a warning to a child three terraces down, her voice piercing the roar of the sluices like a bone flute.

Their skin is the color of alder-bark that has been soaked in a stream—a pale, translucent grey-brown that flushes ruddy and dark at the ears and cheeks where the mountain cold bites hardest. They have no body hair; it would only trap the constant, rotting moisture of the air and breed the emerald mold that sometimes takes root in the wool of their capes. Instead, their hair is thick, oily, and black-green, kept long and woven into dense, grease-bound braids that hang down their backs like ropes of wet horsehair. These braids act as natural gutters, directing the cold moss-water away from the napes of their necks and down over their oiled capes.

They smell intensely of wet soot, pine-tar, mint-poultices, and the sharp, vinegar-tang of fermenting wild ginger. They are never dry. To them, "dryness" is a rare, brittle state associated with death, withered leaves, and the barren stone of the peaks above. To be healthy is to be slick, cool, and supple—like a fresh-peeled willow branch.

### The Social Thread

Because there is no horizontal space, their social hierarchy is entirely vertical, though it is free of the tyranny that usually comes with height. 

*   **The Crown-Keepers** live at the very top, near the wind-blasted rim where the bitter mountain scrub begins. They are the hardiest, their skins leathery from the direct sun and the scouring mountain winds. They manage the high sheep-pens (beasts no larger than mastiffs, with short legs and thick, greasy wool) and the timber-chutes. They are the lookouts, their eyes constantly scanning the dry, grey wastes outside the gorge for those who might try to descend into their green oasis.
*   **The Middle-Weavers** occupy the vast, green heart of the cleft. They are the structuralists. They spend their days knotted into climbing harnesses made of braided horsehair, tending to the green masonry of the iron-moss and the willow-nests. They patch, they weave, they splice. Their hands are stained a permanent, deep indigo-black from the tannin of the birch-bark and the pine-pitch they use to seal the spindle-nests.
*   **The Silt-Gleaners** live in the shadow of the bottom, where the water is thick and the light is green as pond-water. They are the pale-eyed ones, their skin almost white from the lack of direct sun. They tend the giant salamanders and harvest the blue-black clay that settles in the slow-moving pools. They are the cooks and the rot-masters, managing the great, steaming compost-terraces where the waste of the upper neighborhoods is transformed into rich, black peat to feed the suspended ginger gardens.

Despite these divisions, there is no ownership of the bridges or the ladders. If a ladder rots and snaps, it is not the fault of the one who fell, but the shame of the entire neighborhood that let the wood go gray.

### The Co-Inhabitants: Neighbors of Moss and Membrane

The humans are not the dominant species of the Weave; they are merely the most talkative. They share every square inch of their vertical village with a host of creatures that have adapted to the same damp, crowded reality.

#### The Hearth-Mice (Sap-Frogs)
These are not rodents, but tiny, rubbery frogs the color of wet mustard, marked with two bright yellow stripes down their flanks. They are the domestic spirits of the spindle-nests. They are intensely attracted to heat and the salt of human sweat. During the cold mountain nights, they gather in the bedding of the weavers, tucking themselves into the armpits and behind the knees of sleeping children. 

They are welcomed guests. They do not eat grain—which is rare and kept in sealed birch canisters—but rather the small, blood-sucking blackflies that would otherwise make sleep impossible. A nest with a healthy population of "singing hearth-mice" is considered blessed. Their song is a soft, rhythmic *clink-clink-clink*, like two small river stones being tapped together under water, which lulls the inhabitants to sleep against the deeper roar of the canyon.

#### The Oar-Mouths (Sump-Salamanders)
At the bottom of the cleft, where the waterfalls finally exhaust themselves into deep, foam-flecked pools, live the *Oar-Mouths*. These four-foot-long amphibians are the color of old copper, with bellies that glow with a dull, internal orange light during their autumn mating season. 

They are blind, relying on the thousands of tiny, sensitive whiskers that fringe their wide, shovel-like mouths to feel the vibrations of the water. The Silt-Gleaners treat them with a mixture of reverence and utility. They are not hunted for food—their flesh is bitter and induces violent, hallucinatory fevers—but they are the sanitation workers of the Weave. Any organic waste that cannot be composted is dropped into the lower pools, where the Oar-Mouths consume it instantly.

In return, the Gleaners harvest the thick, clear slime that the salamanders secrete when they are content. This slime, when dried on birch-sheets, turns into a tough, waterproof membrane that is used to glaze the small round windows of the spindle-nests, keeping out the cold mountain draft while letting in the soft, diffused green light of the gorge.

#### The Clack-Beetles
Fist-sized and armored in shells that shimmer like oil on water, these beetles are the gardeners of the rock. They crawl ceaselessly over the three-foot-thick iron-moss, their serrated mandibles snipping off the dead spore-heads and keeping the moss from choking itself on its own growth. 

Their rhythmic *click-click-click* is the clock by which the Weavers live. Because the gorge is too deep to see the sun for most of the day, the people tell time by the beetles. In the morning, when the cold mist is thickest, the beetles are slow, clicking once every dozen heartbeats. As the sun passes directly overhead and warms the limestone, the click becomes a frantic, metallic rattle. If the beetles suddenly go silent, it is a sign of an oncoming high-altitude storm; they will tuck themselves deep into the moss-roots, and the human population follows their example, retreating into their spindle-nests and pulling the heavy willow-hatches tight.

### The Unseen and the Unspoken

In the deepest parts of the iron-moss, where the limestone walls have been hollowed out by centuries of water-wear, lives something the weavers call the *Sigh-of-the-Weave*. 

It is not a ghost, but a highly complex, subterranean root-fungus that grows in partnership with the iron-moss. It manifests as miles of thin, milk-white threads that run behind the green walls like the nervous system of the canyon. It reacts to pressure. When a heavy step lands on a mossy terrace, the threads below contract, squeezing out tiny droplets of sweet, clear sap that the clack-beetles feed on. 

The Weavers believe that the *Sigh* remembers every footprint. When an elder grows too old to climb—when their joints "rust" from the damp and their fingers can no longer squeeze the willow-stalks—they do not wait to die in their nests. They go down to the Sump. They lie on the damp, deep iron-moss at the edge of the lower pools, and the Silt-Gleaners cover them in fresh green moss-fronds and wet river-clay.

Within days, the white threads of the *Sigh* find them. It is said that the dying do not feel pain, only a deep, sinking warmth as they are slowly woven into the canyon walls. They do not leave the Weave; they simply become part of the green masonry that supports the nests of their grandchildren, their bones turning to limestone, their breath joining the cold, wet steam that forever rises from the throat of the mountain.
