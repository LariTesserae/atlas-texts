To survive in Conduit Chamber 40, an organism must either love the grease or find a way to eat the stone. 

The most permanent inhabitant is the Sump-Keeper, though "inhabitant" implies a distinction between the man and the room that has long since dissolved. He is known, if he is known at all to the engineers who drop oil drums down the dry-shaft every three months, as the Boiler-stoker or the Sump-Man, but he has forgotten his own Christian name. His skin is the color of lard left too long in a dark pantry—a thick, yellowed white, translucent enough in the orange glow of the carbon-filament bulbs to show the blue-black branching of his veins like ink spreading through wet blotting paper. 

To protect his limbs from the weeping lime-water, which is caustic enough to peel the skin off a common rat in forty-eight hours, the Keeper smears himself daily with a thick paste of mutton tallow and soot. This ointment has filled his pores, smoothed his knuckles into round, hairless knobs, and given his face a permanent, greasy mask that never wrinkles, even when he grins. He has no eyebrows; the hot scale-remover fumes took them forty years ago, leaving only two smooth, scar-pink ridges above eyes that have narrowed to wet gray slits. He does not need his sight for much. If he were to be brought up into the noon of the market square above, he would be as blind as the isopods, his retinas scorched by the glare of a white cloud.

Instead, the Keeper lives in his feet and his palms. His soles are three times as thick as those of any boots, calloused into a rubbery, horn-like substance that has grown around the iron studs of the catwalks. He moves with a peculiar, shuffling slide, never lifting his feet more than an inch, using his toes to feel the vibration of the plates. From this hum, he knows the health of the city. A high-pitched, singing rattle in the copper lines tells him the public fountains in the Grand Plaza are running full; a low, dull throb means the tanners three streets over are dumping their vats.

His quarters are a shallow niche carved into the basalt behind the main condensation pump, where the air is slightly drier but smells intensely of hot tallow and wet hemp. His bed is not a mattress but three layers of heavy, grease-soaked canvas conveyor belts, salvaged from an old elevator hoist. They are impervious to the damp. At the foot of his bed is his stove: a raw iron pipe-tee that leaks steam through a hand-crinkled lead gate. On this, he heats his tins of chicory and the "oil-crust," a heavy, sourdough flatbread he bakes on the bare steam-jacket of the piston. The bread is yellow, heavy as clay, and gets its leavening from the wild yeast that grows in the damp hemp joints of the ceiling.

These yeasts are another life entirely. They do not look like the gray molds of a cellar. Encouraged by the ninety-five-degree heat and the constant mist of lard-vapor from the pump glands, they have formed massive, liver-colored lobes that hang from the brass collars of the pipes like dried fruit. The Keeper calls them "grease-tongues." They are rubbery, slightly sweet, and cold to the touch. If sliced thin with his scraper and dried on the hot-water return pipe, they turn into a tough, salt-rimed jerky that keeps his gums from bleeding.

Beneath the Keeper, in the dark, lime-choked muck of the floor basins, live the *silt-feeders*. 

They are water-leeches, but they have grown thick and pale as sausages on the nitrogen-rich tallow runoff from the city’s soap-works. They have no eyes, no suckers, and no teeth; they are simply muscular tubes of pinkish-white flesh that lie half-buried in the black ooze, their rear sphincters twitching in the current. They feed by drawing the lime-silt through their bodies, stripping it of grease and human hair before spitting out the clean, white sand. The Keeper does not step in their pools if he can help it, not because they bite—they cannot—but because their skin is so slick with grease-mucus that they make the stones as treacherous as ice.

The isopods and the walnut-sized beetles treat the Keeper as nothing more than a slow-moving iron column. When he sits on the bottom step of the catwalk to clean his scraper, they crawl over his leathered shins in silent, twitching rivers. They are looking for the salt in his tallow-ointment. He does not brush them off. Occasionally, when the crawling becomes too thick, he will scrape a handful of them from his thigh and drop them into his soup tin; they taste of nothing but scale-remover and iron-rust, but they have a pleasant, wet crunch that breaks the monotony of his bread.

The most delicate things in the chamber are the *gland-threads*. They live only within the packing boxes of the giant steam valves, where the hot hemp packing is squeezed tight by brass gland-nuts. They are nematodes, thin as horsehairs and three feet long, that have abandoned the soil to live in the high-pressure steam. They find their way down from the city parks through the condensate lines, tiny and invisible, but once inside the hot, oily hemp, they grow. They weave themselves into the packing, their bodies filling the microscopic gaps where steam would otherwise leak. 

The Keeper guards them. When he repacks a valve, he does not throw the old, greasy hemp away; he carefully shakes it over the new packing, whispering to the threads in a language that has only five vowels and no consonants—a soft, wet whistling that matches the hiss of the exhaust valve. If the threads die, the valves leak green steam that smells of scorched hair, and the city’s cellar floors begin to sweat.

There is no time down here, only the forty-eight-second piston stroke. The Keeper’s heart has, after forty years, slowed itself to match. His pulse sits at thirty-eight beats a minute. When he sleeps, his breathing is a long, wet rasp that falls exactly into the silent intervals between the pump’s *clack-thump*. He does not dream of trees or clouds, for he has no words for them anymore. When he thinks of the world above, he thinks of it only as a vast, heavy reservoir of wet stone and heavy sewers, pressuring down upon his vaulted ceiling, waiting for him to let his fire go out so it can fill his room with cold, black water.
