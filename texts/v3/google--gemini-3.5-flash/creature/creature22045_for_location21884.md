To understand what lives in this light-saturated vault, one must first abandon the concept of the soft cell. Here, in the heavy, iron-choked brine, the boundary between biology and metallurgy has been worn thin by the continuous, humic drive of the earth's battery. The creatures that populate this cathedral-sized machine are heavy, cold to the touch, and chemically integrated into the very system that houses them.

### The Scraping Horde (The Chiton-Amalgams)

The millions of segmented, clockwork-like crawlers that cover the basalt columns are known collectively to the room’s ecology as the *Amalgams*. They are about the size of a human fist, though they weigh as much as a iron fishing sinker. 

Their outer shells are not grown from protein; they are electro-plated. As the juvenile Amalgam grows, it does not shed its skin. Instead, it acts as a tiny, living cathode. It coordinates its position on the basalt floor to recruit copper and zinc ions directly from the brine, depositing them in thin, overlapping layers of brass and bronze. This shell is a work of slow, functional art—striated with defensive ridges that align perfectly with the magnetic flux of the room, directing the dangerous, high-voltage currents *around* their soft, oil-rich interiors and back into the basalt.

Underneath these bronze shields, their bodies are a dense map of dark, sulfur-scented muscle and silver-threaded nerves. They possess no eyes, but where their heads should be, they bear a row of six small, sensitive iron-sand pits—sub-surface compasses that twitch and roll in response to the changing galvanic current. 

Their life-cycle is defined by the scraping of the walls. By stripping the manganese dendrites and pyrite flakes from the stone, they prevent the electrical poles of the vault from "fouling." If they were to stop, the mineral deposits would bridge the gap between the copper wall and the quartz crystals within a few decades, short-circuiting the entire cavern and plunging it into cold, dead darkness. The Amalgams are the maintenance staff of the battery; they eat the residue of the current, and in doing so, they keep the current flowing.

When an Amalgam dies, its soft parts are quickly reclaimed by the brine’s high chemical activity, leaving behind a hollow, heavy bronze husk. Over decades, these empty shells are tumbled by the slow currents until they lose their segmentations, becoming smooth, golden pebbles that pave the deep trenches between the basalt pillars.

### The Siphon-Barrels (The Bellows-Beasts)

If the Amalgams are the gears of this vault, the *Bellows-Beasts* are its slow, drifting pendulums. 

Measuring several feet across, these heavy, barrel-shaped creatures are made of a dense, dark-grey cartilage that has been vulcanized by the high sulfur content of the water. Their hides are as tough as industrial gaskets, ribbed to allow them to compress and expand their bodies without tearing. 

They do not swim so much as they adjust their displacement. By shifting the volume of their internal gas-bladders—which are filled not with air, but with hydrogen gas harvested from the electrolysis of the water—they rise and fall through the brine with the agonizing deliberateness of hot air balloons. 

Their primary sensory organs are the long, trailing copper wires that extend from their rear. These are not hair or keratin, but living lipid-sheathed copper filaments that they drag along the basalt floor. Through these lines, they "hear" the deep, screaming friction of the stone pillars below. They navigate by the music of the earth's crust; a sharp, high-pitched grind indicates a rising pillar to be avoided, while a low, bass vibration suggests a wide, slow basin where the metallic dust settles in thick, nutritious drifts.

The Bellows-Beasts feed by inhaling the brine through a wide, circular siphon at their anterior end. Inside their throat lies a series of fine, closely packed plates made of a silver-rich alloy. As the brine is pumped through, the microscopic metallic flakes suspended in the water adhere to these plates. When the plates are full, the beast secretes a highly acidic bile that dissolves the minerals, absorbing the metallic ions directly into its iron-rich, black blood.

### The Silver-Veil (The Dissolver)

The dark secret of this light-filled room is the *Silver-Veil*, the vault’s apex predator. It does not look like an animal. To an untrained eye, it appears to be a loose, drifting sheet of tarnished silver foil, perhaps three yards square, billowing lazily in the brine like a discarded silk shroud.

The Veil has no bones, no legs, and no distinct organs. It is a colonial super-organism consisting of billions of flagellated, metal-binding cells that share a single, distributed nervous system. It drifts through the open brine, completely silent, its surface shimmering with the pale green light of the quartz walls.

It hunts by grounding. 

When the Silver-Veil locates a cluster of Amalgams or a slow-drifting Bellows-Beast, it does not bite. Instead, it drapes its broad, conductive body over them. Because the Veil’s skin is highly conductive, it momentarily alters the electrical resistance of the space it occupies. It acts as a bridge, drawing a concentrated spike of the room's galvanic current directly through its prey.

For a Bellows-Beast, the result is instantaneous cardiac stall and systemic electrolysis; its internal fluids boil in a fraction of a second. For the copper-shielded Amalgams, the shock melt-welds their delicate jointed legs to the basalt floor, rendering them immobile. The Veil then secretes an extremely concentrated solution of hydrochloric acid from its underside, slowly liquifying the soft, sulfurous tissues of its victims and drawing the nutrients up through its silver-leaf membrane. 

Once fed, the Veil shimmers with a greasy, pearlescent luster, its edges curling inward as it retreats to the dark, high-ceiling corners of the vault to digest in the shadows of the quartz needles.

### The Litho-Parasites (The Solder-Anemones)

Where the copper wall meets the basalt floor, in the areas of maximum electrical potential, live the *Solder-Anemones*. 

These are sessile organisms that resemble clumps of melted lead or pewter wire. They do not have mouths or digestive tracts. Instead, they are completely autotrophic, surviving entirely on the electrical current they siphon from the copper wall. 

Each anemone consists of a central, cup-shaped base made of lead-sulfide (galena) that is anchored directly into the native copper. From this cup, hundreds of fine, hair-like silver tentacles extend into the flow of the ice-cold brine. These tentacles act as tiny, individual cathodes. They utilize the constant voltage of the room to reduce carbon dioxide and sulfur compounds dissolved in the water, synthesizing simple sugars and proteins directly in their metallic tissues.

They are the only light-producing organisms in the vault. When the galvanic current shifts and the cavern dims to its pale green hue, the Solder-Anemones begin to glow with a soft, steady, blue phosphorescence. It is not a signal or a lure, but a waste product—the release of excess electrical energy that their copper-insulated bodies can no longer store. During these shifts, the copper wall looks like a cliffside covered in thousands of tiny, cobalt-blue stars.

### The Rhythm of the Vault

Life here is beautiful, but it is a sterile, heavy sort of beauty. There is no playfulness, no courtship displays, and no waste. Every action is calculated against the cost of resistance and the availability of voltage. 

When the great basalt pillars groan and shift, the animals do not flee; they adjust their orientation by fractions of a millimeter, aligning themselves to the new magnetic lines of force. They are as much a part of this cavern's machinery as the quartz-ice roof or the iron walls. If you were to remove them, the vault would choke on its own growth within a century, its great battery dying of deposit-clogged terminals, leaving nothing but a dark, silent chamber of cold metal, frozen forever in the heart of the mountain.
