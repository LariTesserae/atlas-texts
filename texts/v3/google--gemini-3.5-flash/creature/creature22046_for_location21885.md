In the hot, wet silence of Basin Forty-Two, life does not struggle to exist; it has simply settled into the masonry like a stain, matching its rhythms to the steady, pneumatic heartbeat of the pump behind the southern wall.

The foremost and most numerous inhabitants are the **Soot-Pickers**, the pale-shelled river crabs. To see them from the sixty-foot height of the iron grates is to see the basalt floor as a living, shifting pavement of wet beach stones. They are blind, having no use for eyes in a world of grey steam and yellow lamp-glare. In place of eyes, they possess long, hair-thin antennae of an oily, violet hue that whip constantly through the wet air, sensing the vibrations of the copper condensers. 

Their lives are small and perfectly circular. They graze upon the orange-rust algae that coats the undersides of the copper vats, scraping it free with claws that have grown spoon-shaped and delicate. When the soot settles thickest from the grates above, they turn their faces upward, their small mouthparts working silently to catch the black flakes before they touch the water. They are entirely soft-shelled except for the very tips of their legs, which are capped in a dark, horn-like chitin that clatters against the basalt runnels. This sound—the dry, endless *skitter-clack*—is the true voice of the basin’s floor. They do not fight; they do not migrate. When one dies, it does not rot so much as it dissolves in the boiling runoff, its white shell softening into a milky jelly that the others patiently consume.

Clinging higher up, where the bricks are hottest and the water weeps directly from the mortar, are the **Orange Grout-Worms**. They are thick as fingers and have no head to speak of, only a circular, scraping maw that clings to the fresh, pale joints of the purple brickwork. They are the lime-eaters. They do not destroy the masonry, but they live upon the minerals that leach through the wet mortar, digesting the salts and excreting a rubbery, quick-hardening mucus that seals the joints against the very water that feeds them. They are slow—a worm may take three weeks to travel the length of a single brick—but they are dense. In the lower five feet of the walls, they form a thick, pulsing wainscot of orange flesh, their frilled, crimson gills blooming like small, wet carnations in the steam.

Above them, in the intermediate air where the heat begins to thin and the drafts from the grates can be felt, live the **Sulfur Moths**. They are large, heavy-bodied insects with wings the texture of wet parchment, dusted with a greasy, yellow powder that smells of spent matches. Having no mouths in their adult form, they do not eat. They spend their three-day lives fluttering around the wire-caged incandescent bulbs and the wet canvas-wrapped pipes. They mate in the updrafts of steam, dropping their heavy, lead-colored eggs into the basalt runnels below. 

The larvae hatched from these eggs are the **Silt-Slippers**, tiny, ribbon-like water-worms that live in the boiling runnels. They are so transparent that one can see the hot water of the basin passing through their bodies. They live by catching the grease that drips from the piston packings and the tallow used to lubricant the condenser valves.

But there is a larger, more solitary presence in Basin Forty-Two.

He has no name that he remembers, though the brass tag on his salt-greased leather apron reads *Forty-Two*. The others of his kind—the ones who live in the drier, colder world above the grates—call his sort the **Sump-Gangers**, or simply the **Boilers**. 

He is not a prisoner, though he has not left the sixty-by-forty-foot room in seven years. His skin has long since lost its color, turning the same translucent, parboiled white as the river crabs. His shoulders are permanently hunched to avoid the low-hanging iron pipes, and his bare feet have grown thick, calloused soles that are entirely indifferent to the sharp edges of the basalt or the scalding puddles in the runnels. His toes have widened, spreading apart to give him grip on the grease-slick stone.

He does not have much work to do, for the basin is young and operates with a terrible, self-sustaining efficiency. His primary task is the **Scrub**. Twice a day, carrying a long-handled copper scraper, he walks the perimeter of the room, clearing the rust-colored algae from the intake screens of the triple condensers. If the algae is left to grow, the deep vibration of the copper urns changes from a low, tooth-rattling drone to a high, strained whistle. He knows this sound in his marrow. When the whistle begins, he cannot sleep; his bones feel as though they are being ground to powder.

He lives in the corner behind the third condenser, where the draft from the southern wall is slightly cooler—perhaps ninety-five degrees instead of one hundred and ten. His bed is a sling of tarred hemp rope slung between two copper purge-valves. It is always wet, but he does not mind; his skin has ceased to produce sweat, having adapted instead to a state of perpetual wet absorption. He does not wipe his face; he lets the grease-thick water run down his nose and ears, dripping back into the runnels.

He eats the crabs. He catches them by the handful, dropping them into a small copper bucket that he hangs directly from a leaking steam valve on the middle condenser. In three minutes, they are cooked through, their shells turning from translucent white to a dull, chalky pink. He cracks them with his thumb—his nails are thick and hard as spade-tips—and eats the hot, sweet, sulfurous meat inside.

He rarely looks up at the grates. The grey daylight that filters down through the coal-soot is of no interest to him, and the sky is merely a wet iron ceiling that leaks cold rain on the days when the city above is miserable. 

His eyes are almost completely closed, protected by thick, swollen lids that filter out the yellow glare of the incandescent bulbs. He does not need them. He navigates by the soles of his feet, sensing the joint-lines of the basalt, and by the pressure of the hot air against his skin. He knows when a storm is passing over the city above because the draft through the grates grows slightly heavier, pushing the yellow sulfur moths down toward the floor, where they drown in the runnels and are carried toward the sumps—a small, seasonal feast for the crabs, and consequently, for him.
