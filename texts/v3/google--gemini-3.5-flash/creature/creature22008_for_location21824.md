To understand what lives in this world, one must first discard the concept of a womb, an egg, or a pod. In a landscape that has never known a seed, there is no birth that begins with a soft, green swelling. Nothing here unfolds from a germ of potential. 

Instead, life is extruded, crystallized, or assembled by the very forces that maintain the plumbing of the continent.

### The Tesselates

The ivory-colored shield-creatures that carpet the floor—known to the silence as the *Tesselates*—are not born of one another. They are the children of the seams. 

Beneath the shallow, warm water, the thick black caulking that seals the nickel-iron plates is not inert. It is a dense, organic petroleum-wax, kept soft by the 28-degree warmth of the metal. Every few years, in the quiet spaces between the columns, a seam will begin to swell. A small, dark bubble of the grease rises above the waterline, hardening as it meets the humid air. Inside this dark pod, a chemical crystallization begins. The mineral-rich runoff—heavy with silicate and lime—seeps into the bubble, where it is layered down by the ambient electric current humming through the floor.

Over months, a porcelain dome forms within the caulking, smooth and tooth-white. When it is complete, the black wax splits, releasing a new Tesselate. 

It does not have organs in the sense of heart or liver. Under its porcelain shield lies a grey, solid mass of starch-like grease, dense as tallow, run through with a system of fine glass capillaries. Its leg-bundles are stiff, hair-like fibers of pure silica. They do not flex with muscle, but with the subtle thermal differences between the wet nickel plate below and the warm water above. 

A Tesselate can remain in one spot for eighty years, its tiny vents pulsing with the slow, hydraulic rhythm of the floor-run. It has no brain to think of the columns, nor eyes to see the white glass sky. It knows only three things: the warm slip of the metal beneath its feet, the chemical taste of the water, and the immense, comforting shoulder of its neighbor.

When a Tesselate ceases to function—when its internal capillaries clog with zinc scale—it does not rot. It simply stops sliding. The others do not mourn it, nor do they consume it. But because the floor is always in motion, the dead Tesselate becomes a stationary island. Over decades, the millions of silicon-shod feet of its passing neighbors wear its porcelain dome down. It is polished, rubbed, and thinned, until it is nothing but a translucent wafer, and eventually, a fine white powder that dissolves into the alkaline stream, returning to the water that made it.

### The Columns and the Ribbons

High above the floor, where the copper columns climb into the warm mist, the world belongs to a different sort of inhabitant: the *Ribbons*. 

These are long, flat bands of a dull, lead-grey material, like strips of old inner-tube rubber, that cling vertically to the fluted copper. They have no legs or suction cups; they hold fast through capillary action, sticking to the wet, weeping metal in the same way a wet ribbon of paper sticks to a windowpane. 

The Ribbons are immense—some are forty feet long but no thicker than a leather belt—and they are almost perfectly still. They live where the condensation is heaviest, absorbing the dissolved copper salts that weep from the metal. Their bodies are saturated with copper carbonate, giving them a faint, poisonous green tint along their margins when the flat light strikes them from certain angles.

They do not move to find food. Instead, they wait for the *Drift*, the microscopic flakes of zinc and bismuth that break off from the mineral dendrites below and are carried upward by the rising warm drafts. When these tiny, glittering crystals stick to the wet surface of a Ribbon, the creature’s skin slowly folds over them, dissolving them in an acid that smells of sour milk and batteries.

If you were to watch a Ribbon for a week, you would see no movement at all. But over a year, you would see that it is slowly, imperceptibly dragging itself upward against the current of the weeping column, leaving a clean, bright track of bare copper behind it, stripped of all oxide and grime. They are the cleaners of the pillars, keeping the copper bright so the water can sheet down without obstruction.

### The Glass-Glovers

At the very top of the world, where the columns disappear into the ribbed glass ceiling, the air is dry and smells of hot sand and ozone. Here, suspended directly beneath the glowing panels, live the *Glass-Glovers*.

These are the only things in this world that are soft. They resemble thick, translucent sacks of amber-colored grease, each about the size of a human torso. They do not cling to the glass; they are wedged into the structural channels between the panes, held in place by their own sticky, jelly-like skin. 

Inside their clear bodies, one can see a slow, yellow phosphorescing light that pulses once every few minutes. They are the only creatures that utilize the light of the ceiling. They do not eat; they are phototrophic, absorbing the constant, sourceless noon from the glass above and converting it into a heavy, sweet-smelling oil that fills their interior cavities.

Because they have no mouths, they have no way to discharge this oil except through slow perspiration. As they grow old and saturated, the oil seeps through their skin and hangs in heavy, clear droplets from their bottoms. Eventually, these droplets become too heavy and fall through the two miles of mist. 

As the drops fall, they cool, turning into tiny, soft pellets of yellow wax that land in the water below with a soft *plop*. 

To the Tesselates on the floor, this falling wax is the only change their world ever experiences. When a pellet lands, the nearest Tesselates slide toward it, their silicon feet clicking in a brief, slow-motion scramble. They do not eat the wax, but they surround it, pressing their porcelain rims against it until the warmth of their bodies melts it into the nickel plates, forming a fresh, waterproof seal over the joints that may have begun to wear thin.

### The Solder-Grubs

In the rare, dry corners of the world—inside the hollows of the copper columns or along the undersides of the iron maintenance gantries that have escaped the water—there are the *Solder-Grubs*. 

They are the size of pinecones, but their bodies are made of dense, overlapping scales of dark grey lead. Unlike the other inhabitants of this world, they are completely dry, and they move with a mechanical, jerky gait that sounds like a handful of small bolts rattling in a bucket.

The Grubs are the only things that possess a form of sight. On the front of their lead carapaces are three small, oily lenses of red quartz. They do not see shapes, but they are highly sensitive to the blue-white arcs of static electricity that occasionally leap across the maintenance frames or the high-power junctions. 

Where the current leaks, the Grubs gather. They crawl onto the wires and the joints, their copper-tipped mandibles chewing slowly at the white zinc corrosion that builds up around the electric connections. They are the gardeners of the current, keeping the pathways clear so the deep, sub-audible hum in the floor never falters.

They do not die of age. When a Grub has consumed enough metal, its joints stiffen. It crawls into a corner where two iron beams meet, presses its body flat against the weld, and simply ceases to move. Over time, the heat of the gantry melts the lead of its shell back into the seam, fusing it permanently to the structure, becoming a new rivet in a world that never stops building itself.
