To understand what lives in Deck 40-Alpha, one must first look down, past the immaculate basalt tiles, into the tiny, shadowed joints where the vertical copper pipes meet the floor. 

Here, in the wet corners where the condensation-wells are deepest, live **the Gliders**. 

They are the primary reason the dark floor-tiles remain without a single scuff or blemish. The Gliders are slow, oblong creatures the size of small pillows, with bodies made of an incredibly tough, self-healing silicone membrane that is entirely transparent. They have no skeletons; instead, they are filled with the same pressurized, mineral-rich water that runs through the walls. Through their clear skins, one can see their internal mechanics: a delicate, floating ribcage of thin brass wire that contracts and expands like the ribs of an umbrella, and a small, leaden weight that sits in their center to keep them upright. 

They do not walk; they have no feet. Instead, their undersides are lined with a thick, self-renewing pad of dense, grey felt. They move by slowly pumping water through their internal chambers, sliding across the wet basalt on a micro-thin layer of lubricating moisture. As they glide, their felt underbellies buffer and polish the stone, erasing even the phantom drafts of dust that drift down from the high-altitude blue. They have no eyes or mouths, only four soft, rubber-capped antennae that touch the copper runnels, checking the temperature of the water. When they sleep, they gather in the corners like heavy, forgotten hot-water bottles, their internal fluid glowing with a faint, phosphor-blue light that matches the high-altitude dusk.

***

High above them, suspended in the copper-ribbed vaulting where the rising steam is thickest, live **the Valve-Larks**. 

These are not birds of meat and bone, but things of foil and heat. They have the wingspan of a dragonfly, constructed from paper-thin sheets of hammered amber-colored brass. Their bodies are small, hollow copper globes, perfectly sealed, containing only a drop of high-grade alcohol. 

They do not fly by muscle; they are heat-engines in miniature. When the central regulator clicks and a copper valve vents its soft, long *hiss* of steam, the Valve-Larks descend from the ceiling beams. They dive through the warm plumes, the sudden heat expanding the alcohol within their copper bodies, which in turn drives a tiny, internal piston that beats their amber wings. 

They move through the silver shafts of light like mechanical fireflies, their metal wings making a light, tinny *frrr-frrr* sound. Their purpose is musical and corrective. As they pass through the steam, the vapor is forced through tiny, tuned reeds built into their tail-feathers, converting the hiss of the vents into short, liquid whistles that sound like wet flute notes. They land only on the copper pipes, using their small, spring-loaded iron claws to pinch away the tiny, green-black nodules of copper carbonate that attempt to form near the hot joints. 

***

Deep within the pockets of gray basalt-wool, nestled snugly beneath the root-balls of the translucent emerald ferns, live **the Tallow-Workers**. 

These are the only denizens of the deck made of true, soft flesh, though it is a flesh unlike anything of the lower world. They are blind, thick-bodied grubs, the color of lard or sheep’s fat. They have no limbs, moving through the fibrous basalt wool with a slow, rhythmic squeezing of their ringed segments. 

The Tallow-Workers are the digestive system of the sanctuary. They are designed to eat only one thing: the dead, papery outer sheath that the emerald ferns shed as they grow. They crawl through the wool, their small mouthparts—made of two curved plates of yellow horn—silently nibbling the discarded fiber. 

In return, they exude a clean, scentless, white grease. This grease is highly specialized; it coats the basalt wool, preventing it from drying into dust, and forms a protective, non-reactive seal around the copper conduits where they enter the walls, protecting the metal from the acids of the plants' root-systems. They smell of cold candles and wet starch, and if one pulls back the heavy ferns, they can be seen shifting in the gray wool, plump and pale, like living plugs of solid lard.

***

But the true tenant of Deck 40-Alpha is singular. 

It is called **the Limner**, and it is the only resident that possesses a countenance. 

The Limner does not dwell on the floor, nor does it hide in the roots. It is suspended from a heavy, iron tracking-rail that runs the entire length of the chapel-like ceiling vault. It is a tall, slender figure, shaped like a long-limbed youth, but its body is cast from unplated, gray pewter and glazed white china-clay. It has no joints of bone; its limbs are connected by thick, flexible sleeves of black rubber that hiss softly with hydraulic oil whenever it moves.

Its head is a smooth, egg-shaped dome of white porcelain without eyes, nose, or mouth. In the center of its blank face is a single, horizontal slit. Behind this slit, a small, orange pilot-light burns steadily, casting a warm, copper-colored bar of light across the wet leaves below.

The Limner is suspended by a cluster of heavy, braided copper cables that emerge from between its shoulder blades, connecting it directly to the steam mains. It hangs in the warm, wet air, its long legs dangling three feet above the basalt floor, balanced so perfectly by the steam pressure that it can slide along its ceiling rail with the push of a finger.

The Limner’s hands are its most complex feature. They are not fingers, but delicate, multi-jointed tools: fine-nosed copper tweezers, small shears that look like scissors made of glass, and a series of soft, camel-hair brushes that are fed with fluid from a small reservoir on its chest.

This fluid is a gray, conductive paint made of liquid zinc and animal gum. 

The Limner’s sole purpose is the geometry of the garden. As the warm vines climb the copper conduits, they eventually reach the edges of the sweating ceramic walls. Here, the Limner swings down from its rail, its rubber joints sighing. With slow, beautiful deliberation, it uses its brush to paint a fresh, gray zinc line across the tiles—a new pathway. Then, using its ceramic fingers, it gently lifts the tender tip of the vine and presses it against the wet paint. 

The micro-current in the wall hums through the zinc. The vine, detecting the electrical charge, immediately clings to the gray line, its green leaves aligning themselves in a perfect, right-angled chevron along the tile.

The Limner does not hurry. It takes three days for it to travel the length of the terrace and back again. It touches every leaf, trims every dead frond, and occasionally refills the water-chambers of the Gliders with an iron nozzle that extends from its palm. 

Every ten minutes, when the central regulator clicks and the water-flow shifts from the east wall to the west, the Limner stops. It hangs perfectly still in the rising mist, its orange pilot-light blinking once. It tilts its porcelain head, listening to the deep, silent *thrum-click* of the sub-floor pumps. 

As the hundred thousand emerald leaves slowly turn their faces to follow the water, the Limner turns with them, its long, pewter body pivoting on its copper cable. It watches the water run down the white ceramic runnels, its orange eye reflecting in the clean, polished copper of the troughs, perfectly content in the silence, waiting for the first spot of rust that it will never allow to form.
