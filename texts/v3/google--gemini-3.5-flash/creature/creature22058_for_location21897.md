To live in the cleft is to be made of the cleft: to be heavy, cold, and slow-beating, shaped by an eternity of damp charcoal-darkness. 

At the bottom of this world are the **grease-coils**—the blind, air-breathing snails that graze upon the weeping vertical meadows of the liverwort. They are the slowest things in a world where nothing is fast. Their shells are not spirals of armor but flat, lumpy disks the color of wet tendon, so translucent that the slow, gray pulse of their single lung can be seen beneath the lime. They do not have eyes; instead, two pairs of thick, fleshy horns extend from their brows, constantly tasting the cold moisture for the chemical signature of the copper-mosses. They move by a process that is less like crawling and more like a deliberate, chemical softening: they secrete a mucus so thick and alkaline that it slightly melts the surface of the black peat beneath them, allowing them to slide forward on a microscopic canal of their own making. This mucus never dries; it remains behind them as a faint, silvery grease-trail that other creatures use as highways through the dense green moss-shingles.

Upon these trails walk the **silver-striders**. These are the wingless midges, though they resemble them only in their basic anatomy. They are the size of a dried willow leaf, but their bodies are nearly weightless, constructed of a grey, paper-thin chitin that does not absorb water. Their six legs, twice as long as their bodies and as thin as spun glass, end in tiny paddle-shaped brushes of oil-slick hair. With these, they do not merely walk; they suspension-skim over the deep, black pools and the wet slopes of the moss-ropes. They have no mouthparts for biting; their lives are spent harvesting the invisible, floating spores of the liverwort and the microscopic yeast that blooms on the skin of the salamanders. Their movements are so silent and robotic that a dozen of them may climb over a sleeping salamander’s snout without triggering the twitch of a single nostril. They do not die by predation; rather, when their long, slow clockwork finally runs down after three or four years, they simply tip sideways into the still pools, where their chitinous bodies slowly dissolve into the water, sweetening it with trace minerals.

The **slate-skins**—the thousands of pale-spotted salamanders that pave the floor—are the great, silent engine of the grotto’s temperature. They are not warm-blooded, yet their bodies are slightly, almost imperceptibly warmer than the basalt beneath them—perhaps half a degree. Packed belly-to-back, three or four layers deep in the crevices, they form a vast, living insulation. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and smells faintly of wet iron and cold lard. It is marked with pale, milk-blue spots that seem to hold the dim twilight long after the sky above has gone black. 

They breathe through their skin as much as through their tiny, internal lungs. Once every ten or twelve minutes, a ripple passes through a clump of them—a collective, shallow expansion of their flanks, like a single breath taken by a hundred bodies at once. They do not hunt with the quick, snapping tongue of common newts. Instead, when a silver-strider or a small, pale stone-louse crawls within an inch of their snout, the slate-skin simply opens its mouth with a slow, hydraulic widening. The prey is drawn in by the slight vacuum of the inhalation, swallowed whole into a stomach that takes three weeks to digest a single insect. 

They lay their eggs not in the water, but in the deepest, coldest fissures of the peat. The eggs are large, clear globes of gel, tough as soft gristle, containing a single, dark-grey embryo that does not hatch into a swimming tadpole. Instead, the young develop entirely within the egg, their tiny, feather-like gills absorbing oxygen from the damp air through the jelly wall, until they emerge as fully formed, miniature slate-skins, already slow and patient, ready to take their place in the living shingles of the floor.

Above them all, occupying the small, dripping niches of the walls, are the **silt-weavers**. 

They are the only creatures in the grotto with limbs designed for grasping, though they use them with a heavy, meditative economy. A weaver is no larger than a small rabbit, but its body is long and low, more like a ferret or a star-nosed mole that has adapted to a life of sheer vertical suspension. Their fur is their most remarkable feature: it is a dense, greasy white wool, so thick that if one were to submerge a weaver in the black pools, it would return to the air dry, wrapped in a shimmering sleeve of trapped atmosphere. This grease has the scent of mutton tallow and wet flour, a clean, heavy smell that dominates the small alcoves where they nest.

Lacking eyes, their faces are blunt and conical, dominated by an extraordinary array of sensitive tactile organs. Where their eyes should be are two shallow, star-shaped depressions lined with fine, silver bristles that can detect the path of a condensation-drop sliding down a rock ten feet away. Their ears are internal, protected by flaps of thick, hairless skin, but their long, hairless snout is tipped with a pink, five-lobed nose-star that is constantly wet, tasting the air for the scent of the grease-coils.

Their hands are wonders of skeletal simplicity. They have three fingers on each hand, two of them long and double-jointed, ending in thick, curved claws of black keratin that they use to hook themselves into the basalt pores. The third finger is short and thick, functioning as a thumb. 

A weaver’s day—if day can be said to exist in the wet dusk—is lived in a series of slow, geometric postures. For hours, a weaver will hang from a moss-rope by its hind claws, its body perfectly still, its white wool blending with the hanging mist. It is waiting for a grease-coil snail to crawl within reach of its long, three-fingered arm. When the snail is found, there is no struggle. The weaver’s long fingers reach out, their spade-like nails sliding beneath the snail’s foot, breaking the vacuum seal with a soft, wet sound.

The weaver then retreats to its alcove-hearth. Here lies the **anvil**—a flat, wedge-shaped shard of basalt, worn smooth by centuries of use. Beside it sits the **hammer**, a water-rounded pebble of white quartz, heavy and cold, its white face scarred by thousands of tiny, crescent-shaped bruises where it has struck the dark basalt. 

Using its three-fingered grip, the weaver lifts the quartz pebble. It does not swing it with the force of an angry beast; rather, it raises the hammer precisely three inches and lets the weight of the stone fall upon the snail’s shell. *Thunk.* The sound is damp, flat, and instantly absorbed by the surrounding liverwort. If the shell does not crack, the weaver rises another inch. *Thunk.* 

When the shell gives way, the weaver uses its long, delicate middle finger to scoop out the pale, cold meat inside. It eats with its mouth closed, its small, blunt teeth grinding the flesh into a paste before swallowing. The broken pieces of the shell are then taken and placed in a neat, semi-circular heap behind the anvil. These shell-heaps are the only history the grotto possesses; some of them are three feet deep, the bottom-most shells having long since dissolved into the grey clay, their calcium binding the peat into a floor as solid as limestone.

The weavers do not play. They do not struggle for dominance. When two weavers meet on a narrow ledge of basalt, they do not fight; instead, they press their wet snout-stars together for several minutes, tasting each other’s grease and the mineral content of the water they have been drinking. Having verified each other’s presence in the world, they slowly back away, one climbing over the other with a ginger, respectful placement of claws, until both have returned to their respective stations.

When a weaver dies, it does so with the same quiet orderliness that governs its life. Feeling its limbs grow stiff and its white fur dry out as its grease-glands fail, it climbs down from its wall-alcove for the last time. It does not seek the company of others, nor does it hide. It simply finds a space at the edge of one of the deep pools, curls its three-fingered hands inward toward its chest, and lets its breath slow down from once every five minutes to once an hour, then once a day, until the cold of the grotto becomes the cold of its body. 

Its companions do not touch it. Within days, the slate-skins have crawled over its white fur, their smooth bodies keeping the corpse cool and preventing any rot. Within months, the silver-striders have harvested the grease from its wool. Within a decade, the liverworts have grown through the ribcage, their dark, copper-colored lobes anchoring themselves in the decaying marrow, until the weaver is nothing but a slightly paler mound in the green carpet, a small, soft hill on which a new generation of grease-coils will graze.

In this way, the grotto breathes. It is a single, slow-pulsing organism made of stone, water, moss, and flesh, perfectly sealed within its basalt womb on the high shoulder of the mountain. Outside, the world may burn or freeze, empires may rise from the dust and return to it, but here, the quartz hammer will always fall with the same muffled *thunk*, the water-lobe will always swell to the size of a plum before falling, and the white-furred weavers will continue to sit at their dark stone tables, keeping their long, silent vigil in the everlasting green twilight.
