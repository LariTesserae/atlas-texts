To understand what lives in the Cradle of the White Plinths, one must understand that in a world without decay, the boundary between the living and the mineral is a thin, wet smudge. 

The inhabitants of this basin do not build the city; they *are* the city, in various stages of hardening.

***

### The Silt-Kneaders and the Glass-Glints

At the lowest level of life—crawling through the shallow, frigid pools where the log-aqueducts overflow—are the **Silt-Kneaders**. 

These are the infants of the basin, though they look less like children and more like wet, moving lumps of dough. Lacking bones, organs, or eyes, they are simply detached clots of the rising marrow-stone, animated by the intense, bouncing light and the rich mineral broth of the weeping walls. They are the size of river otters, white and perfectly smooth, with skin that has the texture of wet, freshly mixed plaster. 

They spend their first several decades on their bellies, dragging themselves through the pools. Their only purpose is to eat the soft, translucent ground-cover and the gray silt that settles in the cisterns. They have no teeth; they absorb nourishment through their skin, leaving tracks of pristine, scrubbed-clean stone behind them. When they crawl over one another, they do not fight; they merge temporarily, sharing moisture and minerals, before tearing apart again into individual, mute lumps. 

Living alongside them in the three-millimeter film of water that coats the vertical marrow-stone walls are the **Glass-Glints**. These are the closest things the basin has to insects, though they are entirely quiet. They are no larger than pine needles, made of a clear, flexible, silica-like keratin. They slide up and down the wet facades like living raindrops, moving against gravity. They feed on the microscopic mineral crystals that precipitate out of the weeping water. Their movements are erratic but slow, and when the sun hits them at a certain angle, they turn the vertical walls into sheets of diamond dust, throwing millions of tiny, shifting prisms across the white amphitheater.

***

### The Karst-Snails

Among the wicker scaffolding, the **Karst-Snails** are the keepers of the pace. 

These creatures are massive—some have shells the size of beer kegs—and their weight is immense. Their shells are not made of fragile calcium carbonate, but of a heavy, dense, non-porous porcelain that ring like iron bells if struck. They do not have the mottled browns of lowland snails; they are the color of skimmed milk, polished to a high, reflective shine by the friction of their long journeys.

The karst-snails are the stewards of the great moss-bolsters. If left unchecked, the aggressive, sheep-sized mosses would choke the wind-flutes of the wicker towers and drag the suspension bridges down with their weight. The snails crawl with agonizing, geological slowness along the hempen ropes—taking days to negotiate a single knot—rasping the excess moss away with their black, flint-hard tongues. 

They are the source of the basin’s only organic sound. When two snails meet on a narrow green-vine bridge, they cannot bypass one another. Instead, they press their heavy porcelain shells together. The friction produces a deep, vibrating *shre-e-e-kp* that travels through the bridge-lines, followed by a metallic *click* as one snail slowly, carefully climbs over the other’s back, a process that can occupy an entire sunrise and sunset.

***

### The Wrights (The Loom-Kin)

These are the builders of the wicker towers, the split-pine sky-walks, and the gravity-fed aqueducts. To a human observer, they are almost invisible, lost in the complexity of their own scaffolding.

A **Wright** is a towering, impossibly thin figure—often standing nine or ten feet tall, but weighing no more than an average dog. Their limbs are long and segmented, resembling cured willow branches wrapped in pale, wet leather. They have no head as we understand it; their necks simply taper into a hard, toothless bill made of porcelain-like bone, flanked by two large, circular ear-drums that look like dried skin stretched over tambourines. They have no eyes. In a world of white stone and blinding, reflected light, vision is a liability; they navigate entirely by vibration and touch.

The Wrights do not sleep, nor do they die in the manner of lowland animals. Because there is no rot, their bodies do not fail from infection or disease. Instead, they *petrify*. 

The lifecycle of a Wright is a slow transition from wet clay to hard stone:
1. **The Green Stage:** Emerging from the pools where the Silt-Kneaders gather, a young Wright is soft, flexible, and damp. Their body is highly elastic. They are the weavers. With fingers that are long, fibrous, and constantly secreting a sticky, latex-like sap, they can climb the slick marrow-stone walls and lash the great wicker pillars to the rock faces. They move with a fluid, rhythmic grace, their movements silent save for the wet *slap* of their skin against the stone.
2. **The Cured Stage:** After a century, the minerals in the weeping water begin to settle in the Wright’s joints. They become stiffer, stronger, and more deliberate. This is when they carve the empty dwellings into the marrow-stone. They use no tools other than their own hard, porcelain bills and their stone-tipped fingers, chipping away at the soft mineral knuckles to create the tiers of paths and cisterns. 
3. **The Static Stage:** Eventually—usually after three or four hundred years—the calcification reaches the Wright’s chest cavity. They can no longer move their limbs. When they feel this final stiffness setting in, a Wright will climb to a designated section of the scaffolding, wrap their long arms around a wicker pillar or a bridge joint, and let the weeping water wash over them for the last time. 

This is why the city appears to be "a city of ghosts and scaffolding." It is not empty; it is populated by the dead, who have become the very wood and stone they lived upon. 

If you look closely at the seventy-foot wicker towers, you will see that the vertical supports are not all pine; many are the long, dry, calcified spines of ancient Wrights, woven directly into the basket-work. The handrails of the suspension bridges are made of their interlocking fingers, petrified to a smooth, cool hardness that feels like jade. They do not bury their ancestors; they use them to reinforce the joints where the wind blows hardest.

***

### Their Culture of Sound

The Wrights do not speak, but they are not quiet. 

Their entire society is organized around the deep, whistling drone of the wicker towers. Each tower, woven differently, produces a specific pitch when the mountain wind pushes through its hollow interior. By tightening or loosening the leather cords that lash the towers to the stone, the Wrights can "tune" the basin. 

To the Wrights, this wind-song is a map, a library, and a communal mind. By pressing their chest cavities or their large ear-drums against the wet pine of a sky-bridge, they can feel the vibrations of the towers. A change in the wind's pitch tells them which aqueduct is clogged, which wicker column is suffering from joint-strain, or where a karst-snail is blockading a path. 

They live in a state of constant, resonant meditation. They will stand on the high bridges for weeks at a time, completely motionless, their skin turning the gray-white of the surrounding stone, simply "listening" to the mountain through their feet. 

There is no hunger here, no winter, and no decay. The plants grow forever; the stone rises forever; and the Wrights weave their ancestors into the air, building a ladder to a sky they can only hear.
