In the deep stillness of this high, drowned plaza, the life that remains has adapted to a world without weather, without seasons, and without gravity. 

Here, the distinction between the animal and the stone has begun to blur. The things that live here do not merely inhabit the architecture; they have become its upholstery, its cleaning staff, and its slow, blind custodians.

At the lowest level, clinging to the basalt floor, are the **Pressers**. These are flat, disk-shaped creatures resembling massive, eye-less chitons, each about the size of a dinner plate. Their shells are not hard, but have the texture of tanned, oil-soaked leather, patterned with faint, hexagonal ridges that mimic the basalt tiles beneath them. The Pressers spend their entire multi-century lives in a state of nearly imperceptible motion, sliding over the six-sided tiles at a rate of perhaps twelve inches a week. 

They are the vacuum-cleaners of the plaza. Underneath their leathery skirts, thousands of microscopic, cilia-covered mouthparts scrape the stone clean of the gray bryozoan mold and the calcified grime that constant water-drift deposits on the tiles. Where a Presser has passed, the basalt beneath is revealed for a few days in its original, dark luster before the microscopic carpet of life grows back over it. If two Pressers meet, they do not fight or turn away; they simply press their edges together and remain still for days, sharing warmth through their leathery margins before one slowly climbs over the other.

Within the joints of the masonry, where the hand-hewn basalt blocks lean together to form the walls, lives the **Red Lace**. 

These are not plants, though they look like fine, branching ferns of brilliant crimson. They are colonial hydroids that have nested in the deep, water-filled cracks where the mortar has turned to silt. They do not need light; they feed on the microscopic organic dust that filters down from the open water above. Because the water is so perfectly still, the Red Lace grows in long, delicate, horizontal fans that extend up to three feet out from the wall. They are incredibly sensitive to displacement. If one of the ribbon-like eyeless eels drifts too close, the tiny displacement of water ahead of its snout causes the entire fan of red lace to instantly liquefy—collapsing into a dribble of pale, defensive mucus that withdraws into the safety of the stone joint, only to reform hours later when the stillness returns.

Deep inside the terra-cotta pillars, where the thick rods of mammoth bone serve as structural pins, lives a highly specialized species of **Bone-Drifts**. 

Over millennia, these blind, white annelids have found their way through the porous clay of the drums to the ivory cores within. They do not destroy the bone; instead, they live in symbiotic patience with it, secreting a heavy, alkaline wax that slowly replaces the calcium they dissolve. The interior of each pillar has become a living marrow of these worms, their bodies intertwined in a dense, pale braid that runs from the floor to the capital of the column. They have no eyes and no limbs, but they possess a collective sensitivity to gravity. When the earth deep below the summit shifts—a tremor so deep and distant that it cannot be heard—the Bone-Drifts contract in unison, tightening the interior braid and reinforcing the pillars against the shock. They are the living rebar of the temple.

In the center of the chamber, suspended in the middle of the warm column of water, hovers **The Loom**.

The Loom is a single, ancient siphonophore, a colonial organism that has taken the shape of a grand, unraveling curtain. It is forty feet long, hanging vertically like a sheet of gray, translucent silk. It has no brain, no central heart, and no direction. It simply drifts where the slow, convective heat of the floor-tiles dictates. Its body is composed of thousands of specialized zooids: some are tiny, rhythmically pulsing bells that keep the curtain suspended; others are long, stinging threads as fine as spider silk that drape down toward the basalt floor. 

The Loom is the only predator of size in the basin, yet it hunts with zero aggression. It is a passive filter of the dark. When a school of the translucent shrimp wanders into its translucent folds, they are caught in a quiet, chemical sleep. The Loom does not swallow them; rather, its skin slowly grows over the prey, absorbing them where they hang, so that at any given time, one can see dozens of tiny, half-dissolved white shapes suspended within the creature’s watery body, like seeds frozen in ice.

Underneath the Loom, residing in the very center of the floor where a circular drain once allowed the basin to empty, sits **The Grandmother of the Well**.

This is a solitary, massive sea-squirt, or ascidian, that has grown to the size of a wine barrel. It is the oldest thing in the basin, its outer tunic so thick and encrusted with bryozoans, tube worms, and small sponges that it looks like a fallen fragment of a terra-cotta column. Indeed, it has grown so perfectly into the central drain that it has sealed it forever. 

The Grandmother has only two openings: a wide, leathery inhalation siphon at her top, which drinks the warm water in a slow, continuous draft, and a smaller, exhalation siphon at her side, which expels it in a gentle, warm jet. This single, silent pump is the heart of the entire plaza. It is what keeps the water moving, however slightly, ensuring that the oxygen does not pool in the corners and smother the white crabs. 

Inside the Grandmother’s hollow, fleshy body, the water is even warmer than the basin outside. This interior chamber is a nursery. There, in the dark, gelatinous womb of the ancient filter-feeder, hundreds of the tiny, blind white crabs spend their larval stages, safe from the eels, clinging to the Grandmother’s internal ribbing like treasure hidden in an old chest. When they are large enough to survive the cold indigo draft outside, they crawl out of her upper siphon one by one, drop to the basalt tiles, and begin their long, slow walk toward the edges of the pool.

And at the very edge, where the tiles end and the balustrade crumbles into the bottomless void, there are the **Gazer-Shells**.

These are large, stalked barnacles that grew on the outer lip of the masonry where the drop-off begins. They do not face inward toward the warm, safe plaza. Instead, their long, muscular necks—gray and tough as horsehide—curve outward over the abyss. At the end of each neck is a heavy, ivory-colored shell that gapes open like a parched mouth. 

They do not gather food from the plaza; they wait for the "marine snow"—the tiny, constant drift of organic debris that falls from the surface miles above into the deep ocean. They are the only creatures in the sanctuary that look away from it. When a rare, deep current rises from the abyssal plains below, bringing with it the cold, mineral-rich scent of the trenches, the Gazer-Shells click their ivory plates together in a dry, rhythmic chorus that ripples around the entire perimeter of the pool—a warning to the slow, warm things inside that there is a vast, cold world outside their bath, and that it is still empty.
