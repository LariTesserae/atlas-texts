To live in the Rift of the First Cleaving is to be a component in a machine that has no off switch. Because the geology here is still shifting, still grinding its raw edges into alignment, the things that dwell here do not adapt for stability; they adapt for velocity, friction, and regeneration.

### The Great Swarms and the Slime-Feasts

At the base of the food web are **The Drifts**—the blind, translucent midges that fill the air like a wet, gray blizzard. They do not have mouths in their adult form, nor do they have eyes. They are born as microscopic wrigglers in the greasy, yeast-scented dew that coats the rocks, rising into the air by the billions as soon as their wings dry. Their entire existence is a three-hour frenzy of flight, driven by the hot, sulfurous drafts. They do not mate individually; they collide in the air, drop their egg-packets into the boiling steam, and die, their tiny fat-rich bodies falling back to the stone like a continuous, greasy snow.

This snow of dead midges is what feeds the **Orange Boil**—the rubbery, miles-wide sheets of slime-mold. The mold is not a passive carpet. It is a slow, hydraulic muscle. When a heavy rain falls and the steam rises, the orange and black sheets swell, their margins creeping forward across the razor-sharp talus at a rate of several inches a minute. They hunt the mineral salts and the dead midges, dissolving both with an acid so strong it makes the stone hiss. 

Where the slime is thickest, **The Stalk-Mites** dwell. These are round, soft-bodied creatures no larger than a grain of sand, but they exist in numbers that challenge calculation. They are the architects of the fast-growing fungal chimneys. By tunneling through the pulp of the emerging stalks, they carry the mold's spores upward, feeding on the interior walls until the three-foot tower becomes a hollow, whistling lattice of calcium and chitin. When these towers collapse under their own weight, the Stalk-Mites ride the ruins down, chewing franticly through the wreckage before the slime-mold reabsorbs the mass.

### The Chitin-Grinders and Wall-Clingers

Heavy and loud, **The Clackers** dominate the stone floor. These are the multi-jointed white isopods described in the travelers' logs. The smallest are like scurrying flatstones; the largest—the *Great Clackers*—have thirty legs, are the size of mastiffs, and possess massive, spade-like mouthparts made of organic silica. They do not have blood as surface creatures do; instead, their circulatory system carries a pressurized, mineral-rich fluid that smells heavily of vinegar. 

The Clackers are the true sculptors of the Rift’s floor. Their legs end in hard, mineralized peg-tips that they drive into the gypsum needles with the force of jackhammers. They climb the shifting talus piles, scraping the salt-crusts with a sound that fills the canyon with a dry, rhythmic roar. They are almost entirely deaf to aerial sounds, but they are incredibly sensitive to the deep, volcanic thrumming of the earth. When a rockslide is about to occur, the Clackers feel the micro-fractures in the stone minutes before it yields; they will suddenly halt their scraping, press their flat bellies to the rock, and lock their legs together, forming a biological shingle-armor that can withstand falling slabs.

Above them, on the slick, vertical faces where the salt-glare is most blinding, live **The Leatherskin Leap-Jacks** (the wall-clingers). These lungless, slate-colored beasts are built like a cross between a giant lungfish and a greyhound. Because they have no lungs—which would quickly fill with scalding condensation and sulfur-smoke—they breathe entirely through their textured, mucus-covered skin. Their skin must remain wet to transfer oxygen, which is why they are found only in the "fog-bands" where the geysers spray. 

The Leap-Jacks are the apex predators of the middle heights. They do not walk; they spring. Their four legs are short but incredibly thick, ending in wide, flat pads covered in thousands of microscopic, hook-like cilia that grip the unweathered stone like magnets. They sit motionless in the steam-clouds, their slate-colored backs rendering them invisible against the wet stone. When a Great Clacker wanders too close to a ledge below, a Leap-Jack will launch itself downward, pinning the isopod with its heavy, rubbery chest and using its chisel-shaped front teeth to pry open the plates of the clacker's underside. Their sticky pads make a distinct, wet *thwip-thwip-thwip* as they scramble back up the cliffs to feed in peace.

### The Grit-Breathers (The Salt-Walkers)

And then there are those who built the ladders. 

They are humans, or at least they began as such. In the language of the few surface scholars who know of them, they are called **The Slate-Tongues**, though they call themselves nothing at all; they have no words for identity in a place where survival takes every breath.

They are pale—not the pinkish-white of northern surface dwellers, but a dense, chalky, semi-translucent gray, like wet clay. Their eyes have forgotten color. They are covered by a thick, milky nictitating membrane that shields them from the violent salt-glare and the stinging sulfuric acid of the midge-swarms. They see the world not in shapes of light, but in degrees of heat and vibration; to them, a hot steam vent is a brilliant beacon, and a cold stone path is a dark line of safety.

To live in the Rift, the Slate-Tongues have had to change how they breathe. The air is too heavy and hot for normal lungs; a surface person would drown in their own fluids within hours. The Slate-Tongues have developed a physical symbiosis with a dry, fibrous lichen they call *Breath-Wool*. They harvest this wool from the driest niches of the high walls, roll it into tight plugs, and insert it deep into their nasal passages. The lichen acts as a living filter: it feeds on the moisture of their breath, traps the sulfurous acids, and releases a faint, oxygen-rich yeast-scent. Without their nose-plugs, they are helpless; a Slate-Tongue’s first action upon waking is to replace the dry wool with a fresh, damp wad.

They do not wear clothes, for cloth would rot in the greasy dew in a matter of days. Instead, they paint their bodies with a paste made of ground charcoal and the oil of the Great Clackers. This dark, sticky coating protects their skin from the razor-edges of the gypsum needles and keeps the blind midges from clogging their pores. 

Their movement is a beautiful, terrifying dance. They do not stand upright unless they are on the rare, hand-carved ledges of their shelters. On the talus floor, they move on all fours—though "fours" is a generous term, as they use three legs of obsidian-tipped staves and their own calloused, wide-toed feet to distribute their weight. Their feet are wide, with toes that can splay and grip like fingers.

They do not speak with voices. The roar of the Rift—the steam-hiss, the clacker-scrape, the rock-groan—is too loud for any human throat to conquer. Instead, they use a language of **Vibration and Strike**. They carry small, flat plates of phonolite (ringing-stone) slung from their belts of braided clacker-sinew. By striking these plates with obsidian chisels, they send sharp, metallic pings through the air that can penetrate the midge-clouds. More often, they communicate through the stone itself. They will press their foreheads or their stave-ends against a rock-face and tap out rhythmic signals with their finger-grooved granite hammers. Through these vibrations, they can tell each other of a coming wall-collapse, the movement of a herd of clackers, or the rare, terrifying descent of a "surface-leak" (flooding).

They are nomads of the vertical. They do not own the stone; they simply cling to it. Their roofless, circular slate huts are shelters from the *skitter-lines*—the paths the Clackers use during their migrations. In these dry niches, they huddle together during the violent mud-spasms, wrapped in huge, cured hides of Leap-Jacks that they have sewn together with bone needles. 

In their quietest moments, when the steam-pressure drops and the Rift holds its breath for a heartbeat, the Slate-Tongues can be seen sitting on the edges of their fungal-log ladders. They do not look up at the jagged rip of daylight miles above. The light is nothing to them but a source of heat. Instead, they look down, into the deeper, boiling cracks where the orange mold grows thickest, waiting for the earth to shift again, waiting to see what new, sharp thing the world will throw up next.
