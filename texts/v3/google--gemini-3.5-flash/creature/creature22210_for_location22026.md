### The Basin Voice and the Sinking Shuffle

To understand the people of Sinking-on-the-Rise, one must first understand their silence. 

If a stranger from the dry, hard-soled outer hills were to shout in these narrow canyons, the sound would not echo; it would be instantly smothered by the four-foot-deep quilts of blue-green peat moss and the wet, wool-wrapped pipes. But the residents would flinch as if struck. 

Over generations, the human voice here has folded inward. The citizens speak what is known as the *Basin hum*—a dialect that has systematically shed its hard plosives and sharp consonants. There are no clicks, no hard *T*s or *K*s to rattle against the wet stone. They speak from the lower chest, utilizing long, sliding vowels that sound like wind passing over the mouth of a glass jar. A conversation between two merchants on a copper catwalk sounds to an outsider like the purring of two giant, nested cats.

Their movement is similarly hushed. They practice the *Sinking Shuffle*, a gait where the feet are rarely lifted more than an inch from the surface of the street. To lift the foot is to risk dropping it, and a heel striking the raw obsidian with a sharp *clack* is considered an embarrassing public clumsiness. Their boots, wrapped in thick bandages of gray canvas that have been saturated with tallow and boiled seal-fat, make no more sound on the newly risen basalt than a thumb pressed into dough. 

In this cold, bright, high-latitude light, their skin has taken on a peculiar, marble-like quality. It is a translucent, bluish-white that never tans, but instead thickens against the wet chill. They do not seem to sweat; rather, the dampness of the air condenses upon their high cheekbones and the ridges of their ears, so that every face looks as though it has been freshly carved from wet lard or river-ice.

### The Guild of current-Keepers

Among the human population, the most distinct and revered are the *Current-Keepers*, or the *Glass-Herders*. These are the men and women who tend the high-voltage copper lines that coax the embryonic stone into architectural form. 

They are easily recognized by their massive, brass-rimmed ear-trumpets—not for lack of hearing, but for its extreme specialization. A Current-Keeper spends their life listening to the ground. They can press their brass funnels to a rising rib of basalt and tell, by the frequency of its internal quartz-vibrations, whether the stone is cooling too quickly, whether it is "hungry" for more current, or whether it is about to fracture into a spray of glass-sharp shards.

The fingers of these Keepers are permanently stained a dark, metallic green from their constant contact with the raw, oxidizing copper cables. They carry small leather pouches of "heavy tallow"—a grease mixed with powdered lead—which they smear onto the junctions of the cables to keep the wild, moisture-laden static of the valley from leaping into their own dry bones. 

The Keepers do not build houses; they live in small, suspended copper cabins that hang directly beneath the factory underbellies. They are a nomadic guild within a static city, moving their homes three feet to the left or five feet up as the earth beneath their stations swells and subsides. Their beds are slung from heavy, braided hair-ropes that absorb the tremors of the valley floor, allowing them to sleep through the midnight upheavals that would throw a flat-ground dweller from their sheets.

### The Slicks and the Gutter-Fauna

The domestic and feral animals of the basin have abandoned hair, feathers, and claws, all of which are useless or dangerous in a world of grease and razor-stone.

The *Slicks*—the grease-dogs of the alleyways—are the constant companions of the weavers and steam-fitters. A Slick looks less like a dog and more like a five-foot seal that has grown four short, jointed legs. Their skin is a tough, rubbery hide of wet lead, entirely hairless and continuously secreting a fine, bitter oil that prevents the caustic, mineral-heavy dew of the city from burning their flesh. 

Instead of claws, which would split and dull on the obsidian streets, the Slicks have broad, leathery pads that act like suction cups. They can run vertically up the wet, moss-covered flanks of a three-story boiler housing to chase down the blind gutter-moths. They do not possess vocal cords; instead, they have a large, air-filled sack beneath their throats. When a Slick is content, or when it is warning its pack of an impending steam-vent blow, it thrums. The thrum is so deep it cannot be heard by the human ear, but it can be felt through the soles of one’s boots—a sudden, rhythmic tingling that tells the walker exactly where the pack is nesting beneath the catwalks.

Further down, in the dark, warm crevices where the earth’s crust is still a soft, gray porridge, lives the *Silt-Midge*. These are colonial insects the size of walnuts. They have no eyes, but their bodies are covered in fine, copper-colored hairs that are highly sensitive to electromagnetic fields. They gather in their thousands around the high-voltage cables that run into the ground, arranging themselves in perfect, geometric patterns that mimic the current-lines. The citizens treat them with great care; if the Silt-Midges suddenly abandon a cable, it is a sign that the current has drifted from its safe harmonic frequency and the stone beneath is about to shatter.

### The Chinkers: Life in the Angles

Not everyone can afford to live in the floating, hydraulic-legged villas of the Tallow-Lords. The great majority of the working population—the weavers of the wool pipe-wraps, the tallow-renderers, and the moss-croppers—live in the *Chinks*.

A Chink is the space created when two buildings, tilted by the rising earth, lean against one another at the top, forming an accidental, triangular tunnel over the street below. These spaces are valued for their warmth; the steam pipes run through them like hot veins, and the dense layers of moss on the outer walls insulate them against the flat, icy sky.

The people who inhabit these slanted spaces are called *Chinkers*. They have developed a highly specialized domestic culture centered around the "Rule of the Plumb." 

In a Chinker’s home, nothing is fixed to the floor, because the floor is always changing its angle. Instead, every table, bed, and cupboard is suspended from the ceiling by a system of small, lead-weighted pulleys. If a floor tilts fifteen degrees to the east over the course of a week, the furniture remains perfectly level, hanging an inch above the rising boards. 

Visiting a Chinker’s parlor is an exercise in vertigo. The walls may be crooked, the floor may feel like the deck of a sinking ship, but the tea-kettle hangs level, suspended from a brass chain that has been polished by three generations of hands.

The Chinkers are also the primary harvesters of the *static-moss*. This is a variety of the blue-green peat that has grown close to the electrical generators. It drinks the stray currents and stores them; when squeezed or pressed, the moss emits a soft, cold, lime-green glow that lasts for several hours. The Chinkers stuff this moss into globes of thick, hand-blown green glass, which they hang in their low windowless rooms as light-sources that never burn out, never smoke, and never make a sound.

### The White-Wools of the Upper Tier

At the very top of the city’s iron forest, where the black catwalks give way to wide, copper-clad platforms that bask in the flat, bright daylight, live the *White-Wools*. 

These are the owners of the great tallow-troughs and the copper mines. They are a small, insular aristocracy who have not touched the actual ground of the basin in three or four generations. Their villas are masterpieces of hydraulic engineering—delicate, multi-tiered pavilions of white-painted larch-wood that rise and fall on massive grease-slicked pistons to maintain a perfect, level altitude above the steaming chaos below.

To distinguish themselves from the soot-and-grease stained laborers of the lower tiers, the White-Wools wear garments made of un-dyed, bleached alpine wool, imported at immense cost from the dry northern plateaus outside the basin. These garments are so thick and stiff they do not drape; they stand on their own, making the aristocracy look like slow-moving, white-pulp columns drifting across the high copper decks.

The White-Wools have their own language—not of words, but of silent, gestural signs performed with thin, silver-tipped wands. Because the noise of the hydraulic pistons and the low thrum of the turbines below is constant, they have abandoned speech altogether within their villas. A White-Lord can dismiss a servant, purchase a three-ton shipment of seal-fat, or arrange a marriage with three elegant, silent sweeps of a silver rod through the cold, clean air.

Below them, the city continues its slow, silent digestion of its own geology. The people of the lower tiers look up through the gaps in the iron catwalks and see only these white figures, like pale birds perched on the high copper branches of an iron forest, while beneath their own boots, the earth gives another warm, wet, and silent heave.
