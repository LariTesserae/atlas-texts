### The Cambial Folk: The Silent Tappers

To look upon the people of the Hanging-Towns is to see a mirror of the wood itself. They are not soft; their bodies have been dried and hardened by a sun that never relents and an environment without soil. 

#### Physiology of the Sole and the Hand
The first and most striking feature of these people is their feet. They do not wear shoes; to cover the foot here is to go voluntarily blind. Their soles are wide, flat, and hard as cured leather, yet sensitive enough to read the microscopic grain of the wood beneath them. The skin of their heels and toes is patterned with concentric whorls that match the growth rings of the cambium. By standing still and slightly bending their knees, a tapper can "hear" a pack of pulp-ants chewing through a ridge three miles away, or detect the deep, wet rumble of an approaching Sap-Pulse days before it rises.

Their hands are similarly specialized. Their fingers are long and double-jointed, ending in thick, scoop-like nails that they use to lever up thin strips of green bast—the moist inner layer of the bark plates. Because there is no stone to grind or metal to forge, their bones are incredibly dense, calcified by a diet rich in the mineral-heavy, boiled-down sap of the gourd-wells. Their teeth are not individual pegs but solid, undulating ridges of bone—ribbed plates designed for stripping the nutritious, sweet cambium from young branches and grinding the fibrous pith of the tillandsias.

#### The Language of Light and Tap
In a world that screams with the whistling of wind-pipes and the deafening rattle of giant cicadas, human speech is useless for distance. The Cambial Folk have two languages: one for the dark, and one for the blistering noon.

For close communication within the shade of the Hanging-Towns, they use *The Tap*. It is a language of pure vibration, played with small, carved heartwood mallets against the resonant pine-like floors of their suspended domes. By striking the wood at specific angles and frequencies, they send messages that travel through the suspension ropes and into the cliff-faces, readable only by those who keep their palms or soles pressed against the structure. 

Over the long, flat expanses of the open plains, where the heat distortion makes sight unreliable beyond fifty yards, they use *The Glint*. Each traveler carries a circular plate of polished, fossilized resin—amber that has been rubbed with fine sand and oil until it acts as a mirror. Using the white-gold sun, they flash sharp, rhythmic pulses of light across the shimmering horizon. To an outsider, the plains seem empty, but to a tapper, the horizon is constantly winking with the silent, golden gossip of distant caravans.

#### The Sled-Sails
The multi-legged wooden sleds they use to traverse the grain-lines are marvels of bone-and-wood engineering. They are built of *spring-wood*—slender, highly flexible ribs harvested from the interior of the canyons that curve upward like the runners of iron sleds. 

Because the grain of the plains runs in a single, predictable direction across hundreds of miles, these sleds do not steer with rudders. Instead, they use "groove-fins"—pivoting plates of dense, polished horn that slot directly into the natural longitudinal gaps between the wood-fibers. Once locked into a grain-line, the sled cannot capsize or veer. The traveler hoists a wide, triangular sail woven from the hair-like red moss of the ridges. When the dry breeze blows down the mountain-ridges, the sleds fly across the golden plains, their runners producing a high-pitched, musical hum that sounds like a wet finger drawn across the rim of a crystal glass.

### The Fauna of the Furrows

The creatures that inhabit the plains are characterized by an absence of soft flesh. In a world of teeth-shattering vibrations and abrasive wood-dust, everything is armored, jointed, or dry.

#### The Bark-Stalkers
These are the apex predators of the flat timber, yet they are rarely seen. A Bark-Stalker is a creature of negative space. Its body is flat, scarcely three inches thick, but its eight jointed legs stretch out to a span of twelve feet. These legs are constructed of hollow, dark chitin that perfectly mimics the weathered, sun-baked cork of the canyons.

They do not stalk their prey by sight. Their heads consist of a dry, bulbous dome of sensory pits that detect the heat given off by the friction of other creatures moving through the grain-lines. When hunting, a Bark-Stalker spreads its legs wide, anchoring its claws into the wood, and becomes completely motionless. It listens.

If a glider-pack or a human sled approaches, the Bark-Stalker senses the vibrations through its legs. It calculates the speed and weight of the prey, then shifts its position by sliding its padded, oil-lubricated feet through the longitudinal grooves of the wood. It makes no sound—no clicking of claws, no brushing of limbs. When it strikes, it does not bite; it drives two long, hollow, bone-like stylets into the prey's joints and injects a hot, vaporizing enzyme that dissolves the inner tissues into a rich, easily drinkable gruel, leaving the outer shell or skin completely intact.

#### The Lignin-Weavers (Pulp-Ants)
These six-foot-long, slate-grey creatures are the primary shapers of the landscape. They are entirely blind, their heads dominated by pair after pair of massive, horizontal chewing blades that operate like industrial wood-chippers. 

A colony of Lignin-Weavers lives within a single "chimney"—a towering, hollow spire made of digested wood pulp mixed with their thick, cement-like saliva. These spires can reach eighty feet in height and are incredibly tough; once dry, they are harder than the heartwood itself. 

The Weavers are obsessive gatherers of dry fiber. They march in long, grey lines along the ridges, peeling the weathered outer layers of cork and carrying them back to their chimneys. Inside the spires, they do not eat the wood directly; instead, they spit-glue the pulp into dense, subterranean combs where they cultivate a pale, luminous fungus that feeds on the cellulose. This fungus is their sole sustenance, and it smells of damp earth and vinegar—a scent that the Cambial Folk seek out, as a weaver-nest is often the only source of edible moisture for miles.

#### The Chitin-Kites (Glider-Packs)
Unlike the heavy, earthbound predators, the Chitin-Kites belong to the sky above the canyons. They are not birds; they are lizard-like organisms whose ribs have expanded outward into a vast, rigid frame covered by a thin, leathery membrane that looks precisely like a fallen, dried leaf.

They are incapable of true flight. Instead, they climb to the high peaks of the longitudinal bark-plates using small, curved chest-hooks. Once at the summit, they wait for the afternoon heat-waves to rise from the dry plains. When the thermals are strongest, they launch themselves into the shimmering air.

They drift in packs of thirty or forty, silent and beautiful, their reddish-brown bodies catching the white-gold sun. They hunt the giant grubs that live in the deep splits of the wood. When a Kite spots a grub’s pale head breaking the surface of a sap-vessel, it folds its wings, dropping like a stone, and drives its sharp, chisel-shaped snout into the wood to spear the prey.

### The Macro-Ecology: The Sleeper-Beasts

Deep within the miles-thick body of the world, where the wood is wet, dark, and under immense pressure, live things that never see the light of the sun. The only evidence of their existence is their skeletons, and the terrifying sounds they make during the Cambial Pulse.

#### The Heartwood boring-Hydras (Lignum-Eel)
These are the colossal beasts whose ribs occasionally project from the deep splits in the plains. A mature Lignum-Eel is a segmented worm-like creature that can grow to the length of a three-mast ship. Its entire front end is a rotating drill-shield composed of interlocking, diamond-hard plates of silicate-impregnated enamel.

They do not inhabit the dry surface. They live in the "sap-veins"—the vertical, deep interior highways where the great plant’s lifeblood runs thickest. They chew their way through the heartwood at a rate of inches per day, their passage marked by deep, circular tunnels that are polished as smooth as glass by the heat of their friction.

The tappers occasionally find these abandoned tunnels when a canyon wall collapses. They use them as emergency shelters during the blistering heat of mid-summer, as the interior of an eel-bore remains cool, smelling faintly of old cider and petrified resin.

#### The Great Screech-Singers
Perched on the highest peaks of the cork-cliffs are the Screech-Singers. These are gnarled, boulder-like organisms that look so much like natural burls of the wood that even the tappers cannot always tell them apart. They can remain dormant for decades, their grey, moss-covered hides baking in the sun.

They are the lungs of the mountain ridges. Inside their massive, hollow bodies is a complex system of organic chambers and reeds. When the high-altitude winds blow across the ridges, these creatures do not move; they simply open their forward-facing spiracles and let the wind play through them, creating the deep, tooth-rattling sub-bass hums that dominate the plains.

During the ninety-day Cambial Pulse, however, they wake. As the wood beneath them swells and groans, the Screech-Singers contract their massive, bellows-like bodies, forcing air out in synchronized bursts of high-frequency static. This sound is not a song; it is a territorial warning. As the plains swell, the territory of each ridge shifts, and the singers use their deafening screeches to map the changes in the topography, their voices bouncing off the rising bark-plates like sonar to find the new cracks where they can nest and feed on the rising sap.
