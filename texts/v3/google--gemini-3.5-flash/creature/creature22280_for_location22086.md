The dwellers of these courtyards are few, but they are as permanent as the bricks from which they rise. 

They are the clay-born, though they have no name for themselves, for they have no language of vowels and teeth. They are shaped like men who have been distilled down to their most essential structural planes—narrower in the hip, long-limbed, and lacking the soft, trembling curves of watery flesh. Their skin has the dry, pale texture of biscuit-fired pipe-clay, smooth and matte, with a faint craquelure of hair-fine lines at the knuckles and the throat where their joints obligingly bend.

If you were to touch the cheek of one of these beings, you would find it neither hot nor cold, but possessing that same radiant, bread-oven warmth that rises from the terracotta floors. They have no pulse. Within their hollow chests, there is only a slow, rhythmic movement of dry air, like the draft in a well-built chimney.

They do not have eyes. In the place where a face should be, there is only a smooth, shallow indentation—a gently concave dish of polished white clay. When one of them stands before you, their "face" does not look at you; instead, it catches and pools the white, daylight-glow from the plaster ceiling thirty feet above, reflecting a soft, featureless circle of light back into the courtyard. They navigate not by sight, but by a profound, sympathetic vibration with the masonry. Their long, five-jointed fingers are tipped with flat, spade-like nails of tarnished bronze, identical in color and composition to the scrapings that fill the joints between the tiles. As they walk, they trail these fingers along the mortarless seams of the walls, reading the thumb-prints of the ones who came before them like a raised script.

Their births are silent and take many years. 

They do not gather to mate, nor do they lay eggs. Instead, they emerge from the walls themselves. Along the dry, three-foot terraces, there are places where the pale brick contains a higher concentration of grog—tiny, ground-up bits of older pottery. Here, over decades, the wall will begin to swell. A thumb-print left by an ancient mason will slowly expand, rising from the brick like yeast. First, a pair of long, pale hands will push through the dry joints, the fingers parting the bricks without breaking them. Then, the chest, the hollow face, and the folded legs will slowly slide out of the masonry, leaving behind a smooth, niche-like alcove in the wall that is perfectly shaped to receive them should they ever wish to return.

A newly emerged dweller is soft—pliable as leather-hard clay—but the warm, dry air of the courtyard quickly sets them. Within three days, they are firm, dry, and silent.

Because there is no night, they do not sleep, but they have periods of "setting." When the weight of the flat plaster sky becomes too heavy, a dweller will seek out the five-step staircase of white limestone near the center of their courtyard. They will descend the five steps, step off the bottom stair, and find themselves standing on the top stair, looking down at their own heels. There, they will sit. Because of the folded nature of the space, they can reach forward and rest their chin upon their own shoulder from behind, or clasp their own dry hands in a self-contained circle of perfect symmetry. They will remain in this loop for months, their hollow chests rising and falling only once every dozen hours, smelling of camphor and warm earth.

They do not eat the blue-grey parchment leaves, nor do they drink the heavy camphor oil. They require no sustenance other than the radiant warmth of the tiles. However, they are obsessed with the shadows.

To a dweller, the violet-black velvet that grows in the shade of the walls is a precious textile. They collect it with their bronze-tipped fingers, using their sharp nails to slide beneath the edge of a shadow and peel it from the clay in long, whisper-thin ribbons. They are incredibly deft at this. If you watch them, they will spend half a year peeling a single, perfect sheet of shadow from a twelve-foot wall, careful not to tear the velvety nap. 

They wear these shadow-shrouds. They fold them with exquisite, geometric precision, draping them over their pale shoulders or winding them around their heads like turbans. The shadows do not lose their darkness when moved; even in the middle of the bright, unblinking common, a dweller wrapped in shadow remains a silhouette of pure, deep violet-black, warm to the touch and slightly fuzzy, like the skin of a dark peach. The shadow protects their clay bodies from the direct glow of the ceiling, keeping them from drying out so completely that they fracture.

In the margins of their quiet lives, other things move.

Beneath the waxy ropes of the succulent plants live the "reed-grooms." These are tiny creatures that look like knots of dried grass-stems, no longer than a finger-joint. They have no legs, but they moves by flexing their segmented bodies with a dry, rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck*. They are the caretakers of the bronze joints. They crawl into the crevices between the bricks, using their pointed heads to scrape away the green tarnish that accumulates there, swallowing the dust and spitting it out in small, hard green beads that the dwellers sometimes collect to use as buttons for their shadow-robes.

And then there are the "hollow-horns." These are rare, solitary beasts that wander from courtyard to courtyard through the gaps in the low walls. They are the size of sheep, but their bodies are constructed entirely of thin, curved plates of unglazed red terracotta, like roof tiles that have been stitched together with dried roots. They have no heads; instead, their necks end in a flared, open cone—a trumpet of dark clay that breathes out a faint, continuous whistle as the warm air passes through them. 

The hollow-horns are the guardians of the loop. They are drawn to the five-step staircases. When one of them finds a staircase, it will climb to the third step, stick its flared neck into the folded space, and whistle. The sound does not travel outward; it enters the loop, passes down the stairs, out the bottom, and re-enters the hollow-horn's own rear flank, creating a soft, resonant hum that can be felt through the soles of the terracotta tiles three courtyards away. When the dwellers hear this hum, they know that the geometry of their world is correct, and they settle deeper into their warm, patient brick.

There is no death here in the sense of decay. When a dweller’s time in the courtyards is done—perhaps after ten thousand years, when their joints have become too stiff with calcite deposits to move—they do not fall or crumble.

Instead, they walk to a corner where the walls are low. They find an empty niche—perhaps the very one they emerged from, or one left by another who came before. They slide back into the hollow, folding their long limbs tightly against their chests. Slowly, the mortarless bricks on either side press inward, welcoming them back. The bronze tarnish in the crevices creeps over their feet; the waxy ropes pool their camphor oil around their knees, sealing them in.

Within a year, the line between the dweller and the wall disappears. They become a single, solid block of pale, hand-pressed clay, marked only by the faint, elegant indentation of a hollow face, waiting for the hand of a future mason to find them.
