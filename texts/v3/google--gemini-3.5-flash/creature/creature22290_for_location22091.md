The ones who claim this cleft—the scuttling, hot-blooded tenders of the terraces—are known only to the silence of the stone. If they had a name for themselves, it would not be a word spoken with breath, for breath is a dry, precious thing to be hoarded. Instead, their self-name would be a specific pattern of dry fingernails clicking against a seed-casing: *tup-tup-tasp*. 

To look closely at one is to see an organism that has struck a radical, exquisite bargain with drought. 

### The Citizens: Physicality and Sensation

They are no larger than a child’s thumb, yet their bodies are dense with a furious, vibrating heat. They do not have fur; fur is a trap for dust, and in this world, dust must flow off the body like water. Instead, they are clad in an armor of microscopic, overlapping scales, fine as poppy seeds and colored in shades of toasted nutmeg, cinnamon, and dried leaf-gold. These scales are slightly oily to the touch, coated in a natural sebum that prevents their internal heat from dry-evaporating into the thin plateau air. 

Their heads are wide and wedge-shaped, dominated by two enormous, spherical eyes of a deep, polished obsidian. These eyes have no whites; they are pure, liquid wells of dark light, covered by a tough, glassy spectacle—like those of a snake—which protects them from the drifting silt. They do not blink; instead, they have a tiny, crescent-shaped groomer-claw on their wrists which they use to wipe their eyes clean of pollen-dust in a single, fluid motion.

Because they have no access to free-standing water, their biology is a marvel of closed-loop efficiency. They do not sweat. Their breath, when it leaves their nostrils, passes through a labyrinth of cooling nasal chambers that condense and reclaim every speck of moisture before it can escape. They do not urinate; their waste is a dry, chalky pellet of pure uric acid, which is collected by the young and used as a mineral pigment to paint the doorways of their walnut-shell homes.

To them, the world is not heard so much as it is felt through the pads of their four-toed feet. Each toe is tipped with a tiny, needle-sharp claw that can find purchase in the microscopic pores of the sandstone. In their chests, a large, heavy sternum acts as a drumhead, picking up the low-frequency vibrations of the canyon walls. They can feel the approach of a high-altitude dust storm three hours before the first gust curls into their cleft, simply by listening to the "singing" of the deep stone.

### The Social Fabric: The Scent-Moulders and the Gliders

The society is not a hierarchy of strength, but of *oil*. 

At the center of their life is the **Wax-Mother**, or the *Oiler*. In every terrace-layer of the city, there is a single, large, communal chamber carved deep into the cool heart of the sandstone. Here sits the oldest female of the block. She is nearly double the size of the others, her scales having grown dull and thick with age, resembling grey lichen. She does not join the frantic seed-harvests. Instead, she sits atop a cistern of the dark, amber moss-oil, which has been gathered drop by drop by the younger foragers. 

It is the Wax-Mother’s duty to digest this oil, blending it in her throat with the fine sandstone flour to create the "Marrow"—the gray, bone-hard mortar from which their entire city is built. The younger workers crawl to her, offering dry seeds and receiving in return a glob of wet, warm Marrow from her mouth. They carry this paste in their cheeks to the construction sites, using their flat, paddle-like thumbs to smear and shape the walls of the bulbous huts before the hot air cures the mixture into stone.

The young who have not yet reached breeding age are the **Gliders**. Their scales are still soft, almost velvety, and they possess dry, leathery flaps of skin that stretch from their wrists to their ankles. While the older, heavier citizens climb the staircases, these youngsters leap fearlessly into the open air of the cleft. They glide from the high, ochre-colored ferns down to the lowest bridges, their tiny bodies catching the thermal drafts that rise from the sun-heated floor. They are the couriers, the lookouts, and the gatherers of the wind-pollen.

### The Domestic Life of the Huts

Inside one of the walnut-sized huts, the air is unimaginably warm and still. The interior walls are polished with animal-wax until they reflect the amber light like glass. There are no furniture pieces; the floor is a shallow bowl filled with the finest, silkiest silt, scooped from the canyon floor. 

Here, a family sleeps grouped together in a single, tangled ball of bodies. This is not just for warmth—though the plateau nights can drop to a bitter freeze—but to minimize the surface area of their skin exposed to the dry air. By sleeping in a dense, scaly sphere, they share and preserve their skin-oil.

In the corner of each hut hangs a **Dew-Drop**. This is not water, but a single, large seed of the *oil-vine*, suspended from the ceiling by a thread of spider-silk. The family will take turns licking this seed, absorbing the lipid-rich fats that sustain their metabolism. A single seed can nourish a family of five for three moons before it is reduced to a dry, grey husk, which is then ground into flour.

### The Scribes of the Gloss

Because they have no paper and their tools are simple flint, their history is not written; it is *crusted*. 

In the deepest cleft of the sanctuary, where the sun never shines and the air is thick with the scent of toasted pine-resin, lies the **Wall of the Gloss**. For ten thousand years, the elders of each generation have come to this wall to rub their cheeks and flanks against the stone. 

Each citizen possesses a small scent-gland just below the ear, which secretes a unique, highly complex grease. As thousands of individuals have rubbed against this rock over the millennia, they have deposited a thick, dark, waxy laminate upon the sandstone—a literal "Gloss" that is now several inches deep.

To a human, it would look like a greasy, blackened streak on the rock. To the *tup-tup-tasp*, it is an archive. The specialized elders, whose eyes have grown blind from a lifetime in the dark, spend their days crawling slowly over the Gloss. By tasting the wax with their split, dry tongues and smelling the ancient, oxidized lipids, they can read the history of the cleft. They know when the Great Heat dried the mosses three hundred summers ago; they can trace the lineage of the Wax-Mothers; they can feel the phantom presence of ancestors who died when the plateau was still a jagged ridge of sharp peaks, long before the wind wore them into mounds.

### The Under-World: The Dust-Mutes and the Beetles

Below the hanging city, in the flour-soft silt-bed that covers the floor of the cleft, lives a different class of inhabitant. These are the **Dust-Mutes**—blind, pale-skinned grubs that are the larval form of the great obsidian beetles.

The Mutes live entirely submerged in the silk-dust, moving through it like fish through water. They are the garbage collectors of the metropolis. Anything that falls from the terraces—discarded seed-husks, shedded skins, the dry mummies of the dead—is immediately pulled down into the dust by the Mutes. Inside their gut-tracts, this material is broken down, fermented, and excreted as a rich, dry soil-paste that feeds the roots of the ochre-ferns.

When a Dust-Mute reaches its maturity, it undergoes a metallic hardening. It emerges from the dust as an **Obsidian Beetle**, a slow, six-legged tank of black chitin. These beetles are the friends of the city. Though they have no intellect, they are highly sensitive to the smell of the Wax-Mother’s oil. By placing trails of this oil along the narrow stone ledges, the citizens can guide the beetles anywhere they wish, using them as living elevators to haul heavy stones or massive pods of dry seeds up to the high granaries.

### The Great Threat: The Shiver-Vines

Life in the cleft is stable, but it is not without its terrors. The greatest of these is the **Shiver-Vine**.

Most of the vegetation in the cleft is benign, but the Shiver-Vine is an invasive, predatory plant that occasionally sprouts from seeds carried in by the high gales. It looks identical to the tough, dry vines that form the curtains of the city, but it does not seek the moss-oil. It seeks the hot, fast-beating blood of the scuttlers.

A Shiver-Vine waits until a citizen crawls near, then it twitches. The movement is so fast it is almost invisible—a sudden, dry snap of a leathery tendril that winds around the victim’s body. The vine’s surface is covered in microscopic, hollow thorns that puncture the scales, drinking the rich, oily blood of the rodent.

When a Shiver-Vine is discovered, the city falls silent. The clicking stops. The rodents organize into **Cutter-Bands**. Armed with sharp, serrated flint saws, they climb above the vine and drip the caustic, acidic sap of the *velvet-moss* onto its anchor-roots. The vine shivers, screams in a low, dry whistle, and dies, its dry carcass immediately harvested by the beetles to be ground into timber for the suspension bridges.

### The Cosmology of the Edge

For a citizen of the cleft, there is no "World" beyond the stone. 

The edge of the cleft, where the amber light turns to the blinding, blue-white glare of the outer canyon, is known as the **Void of the Great Wind**. To them, the outer world is not a place, but a physical state of non-being. It is where things go to be un-made. They believe that if a scuttler were to step beyond the shadow-line of the cleft, the Great Wind would instantly peel their scales from their body, dry their blood to sand, and blow their spirit into the sun.

When a citizen grows old and their scales begin to lose their oil, they do not wait to die in the nest. They climb down the spiral staircases to the very edge of the dust-bed, near the line where the wind begins to hiss. 

They lie down in the warm, dry silt, facing the blue sky they can never enter. As the wind-dust slowly buries them, they let their breath go, allowing their dry, light bones to dissolve back into the flour of the floor. They do not see this as death, but as a return to the mortar. They believe that their dust will be gathered by the next generation, mixed with the oil of the Wax-Mother, and built into the very walls of the homes where their great-grandchildren will sleep.
