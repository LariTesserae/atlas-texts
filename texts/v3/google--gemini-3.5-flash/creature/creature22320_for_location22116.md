To understand what lives in this wet, golden-lit basin, one must first look at the margins where the water meets the stone. 

The thousands of pale, legless frogs that cling to the pillars are not visitors; they are part of the masonry. Their wet porcelain skin is so thin and translucent that the gray veins of the limestone are visible through their bellies when they press against it. They have no skeleton as we understand it—only an internal scaffolding of wet cartilage that hums when they imbibe the rising water. They do not reproduce by spawn or egg; instead, they emerge directly from the damp seams where two limestone blocks press together too tightly. A pocket of water, trapped under immense geologic pressure between two hand-carved surfaces, slowly thickens, grows twin points of unblinking gold light, and slides out as a living thing. They do not die, because there is nothing to rot them. When one has drank its fill of the skyward-flowing water—a process that may take three centuries—it slowly stops pulsing. Its skin dries, hardens, and chalks over, turning back into a small, decorative boss of white limestone, permanently fused to the pillar.

Beneath them, the flat-bodied rug-worms do not swim; they slide, functioning as clean-up agents for an environment that has no garbage. They seek out the minuscule flakes of flint left behind by the builders’ chisels. When a worm finds a grain of silica or a speck of iron-dust, it does not eat it; it slides over it, and the dust is absorbed directly through its patterned skin, where it aligns with the red and black geometric bands. If you were to pick up one of these flat ribbons, it would feel like a wet strip of heavy brocade, stiffened with metallic thread. When they pass through one another in the shallow, two-inch lagoons, they do not collide; they simply merge their patterns for the span of a second—a brief, beautiful chessboard of red, black, and gold—before sliding apart, each taking half of the other’s memory of the stone floor.

But these are only the surface details, the decorative trim of the basin. The true masters of this warm, eternal afternoon are the **Plaster-Skins**.

They are the ones who tied the hemp knots around the bamboo frames. They are the ones who left the adzes and the flint chisels lying on the blocks. They have not left; indeed, they cannot leave, for they are built of the same lime-mortar and river-silt as the terrace itself.

A Plaster-Skin is tall—nearly nine feet from its flat, heel-less soles to the blunt, square top of its head—but it is so thin that from the side it almost disappears against the vertical pillars. Their bodies are dry, despite the heavy, greenhouse heat. Their skin is the color of unwashed fleece, powdery and smelling faintly of slaked lime and old well-water. They have no hair, no ears, and no noses; their faces are flat plates of smooth, polished marl, broken only by two horizontal slits that burn with the same unblinking gold light that fills the sky.

They do not walk with a continuous motion. Because the physics of this place are slightly fractured, the Plaster-Skins move in a series of static positions. You will look toward a bamboo frame, and it will be empty. You will look away to watch a floating water-sphere, and when you look back, a Plaster-Skin is there. There was no sound of footsteps, no displacement of the heavy, wet air. They exist at coordinates, shifting from one point of the interlocking stone grid to another like chess pieces moved by an unseen hand.

They are always at work, though their work has no end and no progress. 

One might see a Plaster-Skin standing before a heart-shaped elephant-ear leaf. It does not touch the leaf; instead, its long, four-jointed fingers—which end in blunt, spatula-like nails of gray slate—reach into the empty air three inches to the left of the stem. It begins to tie a knot. Its fingers twist and loop around nothingness, yet as they move, a thin, yellow cord of split vine *manifests* between them, tightening around an invisible vertical axis. The Plaster-Skin is tying the plants of the *next* world—the one that should have started when the sun rose, had the sun not stopped beneath the horizon—to the frames of this one.

They have no language, for they have no voices. The only sound they make is a dry, rhythmic *scritch-click* when they clean their fingers, scraping the dry masonry dust from one slate fingernail with another. This sound is incredibly soft, muffled instantly by the wet, wool-like air, but if you listen closely, you can hear it echoing across the miles of wet stone—a thousand tiny, synchronized scrapings, like beetles burrowing through dry bark.

Occasionally, a Plaster-Skin will encounter one of the large palm-fiber insects suspended in the air. The insect, motionless in its driftless hover, represents an obstruction in the grid. The Plaster-Skin does not swat it or kill it. Instead, with infinite, slow patience, it will take the insect between two fingers—its dry touch causing no damage to the brittle, bean-shuck body—and slide it three inches to the left, setting it down on a different pocket of warm air, where it will remain, suspended and silent, for another age.

There is a communal mind among them, but it is not a mind of thoughts; it is a mind of *alignment*. If a single limestone block in the western distance shifts by so much as a hair's breadth—perhaps because the water rising through its joints has carved a microscopic channel in the mortar—every Plaster-Skin in the basin will instantly turn its flat face toward that western point. They will stand frozen for hours, their gold-slit eyes glowing slightly brighter, until the water corrects the pressure and the block settles back into its desperate, hand-wrought precision. Only then do they return to their invisible knottings.

They do not eat, nor do they sleep. However, they must drink, and their manner of drinking is the only time they show any vulnerability. 

A Plaster-Skin will approach one of the dry-stone pylons where the water is climbing toward the sky. It will press its entire flat, chest-first torso against the wet, vertical stone. For a few minutes, it remains perfectly still. Under the influence of its dry, lime-rich body, the water-skin on the pillar accelerates, rushing into the Plaster-Skin’s skin through capillary action. As it drinks, the creature’s body becomes semi-translucent; you can see the internal channels—like the hollow chambers of a reed—filling with the clear, cool fluid. A faint, sweet steam rises from its shoulders as the water hits the immense, dry heat of its core. 

Once it is saturated, the Plaster-Skin detaches itself with a wet, sucking sound—the only loud noise in the entire valley. It steps back, its body once again opaque, white, and dusty, ready to resume its eternal, silent maintenance of a world that is waiting to begin.
