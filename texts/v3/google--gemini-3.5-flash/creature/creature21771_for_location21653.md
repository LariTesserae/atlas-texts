To watch the inhabitants of this limestone crack is to watch the slow, mineral pulse of the earth itself. They are the *An-Kala*, though they have no spoken word for themselves—the name, if it exists, is merely the shape of a collective memory held in the light of their eyes.

They are small and compact, their six-legged forms resembling frost-dusted pinecones that have found a way to stand. Between their four lower limbs, which grasp the vertical slate walls with the iron-grip of climbing-irons, and their two upper arms, there is an absolute harmony of economy. They do not waste a single movement. To step from one bone-bridge to another is a task of several hours, during which the leg is lifted, suspended, and lowered with the micro-measured care of a surveyor placing a transit. 

Their bodies are cloaked in a dense, felted matting of white wool, so thick and seamless that it resembles the rind of a winter gourd. This wool is not mere fur; its fibers are hollow, trapping the dry, freezing air of the crack and holding it close to their blue-grey, bloodless skin. Over decades, this wool collects the microscopic lime-dust that drifts down from the plains above. It settles in their coats, reacting with the delicate, unspilling condensation to form a thin, protective crust—a natural armor of white-washed plaster that makes them appear as though they have been sculpted directly out of the fissure’s walls.

Their faces are nearly featureless, save for their eyes. They have no visible mouths, no nostrils, no ears. Where a human face would have expression, they have only two large, liquid spheres of the deepest, most reflective obsidian. 

These eyes are their language, their hearths, and their history. 

Within the dark depths of each eye, a faint, rhythmic glow rises and falls—a pale, milky luminescence that beats like a heart behind a pane of dark glass. When three or four of the wool-dwellers stand in a public square, they do not touch or sound. Instead, they turn their faces toward one another, and their eyes begin to pulse in an intricate, silent polyrhythm. One eye will swell with a soft, pearly light for forty minutes; another will respond with three short, sharp dilations that take an hour to resolve. In these slow, optical waves, they share everything: the discovery of a new hairline fracture in a supporting terrace, the gradual drying of a moss-patch three blocks away, or the memory of an ice-sheet that passed over the tablelands a million years ago, when their ancestors first retreated into the safety of the stone.

Because they do not eat in the way of noisy, devouring creatures, their survival is as quiet as their communication. They exist on the breath of the stone. 

At the base of every slate house, where the walls join the limestone, there are tiny, hand-carved troughs. In these troughs, over weeks of absolute stillness, the trace minerals of the karst plains—calcium, salt, and microscopic spores of blue algae—precipitate out of the air, carried by the pearls of condensation that cling to the rock. The wool-dwellers harvest these mineral-rich droplets using hollow needles made from the hairs of deep-reaching grass roots. They do not drink; they place the hollow root-hair against their skin, letting the nutrient-heavy moisture draw directly into their wool and through their porous hides by simple capillary action. They are, in essence, fed by capillary pressure, nourished of the same stone that shelters them.

In this world of frozen time, death is not a rot, but a completion. 

When an inhabitant’s dark eyes finally cease to pulse—when the last pearl-like glow fades into the black sphere—they are not buried. There is no room for graves in a city covered by a blanket. Instead, they are placed by their neighbors into the empty niches of the terraces, or stood upright at the corners of the bone-bridges. 

The perpetual, mineral-laden dampness of the fissure goes to work on them with exquisite slowness. Within a century, the lime-dust in their woolen coats hardens into solid calcium carbonate. Within three centuries, they have become part of the masonry. Their legs bond with the slate tiles; their three-fingered hands, still holding a bone needle or a roof-shingle, become brackets that support the walkways of the next generation. The very city they inhabit is built of their ancestors, quite literally—a vertical coral reef of silent, limestone-hardened souls, where the dead do not leave, but simply become the foundation for the living.

Beside them, the animals of the fissure live out their own frozen lives. 

The milk-beetles, whose small, white domes move along the bone-bridges, are the true custodians of the lanes. They do not graze on plants, for the star-moss is sacred and slow-growing; rather, they consume the minute flakes of sloughed-off plaster and dead skin shed by the wool-dwellers. They are the living brooms of the metropolis, clearing the paths of any debris that might disrupt the perfect, millimeter-wide alignment of the limestone tracks. They move so slowly that a passenger on a beetle’s back—were there anyone fast enough to ride—would find the journey across a single bridge to last a human season.

And in the dark cornices, beneath the star-moss domes, sit the jade-green hunting spiders. Their funnels of silk are grey with age, spun from a thread that is almost entirely composed of mineralized web-paste. They do not scurry. They do not leap. They are masters of the long wait. Their prey are the lace-winged moths, who drift down from the upper world like falling ash on the rare days when a draft enters the fissure. When a moth touches the web, the spider does not rush out. It waits. It may wait three days, monitoring the vibration of the silk, allowing the moth to tire itself out of its own accord in the dense, heavy air of the slot. Only when the moth has become as still as the stone does the spider creep forward to claim its cold, dry meal, its movements as deliberate and clean as the stroke of a clock.

To look into this fissure is to look at a machine that has found its perfect grease. There is no friction here. There is no ambition, no empire, no waste. The inhabitants have climbed into the heart of the stone, pulled its cold mantle around their shoulders, and decided that the highest form of living is to be still. They are a monument to the beauty of the pause—a small, white, pulse of light in the vast, grey silence of the northern plains, waiting out the universe one drop of water at a time.
