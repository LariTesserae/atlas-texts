At first, there is only the stone and the white fire. 

If you were to sit in the Chamber of the Salt-Loom for an hour, you would see nothing but the lime-basin and the great, smoking pillar of sun. The eye, defeated by the sheer scarcity of form, assumes the room is dead. 

But if you remain until the sun-shaft begins its late-afternoon shift—when the air cools by a fraction of a degree and the shadows at the room’s perimeter turn from gray to a deep, bruised violet—the limestone walls will appear to dissolve. Or rather, parts of the wall will detach themselves.

These are the *Stones-With-Hands*. They are the only breathing things in the chamber, flat-bodied reptiles whose ancestors must have entered this pocket when the plateau above was still a swamp of reeds, and who stayed as the water shrank away into these secret, white-ribbed depths.

### The Shell of the Air

To understand the hand-sized reptiles, one must understand their relationship to the light-column. They do not cross it. To them, the great white pillar in the center of the chamber is not space; it is a solid mountain of heat, a wall of pure energy that would desiccate their skin in seconds. They live entirely in the "ring"—the circular gallery of shade that runs around the chamber’s circumference.

Their bodies are built for the rim. They are so flat that they have virtually no profile; their bones are like dry elm leaves, thin and slightly curved to match the concave sweep of the outer limestone. Their skin is not scaled in the manner of desert snakes, but is instead a dry, chalky rind—an epidermis of porous calcium and ancient dust that mimics the white-gold limestone so perfectly that even their shadows are minimized by the bevel of their flanks. 

Their eyes are their most striking feature: large, obsidian-dark spheres, entirely lidless, but set deep beneath heavy, shelf-like brows of translucent, honey-colored bone. These brows act like the brim of a plasterer’s cap, keeping the terrible glare of the central column from blinding them, while allowing them to hunt for the slow, regular drift of moisture along the walls.

They move with a geological patience. To a human, their motion is imperceptible. A hand-shaped reptile decides to move three feet to the left to intercept a descending water-droplet; this journey will occupy its entire morning. It lifts one five-fingered hand—the bones inside as fine as grass-stems—and places it down. It waits for the heart to beat twice. It moves the second hand. This slowness is not laziness; it is a calculated, low-burning economy. In a world where the only food is the occasional paper-dry flower of a jade succulent and the only water is a succession of beads, to sweat is to die.

### The Garden of the Beads

Around the upper rim of the basin, where the rising heat of the day meets the cool, deep stone of the ceiling, the "moisture-fall" begins.

The reptiles are gardeners of this moisture. It is here that their quiet, manual intelligence reveals itself. They do not build nests—the bare limestone offers no material for walls or roofs, and they have no need of shelter in a room that never changes—but they are obsessive caretakers of the grooves.

Each reptile maintains a "run"—a three- or four-yard stretch of wall containing two or three of the hairline channels eroded into the limestone. If a channel becomes clogged with salt crust or the powdery residue of the upper-world dust that falls through the selenite lens, the reptile will clean it. 

Using their five-fingered hands, they select tools from their small, clean piles at the base of the wall:
*   **The Siphons:** These are the hollow wing-bones of swifts or bats that fell through the selenite throat centuries ago and died in the heat-shaft. The reptiles clean these bones, keeping them free of grit. When a water-drop lodges in a crevice too deep for their tongues to reach, they insert the bone, seal the end with a finger, and draw the drop out by primitive vacuum, transferring it to their mouths or to a dry crevice where a young succulent is trying to take root.
*   **The Chert Scrapers:** Small, almond-shaped flakes of red silica. Red is the only color in the chamber that does not belong to the limestone or the jade-buttons. These chert pieces are precious; they do not belong to the chamber, but have been brought up over generations from some deeper, dark gravel-bed through a narrow crawlway near the basin's floor. The reptiles use them to scrape away the mineral crusts that threaten to choke the moisture-grooves.

As they scrape, a dry, gravelly *clink-tock* echoes through the vault—a tiny, metallic music that joins the soft *shhh* of the air-column's draft.

### The Salt-Loom and the Skin

The salt they scrape from the walls is not discarded. It is their medicine, their soap, and their clothing. 

Because they live in a humid ring beneath a dry dome, the reptiles are constantly threatened by a white, powdery fungus that grows in the damp limestone seeps. Left unchecked, this fungus would invade the joints of their fingers and rot their thin, translucent eyelids. 

To combat this, the reptiles gather the scraped salt into hollows in the basin floor. They grind it into an ultra-fine flour using flat, black river-pebbles as pestles. Once ground, a reptile will spend hours rubbing the dry salt into its skin, using its long, double-jointed fingers to reach every crease of its neck and underbelly. The salt kills the fungus, leaving the reptile smelling of old seawater and sun-baked stone. 

When they have finished their salt-bath, they do not look white; they look silver, their bodies reflecting the dim, secondary light that bounces off the limestone walls from the central fire-column.

### The Marriage of the Jade

Near the water-runs grow the jade-buttons. They are the third part of this closed system. 

The buttons are entirely dependent on the reptiles for survival. Left to themselves, the plants would be choked by their own salt-crusts within seasons. The reptiles clear the salt from around the green leather crowns of the plants, and in return, they eat the dry, papery husks of the flowers.

These flowers do not bloom like common desert plants. They do not care about the rain, which may not fall on the plateau for three years at a time. Instead, they bloom by the calendar of the sun-shaft. 

As the earth tilts through the seasons, the angle of the great selenite lens shifts by a fraction of an inch. For a few days in winter, the edges of the central light-column will touch a specific ledge of the wall that is usually in shadow. When that hot light kisses a jade-button, the plant reacts within minutes. A single, white, trumpet-shaped bloom opens. It does not smell of nectar; it smells of hot wax and iron.

The reptiles know the path of the sun-shaft as if it were carved into their skin. They will gather at the edge of the light-zone, waiting just out of range of the heat. They do not fight for the flower; there is no aggression in their quiet, cold-blooded world. Instead, they wait with their lidless eyes fixed on the white cup. 

After three hours, the sun-shaft moves on. The flower, deprived of the light and dried by the super-heated air, collapses into a crisp, brown wafer. The nearest reptile—the one whose turn it is by some wordless estimation of seniority or need—reaches out a hand, plucks the dry wafer, and places it in its mouth. It is their only food. It tastes of dust and concentrated potassium, but it is enough to keep their slow hearts beating for another moon.

### The Sleep of the Basin

When the sun finally dips below the rim of the sinkhole on the plateau above, the light-column does not fade; it is snuffed out instantly, as if a hand had been placed over a lamp.

The chamber does not grow dark immediately. The selenite crystal in the ceiling remains luminous for an hour, holding the blue, twilight sky of the desert like a cold gem. 

Now, the reptiles move down from the walls. They do not seek crevices or holes; there are none in this smooth basin. Instead, they lie down together in the lowest, flattest part of the room, near where the hot column of air was just rising. 

They do not pile on top of one another. They lie side by side, their flat bodies fitting together like the tiles of a hearth, their five-fingered hands occasionally touching. They do not sleep in the human sense. They simply enter a state of stillness so profound that their breathing can no longer be heard.

Their skin, which was gold in the morning and silver in the afternoon, turns the cold, blue-gray of slate. They wait. They are as much a part of the Chamber of the Salt-Loom as the limestone channels or the crystal lens above, keeping the water flowing, keeping the salt clean, until the sun returns to wake the engine once more.
