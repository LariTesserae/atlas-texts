At the bottom of the mirror-flat pools, beneath the thin sheets of weeping water, live the first of the ledge’s inhabitants. 

They are small, circular discs, no larger than a child’s palm, completely transparent and nearly invisible to the casual eye. They are known only by the way they bend the light. Like living magnifying lenses, they lie flat against the grey-gold silt, rendering the fine grain of the sandstone beneath them in sharp, hyper-clear focus. 

These are the *Lenses*. They possess no limbs, no eyes, and no organs of scent or sound. They are composed entirely of a dense, structured gel, similar in consistency to the sap of the giant spade-leafed plants above them, but shielded by a tough, vitreous membrane. Their life is spent in a state of absolute, motionless feeding. They do not hunt; they absorb. As the mineral-heavy water of the weeping walls slides over them, they extract the dissolved silica and calcium, using it to slowly reinforce their own glassy skins. 

Their movement is geological. To cross a single foot of the sandstone basin takes a Lens several summers. They achieve this by dissolving a minuscule layer of the stone directly beneath their forward edge, sliding into the self-made microscopic groove, and redepositing the dissolved minerals behind them as a trail of smooth, glazed quartz. When two Lenses meet, they do not fight or flee. They merge. They lie one atop the other for years, slowly sharing chemical memories and exchanging genetic material through their porous skins, before parting as three or four smaller, perfectly round discs.

***

Higher up, within the five or six green "islands" of velvet moss, lives a different kind of presence. 

If you were to gently part the thick, emerald fibers of a moss hummock—a task that would feel like separating the hairs of a sleeping animal—you would find the *Nodes*. 

These are soft, pale, egg-shaped bodies, colored like peeled almonds, nestled deep in the damp, dark interior of the moss. They are the pneumatic hearts of the islands. They have no faces, but their skin is covered in a down of fine, golden cilia that detect the subtlest changes in barometric pressure. 

Because the cliff face behaves like a giant lung, drawing air in and out of the valley, these Nodes act as secondary, internal bellows. When the valley air rises and the pressure within the amphitheater drops, the Nodes slowly compress, expelling thin, sweet-scented vapors into the roots of the spade-leafed plants. When the cliff-lung inhales, the Nodes swell, drawing the humid air through the filtering moss to extract microscopic spores and pollen that have drifted up from the lower world.

They are incredibly sensitive to vibration. When the giant, plum-sized drop of water finally falls from the tip of the translucent leaf and strikes the stone basin below, the impact—though silent to human ears—sends a physical shockwave through the wet sandstone. Within the moss hummocks, the Nodes register this vibrations instantly. They respond with a slow, coordinated contraction, a ripple of movement that passes through the entire green island like a sigh, causing the giant, rubbery leaves to tilt slightly, catching the next slow weep of the wall.

***

But the true masters of this high sanctuary are those who shaped the stone basins and left the volcanic tool. 

They did not depart. They simply stopped moving. 

In the deepest curve of the red-gold amphitheater, where the sandstone wall curves inward to form a shallow, protective shadow, there are several large, undulating pillars. At first glance, they look like natural buttresses, licked smooth by ten thousand years of mineral-heavy water. 

But if one stands before them and allows the eyes to adjust to the direct, full light, their true nature emerges. Inside the semi-translucent, glass-like sheath of calcium carbonate that has built up over millennia, the silhouettes of ancient figures are visible. They are sitting. Their knees are drawn up to their chests, their long, mineral-encrusted arms wrapped around their shins, their heads bowed low toward the wet stone floor.

These are the *Sleepers*. 

Epochs ago, they were the ones who carried the river-rolled volcanic stones up from the valley, who patiently ground the circular basins into the bedrock to catch the weeping water. But here, in this warm, humid place where nothing rots and the air never stirs, the boundary between the living and the stone is thin. 

Over centuries of contemplation, they sat against the weeping walls. The mineral glaze that slides down the sandstone did not bury them; it integrated them. Molecule by molecule, as the water licked their skin, their biological tissues were replaced with silica and calcite. 

They are not dead. Their hearts, now composed of a porous, red-gold jasper, beat once every spring. The pulse is so slow, so deep, that it registers only as a faint, sub-sonic hum in the bedrock—a low *thrum* that sustains the rhythm of the entire alcove. Their nervous systems have petrified into highly conductive pathways of white quartz, through which thoughts pass with the majestic slowness of tectonic plates. They are dreaming the history of the valley below.

The green islands of velvet moss are anchored directly to their shoulders and bowed heads. The giant, spade-leafed plants draw their sap from the mineral-rich runoff that has filtered through the Sleepers' stone limbs. The living jade beneath the moss is made of their own calcified fingers, their petrified breath, and the ancient, fallen leaves of the garden they planted.

Everything here belongs to a single, slow transaction. The water weeps, the Lenses polish, the Nodes breathe, the plants petrify, and the Sleepers dream, balanced forever on the high ledge of the world.
