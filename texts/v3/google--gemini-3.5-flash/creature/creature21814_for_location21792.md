### The Great Biofilm and the Slit-Feeders

At the lowest level of this world, where the warm mineral runoff spills from the millions of low pipe-mouths, lives the foundation of all things: the biofilm. It is not a passive residue, but a complex, cooperative tissue of grey, white, and ochre mats. In the slower channels, this slime grows to the thickness of a human hand, self-organizing into long, undulating ribbons that mimic the movement of river weeds. It is cool to the touch but smells intensely of sulfur, wet mortar, and yeast. 

Within these mats lives a creature that is half-shell, half-sponge: the **Silt-Sponge**. These are small, stationary lumps of porous, lime-grey calcium that anchor themselves to the joints in the concrete. They do not have eyes, limbs, or mouths; instead, they draw the mineral-rich water through their porous bodies, filtering out iron oxides and sulfur-loving bacteria. When the automatic sluice gates shift and the water pressure drops, the Silt-Sponges hiss—a tiny, collective sigh as they contract, squeezing out excess moisture to keep their internal chambers wet until the next warm wave arrives.

### The Concrete-Eaters (The Shinglers)

Clinging to the monumental vertical walls of the cubic structures are the **Calcrete Snails**. Their existence is an extraordinary loop of chemical recycling. They do not eat plants, for there are none; instead, their radulae—their file-like tongues—are hardened with iron. They scrape the very concrete and slate, rasping away microscopic layers of the stone to digest the lime and the dense bacterial crusts that grow upon it. 

Their shells are not spiral domes but flat, heavy shields, overlapping one another like slate shingles on a roof. These shells are indistinguishable from the grey concrete beneath them, save for the faint, damp glisten of their mucus trails. When millions of these snails move in response to a shift in the mist, the sound is like a slow, heavy sigh—the scratch of a billion tiny stones dragging across a sidewalk. They are the architects of the buildings’ erosion, slowly rounding the sharp, modern edges of the concrete blocks into the soft, melted shapes of ancient river bluffs.

### The Dwellers of the Grates (The Iron-Lurkers)

Beneath the cast-iron floor grates, where the boiling water bubbles up from the deep dark, lives a specialized class of crustaceans known as **Iron-Lurkers**. 

These are pale, flattened crabs, the size of a human palm, but as thin as a coin. Their legs are tipped with sharp, hook-like claws designed specifically to grip the rusted webbing of the grates against the violent rush of steaming water. They have no eyes—only long, whip-like antennae that are sensitive to the slightest change in water temperature and vibration. They live in the thermal gradient, suspended directly over the rising steam. 

The Iron-Lurkers are the mechanics of this world, though they do not know it. They survive by picking off the dead midges that fall through the grates and scraping the thick, black iron-sulfide crusts from the plumbing. By clearing these vents of blockages, they ensure the steam continues to flow, keeping their nurseries warm. Their shells are stained an oily, iridescent black-blue, matching the patina of the ancient metal they cling to.

### The Silt-Sleepers

In the deep, slow-moving aqueducts, where the concrete has cracked and gathered a thick layer of fine, grey mineral silt, the **Silt-Sleepers** reside. These are the three-foot-long salamanders, but closer inspection reveals them to be almost larval in their arrested development. Their bodies are bloated, rubbery, and a translucent, vein-mapped pinkish-white, like peeled fruit. 

They are nearly blind, possessing only dark, light-sensitive spots beneath their skin that can tell the difference between the grey daylight and the shadow of a passing bat-bird. Their red, external gills look like plumes of delicate, underwater ferns, waving gently in the current to draw oxygen from the warm, mineral-heavy water. 

The Sleepers are slow to the point of near-stillness. They can lie in the same concrete depression for weeks, their mouths slightly agape, waiting for a blind crayfish to bump into their sensitive lips. When they do move, their transition is surprisingly graceful; they swim with a slow, lateral undulation of their thick tails, leaving a wake of swirling grey silt behind them that slowly settles back onto the floor like falling snow.

### The Sky of Ash (The Vapor-Flitters)

In the humid air above the plazas, where the amber mist never clears, the sky belongs to the **Vapor-Flitters**. 

These eyeless flying creatures have bodies no larger than a sparrow, but their wingspan is wide and silent. Their wings are made of a greasy, charcoal-colored hide that repels the constant, weeping moisture; the water beads on their backs and rolls off in tiny droplets as they fly. 

The Flitters do not possess feathers or fur, but are covered in a fine, velvet-like down that dampens sound. They navigate the maze of concrete monoliths and spinning iron flywheels through a delicate, heat-sensitive pit organ in their snout. They can "see" the warm plumes of steam rising from the grates and the cold drafts coming off the large concrete slabs. 

They hunt the clouds of hum-midges by flying with their wide, scoop-like mouths open. To watch them from below is to see a thousand black flakes of ash drifting lazily through the amber haze, suddenly twisting with a sudden, jerky agility to catch an insect before dissolving back into the grey ceiling of the sky. At night—or what passes for night in this world of perpetual twilight—they roost on the warm, dry undersides of the iron catwalks, packed so tightly together they look like a continuous, breathing coat of dark felt.

### The Steam-Striders

Perhaps the strangest occupants of the wet plazas are the **Steam-Striders**. These are extraordinarily long-legged, spindle-jointed arachnids that stand nearly two feet tall, though their bodies are no larger than a walnut. 

Their legs are thin as wire and tipped with dense pads of hydrophobic hairs, allowing them to walk upon the surface tension of the shallow, hot canals without sinking. They format a thin, delicate layer of air beneath their feet, gliding across the water like skaters on ice.

The Striders are scavengers of the surface. When a flush of boiling water kills a colony of midges or drowns a cluster of snails, the Striders are the first to arrive, skimming over the current to pluck the dead from the surface with long, needle-like pedipalps. They are incredibly sensitive to the rhythmic vibrations of the deep steam engines; they can feel the *thump... thump... thump* through the water's surface, and they use these vibrations to coordinate their movements, gathering in large, silent circles around the vents just before a fresh discharge of warm water is scheduled to arrive.

### The Great Loom-Worms

Deep within the flooded tunnels, where the concrete pipes disappear into the subterranean foundations of the basin, live the **Loom-Worms**. These are blind, white annelids that grow up to ten feet in length and as thick as a man's thigh. 

They do not swim; they anchor their tails inside the joints of the iron pipes and let their long, muscular bodies stream out in the high-pressure water currents. Their skin is covered in thousands of tiny, hair-like cilia that catch the organic debris, bacterial clumps, and dead crayfish swept down from the plazas above. 

As they feed, they excrete a tough, fibrous mucus that hardens in the water into a substance like wet nylon. Over centuries, these secretions have lined the interiors of the ancient pipes, coating the rusting iron in a resilient, self-healing sleeve of organic plastic. The Loom-Worms are the reason the plumbing still holds pressure. Without their constant, secretory maintenance, the ancient iron pipes would have burst and crumbled into the silt ages ago.

***

In this basin, there is no distinction between the mechanical and the organic. The machines are not invaders; they are the ancient, cold-blooded parents of this ecosystem. The crayfish do not know that the canal they crawl through was poured by human hands; to them, the concrete is simply a flat, warm bedrock that never shifts, and the great, sixty-foot flywheel is merely a slow, metallic moon that rises and falls through the steam, turning their world with a heavy, predictable grace.
