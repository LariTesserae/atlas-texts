To live in this place is to be made of the dry world itself, to have traded the soft, weeping vulnerabilities of flesh for the dry resilience of horn, lacquer, and husk. 

The inhabitants of the hanging city—the weavers, the dwellers of the copper hives—are small, narrow creatures, rarely taller than a child’s forearm. In a world without water, they have no blood as we understand it. Their bodies are pressurized systems of golden, highly volatile resins that smell of pitch and pine-soot, encased in a jointed exoskeleton of dried, fibrous chitin. This outer shell sits in overlapping plates, colored the same dry orange of the Cinder-reeds and the pale ochre of the limestone. To look at one is to look at a small, upright figure woven from dried grass and varnished with some heavy, amber glaze.

They do not have lips or wet mouths; their faces are smooth masks of polished, horn-like material, interrupted only by the broad, dark slits of their breathing-pores and their eyes. 

Their eyes are their most striking feature: large, hemispherical lenses of solid, dry amber. They have no eyelids to blink, for there is no moisture to clear away; instead, they have delicate, comb-like fans of stiff bristle that sweep across the lenses with a dry *scritch* when the limestone dust grows too thick. These amber eyes do not see colors as a wet-world eye does. They are built to filter out the brilliant, murderous glare of the vertical noon and to register the subtle, vibrating currents of static electricity that cling to the canyon walls. To them, the yellow-ochre moss does not merely sit on the rock; it glows with a shifting, crackling aura of pale blue friction.

Because their bodies are dry and light—their hollow bones are composed of structural calcium similar to the Cinder-reeds—they do not walk so much as they cling, leap, and glide. Their hands and feet are five-fingered, but the digits are impossibly long, ending in tiny, needle-sharp hooks of black horn. They climb the vertical ropes of their suspended city with a frantic, silent grace, moving sideways and upside-down along the underside of their clay-hive homes. They do not know what a flat floor is. To them, space is a three-dimensional web, and "down" is not a destination but a lethal, hot abyss where the dead dust settles.

Their hearing is not a matter of delicate ear-drums, which would be ruptured within minutes of birth by the deafening, bone-shaking screech of the wind-pipes. Instead, they are anatomically deaf to the high-frequency roar of the Cinder-reeds. Their bodies are built to filter out the scream. Inside their hollow legs and chests are dry, suspended membranes of thin reed-parchment that act as internal seismographs. They do not "hear" the canyon's roar; they feel it as a low, structural heartbeat—a constant, comforting purr that tells them the wind is still driving the city’s updraft. 

But when a finger taps on a beaten copper plate, the sharp, clean vibration travels through the tension lines of the city, up through their hooked feet, and directly into their skeletal frame. 

This is how they converse. Two weavers sitting at opposite ends of a long rope-bridge do not look at each other's faces. They touch the line with their long fingers. One will tap a rapid, syncopated rhythm against a copper pin embedded in the rope—*click-click-clat*—and the other will feel the message translate through the soles of their feet as a series of sharp, rhythmic tugs. Their language is a complex system of percussive poetry. A mother can soothe her child in the dark of a clay hive by striking a tiny copper plate with her hand-claw, sending a warm, rhythmic hum through the walls of their cell that feels like a physical embrace.

For longer distances, across the wide, sun-slit voids between the two canyon outer-walls, they use the sails. These are large, triangular flags made of the paper-thin, orange fans of the Cinder-reed, stitched together with static-charged moss-silk. Two signalers, standing on tiny platforms jutting from the highest clay-hives, will wave these flags through the blinding bar of sunlight. Because the light here is so sharp and the shadows so dark, a single tilt of an orange sail can flash a beam of reflected light across the chasm like a mirror, a silent shout that can be read miles down the slot.

In the dark, secure interiors of the clay-hives, life is quieter. The hives are built from the limestone dust itself, mixed with the masticated resins of the reed-gourds and baked by the vertical sun into a material as hard as porcelain. Within these chambers, the deafening external screech is muffled to a dry, raspy whisper. Each hive is a labyrinth of tiny, horizontal shelves where the inhabitants sleep. They do not lie down; they hang. Suspended from the ceiling by their foot-claws, their bodies fold in upon themselves, joint by joint, until they look like dry, dormant seed-pods hanging from the rafters.

Living alongside them in this suspended ecology are the *Slick-Beetles*—but the weavers do not treat them as wild beasts. The beetles are their cattle, their source of oil and shelter-glaze. The beetles are heavy, slow, and completely indifferent to the wind. They drift through the yellow moss-beds like blue pebbles, grazing on the dry mineral spores that accumulate there through static attraction. The weavers herd them with long poles of split cane, guiding them back to the hanging stalls beneath the hives. 

There, they gently scrape the thick, blue wax from the beetles’ backs. This wax is a precious substance: it is the only thing in the canyon that is entirely frictionless and impervious to the drying heat. The weavers use it to lubricate the hand-cranked basket-pulleys that haul dry reeds up from the lower depths, and to coat the suspension lines where they rub against the sharp limestone edges of the canyon wall, preventing the constant, howling wind from sawing the city loose.

There are also the *Drifters*—creatures that are almost entirely hair and silk. They are no larger than a grain of dust, but they live in colonies of millions, drifting on the warm updrafts like clouds of dry lint. When the wind dies for a brief hour at sunset, these drifters settle upon the suspension lines, weaving incredibly fine, sticky webs that catch the floating pollen of the Cinder-reeds. The weavers harvest these webs, spinning them on small, handheld spindles into the incredibly strong, static-charged threads that hold their entire world together.

Nothing is wasted. There is no decay here, only slow, wind-driven reclamation. When an inhabitant dies, their body does not rot; it dry-cures within days, becoming as hard and light as a piece of driftwood. Their companions do not bury them. Instead, they gently dismantle the deceased’s exoskeleton, using the tough, flat plates of the chest and limbs to patch the worn walls of the clay-hives, or carving the long, hollow leg-bones into new needles for the loom. 

To them, there is no sacred boundary between the living city and the people who build it. They are made of the same fiber, held together by the same resin, singing the same dry, bone-rattling song as the wind that rushes forever upward toward the white, blinding sky.
