They are shaped like the basalt spires they inhabit, though they are made of a softer, more yielding substance. To look at them is to see the human form stripped of its urgency. 

They are tall and singularly narrow, with long, dense limbs that move with the heavy grace of a pendulum swinging in a vacuum. Their skin has the cool, matte texture of river-slate or fine grey horn. It is entirely free of the pinkness of blood or the yellow of bile; their internal fluids are clear, thick, and slow-moving, like cold sap. They do not sweat, nor do they produce oil. When they stand for hours in the open air, the silver condensation of the mountain forms on their shoulders and the bridges of their noses in perfect, tiny beads, exactly as it does on the stone walls and the grass ropes. Under the 45-degree sun, these water droplets act as tiny, trembling lenses, making their grey skin appear to glitter with a faint, dew-covered frost.

Their eyes are large and set wide apart in their long skulls. The pupils are not round but horizontal slits, dark and deep, mirroring the steady, flat horizon. They do not squint against the sharp afternoon light, nor do they blink to moisten their gaze, for their eyes are permanently lubricated by the thin, cold moisture of the air. They can look directly at the sun without injury, seeing it not as a blinding glare but as a distinct, solid disk of pale gold set into the indigo sky, surrounded by the silver pricks of the daytime stars.

They do not wear clothes for warmth or modesty, as the temperature never changes and they have no sense of shame. They wear garments only as a way of carrying their tools or marking their place within the geography. These garments are aprons and kilts woven from the tough mountain grass, stiff and smelling of sun-dried lichen. Because there is no dust or soot in this high air, these clothes never stain or rot; a kilt woven by a woman’s grandmother five hundred years ago is still as clean and pale green as the day the grass was harvested.

There are no children among them, nor has anyone here ever seen a person die of old age. Their population is fixed, a static circle of individuals who have known one another since the beginning of memory. There is no word in their tongue for *birth*, *growth*, or *decay*. When they speak of a person, they do not use a name that implies descent or lineage; instead, they name them by their physical coordinates or their habitual occupation. One is "The Long-Armed Maker who occupies the third spire from the eastern gap." Another is "The Silent One who watches the white sheep from the terrace of the cistern." 

Their language is not a fast, fluid stream of syllables, but a series of dry, percussive tones and low, resonant whistles that carry across the vast chasms with startling clarity. They speak rarely. Two people might stand on opposite sides of a five-hundred-yard gorge, holding a conversation that lasts for three days. One will strike a flat piece of slate with a copper rod—a clear, metallic *ping* that rings out through the thin air. An hour later, having considered the sound, the other will respond with a low, vocal breath that mimics the wind passing through a hollow stone tower. There is no impatience in these exchanges. To them, an hour is no more than a breath, and a century is merely a long afternoon.

Their lives are occupied by tasks that would seem agonizingly slow to any mortal creature, yet to them, these tasks are a form of quiet, vegetative existence. They split the dark slate with copper wedges, tapping the metal into the rock with soft, rhythmic blows of a wooden mallet. They might spend fifty years shaping a single stone for a terrace wall, ensuring its joint lines are so perfectly flush that not even the mountain condensation can seep between them. They do not build out of a need for shelter—for the weather is never harsh—but because the act of stacking stone is how they think. Their architecture is their memory; to look at a tower is to read a thought that was settled ten centuries ago and has never been revised.

When they are not working, they enter a state they call "the stillness." They will sit on the edge of a dry-stone terrace, their long legs dangling over the thousands of feet of empty air, their dry-slate eyes fixed on the distant horizon. They can remain in this position for weeks without moving a muscle. During these times, the glass-winged moths will settle on their shoulders, vibrating their wings in silent unison, and the mountain sheep will graze right up to their sides, untroubled by their presence. In the stillness, they do not think in words or pictures; they simply allow the permanent, slantwise light to fill them, becoming as much a part of the landscape as the basalt needles.

They have no myths of a beginning, and no prophecies of an end. If you were to ask them where they came from, they would not understand the question. They would point to the basalt cliffs, then to the sky, and finally to their own grey hands, indicating that all three are the same thing, made of the same cold, eternal substance, set here forever under the unwashed indigo of the sky.
