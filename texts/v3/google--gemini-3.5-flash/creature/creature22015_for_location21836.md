The people who live in the Phloem Basin do not think of themselves as occupants of a house, but as cells within an organ. They are few—scarcely more than a hundred individuals across the thirty households—and they have the patient, unhurried demeanor of creatures whose lives are measured by the slow rise of sap rather than the ticking of brass gears.

They are a people of slender, fluid proportions, shaped by generations of negotiating three-dimensional space on narrow footings. Their bodies have adapted to the three-g gravity of their vertical home—not a physical gravity, which remains normal, but a psychological gravity that forces the eye and the foot always upward or downward. They have long, prehensile toes that can grip the damp hempen rigging of the catwalks through the split soles of their boots, and their shoulders are remarkably broad and supple, developed from a lifetime of hauling themselves up the grey, ribbed stairs carved into the cambium.

In the green, submarine daylight of the Basin, their skin has a peculiar, translucent quality. It is not the chalky paleness of cave-dwellers, but a smooth, water-gilded ivory with a faint, greenish undertone, as if the chlorophyll of the skylight fronds had stained their blood. They do not have the harsh lines of sun-weathered people; the humid, mint-scented air keeps their skin plump and slick, like the surface of a peeled willow wand. Their hair is universally dark—a wet, river-weed black—and they wear it braided tight to their scalps to keep it from snagging on the copper mist-nets or catching in the slow-turning gearboxes of the looms.

Because the Basin is an acoustic sponge, deadening the voice before it can travel twenty yards, these people have developed two distinct ways of communicating. 

The first is the *Sigh-Talk*. When speaking face-to-face, they do not use the throat. They speak entirely in sibilants, soft dental clicks, and breathy fricatives that do not carry past the shoulder of the listener. To a stranger, a conversation between two Basin-dwellers sounds like a dry breeze rustling through dead ferns. 

The second, and more common, is the *Tread-Talk*. Because the entire wooden structure of the node conducts vibration with exquisite clarity, they have turned the iron-shod soles of their boots and the tips of their long copper wrenches into instruments. A worker on the highest catwalk can signal a valve-turner eighty feet below by tapping a rhythmic code onto an iron masonry bolt. One sharp strike of a wrench means *the tide is rising*; three soft, rolling taps of the heel mean *help me haul this basket*. This "wood-telegraph" is constant and subconscious. Even while sleeping in their bracketed hammocks, they keep one palm pressed against the living cambium wall, reading the vibrations of the hamlet—the *chuff-thump* of the pumps, the distant scuttle of an elder returning home, the warning creak of an overloaded gantry—as a sailor reads the stars.

Their clothing is a testament to their relationship with the giant vegetable node. They do not use wool or cotton, which would rot within a month in the perpetual room-rain. Instead, they wear garments woven from *bast*—the tough, fibrous inner bark of the trees outside, harvested during dangerous expeditions through the node's outer pores. These fibers are spun on clicking wooden looms, then steeped in vats of the pale amber latex drawn up by the transpiration pumps. The resulting fabric is stiff but entirely waterproof, smelling of scorched sugar and tar. Over years of wear, these garments take on the shape of the wearer’s body, creasing into permanent, shining folds that look like polished horn.

At the center of their society are the *Hydrologists*—though they do not use so formal a title, referring to themselves simply as "The Cups." These are the individuals responsible for the copper-wire nets and the vertical pipes that cling to the bark. They are easily recognized by the heavy, green-patinated copper collars they wear to protect their clavicles from the constant weight of their carrying-frames, and by the thick, calloused pads on their fingertips where the copper wire has worn away the skin. The Cups are the most active residents, constantly climbing into the upper void to clear debris—bits of dried leaf-scale or dead moss—from the high nets. They move with an alarming, spider-like grace, swinging from the rib-vaulted ceiling sixty feet in the air, held only by a single line of braided bast.

Beneath the Hydrologists are the *Saturators*, who tend the fermentation vats beneath the petrified peat floor. They are shorter, thicker-set, and smell of wood-marrow and fermented mint. They spend their days in the warm, dark crawlspaces beneath the roots, shoveling leaf-mulch—which falls from the skylight fronds during the autumn of the outer world—into the iron digesters that feed the steam-pistons. Their skin is darker, stained a permanent grey-purple from contact with the raw cambium sap.

The Basin-dwellers are not long-lived, but they do not rot. As they age, their bodies do not soften; they stiffen. Their joints become dry and click when they move, a sound remarkably similar to the gasket-beetles. An elder who has spent seventy years in the damp chill of the Basin will begin to spend more and more time on the lowest peat floor, where the warmth of the fermentation vats rises. They sit silently on low benches carved from petrified root, their eyes half-closed, their hands resting flat against the floor. 

When a Basin-dweller dies, there is no burial in the ground, for the petrified peat floor is too hard and the living root below must not be fouled by decay. Instead, the family carries the body to a "healing niche"—a place where the inner cambium has been wounded or pruned in the past. They lay the body into the depression, secure it with copper bands, and coat it with a thick paste of ground charcoal and latex. 

Over the course of several seasons, the great tree responds. The smooth, grey lips of scar tissue roll back over the niche, sealing the body within the wood. The iron decorations they wore in life—their hooks, their copper collars, their boot-plates—are left protruding from the bark, identifying who lies within. Over generations, the lower walls of the Basin have become thick with these silent, iron-sprouted bulges, each one a monument to a worker whose calcium and nitrogen have been returned to the great vascular flow that keeps the skylight fronds green eighty feet above.

The only other living things are the gasket-beetles, and they are treated with a quiet, domestic familiarity. Children often keep them as pets, polishing their bronze backs with oil-rags until they shine like new kettles. A child will sit on a catwalk with a beetle on their knees, feeding it scraps of grease from the steam-pistons, watching the slow, mechanical movement of its blind head. In the silence of the Basin, the dry *skritch-skritch* of a beetle’s legs on an iron pipe is the closest thing these people have to a lullaby.
