To survive in this broth, life has had to discard the luxuries of the surface. There is no use down here for vision, for green pigment, or for lungs that require dry, clean air. Everything that crawls, swims, or clings in this suffocating basin is an expression of the environment’s two great resources: endless, warm, mineral-thick water and the endless, black industrial grease.

At the lowest tier of existence, where the water meets the iron, are the *Greaselickers*. These are not single creatures but dense, colonial mats of single-celled organisms that have bypassed the need for sunlight entirely. They appear as a slick, purple-black sheen that coats the reciprocating rods of the pump houses. They do not merely tolerate the vaporized lubricating oil that settles out of the air; they drink it. The oil is their starch, their sugar. Where the steel cylinders plunge into the rock, these colonies form thick, undulating sleeves of slime that scream softly as the iron slides through them, instantly regenerating their sheared-away outer layers with every stroke of the piston. 

Scavenging upon these mats are the curd-colored cave-crabs, but they are not the only things that harvest the greasy harvest of the engines. 

Among the rusted gears and the half-drowned boilers lives a class of blind, soft-shelled barnacles known to the basin’s rare human visitors as *Clinker-Buds*. Unlike their marine cousins, these barnacles do not stick to clean rock; they anchor themselves directly to the hot bronze valves and the vibrating copper pipes. Their shells are not made of lime, but of a heavy, dark laminate of calcium and rust-scale that they scrape from their hosts. They have a single, long, feathery appendage—not a leg, but a modified gill-net—which they wave rhythmically in the steam vents. They feed on the hot mist, straining out the sulfur-reducing bacteria and the droplets of tallow. When a valve blows, releasing a jet of scalding steam that would strip human flesh to the bone, the Clinker-Buds open wide, basking in the heat, their dark shells glowing a dull, hot orange.

Deep in the warm, agitated pools where the three-foot salamanders drift, the food chain is anchored by the *Silt-Sieves*. These are massive, sluggish creatures resembling eyeless, skinless harbor seals, though their hides are the color of raw lard and their limbs have degenerated into flat, spade-like paddles of cartilage. They do not hunt with speed; instead, they wallow in the grey mud beneath the churning pistons. They have broad, flat heads that are almost entirely mouth, lined with hundreds of horn-like ridges instead of teeth. They plow through the sediment, scooping up mouthfuls of the metal-heavy silt, straining out the blind crabs and the soft-bodied grubs that nest in the rot. Their bodies have become so adapted to the heavy vibrations of the engines that their hearts are said to beat in synchronization with the nearest pump house. If a piston ceases to slide, the Silt-Sieves in that pool will often grow listless, roll onto their backs, and slowly suffocate, their internal rhythms broken by the silence.

But there are also those who are not entirely of this geology, yet have been claimed by it.

In the interstitial spaces—the dry cavities behind the steam pipes, the hollows inside abandoned boilers, and the high, crumbling ledges above the water line—lives a population of degenerated humanoids. They are called the *Tallow-Men*, though they have long since lost the right to the name of man. They are the descendants of the long-forgotten shift-workers and engineers who were sent down to bolt these machines to the basalt centuries ago and were left behind when the shafts collapsed or the surface forgot them.

The Tallow-Men are tall, thin, and spindly, their limbs elongated by generations of reaching for handholds on the iron guide-wires. Their skin is translucent, the color of skimmed milk, showing the dark, sluggish map of their veins beneath. They have no eyes; the lids have fused shut over empty, fluid-filled sockets, though their brows are heavy and deeply lined from a lifetime of squinting against the invisible wind of the draft-shafts. 

They do not wear clothes of woven wool or cotton, which would rot within days in this steam. Instead, they dress in "flitches"—broad, cured sheets of the black slime-mold that they peel from the ironwork, treated with sulfur-grease until it is as supple and tough as wet leather. 

Because the roaring of the machinery is a physical, deafening wall that drowns out all spoken language, the Tallow-Men are entirely silent. They communicate using *The Clack*. Each of them carries a short, heavy rod of cold-rolled iron, worn smooth by the oil of their palms. By striking these rods against the copper conduits and the iron guide-wires, they send vibrations through the metal framework of the entire basin. They can feel these vibrations through their feet, through their fingertips, and through the bones of their jaws when they press their heads against the pipes. To a Tallow-Man, the ironwork is not dead metal; it is a giant, communal nervous system. A sequence of sharp, metallic raps can describe a rising water level three miles away, the failure of a pressure valve, or the approach of a hunting party from a neighboring turbine station.

They are the true symbionts of the machines. They do not understand the engineering of the engines—the science of the steam has long since degraded into a system of religious rites—but they know that if the pistons stop, the water rises, and if the water rises, they drown. 

They move through the steam like pale, greasy ghosts, carrying small buckets of rendered crab-fat. They climb the towering, shaking frames of the pumps, blind fingers searching for the hot, dry joints that need grease, slathering the fat onto the grinding steel with their bare hands. They clean the mineral crusts from the intake grates, scraping away the white calcium with their iron rods so the pumps can breathe. 

When a Tallow-Man dies, he is not buried in the shale, for there is no soil deep enough. Instead, his companions carry his body to the great, open gears of the lowest pump house. They lay him gently between the teeth of the slow-turning iron wheels. With a wet, heavy crunch, the machine takes him, grinding his bones into the grey silt, his grease into the gears, returning him to the engine that sustain them all.
