Because the world is a wool room, those who dwell here have no need for hard shells or defenses. They are soft-bodied, cartilaginous, and quiet. They are the **Ool**, though they have no spoken word for themselves; the name is merely the low, double-noted hum that vibrates through their throat-sacks when they touch foreheads in the green dusk.

### The Ool (The Quiet Ones)

To look upon an Ool is to see a creature made of the same damp, yielding material as the Weft itself. They stand nearly eight feet tall when drawn up to their full height, but they rarely stand straight. Their bodies are long and ribbon-supple, lacking a spine of true bone; instead, they are supported by a dense, pressurized column of clear fluid and interlocking rings of gristle that bend with a rubbery, hydraulic silence. 

They have no feet as we know them. Instead, their lower limbs terminate in broad, fleshy disks—like the undersides of great portobello mushrooms—which distribute their weight so perfectly across the mossy mattress that they do not break its surface-skin. Underneath these pads are thousands of microscopic, hair-like cilia that drink the condensation from the moss as they walk. They do not leave footprints; they leave only briefly flattened circles of velvet that rise back to their original loft a few minutes after the traveler has passed.

The skin of the Ool is the color of peeled almonds or unripe pears, covered in a fine, silver-green down that keeps them perpetually dry despite the warm, grease-saturated air. They have no hair, no nails, and no teeth: their mouths are wide, soft rings lined with ridges of hard, horn-like cartilage used to scrape the sugary grease from the giant liverwort stalks. 

They do not have ears on their heads. In this world where sound dies twenty paces from its source, ears are useless. Instead, the Ool "hear" through the soles of their feet and the palms of their flat, four-fingered hands. The skin there is thin and packed with specialized nerves that translate the low-frequency thrumming of the Weft into a complex map of the landscape. They can feel the slow, heavy movement of a Pith-Walker two miles away through the root-weave; they can feel the birth of a new cistern-pool in the valley before the water even begins to seep through the crust.

When they must speak, they do not shout into the swallowing air. They step close, pressing their palms to each other’s chests, or leaning their temples together. The vibrations of their vocal chambers pass directly through their skin and gristle, a silent, warm conversation of tremors and ticks that never enters the air at all. 

### The Nursery-Keepers of the Siphonophores

The Ool do not build. The concept of a wall or a roof is foreign to them in a world where the air is a constant, balmy blanket. They gather instead in the dips between the green domes, where the white, wicker-like skeletons of the ancient siphonophores sit like half-buried palaces.

Here, they cultivate the *Lanth-Ferns* that grow inside the calcified chambers. These ferns do not need soil; they feed on the damp marrow-dust of the old towers, and their fronds weep a thick, glowing milk that is both sweet and mildly numbing. In the white hollows of these towers, the Ool hang their young.

The Ool do not give birth to live young, nor do they lay hard eggs. Instead, they produce soft, leathery green galls—shrub-like pods that they graft directly onto the ribs of the siphonophore towers. For five summers, these pods grow, fed by the dripping milk of the Lanth-Ferns and the warm, circulating vapor of the valley. The parents visit them daily, not to guard them—for there are no predators in the Weft—but to scrape the moss from their skins and to press their palms against the swelling pods, singing to the unborn children through the lime-chalk of the towers.

When the child is ready, the pod does not crack; it softens into a jelly that the young Ool simply absorbs through its skin as it slips out onto the wet moss.

### The Great Bowers of the Pith-Walkers

Though the Ool are content to spend decades in a single valley, they do occasionally migrate. This is not done on foot, but aboard the *Pith-Walkers*. 

Because a Pith-Walker moves with the agonizing slowness of growing lichen, a journey across a single city-sized dome can take several human generations. To the Ool, a Pith-Walker is not a beast of burden, but a traveling island. When a clan decides to move, they build a *Bower* upon the creature’s broad, flat back. They do this by planting the runners of fast-growing *clinger-vines* into the beast’s thick, mossy hide. Within weeks, the vines weave themselves into a living, dome-shaped basket that covers the creature’s grey-green flank.

Inside this bower, three or four families will live. They do not steer the Pith-Walker; the concept of direction is loose in a world of endless domes. They simply go where the beast goes, feeding on the sweet grease that condenses on its long, drooping neck-flaps and the small, yellow starch-warts that grow in the folds of its skin. As the beast drifts across the green tableland, the Ool sit in the bower, exchanging long, silent histories through their fingertips, watching the bronze sun slide through the perpetual red afternoon. 

### The Blind Things of the Black Pools

Deep within the perfectly circular cistern-pools that lie between the domes, there is another kind of life entirely—one that never sees even the pale bronze light of the sky.

These are the *Sieve-Ribbons*. They are transparent, flat worms, some of them thirty cubits long and as wide as a cart-path. They have no eyes and no organs for sensing vibration, for the water in the cisterns is absolutely still. Instead, they float vertically in the black depths, looking like long strips of pale silk suspended in ice. 

The Sieve-Ribbons feed on the "marrow-soup" of the ancient, buried generations of plants that have dissolved at the bottom of the cisterns. Every few decades, when the internal pressures of the Weft shift, these pools will "sigh"—a giant bubble of warm, nitrogen-rich gas will rise from the dark interior, pushing a column of nutrient-rich broth to the surface. When this happens, the Sieve-Ribbons rise with it, their transparent skins flushing a delicate, bruised purple as they feed on the sudden abundance of carbon.

The Ool watch for these sighs. They gather at the edges of the pool, lying flat on their bellies with their arms submerged in the cold, clear water. They do not hunt the Ribbons; they wait for them to rise, and when the long, silk-like bodies brush against their palms, the Ool gently harvest the thin, nutrient-rich oil that coat the worms' skins. This oil is the only substance in the Weft that does not evaporate or dissolve; the Ool use it to seal their green egg-galls and to coat their skin when they must travel through the rare, dry rifts where the wind from the lower world blows cold and thin.

### The Transition

In the Weft, death is not an ending but a thickening. 

An Ool who has reached the end of their long, slow life—lasting perhaps three hundred years—does not sicken or wither. Instead, they simply grow heavy. Their joints lose their hydraulic pressure; their velvety skin begins to turn a deeper shade of emerald, and they find it increasingly difficult to lift their broad feet from the moss.

When this time comes, the Ool travels to the dip between two domes, where the soil is thickest and the sound of the deep water-tides is loudest. They lie down on their side, curled into a gentle crescent, and press their palms deep into the dry root-fibers of the ground.

Within days, the capillaries of the Weft begin to respond. The tiny, thread-like roots of the peat-mosses creep over the Ool’s body, weaving themselves into the silver-green down of their skin. There is no decay, no odor of rot, only a slow, quiet greening. After a single summer, the Ool is gone, turned into a soft, long mound of velvet that is indistinguishable from the rest of the floor.

For centuries after, their children will come to walk upon that mound. They will stand there, bare-soled in the warm green twilight, and they will feel the faint, rhythmic thrumming of their ancestor’s old gristle, still vibrating in the deep, wet mattress of the world.
