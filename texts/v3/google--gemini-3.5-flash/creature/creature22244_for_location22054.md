To live in Coris-under-the-Crawl is to be made of the same wet iron, purple brick, and slow time as the city itself. 

The people who tend this colossal engine—known simply as the Sillers—are not like the quick, dry folk of the lowlands. They are a heavy-boned, thick-skinned lineage, shaped by generations of constant humidity and the low-frequency thunder of the earth’s ribs. To survive the absolute, chest-crushing roar of the turbines, every Siller child is fitted early with plugs of sheep-tallow and beeswax, which are pressed deep into the ear canals until the skin grows thick and unresponsive over them. They do not hear the world; they feel it. 

To a Siller, sound is a tactile map. They read the health of the city through their feet and their teeth. An engineer will stand for minutes at a time on the wet sandstone dome, knees slightly bent, his boots of thick, grease-cured ox-hide transmitting the rhythm of the three-hundred-foot turbines directly into his shins. If a bearing is worn or an impeller is choked with river-silt, he feels the hitch in his collarbones. When they must perform "fine-listening" on a high-pressure valve, they carry a "hearing-iron"—a small rod of solid bronze. They press one end against the iron valve housing and bite down on the other; the vibration travels through their teeth and skull, telling them if the steam inside is dry or wet, steady or turbulent.

Because of this constant, bone-level vibration, the Sillers walk with what is known as the "Sills-slide." It is a wide-legged, low-slung gait, their feet never lifting more than an inch from the slick sandstone. To lift a heel too high is to lose one’s anchor to the rock and risk being thrown off balance by the rhythmic shuddering of the domes.

Their skin has the pale, translucent quality of lard, perpetually slicked with a fine coating of oil-mist and sulfur-water. Around their eyes, where the heavy brass goggles press against their temples, they carry permanent, dark-red rings—the "goggle-girdles"—which are regarded among them as a mark of adulthood and diligence. They do not sweat as lowland humans do; their sweat glands have become sluggish, their bodies instead relying on the external, ninety-degree mist to keep them cool.

In the dry, dark interiors of the purple-tile citadels, life is spent in an orderly, almost monastic stillness. Because they cannot speak, and because gesturing in the dark is useless, the Sillers have developed a silent touch-language called *The Seam*. In the communal dining halls, where they eat a heavy, unsalted paste made from the white sulfur-ferns and dried snail-foot, a husband and wife will sit with their wrists touching across the table, their fingers tapping out long, quiet histories of their day against each other’s pulse-points.

They do not sleep on beds. If one were to lie on a frame resting on the stone floor, the vibration would rattle the kidneys and loosen the teeth from the gums within a few hours. Instead, they sleep in "cradles"—wide hammocks made of thick, braided felt-rope, suspended from the rafters by massive, oil-tempered steel springs that damp the mountain's pulse. Here, suspended in the warm, dry air of the upper brick tiers, they drift in a state of weightless silence, swaying slightly whenever a five-ton drawbeam clicks three hundred yards away.

***

In the margins of this human machine lives the true gentry of the wet stone: the *Ferracochlea*, or iron-back snails. 

These are not the soft garden pests of the valleys. A mature iron-back is the size of an anvil and can weigh upward of two hundred pounds. Their shells are not made of simple calcium, but of thick, overlapping plates of dark brown limonite, which they precipitate from the mineral-heavy water sliding across the sandstone domes. Under their shells, their bodies are muscular, muscular-gray, and smelling strongly of iron filings.

They are the city’s natural maintenance crew. The iron-backs graze on the black, vinegar-scented lichen that grows on the brick flanks of the pump-houses, their tongue-ribbons (or *radulae*) so hard that their feeding makes a steady, rasping *scritch-scritch* that can be felt through the tiles. This grazing is vital; if left unchecked, the lichen would rot the bitumen seams between the tiles in a matter of decades. In return, the Sillers treat the snails with a quiet, superstitious reverence. They do not eat them—the meat is incredibly tough, stringy, and tastes of wet coal-ash—but they "milk" them. By gently tickling the mantle of a resting snail with a seasoned wooden peg, an engineer can induce it to secrete a thick, yellowish slime. This slime does not boil or break down even under the highest steam pressures; the Sillers collect it in copper pots to grease the pack-glands of the great aqueduct valves.

Beneath the snails, scurrying through the shallow, lime-crusted gutters where the hot outfalls weep from the tiles, are the *white-lice*. These are blind isopods, six inches long, with flat, segmented bodies as white and hard as porcelain. They are the scavengers of the sills, moving in small, clattering packs to clean the stone of dead lichen, shed snail-skin, and the organic waste of the brick citadels. They are completely deaf, their long, whip-like antennae tuned instead to the high-frequency hiss of steam leaks, which they follow like lighthouses to find the warmest, wettest crannies.

***

High above the wet domes, where the great, felt-wrapped pipelines span the air like the petrified intestines of some iron behemoth, there is another lifeworld entirely. 

The sodden wool-felt that insulates the hot steam lines is a rich, artificial peat bog. It never dries; it is constantly soaked by the sulfur-mist and heated from within by steam that exceeds three hundred degrees. Here lives the *felt-moth*, a wingless, greasy insect the size of a thumb. The felt-moth has no need for flight in this windless, heavy air; instead, it has long, hook-tipped legs that allow it to cling to the underside of the flumes even during the violent shuddering of a pressure-blowback. Its larvae—fat, orange grubs—eat the decaying wool fibers, turning them into a dense, waxen silk that they spin into tunnel-networks throughout the pipe wrapping. 

The Sillers do not destroy the felt-moths. The silk tunnels spun by the grubs actually help bind the rotting felt together, extending the life of the pipe-insulation by decades. During the winter months of the lowlands—which are marked here only by a slight yellowing of the golden vapor—the Siller children climb the granite trestles with small iron hooks, delicately scraping the orange grubs from the seams. They squeeze the grubs into copper kettles to render down their fat, which produces a clean, odorless oil that burns with a brilliant white flame in the signalling lanterns.

***

Deepest of all, down in the black, wet bellies of the sandstone domes where the turbines turn, live the *Low-Keepers*. 

If the Sillers of the gantries are stoic and slow, the Low-Keepers are practically limestone themselves. They are a smaller, broader caste who rarely climb to the sunlit, golden mist of the surface. They live in the damp, dripping galleries carved directly into the heart of the sandstone, where the water from the mountain's internal springs is forced through the bronze impellers.

The air down here is not golden; it is pitch-black, screaming with water-spray, and cold—for the water has not yet been heated by the deep-earth vents. The Low-Keepers work in the dark, naked but for aprons of oil-soaked leather, their skin stained a permanent, dark iron-rust orange. Their fingers are short, thick, and tipped with nails that have grown as hard and spade-like as iron trowels from years of clearing gravel and scale from the turbine grates.

They are the ones who listen to the deep earth. They do not use flags or lanterns; they communicate across the roaring turbine halls by striking the iron conduits with heavy brass sledges, sending patterns of sharp, metallic waves through the metal that can be felt miles away. They are the true heartbeat of Coris. They know when the mountain is gathers its waters, when a silt-bed has shifted three miles to the north, and when the deep, volcanic fires have breathed a little harder against the roots of the world.

When a Low-Keeper dies, he is not buried in the stone, for the sandstone is sacred and must not be fouled. Instead, his body is wrapped in the green-copper bands of a retired steam pipe and lowered down into the main turbine intake. Within seconds, the bronze impellers grind him into the white, mineral-rich froth that feeds the great outfalls, returning his bones to the thin, weeping glaze that coats the silent, golden domes of the surface.
