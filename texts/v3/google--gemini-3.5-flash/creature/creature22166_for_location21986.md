To see them for the first time is to mistake them for the architecture itself, and this is not a mistake they would bother to correct, for they do not distinguish between the city’s bones and their own.

They are the ***Lignum-Kin***—though they have no name for themselves, having no need for a language of self-definition in a world where everything is one interconnected, damp machine. If they are fauna at all, it is only in the most technical, sluggish sense. They have no red blood; they possess no warm, frantic hearts that beat in the dark. Instead, they are run on the same hydrostatic pressure that feeds the ironwoods and drives the copper pistons.

### The Shell and the Sap

A Citizen of the Sump stands tall, usually seven or eight feet, but their height is deceptive because they rarely stand straight. They bend with the curve of the ironwood trunks, their lean, jointless limbs mimicking the heavy hang of wet branches. 

Their skin is not skin, but a thick, fibrous rind—the color of river silt and oxidized copper, textured like wet cork. It is permanently slick, coated in a protective sheen of tallow and fern-oil that smells faintly of turpentine and old cellar floor. They do not have hair; instead, their heads are capped with dense, brain-like growths of the same waxy, bioluminescent shelf-fungus that illuminates the handrails of the public steps. This fungus glows with a faint, cold greenish-blue aura, casting long, sliding shadows across their features as they move.

They have no eyes in the traditional sense. In this soup of steam and shadow, eyes are useless, delicate things that would only rot. Instead, they possess three or four horizontal, slit-like pits across their brows—narrow vents lined with highly sensitive, heat-receptive membranes. They "see" the world as a shifting map of thermal draft: the hot, screaming white of a steam pipe; the deep, cold black of a basalt wall; the dull, warm orange of another citizen passing through a condensation cloud.

### The Sleep of the Grafted

Their life is a slow pendulum between movement and mineral. 

They do not sleep in beds. Each resident has a specific niche, a "nook-graft," inside the hollowed-out ironwood pillars. When a citizen is weary—which happens only once every few months—they retreat to their niche and back themselves into the live, wet heartwood of the tree. 

Over the course of several hours, their fibrous heels and calves begin to weep a sticky, amber-colored resin. This resin cures instantly in the damp air, gluing them to the trunk. The fine, hair-like capillaries on their backs slide into the natural pores of the mineralized wood, linking their vascular systems directly to the tree’s slow, iron-rich sap-flow. 

During these long rests, which can last for weeks, the citizen is indistinguishable from a decorative wooden column or a massive, gnarled root-knot. They feel the vibrations of the entire sinkhole through the wood—every stroke of the steam-pumps sixty feet below, every drop of the ceiling-rain, every tremor of the deep bedrock. They sleep not in dreams, but in a vast, low-frequency hum.

### The Song of the Rattle-Plate

They are mute, lacking vocal cords, lungs, or tongues. Yet, they are not quiet. 

They communicate through a language of mechanical vibration, using a specialized organ located on their sternums—the *Rattle-Plate*. This is a hard, ribbed shield of petrified wood and copper-infused bone. By rubbing their thick, three-jointed fingers (which are tipped with broad, horn-like spades that fit perfectly into the bolts of the steam-valves) across this plate, they produce a variety of clicking, rasping, and purring sounds. 

In the ironwood streets, a conversation sounds like two dry cicadas nesting inside a grand piano. High-status news is told in sharp, rhythmic *taps* that travel miles through the copper conduits; intimate whispers are expressed as low, chest-vibrating rumbles that can only be felt by touching your forehead to another’s bark-like shoulder.

### The Sump-Born

They do not mate, nor do they divide. They are budded from the city's waste.

Deep in the sumps, below the lowest level of catwalks, the black, grease-choked water pools in great, stagnant iron cisterns. Here, the run-off of the oil-ferns, the lime-scale of the boilers, and the organic dust of the ironwood bark collect into a thick, nutrient-dense peat. 

Every few decades, when the geothermal heat beneath the sumps reaches a particular, feverish peak, the peat begins to dome. Great, bulbous casks—like copper-plated acorns the size of boilers—rise from the muck. They are fed by the hot, mineral rain, absorbing the iron and copper ions from the water. 

Within these casks, the new citizens are gestated in a womb of hot oil and silt. When the internal steam pressure of the cask reaches its limit, it splits with a loud, wet *crack* that echoes through the district like a pistol shot. The emerging youth is fully formed but soft, its skin a pale, milky green. It is immediately taken by the elders, who rub it down with boiler-grease to cure its rind and guide its splayed feet to the wet iron grates.

### The Long Petrification

There is no death here in the animal sense—no rotting corpses, no sickness, no sudden collapses. Instead, there is only the *Stiffening*.

As a citizen ages—usually after four or five hundred years of maintaining the pumps—their circulation slows. The amber-oil in their veins grows thicker, more laden with the copper and iron they absorb from the water they drink. Their movements, already deliberate, become geologic. A step that once took a second takes a minute; a turn of a steam-valve takes an hour.

Eventually, they find a place where the architecture needs reinforcement. They stand against a crumbling gallery wall, or they wedge themselves beneath a buckling copper aqueduct. They reach out their long, branch-like arms, pressing their splayed fingers deep into the surrounding wood and ironwork.

And there they remain. 

Over the course of a decade, the green-grey rind of their skin turns completely to stone and ironwood. Their internal cisterns dry up, replaced by mineral deposits. They become the brackets, the columns, the lintels, and the buttresses that hold the settlement together. 

The young citizens passing by do not mourn them. Instead, they rub their fingers along the petrified hands of their ancestors to check for lime-scale, and they grease the joints of the dead so that the weight of the city does not crack them.
