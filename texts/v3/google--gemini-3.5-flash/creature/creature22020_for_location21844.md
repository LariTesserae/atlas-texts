In the Casing-Well, the line between the machine and the organism has grown indistinct. What lives here does not challenge the metal; it has surrendered to it, adopting its stillness, its weight, and its chemical composition. 

At the base of this food chain is the yellow, hair-like mold—the **Grease-Felt**. It is not a single organism but a collective tapestry of fungal hyphae that has spent centuries digesting the tallow and flax packed into the joints of the old valves. The Felt does not grow outward into the open air; it hugs the undersides of the pipe bundles, forming thick, yellow-white sleeves that smell faintly of rancid lard and cellar-dust. Because there is no wind to carry its spores, the Felt has adapted to use the room’s only movement: gravity and vibration. When the deep hydraulic groan (*hhhhhh-ung*) shudders through the iron floorplates, the Felt releases a dry, invisible puff of microscopic spores. They do not float so much as slide down the warm, rising air currents, settling on the wet plates below like a dusting of sulfur.

Feeding on this yellow plush are the **Ferrules**—the blind, pale-bodied isopods. To call them insects or crustaceans feels too soft. Their shells are not made of chitin but of a sintered, mineralized compound of calcium and iron-oxide, scraped from their diet of rust-stone. They are the color of old lead, their backs segmented into hard, interlocking plates that mimic the thread-patterns of the bolts they crawl upon. 

A Ferrule’s life is an exercise in geologic patience. It does not possess a heart; instead, its fluid is pushed through its body by the physical bending of its joints as it creeps. If it ceases to move, its circulation stops, and it enters a state of dry torpor that can last for years. When it does move, its six pairs of wire-like legs find footing in the microscopic pits of the corroding iron. It moves toward the scent of the Grease-Felt at a speed of perhaps three inches a day, its feathery antennae sweeping the pipe before it. 

When a Ferrule dies, it does not rot. The dry, greasy heat of the well mummifies its soft interior within hours, leaving the iron-heavy shell behind. Over decades, the dripping water from the cold lines deposits a crust of pale green copper carbonate or red rust-tuff over the body, fusing it forever to the pipe. The walls of the well are studded with thousands of these small, mineralized bumps—the graves of ten thousand generations of Ferrules, now serving as structural rivets for the next.

In the deep, black pools of oil-slicked water at the corners of the room, a different kind of life has settled. 

Here, where the water is stagnant and sealed beneath a skin of iridescent grease, lives the **Lard-Skin**. It is a colonial bacterial sheet, grey-white and thick as wet parchment, that floats just beneath the oil barrier. It has no mouth, no legs, and no senses. It survives by reducing the sulfates in the rust-stone and drinking the hydrocarbons that slowly leach from the ancient petroleum lubricants. 

The Lard-Skin is incredibly sensitive to the dripping water. When a drop falls thirty feet and strikes the surface with that soft *thupt*, the vibration ripples through the greasy skin. In response, the colony produces a slow, rhythmic contraction, pulling its edges inward toward the center of the pool like a dying lung, then slowly expanding back into the corners as the surface settles. This movement is so slow that to a human eye it would look like the natural settling of liquid, but it is an intentional, metabolic breath, pulling fresh iron-bearing water beneath its underbelly.

Hanging from the rusted iron grates above these pools are the **Condensate-Weepers**. They look like grease-drools—long, translucent-brown icicles of mucus that hang three or four inches down from the metal grids. They are living, colonial amoeboids that have organized into structural ropes. They do not hunt; they wait. Their surfaces are covered in a sticky, tallow-based slime that traps the sulfur-dust and the occasional drifting Ferrule spore. Once every few months, if a Ferrule itself crawls onto the grate and slips, it becomes mired in the Weeper’s glue. The Weeper does not digest the shell; it slowly dissolves the organic tissue inside over a period of weeks, eventually dropping the empty, iron-heavy husk into the black pool below.

Finally, there are the occupants of the center—those that live on the massive, double-walled steam register that glows with a faint, cherry-red heat. 

Here, where the temperature is high enough to scorch regular organic tissue, lives the **Soot-Lace**. It is a lithotrophic lichen, black and brittle as charred paper, that grows in long, vertical fronds directly on the hot iron. The Soot-Lace contains no water; its cellular structure is based on complexes of silica and lead, which it draws from the ancient solder joints of the register. 

The Lace does not look alive. It looks like the soot-streamers that accumulate in the flue of a coal stove. But in the presence of the shimmering heat, the fronds do not hang limp. They curve gently outward, away from the dry metal, to catch the cooler, greasy humidity that drifts off the sweating water-lines. When the air shimmers, the Soot-Lace moves with it, a slow, hypnotic swaying that is the only continuous motion in the entire fifty-foot height of the chamber.

Near the base of these hot, black fronds, in the deepest crevices of the glowing register, live the **Mite-Sleeves**. They are microscopic, tick-like entities that exist in such dense concentrations they cover the hot iron like a layer of grey velvet. They have no eyes and no legs; they are small, hexagonal scales of silver-grey mineral that reproduce by splitting. They do not move from their spots on the hot register. They simply sit, absorbing the geothermal draft, their crystalline bodies vibrating in sympathy with the deep, low groans of the floorplates below.

If you were to sit in the Casing-Well for a century, this is what you would see: No growth that can be measured by the day. No sound but the *thupt* of the water and the *hhhhhh-ung* of the valve. But if you looked closely at the iron-tuff—at those cold, hard mineral deposits that have sealed the machinery—you would see that the rust is slowly, grain by grain, being shaped. The Ferrules are scraping it; the Grease-Felt is softening it; the Soot-Lace is binding it with silica. 

The inhabitants of the well are not merely surviving in the ruins of the Second Auxiliary. They are slowly, with the infinite patience of the deep earth, turning the steel plates back into ore, and the ore into a body.
