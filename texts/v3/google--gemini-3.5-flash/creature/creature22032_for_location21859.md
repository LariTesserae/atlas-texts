To understand what lives in this drowned highland, one must first discard the concept of the beast. There is no blood here, no bone, no shell that was grown to house a heart. There are no eyes to pierce the green-and-violet gloom, nor any mouths to scream. 

Yet the world is occupied. The vacuum left by the absent animal kingdom has not resulted in a static garden, but in a slow, heavy, and wet kingdom of vascular ambition. Here, the boundary between plant and stone, between growth and construction, has weathered away.

The primary masters of the basalt ridges are the **Corded**.

They are the ones who built the dry-stone circles and wrapped them in crude, hammered copper. To a human observer, they would not initially look like living things; they resemble massive, upright pillars of braided, dark-green rope, standing between eight and twelve feet high. They are made of the same fibrous, charcoal-green pulp as the house-sized Siphon-Mosses, but their outer skins have been cured by the extreme mineral content of the water into something resembling wet, corrugated leather.

The Corded have no front or back. They are radial, composed of dozens of thick, twisted vascular strands that spiral upward from a flared, root-like base. They do not possess a central brain; instead, their intelligence is distributed through a network of copper-rich sap that flows through their trunks under immense hydraulic pressure. They think in surges of turgor pressure—a slow, chemical calculus of salt, temperature, and weight.

To survive in a world where the water is viscous and freezing, the Corded have become masters of tension. They have no skeleton, so they use the stones. They gather on the high saddles of the mountains where the basalt rises in hexagonal pillars. By creeping their root-tendrils into the hair-cracks of the basalt, they can pry loose blocks of stone over the course of decades.

They do not carry these stones; they use the natural buoyancy of their water-filled tissues and the slow-rolling thermal currents to slide them into place. 

The "marks of stone mallets" on the basalt pillars and the "cold-hammered" copper bands are the result of the Corded’s primary physical agency: hydraulic snapping. A Corded possesses several specialized, branch-like appendages that are hollow and dry. By pumping water out of these limbs and then suddenly venting fifty atmospheres of pressure back into them, the Corded can snap these wooden nodules forward with the speed and force of a steel piston. 

*Thwack.* 

They use this hydraulic strike to drive basalt wedge-stones into place, clearing silt and fitting the dry-stone walls together without mortar. The copper bands are similarly worked. The Corded accumulate copper ions from the thermal geysers, extruding the pure metal through specialized pores in their bark as thick, soft ribbons. Once these ribbons are wrapped around the stone structures to stabilize them against the heavy currents, the Corded use their hydraulic hammers to beat the metal flat, securing the joints.

They build these circles and beehive chambers not to live in—for they require no shelter from the wet—but to hear. 

In the absolute darkness, the Corded are entirely blind, but they are exquisitely sensitive to vibration. The hollow beehive chambers act as acoustic lenses. When a Corded stands inside one of these dry-stone domes, the low-frequency *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the valley's Siphon-Mosses is amplified, and the high-pitched chiming of the Glass-Ferns is focused into a concentrated hum. To the Corded, these structures are not homes; they are places of deep, resonant contemplation, where they can "see" the entire shape of the valley through the lens of concentrated sound.

***

Far above the Corded, in the mid-water column where the pressure listhens slightly but the cold remains absolute, live the **Lace-Skins**.

These are the closest this world comes to a sky-dwelling species. They are colossal, translucent sheets of colonial algae, some measuring three miles across but only a fraction of an inch thick. They float horizontally in the heavy, viscous currents like soft, emerald-green clouds. 

The Lace-Skins have no thoughts, only a collective tropism. They spend their vast lifespans chasing the faint, chemical violet light emitted by the Glass-Fern groves below. They drift from ridge to ridge, descending lower when the thermal geysers hiss warmly, and rising toward the frozen ceiling when the currents shift.

As they drift, their gossamer surfaces collect the falling spores of the valley floor and the fine, dark silt raised by the breathing of the Siphon-Mosses. The Lace-Skins digest these minerals directly through their semi-permeable membranes. 

When two Lace-Skins meet in the black water, they do not fight or merge. They slowly drape over one another, sharing nutrients through their porous skins in a silent, weeks-long exchange of chemical memories, before the slow-rolling currents pull them apart once more. 

***

Within the crystal groves of the fifty-foot-high Glass-Ferns, a different sort of life has found its niche. These are the **Needle-Grafts**.

The Glass-Ferns are made of biological silica, stiff and brittle. When the currents sweep through them, they do not bend; they vibrate. This vibration is not merely acoustic; it is electrical. The friction of the water against the silica fronds generates a constant, weak piezoelectric charge.

The Needle-Grafts are small, parasitic lichen-like organisms that look like clumps of violet tinsel. They do not grow in the silt; they graft themselves directly to the crystalline fronds of the Glass-Ferns. They do not feed on organic matter, for there is none. Instead, they feed directly on the electrical hum of the ferns.

They are energy-parasites, converting the physical vibration of the glass into chemical sugars. Where the Needle-Grafts are thickest, the Glass-Ferns glow with a deep, pulsing violet light that flares whenever the thermal currents run strong. 

There is a strange symbiosis here: the Needle-Grafts, in absorbing the electrical charges, prevent the Glass-Ferns from becoming overcharged and shattering under their own bio-electric tension. In return, the ferns give them a height from which to catch the mineral-rich waters of the ridges.

***

And then, there are the times of the **Ice-Fall**.

When the freezing, super-saline brine cascades down from the frozen ceiling of the world, the life of the highland undergoes a sudden, dramatic shift. 

The Lace-Skins feel the drop in salinity first. They fold themselves like giant, green napkins, reducing their surface area to avoid being torn by the falling ice-shards, and sink rapidly into the deepest, mud-filled trenches where the thermal geysers keep the water thick and warm.

The Siphon-Mosses withdraw their outer membranes, their copper-colored valves clacking shut with a sound that echoes across the basins like muffled cannon fire. They go silent, their rhythmic *thrum-thrum* dying away.

The Corded do not flee. They cannot move fast enough. A Corded moves at the speed of growth—perhaps a yard in a season. When the freezing brine plume hits their ridge, they retreat into their stone circles. They wrap their fibrous limbs tightly around their central trunks, squeeze the water out of their outer layers to prevent their cells from bursting, and freeze.

For weeks, while the brine waterfall roars and the Glass-Ferns shed their crystal needles in the sub-zero chill, the Corded stand as solid, ice-rimed statues within their basalt sanctuaries. They do not breathe; their copper sap ceases to flow. They become indistinguishable from the pillars they built.

Only when the brine plume dissipates and the warm, mineral currents from the geysers creep back up the mountain slopes do they begin to thaw. The copper-rich sap begins to flow again. The turgor pressure returns, limb by limb, with a series of wet, creaking groans. 

The Siphon-Mosses open their valves with a heavy *clack*, and their deep *thrum-thrum* begins anew. The Needle-Grafts pulse with violet light. And the Corded, recovering their slow, hydraulic senses, reach out with their root-ends to check if the stones of their silent temples have shifted in the dark.
