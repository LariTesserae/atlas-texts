To watch the people of the Ochre Spindle move is to watch an community of divers who have forgotten the surface exists. 

They do not walk; they *thread*. Because there are no flat streets—only the wet, vertical puzzle of ladders, iron struts, and suspended platforms—their bodies have developed a peculiar, three-dimensional geometry. They are short-stature people, broad in the shoulder but remarkably thin from breastbone to spine, allowing them to slip sideways through the narrow slots between the copper boiler-sleeves and the raw basalt. 

Their skin has the grey-white quality of salt pork, but it is rarely clean enough to see. A permanent lacquer of tallow, coal-soot, and the heavy violet oil of the waxy ferns coats their limbs. This is not dirt to them; it is a necessary skin—a barrier against the "rot-itch" that comes from living in air that is ninety-nine percent steam. The few patches of bare flesh they expose, usually around the throat and temples, are slick and water-repellent, beaded with tiny droplets of condensation that never seem to soak in.

They have developed "grease-palms." The skin of their hands is thick, yellow, and deeply creased, resembling old saddle leather that has bunked in a swamp. These hands are impervious to the heat of the lower steam lines; a Spindle-worker can grab a copper pipe carrying two atmospheres of pressure and turn the valve without a rag, their calluses whistling slightly as the dampness on their skin turns instantly to vapor.

Speech among them is a vestigial thing, reserved for the interior of the copper-shielded cabins where three or four people might huddle together, their foreheads nearly touching. Out on the gangways, the moss and the ferns eat words before they can cross a six-foot chasm. In its place, they have perfected the *Trench-Clink*. 

They carry small, drop-forged iron wrenches or heavy brass rings on their knuckles. By striking these against the copper steam lines and the iron structural stays, they send vibrations traveling through the entire metal skeleton of the Sinking. Every boiler-man knows the difference between the sharp, double-ring of a lease-holder calling for more pressure and the heavy, sliding rattle of a drift-worker warning of a rotten timber below. A child can stand on a wet grate eighty yards above the sump, close their eyes, and feel through the soles of their bare feet exactly which boiler is being stoked and which loom has thrown its drive-belt.

These looms are the heart of their cramped domestic life. In tiny, floorless cabins that hang like copper wasps' nests from the basalt buttresses, the weavers work. They do not use wool or flax; there is none. Instead, they harvest the bone-white creepers that strangle the hot steam pipes. They boil the vines in alkaline grey lye until the fibers split, then spin them into a heavy, stiff cordage they call *creeper-cloth*. It is grease-colored and smells faintly of boiled cabbage and iron filings, but it does not rot in the wet. Everyone wears it. The trousers are stiff enough to stand on their own in the corner of a cabin; the coats are long, sleeveless cowls that shed the hot grease-drips like shingles.

Their eyes are their most striking feature. Because the amber burners and the green patches of fungus provide only a simmer of light, their pupils are permanently dilated, almost swallowing the iris. They have lost the habit of looking at faces. In the Sinking, to look someone in the eye is considered an aggressive, top-world stupidity; instead, they look at hands, at the placement of feet on the rungs, or at the steam-plumes behind a person’s head to judge their distance. They see best in the muddy orange end of the spectrum. To them, the cold white light of a match is a blinding, painful insult that causes their eyes to water and run with yellow grease.

The children—the "Spindle-whelps"—are born into the high, narrow nests. They learn to climb before they can stand. A Spindle-baby does not crawl on its knees; it clings to the iron wire-mesh of its parents’ cabin floor like a young bat, looking down through the gaps into the forty-yard drop with the blank, untroubled gaze of an eagle. They are small, silent, and prehensile-toed. By the age of six, they are draft-creepers, climbing into the blind-ends of the steam tunnels where the adults cannot fit, carrying grease-tallow jars on their heads to lubricate the buried bearings of the great fans.

There is no priesthood here, but there are the *Boiler-Tenders*. They are the elders of the rift, men and women whose joints have grown thick and stiff from forty years of steam-kneading. They live closest to the heat, in high-pressure niches right beside the main basalt-vents. They are nearly deaf from the close-up thrum of the expansion valves, but they "hear" with their bones. A Boiler-Tender can lie down on a wet iron plate, press their chin to the rivet, and diagnose a leak three hundred feet away by the texture of the shudder in their jaw. They are the ones who decide when a section of the Sinking must be starved of steam to prevent a boiler-burst, a task they perform with the cold, silent authority of captains cutting away a fouled sail.

They eat from the sump. Once every three shifts, the children crawl down the grease-slick ladders to the "well-eye"—the lowest point of the Sinking where the condensation pools into a black, oily soup before draining into the deeper faults. Here, they scrape the waxy, purple ferns for their bitter, starch-rich roots, which they mash with salt and the dried, ground shells of the blind white land crabs. The resulting gray paste is cooked on the hot tops of the steam chests until it forms a hard, oily biscuit. It tastes of river-bed and sulfur, but it keeps the rot from the gums.

Nothing is buried in the Ochre Spindle. When a worker dies—usually from a slipped foot on the grease-gangways or the quiet, wet lung-softening they call "the boil"—their clothes are stripped and returned to the weavers. The body is not burned; the peat-boilers are too precious, and the soot would choke the narrow vents. Instead, they wrap the dead in raw, unboiled creeper-vines and lower them into the "Sinks"—the narrow, boiling-hot rifts where the deep water comes up. Within three days, the bone-white creepers, hunting the heat of the decaying flesh, will have completely woven the body into the living lattice of the cliff-face. 

In this way, the builders do not leave the Sinking. They simply become part of the dark, warm scaffolding that the next generation climbs upon to reach the valves.
