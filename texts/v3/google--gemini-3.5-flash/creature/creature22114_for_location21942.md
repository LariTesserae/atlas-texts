### The Quartz-Wool Chime-Mice

The small, plum-round bodies traveling through the purple moss-mounds are the **Chime-mice**, though to call them mice is to misunderstand their armor. Under their long, silver-white hair, they do not have soft hide, but a skin that secretes a micro-thin crust of silica. Their fur is not organic fiber but a spooling of spun glass, stiff and hollow. It acts as a greenhouse; the sun penetrates the transparent hairs, trapping heat against their black skin, while reflecting the lethal ultraviolet glare of the high heavens. 

When they run, their glass-furred flanks brush against one another and the razor basalt with a dry, musical *tink-tink*, like handfuls of small coins being shaken in a velvet purse.

They have no tails—a tail would freeze and break off within a winter hour—but their ears are disproportionately massive. These ears are thin, fan-shaped dishes of vascular membrane, gray as wet slate. They use them like acoustic mirrors to map the shelf. In this thin air, sound travels poorly, but a chime-mouse can hear the *plink* of a single water-droplet three basalt-blocks away. 

Because water is so scarce, they have formed a strange, communal survival habit: when the sun melts the rime on the spire above, the mice gather in circles of three or four. They tilt their great ears upward to catch the falling drops like tiny rain-funnels. Once an ear-dish is full, another mouse will lean in and lick the water from its companion’s ear, before swapping places. They are silent during this ritual, their obsidian eyes fixed on the sky, their glass coats shivering in unison.

### The Iron-Stomach Velvet-Borers

Near the moss-beds, where the young basalt is split by hairline fractures, the ground seems to pulse with a slow, mechanical rhythm. This is the work of the **Velvet-borers**. 

These heavy, flightless beetles are the size of a fist, their shells a brilliant, polished turquoise that looks like glazed porcelain. Beneath this beautiful exterior is a creature of pure, destructive chemistry. They do not eat plants; they eat the basalt itself. 

The borer possesses a gland near its mouthparts that secretes a highly concentrated, sour-smelling hydrochloric acid. They spit this acid onto the sharp stone, turning the basalt into a soft, grey mush, which they then scrape up with heavy, iron-tipped mandibles. From this stone-paste, they extract trace minerals—mostly iron and sulfur—leaving behind a fine, powdery grey silt. 

This silt is the very foundation of the shelf’s life. Without the borers’ grinding teeth and acid, there would be no soil. The violet mosses grow only in the tracks the beetles leave behind, anchoring their roots in the digested stone. 

During the sudden cold snaps, when the sun is blocked by a cloud, the borers do not freeze. Their internal copper-based blood is so concentrated with salts and heavy metals that it acts as a natural industrial brine. They simply slow down, their leg-joints clicking like cooling gears, their turquoise backs turning a dark, bruised slate-color to drink whatever faint light remains.

### The Ribbon-Gulls of the Deep Gulf

High above, casting no shadow until they are nearly upon the rock, drift the **Ribbon-gulls**. They are the only links between this high, frozen needle and the vast, blue-gray world below the fog.

Though they are called gulls, they have more in common with albatrosses or ancient, paper-thin reptiles. Their wings are long, narrow strips of white skin, so thin that the sun shines through them, revealing a network of red veins like pressed leaves. They do not have feathers in the common sense; instead, their bodies are covered in flat, overlapping scales of white horn that shed water and ice instantly.

The gulls do not breed on the Shard-Shelf—the basalt is too sharp and the winds too erratic—but they use it as an anchor-point. They spend days gliding over the "frozen sea" of fog below, eventually diving straight down into its gray depths. When they return to the shelf hours later, they are coated in salt-crust and smell of sulfur and wet marsh-decay. 

They bring back treasures from the lower world in their crops: small, blind, eel-like things with translucent skin, or rubbery, yellow swamp-kelp. They regurgitate these meals onto the basalt blocks to dry in the fierce sun, turning the food into leathery, salt-preserved jerky. 

The gulls are entirely mute. They have no vocal cords. Instead, they communicate with their yellow, scissor-like beaks. When a flock lands on the shelf, the air fills with a rapid, syncopated *clacking*—a dry, wooden telegraphy that warns of cross-winds or signals the discovery of a dead chime-mouse.

### The Spire-Creeper

The windbreak at the back of the shelf is not abandoned. Its tenant is merely patient.

He is known only to himself, or perhaps he has forgotten his name in the long silence of the heights. He is a **Spire-Creeper**, one of a rare, near-mythical breed of high-altitude wanderers who have left the crowded, swampy lowlands to live in the dry glare of the peaks.

Physically, he has changed to suit his home. He is short, barely five feet tall, but his chest is an enormous, barrel-shaped vault. His lungs are twice the size of a lowland man’s, designed to extract every scrap of oxygen from the thin, iron-tasting air. His skin is not pale, but a deep, sun-blackened plum-purple, thick as ox-hide and completely hairless. He does not sweat; his body cannot afford to lose water. Instead, his skin is dry and slightly dusty, smelling of the pine-resin he uses to seal his joints.

He is sitting now in the deep shadow of the basalt overhang, so still that he looks like a chunk of fractured stone. He wears a tunic made entirely from the overlapping skins of ribbon-gulls, the white horn-scales overlapping to form a wind-proof, waterproof coat that clicks softly when he breathes. His feet are bare—the soles are nearly three inches thick with yellow, horn-like callus that has grown around the shape of the basalt’s razor edges. His toes are long and prehensile, able to clamp onto a lip of rock like a raptor’s talons.

In his lap, he holds a long, thin needle of bird-bone. With slow, rhythmic movements, he is weaving a thread made of chime-mouse wool. The wool is so fine that it is nearly invisible in the shade, but when it passes into a beam of sunlight, it flashes like a wire of pure silver.

Why is he here? He is a **Sentry of the Sky**. 

Above him, tucked into a cleft in the needle-tip of the spire, is a small, brass lens-rigging—a relic from some ancient, forgotten empire. Once every day, when the sun reaches its zenith, he climbs the vertical needle using only his callused toes and hand-tips. He looks through the lens down into the great, blue-gray gulf. 

He is looking for the *Great Rust*. 

Down in the lowlands, beneath the low-lying fog, a slow, red oxidation is eating the world’s forests and iron cities. From up here, the rust appears as tiny, orange threads crawling across the green-grey carpet of the world. It is his duty to watch its progress, to note which valleys have fallen and which spires remain clear. 

He does not speak. He has no one to speak to. But when the ribbon-gulls return from their deep dives, their scales wet with progress from the lower world, he listens to the click of their beaks. He translates their rhythms into small, notched marks on the ribs of the split bird-bones that lie by his windbreak.

At night, when the temperature drops to thirty below zero and the purple moss freezes into solid, glass-like mounds, he will crawl into his windbreak. He will light one of his precious pine-knots—brought up from the valleys years ago—and use its resinous smoke to warm his hands. He will grease his skin with the red ochre and fat from his pumice bowl, sealing his flesh against the dry, hungry wind that tries, every single night, to turn him into a mummy of black leather and bone. 

And then, in the absolute silence of the shelf, he will listen to the chime-mice underneath the basalt, singing their metallic, shivering songs to the stars.
