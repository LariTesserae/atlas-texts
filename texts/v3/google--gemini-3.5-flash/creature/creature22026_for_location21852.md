Beneath the three-foot carpet of star-moss, where the basalt terraces yield to the ancient, waterlogged peat, lives the silent architecture of this world's circulation. 

Here, in the cold, root-choked dark, dwell the **Siphon-worms**. They are not worms in the common sense, but long, segmented tubes of pale, cartilage-like fiber, some the thickness of a ship’s mast and stretching for dozens of yards through the peat. They are blind, lungless, and nearly static. Their entire existence is hydraulic. By contracting their ringed segments in slow, minutes-long waves, they draw the iron-rich bottom-waters up from the deep basalt fissures, filtering the liquid through their porous skin to feed the mosses above. To walk across the plateau is to tread upon thousands of these living pumps; if you press your ear to the wet star-moss, you can hear them—not as a heartbeat, but as a low, wet *shuck-shuck* of shifting fluid, miles of natural valves working in the dark.

Where the basalt flumes cut their straight grids through the moors, the **Solder-backs** rule the currents. These are the flat-bodied, eyeless grazers of the iron-crusts. They are shaped like massive, segmented shields, three feet wide and safe from the cold in hides that feel like vulcanized rubber. Their undersides are a complex machinery of scraping plates made of a hard, bio-mineralized iron compound. As they glide along the basalt channels, they do not swim; they adhere to the slick stone by suction, dragging themselves forward against the viscous paraffin flow. 

The *tick-tick-tick* of their feeding is the most common sound in the waterways. They graze on the dark manganese and iron ores that precipitate out of the water, refining the stone flumes as they go. Without the Solder-backs, the channels would soon choke on their own mineral crusts and overflow, turning the plateau into a shallow, stagnant lake. In return, the Solder-backs receive sustenance and lubrication; their shells are continuously coated in the natural paraffin of the water, which keeps them from freezing during the long, silver nights when the temperature drops to the brink of ice.

The life cycle of the Solder-back is tied to the gargantuan settling vats. Once every decade, as the heavy oils rise and form their brilliant, metallic swirls of copper and zinc-blue, the Solder-backs gather in the deep circular basins. They release their eggs—heavy, iron-hulled spheres about the size of walnuts—into the floating oil slicks. The eggs float in the warm, insulating lipids, protected from the biting cold of the water below, until they hatch into tiny, clear-shelled larvae that sink to the bottom to begin their long, scraping work.

Higher up, where the moss-mattress meets the open mist, move the **Slate-giants**. 

These herds of massive, long-necked herbivores are the curators of the peat-moors. Their bodies are built like low-slung, multi-ton barrels, supported by four column-like legs that end in broad, spongy pads. These feet act exactly like snowshoes, spreading their immense weight so perfectly that they leave only shallow, temporary depressions in the matted liverworts. Their skin is covered in a dense, moss-like down of slate-gray fibers that never holds water; rain and mist bead instantly upon their backs and roll off in tiny, silver rivers, leaving them dry underneath.

The Slate-giants possess no teeth. Instead, their mouths are fitted with heavy, horizontal plates of horn that they use to shear off the tops of the rubbery, water-logged liverworts. Their digestion is a slow, cold-fermentation process. They have four stomachs, the first of which is a cavernous chamber filled with specialized anaerobic bacteria that can break down the tough waxes and paraffins of the plateau plants. 

The byproduct of this digestion is a thick, highly concentrated tallow which the giants secrete through specialized glands along their spines. This grease is their primary defense against the climate; it grooms their down, sealing them against the biting cold weather systems that sweep across the wide valleys. When the giants sleep—huddled together in great, breathing circles to conserve warmth—they look like humped mounds of basalt, their breath rising in thick, collective chimneys of steam that merge with the geothermal vents of the plateau.

Inside the calcified chimneys themselves, where the high-pressure geothermal steam vents with its mechanical *hiss… hiss…*, lives a highly specialized micro-fauna: the **Steam-vane Crabs**. 

These are tiny, porcelain-white crustaceans no larger than a coin. They live in the roaring gallery of the calcified algae tubes, clinging to the interior walls where the steam is hottest. Their shells are rich in copper, allowing them to withstand temperatures that would cook any other creature on the plateau. They feed on the thick mats of chemosynthetic, sulfur-eating bacteria that line the vents. 

The crabs are the chimney-sweeps of the plateau. As they scuttle in and out of the black pipes, their flat, paddle-like legs scrape away the lime deposits that would otherwise clog the vents. If a chimney becomes blocked, the high-pressure steam eventually blows the tube apart in a destructive geyser, tearing the surrounding moss-carpets to shreds. The little crabs prevent this; they are the pressure-release valves of the living factory, maintaining the delicate balance between the planetary fires below and the cold sponge above.

High above the chimneys, darting through the perpetual silver-gray mist, are the **Slick-chits**. 

These are small, stubby-winged birds that look more like flying seals than feathered fowl. Their plumage is a marvel of adaptation: a dense, seamless coat of stiff, scale-like feathers that have been saturated with the paraffin oil from the settling vats. They do not fly so much as swim through the heavy, water-logged air, their wings moving in a short, rapid blur. 

The Slick-chits are the only creatures that bridge the gap between the sky and the deep water. They dive into the oily channels with spectacular, silent plunges, using their wedge-shaped heads to cut through the viscous lipids to hunt the small, translucent "grease-shrimp" that live in the mineral currents. 

Because the wet air deadens all vocal sound, the Slick-chits have abandoned song. Instead, they communicate through the metallic clicks of their hard, iron-tipped bills. A flock of Slick-chits descending upon a moss-pool sounds like a handful of iron nails dropped onto a cold anvil—a sharp, mechanical *clink-clink-shuff* that cuts through the muffled quiet of the plateau, signaling a patch of clear water or the approach of a storm.

And, of course, because there is life, there is also the quiet end of life. But here, there are no wolves, no swift-pounced cats. The only predator of the great wet roof is the **Maw-moss**.

It is indistinguishable from the ordinary star-moss that covers the basalt terraces, save for one detail: it does not cling to the stone. It floats. The Maw-moss is a predatory colonial bryophyte that forms rafts over the deep, tea-colored pools. When a young Slate-giant or an old, slow Solder-back accidentally steps onto its surface, the "moss" does not give way like a mattress; it parts like liquid. 

The target sinks into the cold, anaerobic water beneath, where the Maw-moss slowly surrounds it, sealing the surface above like a lid. There is no struggle; the cold water quickly numbs the victim, and the heavy lipids in the pool preserve the body as the moss slowly, over decades, absorbs its minerals. In this way, the Maw-moss acts as a natural carbon sink, recycling the nutrients of the plateau back into the basalt floor, keeping the great, cold engine running, forever clean, forever wet, and forever self-sustaining.
