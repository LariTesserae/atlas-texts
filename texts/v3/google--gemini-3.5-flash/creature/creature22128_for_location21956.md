The people who inhabit the floating white Hubs are as long-limbed, silent, and spare as the spires they build. 

They do not call themselves by a name that translates to "human," nor do they have a word for "conqueror" or "settler." In their quiet, sibilant tongue—which consists more of exhaled whistles and soft, dental clicks that mimic the dripping of water on stone—they are simply the *Those Who Listen*.

These beings stand nearly eight feet tall, though they weigh no more than a human child. Their skeletons are not made of heavy, calcium-rich bone, but of a dark, flexible cartilage that is both incredibly light and resilient to the extreme atmospheric pressure of the high plateau. Their skin is the color of wet slate or pale lavender, with a faint, pearlescent sheen that reflects the deep violet of the noon sky. Because the air is so thin and cold, they do not have nostrils or wide mouths; instead, they breathe through three pairs of elegant, vertical gill-slits situated along their long necks, which filter the damp, highly oxygenated mist directly into their oversized, sail-like lungs.

They have no hair. Their heads are smooth and elongated, tapering back to a delicate ridge. Their eyes are their most striking feature: massive, lidless pools of polished obsidian that occupy nearly half their faces. These eyes do not see the world in the narrow spectrum of warm light; they see in the far infrared and ultraviolet. To them, the cold plateau is not a grey-and-black wasteland, but a vibrating tapestry of thermal plumes. They can see the residual warmth left in a basalt tile where a six-legged grazer stood three hours ago; they can track the freezing currents of the wind as if they were bright, curling ribbons of turquoise gas.

Because they rarely touch the ground, their feet have evolved into delicate, three-toed clamps, while their hands feature four long, triple-jointed fingers ending in smooth, horn-like pads rather than nails. They move through the high, open galleries of the Hubs with a slow, drifting stride, their weight almost entirely supported by the faint, local gravity-wells that run through the white composite floors like sewers or water pipes.

Their garments are as quiet as their movements. They wear long, sleeveless shifts woven from two materials: the incredibly fine, oil-slick wool shed by the blue grazers, and thin, ductile threads of glass pulled from the obsidian deposits below. These clothes do not blow in the wind; they cling to their slender frames, shedding the constant condensation in tiny, perfect beads that roll off their hems like liquid silver.

***

In the domestic chambers of the Hubs, there is no furniture in the traditional sense. The inhabitants do not sleep horizontally—which they find physically distressing—but suspend themselves in vertical harnesses made of woven light-fields, drifting in a state of semi-conscious meditation while the cold mist circulates through their rooms. 

They do not eat solid food. The plateau lacks the soil to grow crops, and they have no desire to slaughter the great grazers. Instead, their sustenance is drawn from the water itself. On the lower, hanging platforms of the Hubs—where the structures hover just three meters above the shallow basins—they lower long, porous tendrils of white composite into the grey water. These tendrils act as massive, silent filters, drawing up the mineral-rich silt, the micro-algae, and the dissolved salts of the newly fractured basalt. This slurry is refined in the subterranean levels of the cities into a clear, thick, slightly sweet marrow that the inhabitants drink from slender glass vials.

Their society is not structured around laws, governments, or religions, but around *tuning*. 

Because they are so sensitive to vibration, they are deeply affected by the "planetary hum" of the plateau—the low, sub-audible groan of the Earth as the crust continues to lift and fracture. A community within a Hub is considered healthy when its members are "in register" with the local stone. If a spire begins to drift out of alignment, or if its residents become restless, they do not argue; they assemble in the central, hollow wells of their cities and sing. 

These songs are not melodies. They are long, sustained, low-frequency hums that match the resonant frequency of the basalt shelf below them. When thousands of them sing together, the air in the Hub grows so dense with vibration that the dripping water on the walls stops falling and hovers in mid-air, trembling in perfect, spherical coordinates.

***

Below the floating spires, the creatures of the wet stone live in a state of quiet, indifferent symmetry with the city-dwellers.

The great, six-legged *Kaelen*—the blue-wooled grazers—are the true custodians of the plateau’s surface. They are massive, slow-moving beasts, easily the size of houses, but their great weight is distributed across six splayed, spongy hooves that act like snowshoes on the slippery glass of the basalt floor. Their blue wool is so thick and dense that it is nearly solid, feeling more like cold, greasy felt than hair. This wool is saturated with a heavy, aromatic sebum that smells faintly of ozone and crushed mint. When the heavy mists roll over them, the water simply runs off their backs in sheets, never reaching their dark, leathery skin.

The *Kaelen* have no eyes. In the perpetual twilight of the mist, they navigate by "tasting" the wet stone with their hooves, sensing the faint electrical currents that run through the obsidian sheets. They feed on the circular rings of mint-green bioluminescent lichen, scraping it from the rock with broad, chisel-like tooth-plates made of dense, organic silica. They are so slow that a single herd may take three weeks to cross a five-mile patch of basin, leaving behind them a trail of clean, polished black stone, free of lichen and glistening like wet coal.

The relationship between the *Kaelen* and the residents of the Hubs is one of mutual non-interference, bordering on reverence. When the *Kaelen* shed their wool—which happens once every three plateau-cycles, when the cold winds from the rim blow particularly hard—they rub their massive bodies against the sharp, fractured edges of the basalt. They leave behind great, tangled drifts of blue fiber, snagged on the stone like low clouds. The inhabitants of the spires will descend in their silent, black needle-vessels to gather these drifts, using long, kinetic tongs to lift the wool without ever setting foot on the wet, razor-sharp glass.

High above both spires and grazers are the *Vela*—the sail-flyers. These creatures are entirely translucent, their bodies constructed of a glass-like cartilage that is almost completely invisible against the violet sky. Their wings, which can span eighty feet from tip to tip, are not feathers or skin, but rigid, ribbed sails of semi-flexible chitin. 

The *Vela* never land. They cannot. Their legs have degenerated into small, vestigial hooks used only to hang from the high spires of the Hubs during the rare, violent storms that sweep up from the lowlands. They live their entire lives on the thermals, drifting like paper ships. They do not hunt; they are atmospheric sift spiders, opening their cavernous, glassy mouths as they glide to catch the microscopic spores of the mint-green lichen and the tiny, frozen ice crystals that drift in the upper troposphere. When the sun hits them at just the right angle, the *Vela* light up like prisms, casting moving, rainbow-colored bars across the dark, wet basalt miles below.

***

In this world, the greatest mystery is what happens when something ends.

Because the air is sterile, freezing, and damp, there are no insects, no fungi, and no bacteria to facilitate decay. The plateau has "not yet learned how to rot." When a *Kaelen* dies, it does not decompose. It simply stops moving. Its great blue body remains on the wet stone, a silent monument. Over decades, the constant, mineral-rich weeping of the atmosphere deposits layer after layer of silica and iron-carbonate over the wool. The beast slowly hardens, its tissues turning to mineral, until it is nothing but a massive, blue-grey mound of chalcedony, a permanent feature of the geography.

The inhabitants of the Hubs treat their own death with the same quiet transition into stone.

When an inhabitant knows their "pulse" is beginning to fail—a process that takes place over several centuries as their cartilage slowly ossifies—they do not wait for weakness. They gather their few possessions (usually a single, highly tuned glass octave-flute or a vial of pure water from the highest clouds) and descend from the floating Hubs for the first and last time.

They step onto the wet, razor-sharp basalt floor. Their tough, splayed feet grip the glass easily. They walk away from the white spires, out into the vast, empty distance where the shallow water sheets reflect the deep violet sky. They find a place where the water is clear and the silence is absolute.

There, they sit. They draw their long knees up to their narrow chests, wrap their long, three-toed arms around themselves, and close their great obsidian eyes.

They do not freeze to death; they simply allow their internal temperature to drop until it matches the stone beneath them. As the centuries pass, the endless, rhythmic dripping of the wet air and the silent flow of the shallow water sheets do their work. The silica-laden runoff coats their slender limbs.

Forty miles from any Hub, if you look through the shifting, low-lying mist, you will see them: hundreds of pale, slender figures, standing or sitting in the shallow water. They are no longer lilac-colored or soft. They are statues of pure white agate and green-veined jasper, their long fingers fused to their knees, their lidless eyes turned up toward the violet sky, perfectly preserved, waiting for the young world to finish growing around them.
