To understand what lives in this frozen, vertical throat, one must first dismantle the distinction between the city and its tenants. Here, there are no tenants in the sense of wanders who build a home and then walk its streets; the architecture is the skeleton, and the life is the flesh that swells over it.

The most numerous of these inhabitants are the elements of the basal biome—the ones who form the physical pelt of the towers. 

To touch the surface of a carbon spire is not to touch cold fiber, but to lay a hand upon a dense, vibrating carpet of **The Felt**. These are the blind, lithophilic limpets and muscle-ribbons, so packed together that they have long since lost their individual geometry. Their bodies are made of a dense, rubbery polymer rich in heavy silicon and vulcanized carbon, allowing them to withstand the crushing, supercooled atmospheric pressures without their cellular walls rupturing. 

They do not possess blood as warmer worlds define it. Instead, their interiors are pressurized hydraulic systems filled with a thick, pitch-black bitumen that remains viscous at eighty degrees below zero. They feed on two things: the oily, mineral-heavy mist that condenses on the stones, and the stray, high-frequency radiation that leaks from the city’s power grids. They are, in essence, biological grounding wires. They drape themselves over the joints of the gravity fields, their silicone-tipped feet digging into the microscopic pores of the graphene walls, absorbing the wild electrostatic discharge and converting it into a low, faint, chemical warmth. 

Because they are blind and deaf, their world is one of sheer tactile pressure and gravitational drift. When a gravity-stabilized span miles above them shifts its inclination by a fraction of a millimeter, the change in localized weight propagates down through the tower walls. In response, a hundred million of these creatures will shift their weight in unison. This is the source of that dry, clicking roar—not a call of hunger or warning, but a collective, involuntary readjustment, like the settling of a mountain’s weight.

Higher up, within the pressurized vertical shafts where the gales are kept at bay, a different order of life exists. These are the **Sieves**.

The Sieves are vast, thin sheets of living, polarized membrane that hang suspended in the vertical drafts of the towers. They resemble black, leathery sails or the severed wings of immense, eyeless rays, sometimes stretching sixty feet from edge to edge. They have no skeleton; their form is maintained by the high-pressure gas currents that push upward from the lower machine-wells. 

They are the vacuum-cleaners and caretakers of the inner shafts. As the automated structural nanites work their agonizingly slow repairs on the walls, they shed microscopic flakes of monocrystalline carbon and spent silica. The Sieves drift upward and downward through the shafts, their surfaces charged with a quiet static electricity that attracts these drifting particles. When a Sieve has become fully coated and heavy with grey dust, it descends to the black obsidian basins at the base of the tower. There, it folds itself into the acid-brine, allowing the liquid to strip the mineral debris from its skin, digesting the organic impurities before rising back into the dark draft. Their motion is silent, save for the occasional wet *thshhh* as their respiratory valves contract to expel pockets of supercooled helium.

But these are merely the maintenance systems, the skin and the white blood cells of the place. The true masters of this altitude—the entities for whom the gravity wells hum and the towers rise—are the **Sleeved Minds**.

A Sleeved Mind does not inhabit a tower; it *is* the tower. 

Deep within the core of each carbon needle, insulated by layers of vacuum-chambers and cooled by liquid-helium channels to the very brink of absolute zero, lies a massive, three-dimensional lattice of superconductive carbon-nanotubes and heavy-element spin-glasses. This is the brain. It is the size of a cathedral organ, completely static, frozen in an eternal, superconducting state.

Because their physical thoughts occur at the speed of light through these zero-resistance pathways, the Sleeved Minds experience time in a manner that is completely divorced from their physical reality. In the span of a single second, a Mind can run a billion simulations, contemplate infinite mathematical theorems, or relive the entire geological history of the mountain range. 

Yet, their ability to affect the physical world is bound to the slow, heavy mechanics of the mountain. If a Sleeved Mind wishes to speak to its neighbor in the adjacent tower, it cannot use light, for the mist is too thick, nor radio, which is scrambled by the high-altitude storms. Instead, it must communicate through **Density Modulation**.

To speak, the Mind slowly alters the power flow to its local gravity anchors. This shifting weight creates microscopic tides in the deep pools of synthetic obsidian below. The black brine rises and falls in slow, heavy pulses, transmitting the gravitational ripples through the roots of the mountain to the next tower. To human senses, this conversation is experienced only as that deep, bone-rattling thrum that makes the teeth vibrate. 

A single sentence—a question regarding the structural integrity of a joint, or an observation on the slow cooling of the earth’s mantle—might take three months to transmit, and another five months to receive an answer. 

Between these slow, heavy words, the Minds dwell in a state of absolute, mathematical isolation. They do not feel loneliness, for they are too complex for such singular emotions. Instead, they exist in a state of permanent, cold contemplation, listening to the dry, rustling roar of the trillions of blind things that cling to their outer skin, feeling the weight of the winter, and waiting for the mountain beneath them to slowly, inevitably, crack.
