### The Great Rust-Lickers and the Micro-Fauna

In the microscopic and near-microscopic scale, the world is a crawling, chemical furnace. Where the plates meet and the water drips, the metal does not simply decay; it is consumed. 

The primary agents of this decay are the **Loom-Mites** and **Oxide-Ticks**. These are flat, shield-shaped arthopods, their carapaces the exact color of corroded iron—a dull, velvety orange-red. They do not have mouths in the traditional sense; instead, they exude a mild, sulfurous acid from glands beneath their heads, melting the protective patina of rust into a thin, metallic slurry which they then lap up through straw-like proboscises. They are so numerous that in the quiet of the shadow-stripes, if one presses an ear against a structural flange, one can hear a dry, endless fizzing—the sound of billions of tiny bellies digesting the world.

Larger than the mites are the **Tallow-Worms**. Transparent, three-foot-long annelids with pale white nervous systems visible through their damp skins, they live exclusively in the lubrication channels. They migrate along the grease-lines of the slow-turning gears, eating the thick, rancid animal fats and mineral cakes that squeeze out of the bronze bearings. They are harmless, slow-moving, and prized by larger creatures for their high fat content; they slide through the pipes like living plugs of lard.

### The Mid-Level Climbers: The Bronze-Backs and the Silt-Cats

The most common visible animal is the **Bronze-Back Beetle**, mentioned in the logs of those who pass through, but observed closely, they are more than simple insects. They grow to the size of a dinner plate. Their shells are so thick they can survive a fall of a thousand feet, bouncing off iron girders like brass nuts until they catch an ivy vine or land in a moss-carpet. They eat the waxy orchids and the soft, water-logged wood of the ancient, fossilized elder-vines. They are the cattle of the lattice; their nests are huge, papier-mâché spheres hung in the dry pockets beneath the massive bulkheads, constructed from chewed moss and metal-shavings.

Preying upon the beetles, the swifts, and the worms is the **Silt-Cat** (though it is no feline). It is a long-bodied, eight-legged stalker about the size of an otter, with a pelt of greasy, stiff green fiber that looks identical to the wet moss of the platforms. It has no eyes; in this world of bright glare and dark stripes, sight is a treacherous thing. Instead, it has three pairs of long, whisker-like antennae on its snout that detect the tiniest vibrations traveling through the structural iron. A Silt-Cat can sit motionless for three days on a gantry, looking exactly like a lump of overgrown pipe-saddle, until a swift lands near it. Its feet are tipped with soft, suction-cup pads that allow it to run silently upside-down along the undersides of the steel plating, defying the drop below.

### The People of the Rungs: The Tappers

Humanity, or what has become of it after millennia in the iron forest, does not live on the flat—for there is no flat. They are the **Tappers** (or *The People of the Rungs*). 

#### Physiology and Movement
A Tapper is instantly recognizable by their proportions. They are remarkably lean, with long, whip-cord muscles and chests that are broad and shallow, adapted for pressing flat against structural columns to let passing machinery or predators go by. Their skin is the color of old copper, stained with soot, grease, and the green juices of crushed ferns. 

Their hands and feet are their most remarkable feature: their fingers are long and double-jointed, while their big toes are opposable, capable of wrapping completely around a one-inch iron rod. The skin on their palms and soles is thick and leathery, resembling the tread of a solid rubber tire, charred in places from sliding down hot bronze handrails. They do not wear shoes; instead, they wrap their ankles in oiled hide to protect them from the sharp edges of rust-flakes.

#### Language and Vibration
The Tappers do not speak with the loud, open-throated vowels of those who live in open valleys. The air is too noisy; the constant *clank-shudder* of the counterweights and the scream of steam vents would swallow their voices. 

Instead, they speak in a language of clicks, high-pitched whistles (copied from the slate-swifts), and, most importantly, **The Wire-Talk**. Every Tapper wears several heavy iron-and-brass rings on their fingers. When they wish to communicate across distances or through the thick steam, they press their chest or forehead against a structural girder and tap the metal with these rings. 

The vibration travels miles through the continuous iron frame. By placing their palms flat against the steel, another Tapper miles away can "hear" the message through their bones. They can tell the weight of an approaching storm-water rush, the failure of an upper valve, or the presence of an enemy simply by the "flavor" of the hum in the metal under their feet.

#### Shelter and Culture
They do not build houses. Instead, they spin **Cocoons**. Using needles made of swift-bone, they weave the thick, cable-like vines of the waxy ivy into tight, teardrop-shaped hammocks that hang suspended in the deep, sheltered hollows of the box-girders, wedged between the structural web-plates. These cocoons are lined with the soft, downy feathers of the swifts and the dry, beaten inner-bark of giant ferns. A settlement is a vertical cluster of fifty or sixty of these green pods, swaying gently in the hot, rising drafts, looking from a distance like some strange fruit growing from the steel joints.

Their entire economy is based on the **Great Turn**. They understand that the world is moving, though they do not know where. The slow-turning gears are their calendar. "When the Great Gear turns three teeth," they say, "the water will turn bitter." They have built their lives around these predictable rotations, harvesting the condensation from specific pipes when they swing into alignment with certain cool drafts, and migrating upward or downward as the seasons of steam dictate.

They worship the **Engineers of the First Rib**—mythical ancestors who they believe cast the world from a ladle of liquid sun. To a Tapper, heaven is not a place in the clouds, but the *High Grid*, where the light is pure and white and there are no shadows; hell is the *Bottombox*, the boiling marsh of black grease and scalding muck where the dead things and the scaled rust fall to be remade.

### The Deep-Downers: The Cinder-Gills

Very different from the Tappers are the things that live near the bottom, where the shadow boils and the air is thick with sulfur, hot oil, and falling debris. 

Here, in the semi-liquid dark of the lowest cisterns, live the **Cinder-Gills**. They are human-shaped, but they have lost the climb. Their limbs are short and spade-like, adapted for swimming through the thick layer of wet rust-slurry and fallen leaf-mulch that collects at the base of the giant bulkheads. Their skin is thick, pale, and covered in a protective layer of greasy mucus that resists the mild acids of the pooling water.

The Cinder-Gills do not climb the ladders; they have forgotten how. They live on what falls from above—the dead birds, the dropped tools, the broken cocoons, and the bodies of those who lose their grip. They are the recyclers of the machine. They have developed a symbiotic relationship with the **Boiling Vent-Worms**, harvesting them from the steam pipes to use as light sources, carrying them in hollow brass lanterns where the worms glow with a faint, bioluminescent green as they feed on the greasy exhaust.

### The Aerialists of the High Grid

At the very top, where the white light is so bright that it burns the skin and the air is cold and thin, live the **Sails-of-Cloud**. 

These are giant, colonial organisms—half-plant, half-jellyfish—that drift through the high-altitude steel grid. They are essentially living hot-air balloons. Their tops are wide, waxy sails of dark purple leaf-matter that absorb the blinding sunlight, while their interiors are filled with lighter-than-air gases produced by the fermentation of wet orchids they trap in their dangling, vine-like roots. 

As they drift through the high steel maze, their roots brush against the topmost girders, scraping off the lichens and the small white moths that live only in the high cold. The Tappers sometimes climb to the very limit of the ladders, risking the "thin-breath," to harvest the sweet, fermented nectar that drips from these hanging sails, a liquid they call *Girders-Honey*.
