To understand what lives in this stone labyrinth, one must first unlearn the warm, wet rush of blood. There is nothing here made of soft red meat, nor is there any leaf to rot and make soil. The life that occupies these vertical corridors is slow, mineral, and acoustic, matching the cold patience of the basalt.

### The Sebum-Kin (The Tallow-Dwellers)

The "whoever" who carved the niches, raised the jadeite wedges, and left grease-smudges on the low stone ceilings are still here. They have not vanished; they are simply very difficult to see when they are not moving, for their skin is identical to the wet, gray-white tufa crusts.

They are known—insofar as they have a name in the vibration of the spires—as the Sebum-Kin, or the Wax-Fleshed. They are not built of bone and marrow, but of a dense, industrial tallow—an organic mineral wax, cold to the touch and smelling faintly of paraffin and volcanic sulfur. This wax-flesh is highly hydrophobic, allowing them to cling to the wet, weeping faces of the spires for centuries without their bodies dissolving or softening in the rising water. 

In shape, a Sebum-Kin is flat and segmented, like a heavy, three-foot-wide cedar shingle or an elongated trilobite. Their bodies are designed to fit perfectly within the narrow, three-foot gaps between the basalt columns. They have no legs; instead, their sides are lined with jointed, copper-hard ribs that they wedge into the vertical fractures of the stone. By expanding and contracting these lateral ribs, they climb the spires with an agonizing, rhythmic click-clack that sounds exactly like the water-globes colliding.

They have no eyes. The raw, naked sun at this altitude would blind any organic lens within seconds. Instead, their heads are broad, flat plates of polished, horn-like silica that are tuned to the vibrations of the stone. The Sebum-Kin "see" through the great, wind-driven pipe organ of the plateau. They know the shape of their world by the pitch of the wind: a high, glassy whistle means a narrow gap; a deep, bone-rattling bass indicates a drop into a vertical abyss of unknown depth. 

It is the Sebum-Kin who burn the fat-lamps in the dry niches. The fat is their own. When they grow old, or when their outer skin becomes choked and dulled by deposits of silica, they climb into their ancestral niches and use their copper scrapers to peel away their own outer layers of tallow. They knead this scrap-wax into small, heavy cakes and burn it in stone basins. The light of these lamps is greasy, yellow, and smoky, casting long, wavering shadows across the basalt—a domestic fire made from the very substance of the climber’s body.

They climb to live, and they live to climb. Their sole nourishment is the *Opal-Bloom*—the microscopic layers of young silica that crystallize at the very tips of the spires, where the climbing water meets the blinding sun. The Sebum-Kin crawl to these summits during the brief, hot noon, scraping the wet opal into their mouth-slits with their jadeite wedges. Digested opal glows inside them; at night, if you look up into the dark gaps between the towers, you can see the Sebum-Kin moving like slow, milky lanterns, their bellies shimmering with the captured light of the sun.

### The Glass-Weavers of the Abyss

The "braided mineral glass" that lashes the bridge-logs together is not manufactured by any tool. It is harvested from the *Glass-Weavers*, the true denizens of the deep, blue-shadowed abysses.

Where the sun never reaches, the air is cold enough to freeze condensation into microscopic frost-needles. In this eternal twilight live creatures like ribbons of black, flexible obsidian. They are several yards long but barely thicker than a finger, utterly blind, and virtually weightless in the lazy, distracted gravity of the deep chasms.

These ribbon-beasts glide upward along the spires by riding the rising sheets of water, their sleek, vitreous bodies friction-free. As they ascend, they exude a continuous, monofilament thread of pure volcanized glass from their rear segments. This glass hair is as tough as steel and as flexible as silk. 

To the Sebum-Kin, the Glass-Weavers are both a resource and a hazard. A single thread of their glass-hair can sever a wax-flesh limb if stumbled into in the dark of a wind-shriek. Yet, the Kin have learned to gather these threads by dangling copper hooks into the abysses, reeling in the glass-wool in great, shimmering hanks, which they braid by touch into the cables that secure their slate bridges.

### The Core-Mites of the Floating Welkin

The plum-sized and head-sized spheres of water that drift horizontally between the spires are not empty water. Each is a home.

If you look into a floating water-globe as it catches the white sunlight, you will see a tiny, dark spark suspended at its dead center. This is a *Core-Mite*. No larger than a pine-nut, the mite is a dense, metallic crustacean with an outer shell composed of heavy copper-pyrite. 

The relationship between the mite and the water is one of mutual necessity. The mite is too heavy to float on its own, even in this lazy gravity, but its body secretes a powerful, oily surfactant that alters the surface tension of the water around it. When the condensation on the cold spires pools, the mite crawls into the wetness, wraps itself in a thick bubble of water, and detaches from the stone. 

Once airborne, the mite is the navigator of its liquid vessel. By shifting its tiny, heavy body within the sphere, it alters the water-globe's center of gravity, causing it to drift, rise, or descend. It beats its microscopic, comb-like legs to vibrate the water's surface, riding the acoustic pressure waves generated by the wind-organ of the spires. 

When two water globes drift into one another, they do not simply merge; the Core-Mites within them immediately lock in a brief, metallic struggle. The victor consumes the loser, and the merged water-globe—now larger, heavier, and more stable—continues its slow, horizontal voyage. 

When a globe eventually hits a stone spire with a heavy *clack*, it is not an accident. The mite inside has steered it there because it is ready to lay eggs. The water soaks into the porous white tufa, carrying the microscopic larvae of the mite deep into the cold, wet heart of the basalt stack, where they will feed on the dissolved mineral salts for years before emerging to catch their first bubble.

### The Final Transit

There is no death here that resembles the rotting of the earth. Here, everything eventually returns to stone.

When a Sebum-Kin's wax body becomes too heavy with accumulated mineral crusts—when they can no longer scrape the opal-bloom or climb the vertical seams—they do not fall into the abyss. Instead, they make their final climb to the very highest points of the labyrinth, where the spires are beginning to swell and touch one another under the blinding sun.

There, they wedge themselves face-up into the narrowest gaps, directly in the path of the upward-climbing waters. 

Over decades, the wet, silicate-rich streams flow over them. Slowly, atom by atom, the tallow of their bodies is replaced by chalcedony, quartz, and opal. Their waxy limbs become structural stone; their sensory plates are fused into the growing, translucent ceiling that filters the raw light into a milky glare. 

They become the very bridges they once lashed together. The music of the wind-organ passes through their petrified ribs, and they join the great, singing skeleton of the plateau, ringing forever in the clean, unblinking light.
