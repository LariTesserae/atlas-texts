To understand the life that inhabits this world, one must first discard the concept of the soft tissue, the wet eye, and the red blood cell. Here, there are no interior oceans carried in leather bags of skin. The dryness is absolute; the wind is a thief that steals moisture before it can even form; the light is an enemy to any surface that cannot reflect it.

The organisms that claim the *Lignum Silex* have evolved not to resist the dry friction of their world, but to become its gears, its sails, and its collectors.

### The Piezo-Pharynges (The Friction-Shims)

Deep within the interfaces where two peninsula-sized limbs grind against one another under the weight of a hundred leagues of scaffolding, there lives a family of extreme crustacean-like organisms known as the **Friction-Shims**.

These creatures are wedge-shaped, flat as garden slates, and layered like the leaf-springs of an ancient locomotive. Their outershells are not chitin but a composite of compressed silica-fiber and dense, grey graphite-plate. They possess no legs for walking; indeed, they have no need to travel across the open voids where the wind would pluck them like dry leaves. Instead, they seek the narrowest, most violent spaces in the world: the grinding hinge-joints of the great arches.

A Shim’s entire life is spent in the crush. It inserts its wedge-shaped snout into an active, groaning crevice where two zinc-colored trunks rub together. It feeds on the graphite-sap that oozes from the wounded wood under tension, but its true metabolic driver is the pressure itself. 

The interior of a Shim is a solid-state crystalline network. When the wind shifts and the great arch bends, applying dozens of tons of pressure to the Shim's flanks, its internal crystals are compressed. This compression generates a high-voltage piezoelectric charge within its dry nervous system. This charge is its breath, its heartbeat, and its digestion. 

They are communal block-dwellers. In a single joint, three thousand Shims may lie stacked together like a set of carbon washers, their bodies polished to a chrome-like mirror finish by the dry heat of the wood’s friction, singing in a low, metallic hiss as the world rolls beneath them.

### The Aeolian Polyphones (The Flute-Sails)

In the wide, three-mile chasms between the terraces, where the blue air is cold and the light smells of ozone, the empty space belongs to the **Flute-Sails**.

These are the drifters, creatures of vast scale and negligible weight. A Flute-Sail is built like a three-dimensional box-kite, fifteen meters across but weighing less than a dried thistle. Their frames are grown from hollow, paper-thin tubes of yellow cane-wood, braced with gossamer threads of hardened copper-resin. 

Between these canes are stretched membranes of dry, golden parchment—sheets of dead skin that have been tanned by the violent ultraviolet glare of the high sun until they are as stiff and resonant as the head of a snare drum.

The Flute-Sails have no mouths. They do not eat in the traditional sense; their skin is covered in a microscopic velvet of dry, sulfur-sensitive hairs that catch the yellow dust-clouds drifting from the broken columns below. 

They are most remarkable for their voices. Embedded within their hollow cane-frames are series of natural reed-stops and whistle-pipes, aligned with the direction of the wind. As they glide through the voids, the howling air is forced through their bodies, producing a continuous, shifting chord of pipe-organ notes. 

Because they have no eyes, they navigate entirely by this sound. They listen to the echoes of their own chords bouncing off the distant *Lignum Silex* arches. By analyzing the pitch-shift—the dry *scree* of an approaching pillar, the deep *thrum* of a piston-trunk to the north—they can map their way through the shifting lattice without ever touching a surface. 

When two Flute-Sails meet in the blue, they do not touch; they align their sails to create a perfect fifth or a minor third. This acoustic lock holds them together in the slipstream for hours, allowing them to drop their microscopic, winged seed-cases into each other’s air-currents before the wind drifts them apart.

### The Anvil-Backs (The Volt-Eaters)

Where the mechanical forests rise highest, ending in the spinning turbine-fans that scream against the upper drafts, the air is constantly ripe with static electricity. Here, on the copper-veined leaves that rattle like tin plates, live the **Anvil-Backs**.

These creatures are the size of sheep, but shape-wise they resemble flat, hexagonal beetles or iron wedges. Their backs are covered in heavy plates of natural mica and dark, leaden scale, arranged in concentric circles like the plates of a solar battery. From their heads rise two thick, parallel horns made of pure, sparkling fulgurite—the glass tubes formed when lightning strikes sand.

An Anvil-Back is an electrical accumulator. It spends its days clamped to the highest, wind-whipped branches of the turbines, its copper-tipped legs bored directly into the wood’s internal sap-veins. 

As the turbine spins, generating a dry, static field that turns the surrounding needles into blue-galling torches, the Anvil-Back raises its fulgurite horns. It acts as a lightning rod. When a spark leaps across the gap from a neighboring branch, it strikes the creature’s horns with a crack that can be heard for miles.

The charge is absorbed down through the mica plates and stored in a series of dry, carbonaceous layered organs within its gut—organic Leyden jars. The Anvil-Back uses this stored current to smelt the copper dust and silica-sand it scrapes from the bark. When it is fully charged, the creature’s joints glow with a dim, internal orange light, like the filaments of an old vacuum tube, casting a soft, warm warmth into the freezing, high-altitude night.

### The Shroud-Menders (The Tensioners)

The most complex social organisms on the upper terraces are the **Shroud-Menders**. 

They are slender, six-legged creatures constructed of gray jointed cane, looking so much like the dry twigs of *Lignum Silex* that they are virtually invisible when still. They have no eyes; their heads terminate in a pair of delicate, copper-wire antennae that vibrate in harmony with the wind.

The Shroud-Menders are the caretakers of the scaffold. They do not have nests or territories; they are nomadic, moving in long, single-file lines across the miles-long vine-cables that keep the entire world from flies-and-scaffold collapse.

Their feet are tipped with small, brassy pulleys. When they walk along a cable, they do not step; they roll, their pulleys clicking rhythmically against the twisted fibers of the vine. They "hear" the world as a system of tensions. By pressing their antennae against the cables, they can feel the vibrational pitch of the entire district. 
*   A healthy cable sings in a clear, high pitch—a hard, metallic *ping*.
*   A fraying cable, near its breaking point, groans with a wet, gravelly *clack*.

When the Shroud-Menders detect a dying cable, they halt. Working in unison, several hundred individuals will gather at the point of failure. They secrete a thick, gray, graphite-scented resin from their abdomens, which they spin into thread using their front legs. They weave this thread around the fraying cable, tightening it with their own bodies, which act as living turnbuckles.

They will remain there for weeks, locked in place, holding the tension by sheer muscle and dried adhesive. Over time, the sun bakes their resin-wraps into a substance as hard as zinc. Often, a Shroud-Mender will die while holding the line; its body does not rot, but dries out, mineralizes in the dry ozone air, and becomes a permanent, carbon-bonded splice in the world’s structure—an ancestor turned into a load-bearing brace.

### The Core-Borers (The Sinks of the Under-Void)

Far below the upper terraces, three miles down through the light-starved blue and into the black core where the wood has died and petrified under the weight of the world, there is no wind. There are no turbines, no piston-pumps, and no lightning. The air here is heavy, hot, and choked with carbon-monoxide and falling graphite-dust.

Here, in the pressurized dark, live the **Core-Borers**.

These are massive, cylindrical things, thirty meters long, like armored train-cars made of black slag and ironwood. They have no eyes, no limbs, and no sails. Their entire front end is a rotary drill-face composed of concentric rings of black diamond teeth—teeth grown from carbon extracted from the petrified wood they inhabit.

The Borers move through the solid, petrified core of the world at a rate of inches per day. They grind their way through the ancient, silica-choked trunks, digesting the fossil-resins and sulfur-pockets that have been trapped there for geological epochs.

As they burrow, they leave behind them tunnels of perfect, polished smoothness, packed with their dry, pelleted waste—a dense, charcoal-like slag that the upper trees use as a foundation for their deepest roots. 

The sound they make is a low, continuous, tectonic rumble—a grinding *crunch-crunch-crunch* that rises through the wooden columns like a tremor, notifying the creatures of the upper terraces that far beneath the bright, empty air, the ancient foundations of their world are still being turned, digested, and rebuilt.
