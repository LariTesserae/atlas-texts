To understand what lives in the basin, one must first understand that here, the boundary between the living and the non-living has softened. In a place three billion years old, the rock has learned to mimic the flesh, and the flesh has taken on the patient, unhurried temperament of the rock.

### The Ribbon-Drifts

The air is ruled by the amber ribbons. They are not animals in the sense of possessing a brain or a heart, yet they are more than mere plants. They are called the *thread-drifts* by no one, for there is no one here to name them, but they exist as the very weft of the atmosphere. 

Each ribbon is a single, continuous cell, several yards long and as thin as a single layer of onion skin. They are a rich, translucent amber, colored by a copper-based fluid that moves through them in a slow, visible pulse—perhaps three beats every ten minutes. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no limbs. Instead, they operate on a simple, elegant system of thermal draft and osmotic hunger. 

As they glide through the warm, pearlescent mist, they feed upon the microscopic spores of the gold-moss and the mineral-rich steam rising from the wet basalt. When they drift over one of the three matte-black slabs, the rising column of warm, dry air lifts them higher into the basin’s sky. There, in the brighter light, they stretch themselves wide, basking like solar sails. When they pass back into the cool, damp shade of the bellow-palms, they curl into tight, defensive corkscrews to retain their moisture, falling slowly toward the ground where the moss-carpet catches them.

They do not die of old age. Occasionally, a ribbon will become snagged on the rough trunk of a bellow-palm. It does not struggle. It simply dissolves at the point of contact, parting into two smaller, independent ribbons, each continuing its silent, drifting journey.

### The Fan-Headed Lau

On the ground, moving across the sleek, wet basalt with a rhythmic *shlook-shlook* of padded feet, are the *Lau*—the six-legged leather-skins. 

They are the size of small dogs, but their bodies are long and low, like weasels carved from dark grey river stone. Their skin is entirely hairless, perpetually slicked with a fine coating of oil that repels the constant mist, keeping their internal bodies warm and dry. They have no eyes; in this basin, where the light is always diffused and cast from no single source, sight is a useless, energy-costly luxury. 

Instead, the Lau navigate by vibration and temperature. Their heads are dominated by two broad, leaf-like fans of pale pink tissue. These fans are incredibly delicate, veined with thousands of tiny capillaries. They twitch continually, reading the world as a map of air currents and moisture density. 

A Lau can "see" a bellow-palm three hundred yards away by the faint, cool draft its contraction creates. They can hear the tiny, needle-thin water-skaters by the microscopic vibrations they send through the basalt templates. 

The Lau are gardeners of a sort. They feed on the star-shaped succulents that bloom and wither in the afternoon. Their teeth are not sharp clappers but flat, grinding plates of organic silica. They graze with a slow, administrative thoroughness, never stripped-mining a patch of moss but moving in a circular path that takes weeks to complete, allowing the succulents to regenerate behind them. 

When two Lau meet, they do not fight or play. They stand face-to-face, their pink fans touching, exchanging heat and chemical information for hours, completely motionless, before parting to resume their separate, quiet loops.

### The Salt-Striders of the Dry-Zones

Within the twelve-foot domes of dry air projected by the black slabs, a completely different ecology exists—one that would perish instantly if it stepped an inch into the humid basin.

Here, in the pale, dry dust, live the *Salt-Striders*. They are tiny, paper-dry crickets, though they have no legs for jumping. Instead, they glide across the dust on three pairs of long, hair-like spines. They are constructed of a brittle, glass-like chitin that clinks like dry leaves when they move. 

The Salt-Striders are entirely dependent on the gold-dust nanites that orbit within the dry domes. They feed on the shed casings of these tiny machines—highly complex metallic molecules that have fallen out of the programmatic alignment of the spiral patterns. In turn, the Striders’ waste is a rich, mineral-heavy ash that the nanites use to repair themselves, maintaining the perfect, dry equilibrium of the domes.

If a drop of water were to find its way into a dry-zone—perhaps carried on the skin of an inattentive Lau—the Salt-Striders would dissolve like sugar. Consequently, they have developed an intense sensitivity to humidity. If a Lau approaches the edge of their dome to rid itself of skin-parasites in the dry air, the Striders retreat to the very center of the slab, crowding together in a frantic, rattling cluster of dry husks, waiting for the damp intruder to leave.

### The Deep-Sleepers

Beneath the gold-and-red root-moss, in the deep depressions of the basalt bowl, lie the largest inhabitants of the basin. These are the *Basalt-Sleepers*. 

From above, they look like nothing more than natural mounds of the stone itself—large, curved ridges of dark basalt. But if one were to scrape away the thick blanket of moss, they would find a thick, segmented shell, cold to the touch but yielding slightly under immense pressure, like dense vulcanized rubber.

The Sleepers do not move. A young Sleeper may travel a few yards in a century, but once it finds a comfortable groove in the ancient floor, it settles. Their bellies are lined with thousands of microscopic, root-like tendrils that bore into the basalt, drawing up the deep, iron-rich mineral water that seeps through the earth from the dead mountains miles away.

They have no senses that we would recognize. They do not hear the wind; they do not feel the warmth of the dry-zones. They exist in a state of absolute, vegetative meditation. Their only communication with the outer world is a low, rhythmic sigh that occurs once every few days—a soft release of excess nitrogen that bubbles up through the wet moss, smelling faintly of old rain and copper.

### The Unified Pulse

In this basin, death is not a tragedy, nor is birth a triumph. They are simply shifts in the ledger of a very old, very tired accounting system.

When a Lau dies, it does not rot in the damp air. The amber ribbons descend upon it, draping its dark body in a shroud of translucent gold. Within forty-eight hours, the ribbons have absorbed the oils and minerals of the corpse, leaving only a white, chalky outline on the basalt that the root-moss quickly drinks.

When a bellow-palm finally grows too heavy for its fibrous trunk and falls, it does not crash. It sinks slowly into the moss over decades, its massive spade-leaves dissolving into a rich, dark jelly that feeds the succulents for a century to come.

Everything here is closed. The ancient machines on their black slabs continue to hum, providing the thermal currents that keep the ribbons aloft; the ribbons feed the moss; the moss feeds the Lau; the Lau fertilize the palms; the palms drink the water that seeps through the basalt, which is kept clean and porous by the deep-sleepers below.

It is a world that has reached its final draft. The editing is done; the ink is dry. All that remains is the slow, warm, beautiful reading of the page, over and over again, until the star overhead finally goes dark.
