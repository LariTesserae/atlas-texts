In this cold, pressurized grease, life does not move so much as it *endures*. It has abandoned the wasteful extravagance of speed, sight, and skeleton. In a world where the water itself behaves like a sliding solid, to go fast is to drag against an abrasive wall of stone-dust; to see is impossible; to possess hollow bones is to implode. 

What remains is a fauna of absolute density—creatures that are essentially solid muscle, oil, and mineralized plate, matching the specific gravity of the brine-soup so perfectly that they are nearly weightless within it, yet heavy as iron if brought to the lost surface.

***

### The Pelagic Drifters: Silt-Vessels and Oil-Bells

Hovering in the dense, silent layers above the basalt domes are the largest inhabitants of the basin: the *Silt-Vessels*. These are not fish, nor are they jellyfish; they are massive, slow-pulsing bells of fibrous, grey cartilage, some the size of hay-wains. They possess no mouths or digestive tracts in the common sense. Instead, their outer skins are covered in a fine, sticky velvet made of microscopic, mucus-coated cilia. 

As the Silt-Vessels drift along the edges of the polar currents, they gather the "skeleton fall"—the infinite, icy rain of ancient silica and bone-dust suspended in the water. 
* Their bodies are semi-translucent, and within their thick, glassy walls, one can see their history. They contain a central, packed core of white paste—decades of accumulated diatom shells and mineral dust that they have absorbed but cannot break down. This core acts as their ballast. 
* They have no propulsion other than the slow, hydraulic contraction of their entire bodies, a movement that occurs perhaps once every ten minutes. They are the clocks of the deep, living for centuries in the dark, moving only as the planetary currents carry them. 
* When a silt-gale strikes, these vessels do not seek shelter; they cannot. If they are swept into the basalt domes, they do not pop; they shatter like cold lard, releasing their white, mineralized cores back into the grey soup, a brief, silent explosion of ancient chalk in the blackness.

***

### The Lithic Crawlers: Iron-Shields and Wedge-Mites

On the city-sized domes of grey basalt, life is entirely benthic and obsessively anchored. The shield-sized, pale scavenger mentioned in the ancient records—often called the *Iron-Shield*—is the dominant grazer of these slick plains. 

* The carapace of the Iron-Shield is not made of calcium, which would dissolve or weaken under this immense pressure, but of biogenic opal—a glass-tough secretion of fused silica pulled from the skeletal soup. This shell is low-slung, perfectly circular, and smooth as a polished lens, offering no purchase to the howling silt-gales that plow through the valleys.
* Their underside is a single, massive foot of dark, purplish muscle, ringed by dozens of short, stubby legs ending in hooks of raw copper. They do not walk; they slide, using a highly specialized form of adhesion. Their feet secrete a thick, sulfurous grease that prevents the heavy brine from getting under their shells. 
* They feed on the leathery black lichen, scraping it from the rock with a tongue like a rotary iron file. The sound of a thousand Iron-Shields feeding on a distant basalt shelf is a faint, dry *scritch-scritch-scritch* that travels through the rock, a tiny, insectoid counterpoint to the deep, planetary groaning of the seabed.

***

### The Lichen-Forests: The Thread-Gills

The leathery black lichen that clings to the undersides of the basalt shelves is more than a plant; it is a collaborative lithic crust. It grows with an agonizing slowness, taking perhaps three hundred years to spread the width of a human hand. Its roots—actually microscopic acid-tubes—penetrate the microscopic pores of the basalt, drawing out the iron, sulfur, and manganese seeping from the earth's mantle.

Living within the deep, wrinkled folds of this lichen are the *Thread-Gills*. These are vermiform entities, thin as sewing thread and several feet long, pale as milk. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no digestive tracts. They are rooted in the lichen like living hair. They exist entirely to breathe the mineral-rich "sweat" of the basalt. 

The Thread-Gills are the nervous system of the basalt shelves. They are incredibly sensitive to gravity and pressure waves. When a tectonic adjustment occurs miles below—even a shift of a millimeter along a basalt fault—the Thread-Gills twitch in perfect, instantaneous unison. This collective twitch sends a chemical ripple through the lichen, causing the entire colony to tighten its grip on the rock. It is a primitive, silent warning system that tells the Iron-Shields and other crawlers that a silt-gale or a seismic tremor is imminent, allowing them to cement themselves to the stone before the water begins to churn.

***

### The Predators: The Sedge-Bites and Plume-Lines

Where there is prey, there is hunger, but in the absolute dark, hunting requires a terrible, motionless patience. 

Deep in the black sediment valleys, where the slate-hard mud cracks under the weight of the sea, lives the *Sedge-Bite*. It is a predator that has become indistinguishable from the terrain. Its body consists of two massive, hinged plates of iron-impregnated bone, three feet across, rough and grey as the basalt itself. It lies buried in the black sediment, its hinges agap, completely still for months at a time. 
* It does not use light or scent to lure its prey. It relies on the *thrum*. 
* When a blind crawler, lost in a silt-gale, seeks shelter in what feels like a natural crevasse in the slate, it steps into the Sedge-Bite's maw. The response is not muscle-driven but hydraulic. The valve snaps shut with the force of a hydraulic press, using the immense ambient water pressure of the abyss to crush the crawler’s silica shell. The Sedge-Bite then slowly secretes a highly concentrated pool of acid to dissolve the soft tissues inside, a process that can take half a year.

Above them, hanging from the basalt shelves like weeping willow branches, are the *Plume-Lines*. These are colonial organisms that look like fine, black threads dangling into the current. They are made of high-tensile collagen, as elastic as spider silk but far tougher. 
* The threads are coated in tiny, microscopic hooks and a numbing, freezing toxin. 
* As the brine-rivers plow through the basin, they drag drifting organisms into these hanging curtains. Any creature that brushes against a Plume-Line is instantly snagged; the thread, reacting to the chemical signature of life, undergoes a structural collapse, shrinking and winding upward like a spring, dragging the prey up into the dark crevices of the basalt where the parent colony resides.

### The Grafted: The Lost Children of the Shallows

The most mysterious inhabitants of the basin are those that live in the deepest, oldest troughs of black slate—the places where the ancient obsidian hand-axes and basalt sinkers lie buried. 

They are the descendants of the primitive beings who once walked the shallow, sunlit shores of this basin, millions of years before the land sagged and the ocean claimed it. They did not die when the light went out. Instead, over evolutionary epochs, they sank with the stone, their bodies warping, softening, and dense-growing under the slow, vertical crawl of the water.

They are called the *Grafted*, though there is no one left to name them. 
* They have lost all symmetry. They no longer have arms, legs, or heads. They are pale, rubbery, mound-like masses of muscle and fat, roughly the size of a dog, that crawl through the hard-packed sediment by a slow, peristaltic rolling of their entire bodies.
* They have no eyes or ears, but they possess a highly developed sense of touch—a heavy, tactile intelligence centered in their underbellies. 
* Their existence is defined by a single, ancestral obsession: they search the silt for the ancient, hand-tooled stones. When a Grafted encounters a basalt sinker or an obsidian hand-axe, it does not eat it or use it as a tool. Instead, it crawls over the stone and *grows* around it. 
* The soft, pale flesh of the creature slowly envelops the ancient tool, fusing with the basalt or glass until the stone is completely embedded within its body. A mature Grafted may carry three or four of these ancient tools inside its flesh. The weight of these stones serves a vital purpose: it anchors the creature to the slate bed during the decades-long silt-gales, preventing them from being swept away into the freezing current.

In the dark, these creatures lie clustered in the shallow troughs, silent, heavy, and still. They do not speak; they do not build. But they carry the only physical memory of the surface world within their own bodies. When they die, their soft flesh is slowly dissolved by the acidic brine, leaving the ancient tools behind once more, polished a little smoother, waiting in the black sediment for the next generation to find them.
