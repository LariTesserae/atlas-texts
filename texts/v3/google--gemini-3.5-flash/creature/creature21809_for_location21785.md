To find the true inhabitants of the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts, one must first look at what appears to be the debris of the room itself. 

In the shadows behind the massive square pillars, where the yellow tallow-light cannot quite reach, there are mounds of discarded brick-shards and dried, gray heaps of lime-mortar. If you watch these heaps for an hour under the steady, warm drip of the ceiling, you will see that they breathe. 

These are the **Mortar-Wrights**. 

There are perhaps forty of them left in the basin, though they are so quiet and slow-moving that a visitor might count only three. They are small, squat homuncloids, rarely standing taller than a human child’s shoulder, but they possess a density that makes them heavy as iron. Their chests are deep, barrel-shaped bellows, built to drag life out of the hot, oxygen-poor air of the basin. 

Their skin is their most remarkable feature: it is not soft like flesh, but has the rough, porous texture of unglazed pottery. In the damp warmth of the room, it has absorbed the iron-red dust of the bricks until they are the exact color of the walls. Across their shoulders, heads, and the bridges of their wide, flat noses, the constant lime-water drip has deposited a thick, greyish crust of calcium. This "scurf," as it grows, cracks along the lines of their expressions, leaving wild, pale fissures across their faces that look like the lime-wash in the brick joints. 

The Wrights have no hair. In its place, their scalps are covered in a dense, mossy fuzz of liver-colored lichen that they cultivate to shield their skulls from the cold, heavy drops falling from the vaults. Their fingers are impossibly long, double-jointed, and tipped not with nails but with hard, spade-like wedges of yellow bone. They use these digits to scrape the lime-icicles from the ceiling and to clear the hair-roots that constantly try to choke the bronze shunts.

They are blind, or very nearly so. Their eyes are large, wet, and golden, but the pupils have shrunk to tiny pinpricks under a thick, cloudy membrane—an adaptation against the caustic lime-dust and the stinging steam. Instead, they navigate by the *hum* of the room. They possess a map of the room’s six thousand valves carved entirely into the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. By pressing a hand against a damp ceramic pipe, a Wright can tell by the vibration which sluice gate three levels below is choked with silt, or if a leak has sprung in the northwest vault.

***

The Mortar-Wrights live in a state of quiet, ancient symbiosis with the animal life of the basin. 

The thousands of flat-bodied orange crabs do not fear them. In fact, when a Wright sits still for his six-hour sleep—wedged upright into a dry niche between two pillars—the crabs will swarm over him in a clicking orange blanket. They use their tiny pincers to groom his skin, picking away the dead lichen, the gray mold, and the grease-films that collect in his joint-folds. In return, the Wrights protect the crabs’ nests from the large, blind crayfish that stalk the shallow pans.

Deep within the three-foot ceramic pipes, where the water runs fast and dark, lives the second great dweller of the basin: **The Glass-Snout**. 

These are giant, lungless salamanders, some measuring six feet from their blunt noses to their flat, ribbon-like tails. They are entirely translucent; in the light of a tallow flame, one can see the dark silhouette of their spine, the slow pulse of their three-chambered heart, and whatever crab-shells or cedar-chips they have recently swallowed. They have no teeth, only wide, fleshy funnels for mouths, which they use to vacuum the silt and rotted hemp from the bottom of the conduits.

The Glass-Snouts are the source of the basin's light. Once every few months, when the water-rhythms change and the pipes run low, the Mortar-Wrights will wait at the end of a sluice with hempen nets. They do not kill the Glass-Snouts ruthlessly; they catch only the oldest, whose skin has turned a milky, opaque yellow. From these, they render the thick, white lard that fills the clay tallow cups. The bones of the salamanders are scraped clean and carved into the long, thin needles the Wrights use to bind the cedar walkways together.

***

There is no language spoken in the Room of the Six Thousand Shunts. The air is already too loud with the *ping-ping* of bronze and the rush of water for a voice to carry. Instead, the Mortar-Wrights communicate with a system of acoustic signals. 

Each Wright carries a "shoppe"—a hollow cylinder of dried elderwood, plugged at one end. By blowing into the shoppe at different angles against the mouths of the copper pipes, they can send long, booming whistles through the entire hydraulic network. A sharp, double-toned *hroot-hroot* traveling through the ceramic mains might mean *The third gate is jammed*; a low, vibrating drone that makes the floor-pans ripple means *The light-wells are darkening; storm coming above.*

They have no memory of the world above the chimneys. To them, the green-glazed light-wells are not passages to a sky, but merely the places where the "Dry-Giver" lives. The sky is an abstract concept—they believe it to be a massive, blue-glazed dome, much like their ceiling but vast, where giant, silent giants turn keys to let the rain down. 

When a Mortar-Wright grows old and his joints begin to seize with the calcifying "grey-rot," he does not die in a bed. He walks to the outer walls, where the brickwork is thickest. His companions will have already pried loose a three-foot section of the terracotta flats, revealing the wet bricklayers beneath. The old Wright will crawl into the niche, curling his long, spade-fingered hands around his knees. 

The others then brick him in. They use the finest, most butter-like lime-mortar, sealing the joints until the wall is flat and square once more. Within a year, the moisture weeping through the masonry will have carrying its minerals into his porous skin, turning his bones to stone and his flesh to clay. 

He becomes the room. He becomes another shunt, another pillar, holding up the weight of a world he has never seen, while below, his children listen to his silent vibration in the dark.
