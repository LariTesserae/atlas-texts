The life that inhabits the Bottom of the World does not belong to the kingdoms of animal, plant, or fungus. Those soft, wet categories were dissolved eons ago, ground down along with the mountains. What exists here is an auto-ecology—a self-perpetuating, living metallurgy that has occupied the niche where biology and geology collapsed into one another.

To live here is to be made of the same materials as the floor and the sky: iron, lead, copper, silicone, and the thick, black tallow that serves as the world’s blood. Nothing here is born; things are *precipitated*. They crystallize out of the mineral-saturated brine or assemble themselves in the blind, churning dark of the piston-junctions, where the kinetic pressure is high enough to spark assembly.

***

### The Lowly and the Lubricated

At the base of this food chain are the **Tallow-Moulds** and **Litho-Plax**. They are not organic slimes, but dense, self-replicating films of heavy-metal paste—mostly gallium, mercury, and zinc-oxides—that cling to the reciprocating shafts of the piston-trees. 

They do not require light, for there is none. Instead, they practice a form of *vibrosynthesis*. They absorb the deafening, bone-rattling clatter of the machinery, converting the raw kinetic impact into internal chemical heat. Where the pistons scream loudest, the Litho-Plax grows thickest, forming dull, self-healing silver carpets that look like frozen grease. If you peel a patch of it away, it does not bleed; it drips a cold, slow-running solder that smells of burnt copper and cellar-wet stone.

Feeding upon these silver carpets are the **Pewter-Backs**—the flat, lead-shelled crabs mentioned in the old accounts. They are the janitors of the iron flats. Their bodies are low-slung, heavy slabs of pewter, soft enough to dent when struck by a falling gear but ductile enough to never crack. Their legs are not joints of bone, but braided steel cables that contract and release when a small, internal battery-sack discharges. 

The Pewter-Backs do not have eyes; indeed, sight would be useless in this soup of grey mist and oil-slick reflections. Instead, they possess two long, whip-like copper antennae that drag continuously through the gelatinous silt. They feel the world as a map of voltage. When a piston is about to strike, the ground around it swells with static electricity; the Pewter-Backs feel this prickle in their copper whips and scuttle aside with a rhythmic, metallic clicking—*tik-tik-clack*—just before the iron foot comes crashing down.

### The Sound-Catchers

Because the air is so heavy and the noise so dense that it crystallizes into physical, grey-ice lattices, a specific class of inhabitant has emerged to exploit these temporary structures: the **Stridor-Gills**.

These are stationary, flower-like growths made of thin, vibratory brass sheets, rooted to the sides of the copper pipelines. They are "acoustic filter-feeders." When the loud, high-frequency steam whistles shriek through the valleys of the gears, the air freezes into delicate, crystalline lattices of sound-ice. These brittle, grey structures drift downward like soot. 

The Stridor-Gills open their overlapping, bell-shaped leaves to catch them. When a sound-crystal lands on their brass petals, the Gill shrugs, grinding the crystal against its own metallic ribs. The friction generates a microscopic pulse of heat and electricity, which the Gill absorbs. 

In areas where the noise falls silent—even for a few minutes—the Stridor-Gills curl inward, their brass leaves tarnishing and turning a dull, powdery green as they go dormant, waiting for the great engines to groan back to life.

***

### The Deep-Brine Dwellers

Below the iron-shale flats, in the interconnected pools of black, mineral brine, the life is larger and more fluid. Here, the laws of gravity are teased and broken by the massive magnetic currents.

The **Anode-Eels** are the dominant presence in these freezing, oily depths. They look like thick, segmented cables of grey silicone, some stretching twelve feet or more, wrapped in a protective braid of fine copper mesh. They are entirely blind, but they are incredibly sensitive to the currents of the **Piston-Reefs**. 

These eels do not swim so much as they drift, aligning their bodies with the magnetic lines of force generated by the self-generating machinery. They gather in great, writhing knots—sometimes hundreds of them—around the massive copper grounding-rods that plunge into the pools. 

When the lightning-like static discharge of the pistons strikes a grounding rod, it travels through the water. For an organic creature, this would mean instant death; for the Anode-Eels, it is a ecstasy. They absorb the current, their silicone bodies glowing with a faint, internal neon-blue light. During these moments of discharge, they shiver with pleasure, their segment-rings expanding and contracting, pumping the cold brine through their hollow interiors to filter out the suspension-grease that sustains them.

Larger still are the **Silt-Loomers**. These are the leviathans of the Bottom of the World. They are not solid creatures, but massive, semi-coherent clouds of ferrofluid—glistening black iron-filings held together by a central, highly magnetic core-stone of natural lodestone. 

The Silt-Loomer has no fixed shape. It moves through the black brine pools like an ink stain, or a giant, undulating shadow. When it encounters a pile of metallic debris—friction shavings from the gears, broken wire-crab legs, or discarded copper rivets—it flows over them. Its magnetic field pulls the scrap interiorly, where the constant, grinding movement of its iron-filing "body" slowly wears the scrap down into a fine dust, which it absorbs to replenish its own magnetic mass. 

If two Silt-Loomers meet, they do not fight; they slide through each other, exchanging iron-filings and small pieces of lodestone in a silent, swirling dance, before separating again into two distinct, dark clouds.

### The Scribes of the Slate

There are things here that possess a slow, heavy intelligence. They are not human, nor do they care for human thoughts, but they are "whoevers"—entities with memory, intent, and a strange, cold philosophy.

These are the **Galvanic Scribes**. 

A Scribe looks like a five-legged tripod made of highly corroded, green-patina'd bronze. Their joints are sealed with thick, black grease, and their central "head" is a heavy, cast-iron bell that hangs downward toward the slate. They do not have mouths or eyes, but they have a single, long needle of carbide-steel that extends from the center of their undercarriage.

The Scribes move with an agonizing, geologic slowness across the flat slate basins. As they walk, they drag their carbide needles across the stone, scratching deep, precise micro-grooves into the black shale. 

These grooves are not random. They are recordings. 

The Scribes are the historians of the End-Times. They use their iron-bell heads to listen to the vibrations of the entire basin—the rhythm of the pistons, the frequency of the steam leaks, the distant, slow shifting of the bedrock. They translate these vibrations into physical grooves on the floor. 

Because the slate is infinite, the Scribes have been drawing these lines for millions of years. If one were to drag a copper needle through these grooves—like a stylus on a record player—the flat basin would sing. It would play back the history of every piston stroke, every electric arc, and every shudder of the dying world that occurred centuries ago.

The Scribes do not interact with the other beasts, except when a Pewter-Back crawls too close; then, the Scribe will lift one heavy bronze leg, wait for the crab to pass, and set it down again, resuming its eternal, scratching walk. They are the only creatures that seem to know the sky is a mirror. Sometimes, a Scribe will stop for decades, its iron bell tilted upward toward the oil-slick ceiling, as if reading its own reflection, or waiting for the sky to write back.

***

### The Sky-Skins

High above the grinding gears, in the narrow, pressurized sandwich of air between the true basin and the reflected sky, live the **Telluric Drifters**.

These are the "bladder-like occurrences" seen from below. They are massive, translucent envelopes of tough, greasy skin—resembling the bladder-tissue of some long-extinct deep-sea fish, but reinforced with a web of fine, silver thread. They are filled with hot, volatile hydrogen gas, generated by the chemical reaction of the freezing brine against the rusted iron pillars below.

The Drifters are the plants of the upper air. They have no brains, no hearts, and no sensory organs. But they are beautifully, terribly alive. 

From their undercarriages trail long, hair-thin lines of copper wire, sometimes hundreds of feet long. These wires are not meant for catching prey; they are lightning rods. As the Drifters float silently through the mirrored sky, their wires drag across the tops of the rising iron pillars. 

The friction generates massive static charges. When the electric tension becomes too great, a blue spark, ten times brighter than the sky-mirror, leaps from the pillar up the wire and into the bladder. The Drifter’s skin swells, its silver-veined envelope glowing with a hot, orange light as it digests the electricity.

When a Drifter reaches the end of its life—when its copper wires have been worn away by the pillars, or its skin has been torn by a jet of upside-down boiling grease—it does not fall. 

Instead, it loses its buoyancy and sinks slowly, like a deflating balloon, into the dense, labyrinthine jungle of gears below. Long before it touches the slate, the Pewter-Backs are waiting. They gather beneath the dying giant, their wire legs twitching in anticipation. As soon as the skin touches the iron, they fall upon it, stripping the copper wire for their own batteries and scraping the grease from the bladder-skin, leaving nothing behind but a clean, silver skeleton that is quickly claimed by the rust and the black brine.
