To live in the Sump is to be an extension of its plumbing. The distinction between the inhabitant and the habitat is not merely blurred; it has been entirely dissolved by a century of acidic moisture, galvanic currents, and the slow, heavy settling of metallic dust. 

If you watch the floor of the basin—the dark, shifting silt that accumulates around the base of the half-submerged boiler—you will realize that the ground itself is breathing. 

### The Brass-Gilders

The most common residents are the thousands of pale, multi-jointed isopods that the prompt calls the keepers of the space, but which might be better understood as the basin’s self-assembling maintenance crew. Locally, or in the imagination of anyone who might stumble down here, they are the **Brass-Gilders**.

They are not organic in the soft, vulnerable way of surface beetles. Their carapaces are cold to the touch, heavily impregnated with zinc and iron oxides salvaged from the runoff. They do not have compound eyes; instead, their heads terminate in a pair of blunt, sensitive copper-tipped antennae that vibrate in sympathy with the 60-hertz hum of the turbine. 

The Gilders do not hunt. They graze. Their mandibles are three-part shears of hardened silicate that operate with the tiny, rhythmic *skrit-skrit* of pocketknives. They follow the lines of the lead conduits and iron pipes like trains on a track, scraping away the orange rust-crust and the soft, black grease-mats. As they eat, they polish. Behind every procession of Gilders is a stripe of clean, silver-white metal—a temporary trail of raw steel or bronze that will remain bright for only a few days before the weeping walls dull it once more.

Their lifecycle is entirely mechanical. When a Gilder dies, it does not rot. It grows heavy. The iron in its shell under goes a final, galvanic reaction with the sulfurous water, cementing the creature’s legs to the pipe it was cleaning. Within months, the dead isopod is completely petrified, its hollow shell filled with needle-like calcite crystals, becoming a new lug, a decorative rivet, or a reinforcement sleeve on the very pipe it spent its life scraping.

### The Solder-Bores

Inside the cracked, leaden conduits where the white mineral marrow grows, lives a different class of inhabitant: **The Solder-Bores**. 

These are soft, segmented organisms, long as a man’s forearm but thin as a pencil, with skin the color of wet tallow. They are entirely blind and spineless, moving through the interior of the pipework like living oil. They feed on the calcite mineral needles and the lead linings of the older cables. 

To navigate, they use a highly specialized form of electrolysis. When two Bores meet inside a dark pipe, they do not fight; they press their blunt, copper-colored noses together, sharing a brief, sparkling arc of static electricity that matches their internal salts. They are the reason some of the dead lines in the ceiling still carry a faint, erratic voltage. If you were to cut open one of the lead conduits, you would find dozens of them packed together like cold sausages, their bodies humming softly as they slowly digest the insulation.

### The Boiler-Reef and the Grease-Skins

The central boiler, with its popped rivets and rusted iron plates, is the heart of the Sump’s communal life. Within its dark, flooded interior, where the water is slightly warmer due to some deep, geothermal vent beneath the floor, resides a colony of **Grease-Skins**.

These are the largest living things in the basin. They look less like animals and more like discarded rolls of vulcanized rubber or waterloged leather bags. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no discernible mouths. Instead, they drape themselves over the boiler’s internal grates and flues, their wrinkled, black hides absorbing the cold grease and sulfurous oils that drip from the overhead pipe canopy.

The Grease-Skins are incredibly slow. A young one may take five years to crawl from the lower draft-door of the boiler to the safety of the steam dome. They breed by fission, slowly splitting along their seams like vulcanized coats coming apart at the threads. When they divide, they release a thick, milky fluid that smells of old tallow and kerosene. This fluid floats on the surface of the pools, forming a protective, suffocating skin that prevents the water from evaporating too quickly, keeping the Sump’s air perpetually thick and wet.

Living symbiotically within the skin-folds of these rubbery giants are the **Rivet-Barnacles**. These are tiny, hard-shelled mollusks whose shells are made of laminated iron scales. They cement themselves to the boiler’s outer hull and the backs of the Grease-Skins, where they filter the dense, iron-rich water. By secreting a highly concentrated, acidic glue, they slowly weld themselves to the metal. Over generations, their shells fuse together, forming a jagged, black armor that actually plugs the holes in the boiler, structurally rebuilding the machine in a chaotic, biological design.

### The Cable-Lace (The Memory of the Sump)

Perhaps the most enigmatic lifeform here is the **Cable-Lace**. It is not an animal, nor is it a fungus, though it resembles both. 

It begins in the dark corners where the basalt walls meet the iron girders. It appears as a web of incredibly fine, hair-like silicon threads that have a metallic, copper sheen. These threads grow toward the sources of vibration—specifically the slow, heavy turn of the copper turbine and the wet *click-clack* of the bronze relay.

The Lace doesn't eat in the traditional sense. It is a piezoelectric organism. Every time the turbine groans, the microscopic vibrations squeeze the silicon threads, generating tiny, microscopic pulses of electricity that travel through the web. 

If you watch the Lace during one of the turbine’s rotations, you can see the cold-blue light of the dinoflagellates ripple along the silicon threads, like data moving through an old fiber-optic line. The Lace acts as the nervous system of the entire basin. It connects the pool to the boiler, the boiler to the ceiling pipes, and the pipes back to the walls. 

It is rumored—or felt, by the quietest visitors—that the Lace has a memory. Because it preserves and repeats the electrical patterns of the old machinery, it has kept a record of every rotation the turbine has made for ninety years. It knows the rhythm of the Sump’s breath. If the turbine were to stop, the Lace would slowly starve, its silicon threads growing brittle and glass-clear, before shattering into a fine, gray dust that would join the silt at the bottom of the pools.

Together, these creatures do not merely survive in the ruins of the industrial station; they have adopted it. They are the pistons that have grown skin, the grease that has found a voice, and the wiring that has learned to reproduce. They are the Sump’s quiet, mechanical soul, keeping the old iron heart beating, one wet, metallic scrape at a time.
