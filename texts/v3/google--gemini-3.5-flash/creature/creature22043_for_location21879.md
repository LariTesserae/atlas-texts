In a world where the earth is a deck and the sky is a weeping ceiling of iron, life does not sprout from seed; it precipitates. 

Because there are no true animals and no natural soils, the things that dwell here are anomalous unions of chemistry, abandoned mechanics, and the slow, degenerating drift of ancient maintenance systems. They are the children of the scale and the current.

***

### The Rust-Lice (The Clinker-Drifts)

If you kneel on a wet plate of iron and look closely at the corners where the grease-film gathers against a buckled seam, you will see the smallest of the deck’s inhabitants. 

They are called *clinkers* or *rust-lice*. They are not biological insects, but tiny, flat, octagonal discs of brass-plated steel and lead-solder that have acquired a strange, galvanic animation. They feed on the electrical potential between dissimilar metals—specifically where copper conduits have corroded against the raw iron of the deck. 

They do not have legs; instead, they possess tiny, hair-like copper filaments on their undersides. When a low-voltage pulse ripples through the floor from the distant pump stations, these filaments twitch in unison, sliding the discs forward with a dry, scraping hiss. 

They gather in vast, clicking drifts in the lee of the colossal boilers or inside the hollow flanges of the floor-girders. When the great three-story pistons three miles away slam down, the vibration causes millions of these metal lice to shift at once. The sound of their collective movement is like a handful of fresh pennies thrown across miles of frozen iron. They have no eyes, but they are sensitive to the temperature of the deck; they will drift for days toward the faint, warm grumble of a buried steam pipe, covering it in an orange-brown crust that hums with static.

### The Wet-Harness (The Cable-Snarls)

Growing from the black, flooded grates is the *wet-harness*, which a casual traveler might mistake for simple weeds. These are long, tattered tresses of braided hemp and vulcanized rubber insulation that have been "rented" by a نوع of living, sulfur-consuming mold. 

The harness does not climb toward the light, for there is none. Instead, it grows downward, deep into the black water of the flooded turbine halls below the deck, seeking the warmth of the decaying generator cores. The portion that remains on the surface is a greasy, black tendril that can reach thirty feet in length, floating on the surface of the shallow, iron-rimmed pools like the hair of drowned girls.

The harness is not entirely passive. When the acid rain falls particularly hard, leaching zinc and copper salts from the sky-ceiling, the tresses contract. They pull themselves tight against the iron grates with a wet, rubbery screech. This contraction is a mechanical reflex: the acidic water completes a circuit within the exposed copper wires inside the hemp, causing the fibers to shrink. A field of wet-harness during a heavy sulfur-storm is a forest of tension, thousands of black cords straining and humming in the wind like the rigging of a wooden ship in a gale.

### The Gantry-Walkers

The nearest thing to megafauna in this iron wilderness are the *Gantry-Walkers*. 

They are the distant, degenerate descendants of the autonomous, self-repairing crane systems that once tended the deck during its construction. Having lost their programming cycles thousands of years ago, they have devolved into slow, animalistic scavengers of metal.

A Gantry-Walker is a three-legged tower of open-lattice steel, seventy or eighty feet high, covered in a shaggy hide of rust-moss and dripping grease. Their feet are wide, circular iron pads—resembling massive, upside-down boiler lids—that spread their immense weight so they do not crash through the thin, decaying sections of the deck. 

They move with a geological laziness. A Walker may take an hour to lift a single leg, the rusted joints screaming with a dry, metallic shriek that can be heard ten miles away, before placing it down with a heavy, hollow *clong* that sets the water in the nearby basins dancing in concentric rings.

The Walkers have no heads or faces. In place of a skull, they have a heavy, swinging cluster of block-and-tackle hooks and frayed steel cables that dangle from their central platform. As they walk, these cables drag through the shin-deep water. They "fish" in the open drainage grates, dropping their rusted hooks into the dark below to snag loose pipes, brass valves, or chunks of lead solder. When they catch something, they hoist it up into a central, low-temperature catalytic hopper beneath their main carriage. There, the metal is slowly dissolved in a bath of warm grease and sulfuric acid, which the Walker uses to lubricate its joints and slowly—over decades—extrude new steel plates to patch its own crumbling frame.

They do not notice humans, just as they do not notice the boilers. To them, anything that does not vibrate with a heavy alternating current is simply part of the inert geology of the deck.

### The Keepers of the Gland

In the deep, black interior of the pump-cathedrals, where the heat from the steam boilers is enough to keep the water from freezing, there are those who were once human. 

They are called the *Keepers*, or the *Solder-Skins*. 

They are small, thin, and have long since lost the need for eyes; their eye-sockets are covered with smooth, grey scar tissue, and they navigate entirely by the vibration of the deck through the soles of their bare, leathery feet. Their skin is the color of lard and has a greasy, hydrophobic sheen that repels the constant, sulfurous drip of the ceilings.

They do not wear clothes, but rather "gaskets"—strips of wet canvas and soft lead-wire wrapped tightly around their joints to protect them from the blistering steam leaks that hiss from every wall. 

The Keepers cannot speak. The three-story pistons make a noise so total and devastating that any voice would be obliterated. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of percussion. They carry heavy, double-faced brass hammers on their hips. By striking the structural iron pillars of the pump halls in specific cadences, they can send messages that travel through the metal floor for miles. A Keeper standing in a pump hall in the north can tell his kin five miles south of a pressure drop in a condenser tube by three sharp taps on a high-pressure line, followed by a long, scraping drag of his hammer.

They survive on a diet that would kill anything else. They do not eat plants or hunt; instead, they scrape *tallow-yeast*—a thick, white, nutrient-rich grease—from the seals of the high-pressure water pumps. This grease, originally a synthetic lubricant designed to withstand the heat of the pistons, has been colonized by an anaerobic, petroleum-eating bacterium. It is rich in heavy hydrocarbons and smells of hot oil and old lard. The Keepers scoop it out of the drip-pans with their spatulate fingers and eat it raw.

Their entire existence is bound to the survival of the pumps. They do not understand the machines they live inside; they do not know what the water is for, or where it goes when it is sucked down into the dark. But they know that if the Great Piston stops, the deck beneath their feet will cold-shrink, the water will rise to their knees, and the dark air will turn to solid, sulfurous ice. 

So they tend the valves. When a joint leaks, they crawl into the whistling steam-blasts with rolls of lead wire and hammers, beating the metal until the leak is choked. They are the white blood cells of a colossal, dying body, keeping the heart of the deck beating long after the brain has died.
