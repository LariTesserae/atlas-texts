Deep within the three-inch seams, nestled in the jade-colored veins of the thread-like moss, live the **Laths**. 

They are the primary inhabitants of the cracks, so small and so perfectly matched to their environment that a casual observer would mistake them for chips of dried mortar. A Lath is flat, hexagonal, and dry. Its body is composed of a chalky, layered calcite shell, white-grey and hard as tile, with no distinct head or tail. It behaves less like an animal and more like a self-animating mineral flake. 

The Laths remain entirely motionless for the hot, dry duration of the day, resembling a crust of salt on the moss-cushions. But when the midnight dew begins to flow down the seams, they wake. Using dozens of microscopic, hair-thin pins of stiff silica that project from their undersides, they begin to slide along the vertical plaster walls. They do not walk so much as glide on the film of water, grazing on the moss and scraping the curing lime with tiny, rasping mouths made of industrial-grade quartz. 

Their digestion is an alkaline furnace. They extract calcium from the plaster and nitrogen from the atmospheric dew, and in doing so, they perform a slow, transformative chemistry. The waste they exude is not organic; it is a dry, white, crystalline grit—calcium carbonate in its purest, most abrasive form. As they creep through the miles of dark seams, they leave behind tiny drifts of this white powder. The Laths are the authors of the world’s future soil; they are the slow grinding-teeth of the plain, turning the monumental plaster back into dust, one grain at a time.

***

Farther down, where the seams open into the great fifty-mile-wide basins, a different kind of life responds to the daily cycle of water. 

During the night, as the cold runoff pours down the thirty-foot terrace steps, it carries with it **The Lachrymæ**—or the Glass-Beads. They are things of pure surface tension. They look like spheres of thick, oil-slicked water, ranging from the size of a berry to the size of a human skull, rolling silently down the dry plaster slopes. 

The Lachrymæ are not carbon-based. They are packets of complex, self-organizing lipids and hydrophobic proteins that have condensed out of the heavy, high-pressure air. They survive by riding the interface between the water and the plaster. In the dry heat of the afternoon, they are invisible, dissolved into the thick, humid atmosphere as vapor. But when the temperature drops at dusk, they precipitate directly onto the high-altitude terraces, gathering mass as they roll.

They are scavengers of the sky. As they roll, they sweep the pristine plaster clean of the microscopic chemical fallouts of the atmosphere—dust of iron, sulfur, and nitrogen that drifts in from the outer horizon. When they reach the shallow basin lakes at dawn, they float on the mirror-like surface like schools of transparent, shivering eyes. 

As the noon-day sun begins to boil the lakes, the Lachrymæ undergo a collective crisis. The heat increases their internal pressure until, one by one, they do not pop, but gently evaporate from the inside out, releasing their gathered minerals back into the shallow water. This mineral-rich broth is what fuels the mid-day bloom of the basins.

***

This bloom consists of the **Silica-Wells**. 

They do not exist in the morning, and by evening they are gone. When the basin lakes reach their maximum depth of twelve inches at mid-morning, the sudden concentration of minerals and intense, vertical sunlight triggers their germination. From the microscopic pores in the terracotta cement beneath the water, millions of stiff, golden-orange needles shoot upward.

Within an hour, these needles branch into flat, plate-like structures of hydrated silica and iron oxide, looking like sheets of frosted, copper-colored stained glass floating just beneath the water's surface. They are incredibly brittle, thin as a soap bubble. They use the intense heat of the shallow water to catalyze the curing of their own silica skeletons, absorbing the solar energy with absolute efficiency. For two hours, the fifty-mile basins cease to be mirrors; they become vast, glowing carpets of orange and copper, humming with the low vibration of rapidly crystalline growth.

Then comes the afternoon downpour. 

The first heavy, hot raindrops of the three o'clock storm strike the basins. The impact is catastrophic for the Silica-Wells. The fragile glass structures shatter instantly under the weight of the rain. The sound is magnificent: a sharp, metallic, miles-wide chiming, like the breaking of a billion tiny chandeliers, carried across the flat expanse on the rushing wind. 

By the time the storm passes and the water begins to drain back into the seams, the golden plates have been reduced to a fine, sparkling silt. This silt is washed down into the lower terraces, where the moss-roots and the Laths await it, devouring the silica to build their own shells and capillary veins.

***

Yet, the grandest and most mysterious dwellers of this terrace-world are the ones who made it—or, perhaps, are the only things capable of maintaining its terrifying, flat perfection. 

These are **The Slicks**. 

They are rare, solitary, and monumental. To look upon one is to see a slab of charcoal-grey, wet soapstone, perhaps eighty yards long, thirty yards wide, and only two inches thick, lying perfectly flush upon the pale plaster plain. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no warmth. They are so flat that from a distance of a quarter-mile, they disappear entirely into the horizon line.

The Slicks are driven by a simple, thermal-hydraulic engine. Their top surfaces are dark and highly absorbent, reaching blistering temperatures under the noon sun, while their undersides are insulated and pale. During the night, dew gathers thickly under their vast bodies, trapped between the stone-like belly of the Slick and the non-porous plaster floor. 

At noon, as the sun beats down, this trapped water is superheated into pressurized steam. Supported by this cushion of high-pressure vapor, the fifty-ton Slick becomes virtually weightless. It begins to slide. 

It does not slide fast—perhaps a few feet an hour—moving blindly across the continental tiles, pushed only by the slight gradient of the terraces or the pressure of the hot wind. As it glides on its cushion of steam, its ultra-dense, perfectly flat belly acts as a colossal, living trowel. It grinds down the microscopic heaves of the curing lime; it presses any rising salt-crystals back into the seams; it seals the tiny fissures that the heat tries to pry open. 

Behind the path of a sliding Slick, the plaster is not merely smooth; it is polished to a high, pearlescent sheen that reflects the violet sky like a sheet of ice. They are the curators of the plain. They are silent, save for the deep, low-frequency hiss of the steam escaping from beneath their edges—a sound like the breathing of the world itself, keeping the vast masonry young, raw, and waiting.
