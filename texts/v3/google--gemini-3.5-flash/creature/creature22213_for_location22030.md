The water here does not smell of salt or rot; it smells of wet lime, cold lard, and the sharp, clean scent of shaved iron. This is a fat, heavy sea, grease-slicked and thick with the cream-white oil of its own inhabitants. 

If you were to drop into this dim twilight, the first thing to touch you would not be a fish, but the *grease-seed*. 

These are the tiny, shell-less bivalves that line the seams of the basalt blocks. They are small as thumb-nails, soft as peeled plums, and colored the dull, pearlescent white of a lard-bucket. They do not have shells because they do not need armor; they live inside the very teeth of the giant stone joints. They wedge themselves by the billions into the yawning cracks where the three-hundred-foot walls meet the plaza floor. 

To survive here, they have become the lubricant of the world. 

Their bodies are rich in a thick, waxy tallow that they secrete continuously from their skirts. When the great thermal currents rise and the five-thousand-ton stone blocks begin to slide, the grease-seed are crushed by the millions. But they do not die out; they breed with a frantic, silent velocity, their gelatinous eggs drifting through the crevices like porridge. When a block shifts, the weight does not grind the stone to powder because it slides upon a self-renewing carpet of this cold, white lard. The ancient, mortarless architecture does not wear itself out because it is greased by the bodies of its own tenants. The deep, infrasonic hum that shudders through the sea is the sound of miles of stone sliding on a cushion of crushed, sweetish fat.

***

In the empty spaces between the giant walls, where the water is sixty fathoms deep and totally dark but for the grey slate-light from above, the *vane-skins* drift. 

They are the "gliders," but they resemble no bird or fish. They are three fathoms wide and several inches thick, like heavy, wet blankets made of white linen. If you were to touch one, it would feel like cold, soap-washed leather. They have no bones, no skull, and no heart; their pale, thin blood is pumped through their ribbed bodies by the simple, rhythmic flexing of their skirts as they ride the currents. 

The vane-skins are hollow. They are double-walled, like sleeping bags, and the icy, silt-laden water flows continuously through their interiors. Within these hollow chambers live thousands of microscopic, blind commensals—tiny, legless white mites that clean the inner lining of the vane-skin, eating the silt that the large beast inhales. 

When the silent thermal plumes sweep across the plazas, the vane-skins do not fight them. They fold themselves. They roll into tight, pale cylinders, like rugs dropped from a cart, and let the current carry them five miles at a run. When they hit a wall, they do not shatter; they simply splat, flat and harmless, against the basalt, their rubbery tissues absorbing the blow before they slowly peel themselves free and unravel once more into the grey gloom.

***

Far below, in the deep, stepped wells where the block-edges form immense, square staircases that descend into the heart of the plateau, live the *piston-guts*. 

These are the things that crawl. They are the size of horses, but they have the shape of eyeless, white grubs. Their skin is thick, scarred, and rubbery—so tough that a iron spike would struggle to pierce it. They do not have legs, but rather thousands of tiny, cup-shaped suction pores that cover their entire underbelly. They use these to seal themselves to the slick basalt walls, moving with a heavy, sucking *thup-thup-thup* that can be felt in the water from a mile away.

The piston-guts are the vacuum cleaners of the masonry. Their mouths are wide, soft rings of white muscle, containing no teeth but instead a series of rotating, horn-like scrapers. They press these mouths flat against the stone and crawl forward, scraping away the micro-fine silt and the small, dead grease-seed that have washed out of the joints. 

When a stone block begins to shift—when a wall three miles long begins to lean its ancient bulk into a plaza—the piston-guts do not run. They cannot. Instead, they find the nearest vertical seam and crawl *inside* it. Their bodies are hydrostatic; they contain no solid parts, only thick, oily muscle and pockets of compressible water. They allow themselves to be squeezed flat by the settling stone, deforming into white ribbons three inches thin, only to slide out of the other side of the joint once the movement is done, totally unharmed, their leathery hides glistening with the grease of the bivalves they shared the crack with.

***

And then there are the *cables*.

They are the longest things alive in the perched sea, and the most silent. They look like pale, fibrous ropes, some no thicker than a man’s wrist, others as thick as a tree trunk. They stretch from the tops of the three-hundred-foot walls down to the plaza floors, anchored at both ends by massive, root-like discs of calcified flesh that have grown directly into the stone.

The cables are not plants. They are colonial siphonophores—vast, single-strand chains of specialized animals that have joined together to form a living cord. They are held taut by the constant, heavy drift of the deep currents. When the water moves quickly, the cables do not snap; they sing. They vibrate with a high, thin whistle that is the only true sound in the twilight, apart from the deep groan of the stone.

They are the hunters of the mid-water. Along their entire miles-long length hang millions of tiny, transparent threads, invisible in the grey haze. These threads are sticky with a cold, paralyzing slime. When a drifting vane-skin or a cloud of grease-seed eggs hits a cable’s threads, they are stuck. Slowly, over the course of days, the cable contracts, its muscular rings shortening toward its anchor-points, dragging the catch down to the masonry where the piston-guts and the joint-grubs wait to lick the remnants from the stone.

***

In this world, nothing is wasted, because there is nothing to waste. There are no seasons here. There is no storm that can reach five miles high to disturb the heavy, mineral basin. The pale things in the water do not know what the sun is, though they live under its constant, dim slate-glow. 

They do not know that the massive basalt plateau they crawl upon was built by hands that had fingers, or that the walls once formed the streets of something that had a name. To them, the stone is not a ruin; it is simply the ground. It is the cold, sliding bone of the world, and they are the fat that keeps it moving.
