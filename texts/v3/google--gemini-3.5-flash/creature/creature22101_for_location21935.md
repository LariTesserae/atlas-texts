The occupant of this place is not a visitor from the world above, nor a creature that burrowed its way down from the soil. It is a native of the vacuum, a mobile fragment of the bulb itself that has achieved a fleeting, frantic sentience. 

They are the **Pith-Keepers**—though they have no name for themselves—and to see one is to mistake it first for a dead shoot or a peeled, fibrous section of the wall.

### Anatomy of the Pith-Keepers
A Keeper’s body is made of the same starch-rich, white cambium as the terrace flats, but it has been refined into a light, jointless armature. They stand roughly four feet tall, though their height is difficult to gauge because they rarely stand upright. 

Their skin is not skin at all, but a matte, pale integument like the shaved root of a parsnip. It is cool and dry to the touch, with a fine, velvety texture that smells faintly of freshly turned malt. Beneath this pale rind, there are no blood vessels or red meat. Instead, their circulation is visible as a network of hairline capillaries carrying a pressurized, emerald-green sap. When they are excited or when the room’s sap-tides peak, these green vessels throb beneath their throats and along the lengths of their long, three-jointed limbs.

They have no noses, nor do they have ears in the traditional sense; indeed, there is no need for them in a chamber already saturated with the sweet smell of fermentation and the roaring bass of the plant’s internal engines. They sense the thrumming of the walls through the pads of their feet, which are broad and flat, lined with thousands of microscopic, sticky root-hairs that can grip the glossy surface of the giant grass-columns with absolute security.

Their heads are smooth, eyeless domes, save for two massive, fixed lenses of hardened, golden-amber sap set into the sides of their temples. These "eyes" do not move in sockets. Instead, they are deep, liquid-filled wells that gather the bright, piped-in daylight. Because of the room’s distorted gravity, a Keeper does not navigate by stepping. They navigate by *looking*. When a Keeper fixes its amber gaze on a distant shelf of celery-wood, the low-density vacuum of the room tilts for them. They release their grip on the wall and fall horizontally toward their destination, descending through the green air with a slow, floating grace, their long limbs trailing behind them like drifting roots.

### The Threat of the Sweet-Cure
For all their grace, the Keepers live in constant, quiet peril from the very environment that feeds them. 

The walls of the geode weep a constant, pressurized mist of fructose-rich sap. While the Keepers drink this fluid through a soft, fibrous mouth-pore on the underside of their chin, the sap also settles on their bodies. If a Keeper remains still for too long, the air’s warmth evaporates the moisture from the sap, transforming it into a hard, glassy varnish. 

This is the "Sweet-Cure." A Keeper who falls asleep on a terrace may wake to find their joints frozen solid by crystallized sugar. Within days, the rapid growth of the cambium walls will swell over them, absorbing them back into the plant’s structural tissue. Throughout the chamber, one can see half-submerged mounds—distorted, white figures frozen in mid-leap, now entirely encased in transparent green cellulose, their amber eyes still glowing faintly beneath the bark like trapped insects.

To prevent this, the Keepers are in a state of perpetual grooming. This is why they snare the floating marbles of condensation. Using long, hollow straws made of dried grass-stems, they catch the drifting water globes and use the pure, unsweetened moisture to wash the sticky fructose from their joints. 

### The Interventions and the Tally
The crude thorn-ladder and the hemp ropes are the tools of their singular, desperate chore. 

The Keepers are the pruners of the geode. The high-pressure whistling vents in the ceiling—the nodes that release excess gas—often become clogged with rapidly growing green tissue. If these vents are sealed by the plant's aggressive growth, the vacuum chamber will over-pressurize, collapsing the pocket and crushing everything within it into a solid mass of wood.

Using their thorn-ladders as anchors against the shifting gravity, the Keepers climb to these screaming, whistling nodes. With their fingers—which end in hard, chisel-like splinters of fire-treated wood—they scrape away the encroaching green tissue, keeping the airway clear.

The tally marks carved into the soft, white pith are their calendar of mortality. The Keepers know, in whatever dim way they think, that their home is a temporary womb. They are counting the "sap-sighs"—the massive, rhythmic pressure drops that occur when the forest miles above enters its winter dormancy. 

With each winter, the chamber grows smaller. The columns of green basalt-grass thicken; the terraces swell closer together. The Keepers carve their lines to mark how much "breath" the room has left. 

### The Lifespan of the Vacuum
When the last tally-mark is carved and the wall-terrace finally swells to touch the ceiling, the vacuum will vanish. The Keepers do not flee. They cannot; there is nowhere for them to go. 

As the chamber closes, they wedge themselves into the hollows of the grass-stems one last time, locking their limbs and closing their amber eyes. They allow the warm, green sap to fill their lungs. They harden, crystallize, and become the very wood that the next generation of subterranean explorers will crawl through, miles beneath the black humus of the forest floor, never knowing they are climbing through the bones of those who kept the light alive.
