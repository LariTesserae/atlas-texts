### The Sump-Born

To live in Node 12 is to lose the distinction between the skin that contains you and the grease that coats you. The humans who dwell here—referring to themselves simply as the **Sappers**, or more formally as **The Tapped**—have become, over generations, as pale and slick as the roots they harvest. 

They are people of low, crouched postures. Because the shanties are lashed directly to the vertical, curving bark of the Stems, there is very little level ground; the Sappers have wide, spade-like feet with unusually long, prehensile toes that have grown thick-nailed and calloused from gripping iron rungs and wet rope. They go barefoot, their soles blackened and cured by the fossilized heartwood until they are as tough as the vulcanized latex they manufacture. 

Their skin is a striking, luminescent waxen-grey, the result of a life spent under the cold, blue-green flicker of crushed-tunicate lanterns. They do not sweat salty water; their pores secrete a thick, sebum-like grease that smells faintly of boiled starch and mutton tallow. This grease is their primary defense against the environment: it prevents the warm, chemical-laden mist of the "breaths" from scalding their skin, and it keeps the legions of biting midges from finding purchase. 

They do not wear cloth. Fabric rots within weeks in the hot, yeasty breath of the Sump. Instead, their garments are made of **Stem-hide**—thick, flexible sheets of sap-latex that have been cured with sulfur and smoked over grease-fires. These suits are heavy, clicking and groaning with every movement like wet leather, and are held together with brass rivets and copper wire. 

Because of the ceaseless, industrial roar of the steam-valves and the scraping of the millions of bronze beetles, the Sappers have modified their speech. They do not talk with their throats except in the quiet of their sealed "hush-boxes." Across the scaffolds, they communicate in **The Clack**: a complex, percussive sign-language performed by striking brass-tipped thimbles against the iron pipes, supplemented by sharp, whistle-like pops made by clicking their teeth. A master Sapper can transmit a complex engineering report across a sixty-yard gulf of open space with a series of rhythmic taps that sound, to an outsider, like nothing more than a leaking steam-line.

### The Castes of the Stems

Life in Node 12 is stratified by height, with each of the three Stems housing a slightly different class of worker, specialized for the constant maintenance of their specific section of the machine.

#### The High-Crowns (The Deaf)
Living on the highest platforms, sixty yards up where the Stems join the living ceiling of the vault, are the Valve-Keepers. They are the aristocrats of the Sump, though they are also the most physically damaged. They live in constant proximity to the main relief vents, where the pressure screams at a pitch that can liquefy human organs. 

To work the High-Crown, one must be deaf. Most are born that way; those who are not have their eardrums systematically punctured in childhood with a polished copper needle. They are highly respected, moving through the high, shaking iron cobwebs with the grace of spiders. They communicate through **osteophonics**—pressing their foreheads or their long, brass-handled wrenches against the iron pipes to "hear" the vibration of the steam through the bones of their skulls. They can tell, by the quality of a hum in their jawbone, if a valve three levels down is beginning to scale over with mineral crust.

#### The Mid-Stem Coopers
The middle sections of the columns are the domain of the families who manage the fractionators. These are the most stable platforms, where the shanties are larger and the air is slightly cooler. Here live the distillers, the vulcanizers, and the coopers who build the great, stave-sided tuns used to hold the raw sap-spirits. 

The Mid-Stemmers are the social heart of Node 12. They are characterized by their "yeast-bloated" bellies—a consequence of their diet, which consists largely of a sour, fermented mash made from the starch-heavy scrapings of the inner bark, mixed with beetle-broth. They are heavily scarred with yellow-brown chemical burns from the raw latex, which they wear as badges of honor.

#### The Floor-Gleaners (The Low-Sinks)
At the bottom of the world, where the colossal columns plunge into the dark, polished heartwood floor, live the Low-Sinks. They do not have shanties; they live in suspended wicker baskets that drum against the wood when the wind from the vents rises. 

Highly migratory, they are the scavengers and hunters of the Sump. They are thin, incredibly agile, and go about their work with long, grease-smeared wooden poles tipped with iron hooks. It is their job to harvest the dead beetles from the gears of the mid-stem boilers, scrape the glowing tunicates from the lowest dark-flutes, and recover any tools or bodies that fall from the heights. They are the only Sappers who regularly traverse the open, flat floor, navigating the shifting carpet of bronze beetles with stilt-like wooden sandals called *clatters*.

### The Domesticated Wild

Humanity has not tamed the fauna of Node 12; instead, they have slipped into its cycles like a bolt into a threaded nut. 

#### The Boiler-Ticks
The largest animal in the Sump is the **Boiler-Tick**, a blind, flat arachnid the size of an oxen. It is slow-moving, its grey, leathery carapace utterly impervious to the scalding sap-steam. The Sappers have learned to harness these creatures by drilling small brass rings directly into the non-feeling margins of their shell-shields. 

The Ticks are used as living pack-mules. They are fed on the waste-yeast from the distilleries, which they consume through a sucking proboscis. Because they are naturally drawn to heat, they can be guided up the vertical bark of the Stems by hanging small kerosene lanterns just in front of their blind heads. A single Boiler-Tick can carry four barrels of refined sugar-spirit up a sixty-degree slope without slipping, its eight double-clawed legs finding purchase in the deepest fissures of the bark.

#### The Tear-Moths
In the dark, small corners of the shanties, the Sappers keep **Tear-Moths**. These are delicate, grey insects with soft, feathery antennae and a wingspan of about four inches. They do not eat solid food; they survive by drinking the grease-sweat and tears from the eyes of sleeping humans. 

This relationship is not parasitic, but highly prized. The Sump is rich in fungal spores that cause a disease known as **Bark-Eye**, which leads to painful swelling and blindness. The saliva of the Tear-Moth contains a powerful, cooling antibiotic that prevents this infection. A Sapper will go to sleep with several of these moths tethered to their collar-pegs by thin silk threads, allowing the insects to flutter over their face throughout the night, licking their eyelids clean.

### The Customs of the Sump

Because there is no sun, there is no day or night. Time is measured by **The Breaths**—the rhythmic pressure-drops of the root-system, which occur roughly every eight hours. 

#### The Hush-Hour
When the steam-whistles scream their three-minute warning, signaling an impending breath, all industrial activity ceases instantly. The engines are chained down. The lamps are extinguished, as the incoming sugar-mists are highly explosive when atomized.

The Sappers retreat into their **Hush-Boxes**—tight, cedar-planked boxes built inside the shanties, insulated with three inches of felted moss and sealed with grease-soaked rags. Inside these boxes, families lie packed together like spoons in the dark. For forty minutes, they do not speak or move. They listen to the Sump-of-Stems go wild outside. 

Through the wooden walls, they feel the deep, groaning vibration of the Stem’s chest-cavity as the valves release. They hear the terrifying, metallic roar of the steam, and then the sound of the **Swarm-Rush**—the clicking of millions of bronze beetles as they strike the shanties like a storm of gravel, scrambling to lick the condensed syrup from the walls. 

To the Sappers, the Hush-Hour is a sacred, terrifying peace. It is the only time they are quiet, and the only time they are touching.

#### The Graft
In Node 12, there is no waste. Indeed, there is no space for graves. 

When a Sapper dies—whether from an engine-blast, a fall into the dark void of the floor, or the slow drowning of "Wet-Lung" (the chronic mold infection of the chest)—their body is not burned or cast away. It is returned to the machine.

The family of the deceased will choose a **Fold**—a silent, non-producing crevice in the living bark of their home Stem, away from the active sap-veins. Using heavy copper chisels, the Coopers carve a deep, human-sized notch into the living tissue. The body is placed inside, stripped of its brass and latex garments, and packed with raw, unrefined yeast-paste and fresh wood-shavings.

The notch is then sealed with a thick smear of warm, vulcanized latex. Over the next several weeks, the living Stem, stimulated by the yeast and the decaying organic matter, grows around the cavity. The bark swells, rolls over the seams, and heals, absorbing the body into its massive, subterranean structure.

Within a year, the spot is indistinguishable from the rest of the column, save for a slight, warm bulge in the grain. A Sapper will often tap their wrench against the spot where their grandfather was grafted, feeling the vibration of the steam through his calcified bones, and say, in the silent language of the Clack: *He is boiling well.*
