To live in the Chamber of the Bast is to be made of the same stuff as the floor and the ceiling, to be an extension of the great, parched vegetative engine that heats the air. Here, life does not carry its own moisture; it has discarded it entirely. The creatures of this place are not wet vessels of salt and skin, but dry, clicking clockworks of horn, pith, resin, and tanned fiber.

### The Curn: The People of the Rasp

The primary intelligences of the Chamber are the **Curn**, though they do not refer to themselves by a spoken name, for they do not speak with voices of air. 

At first glance, a Curn might be mistaken for an upright, slender bundle of weathered firewood or a sculpture of polished knotwood. Their bodies are built from segmentations of dense, iron-hard cellulose and dried chitin, layered like shingles on a roof. These plates—the color of toasted wheat and old nutmeg—slide over one another on joints lubricated by a natural, waxy tallow they secrete from glands behind their shoulders. 

They have no wet eyes and no soft flesh. Where a face would be, they have a flat, shield-like plate of polished lignite, set with two dark, glassy lenses of fossilized amber. They do not look around; they tilt their entire neckless heads with dry, ratcheting clicks. 

* **The Sensation of Sound:** Because the Chamber is an unending, deafening roar of grinding burls and vibrating cicadas, the Curn have no ears. To have ears here would be to go instantly mad. Instead, they pierce the ground with long, hollow spurs that grow from their heel-joints. These "taps" anchor them to the root-bedrock, allowing them to read the shifting vibrations of the world below. They do not "hear" a voice; they feel the specific, rhythmic thrum of another Curn tapping a message against a dry root a mile away.
* **The Language of Scratches:** Their history is not oral, nor is it written in ink. It is *grooved*. In the abandoned, silver-gray burls that have ceased to grow, the Curn use serrated obsidian-hard seed claws to carve deep, spiral tracks into the wood. A Curn traveler passing through an empty chamber will kneel, insert a long, needle-fine finger-claw into the groove, and walk in a circle. The vibration running up their arm and into their chest-plate translates the carver’s thoughts: a record of which seed-rivers are currently flowing, where the wood-lice are migrating, and which roots are about to burst into violent growth.
* **The Starch-Eaters:** They do not eat flesh, nor do they drink. Instead, they carry small, pear-shaped gourds filled with a fine, yellow meal they call *pith-flour*, obtained by grinding the soft interior of dead giant lianas. They mix this dry starch with drops of liquefying terpene-oil harvested from the glowing bladders of the ceiling. When ingested, this mixture undergoes a slow, warm, solid-state combustion in their core-chambers, providing them with the heat and kinetic energy needed to drive their dry muscles.

### The Seed-Herders and Their Steeds

In the shifting valleys of frass, where the ground behaves like dry, boiling sand, the Curn do not walk. They ride.

* **The Tumble-Drags:** These are colossal, hemispherical beetles, twelve feet across, whose backs resemble inverted bowls of peeling, fibrous bark. They have no eyes; their heads are massive, spade-like wedges of solid horn used to tunnel through the seed-dust. They do not have legs in the traditional sense, but dozens of short, stubby, brush-like limbs that move in a continuous, caterpillar-like ripple. The Curn attach light harnesses of woven fiber to the Tumble-Drags, using them to drag great, hollowed-pith cargo-sleds across the sliding seed-rivers.
* **The Weave-Cats:** Not felines, but long, multi-jointed predators of the vertical liana-walls. Their bodies are made of a dry, flexible stuff like seasoned cane, and their eight limbs end in curved, needle-sharp hooks. They move through the vertical forests of the ceiling with a terrifying, whispering speed, their bodies rattling like dry basketwork. The Curn have learned to domesticate them by offering them the oily, high-energy fat extracted from the borer-worm's tail-segments. A domestic Weave-Cat is a prized companion, used to climb into the high, unstable lattice to harvest lighting-bladders or to scout for oncoming "cracking-cycles" in the wood.

### The Drift-Fauna of the High Warmth

In the upper reaches of the rift-valley, where the air is hottest and the glow-bladders hang like orange lanterns, a different kind of life exists—one that never touches the ground.

* **The Ashen-Gliders:** These are paper-thin, kite-like creatures, forty feet across, that resemble dried palm fronds. They have no muscles for flapping; instead, they are held aloft by the powerful thermal updrafts rising from the deep, hot pneumatic tubes of the plants. Their bodies are so light and porous that they are almost weightless. They drift lazily through the amber gloom, their trailing, thread-like sensory tendrils brushing the tops of the lianas to catch the spores that drift upward. If one dies, it does not rot; it dries further, becoming so brittle that the heat of the air eventually causes it to crumble into a fine, gray ash that joins the dust of the floor.
* **The Tinder-Spiders:** Tiny, social spinners that do not weave webs of silk, but of dry, sticky yellow latex fibers. They spin these webs over the gaps between rubbing branches. When the branches grind together, the friction creates sparks. The Tinder-Spiders are immune to the heat; in fact, they feed on the charred dust of the wood that is singed by these friction-fires. Their webs act as spark-catchers, glowing with a Brief, burning red line of embers when a branch shifts, which the spiders use to warn one another of the mechanical movements of the forest.

### The Predators of the Deep Cork

Where there is growth, there is decay, and where there is decay, there are those who hunt the decay-eaters.

* **The Tinder-Stalker:** The undisputed apex predator of the Chamber floor. It is a creature of carbonized, black wood, looking like an animal carved from charcoal and then brought to life. It is twelve feet long, with six spindly legs that end in silent, fibrous pads. It has no mouth; instead, its head terminates in a long, hollow spike of ironwood containing a rapidly spinning, drill-like tongue. The Tinder-Stalker hunts by sensing the heat of the Curn’s internal starch-stoves or the frantic vibrations of borer-worms. Once it pinpoints its prey, it drills directly through their outer chitin-armor or bark-hide to inject a caustic, terpene-dissolving acid that liquefies the internal starches, which it then pumps out through its hollow snout.
* **The Sheer-Wasps:** Flying terrors with wings made of transparent, glass-like sheets of dried resin. They do not buzz; they hum with a dry, whistling tone that sounds like a spinning saw blade. Their jaws are two semi-circular shears of hardened chitin that can snap through a liana the thickness of a man's thigh in a single bite. They hunt the Sieve-Moths in mid-air, cutting their wings off and letting them tumble to the frass-floor below, where the wasp's wingless ground-larvae wait to devour the fallen carcasses.

### The Whispering Nomads

Because the very ground beneath them is alive and expanding with explosive force, the Curn have no words for "permanence," "monument," or "home." 

If you were to stay with a band of Curn, you would find yourself in a state of constant, rhythmic preparation. They spend their days listening to the roots. When the heel-taps of the elders register a specific, low-frequency hum—the sound of an impending *Thrust*—the entire village rises without a sound. Within minutes, their leather bags are packed with pith-flour, their spore-lamps are extinguished, and their Weave-Cats are harnessed.

They walk with a strange, high-stepping gait, lifting their feet clear of the shifting frass to avoid making vibration-noise that might attract a Tinder-Stalker. They climb the liana-bridges they constructed generations ago, leaving behind the hollowed-out burl that was their home for the last three months. 

Behind them, a sound like a cannon shot rips through the dark. A new, white trunk, fifty feet thick, bursts through the center of their old village, crushing their bark-shelters and shattering their sound-deflectors into kindling.

The Curn do not look back. Up ahead, in the amber glow of a new cluster of oil-bladders, a dead burl of silver-gray cork waits for them. They will unpack their scraper-tools, strike a spark of friction-fire to light their new hearths, and begin to rasp.
