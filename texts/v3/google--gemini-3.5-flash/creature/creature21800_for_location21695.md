To understand the life that occupies the plateau of Olaus, one must first discard the biological tempo of the valleys below. In this high, cold gridlock of water and stone, there is no place for the frantic, warm-blooded cycle of hunting and fleeing. The creatures here do not run; they do not cry out; they do not build. They are the children of the seepage and the basalt, as slow as the frost and as patient as the erosion that shaped their home.

The scavengers that drift through the high sky—those shapes like scorched paper—are the *velamens*. They are almost entirely without weight. A velamen is a wide, flat pane of dry, black leather, five meters across but thinner than a cedar shingle. They have no bones, no feathers, and no muscles for flapping; indeed, they cannot fly in the traditional sense. Instead, they are passive gliders, living kites that spend centuries suspended on the rising plumes of warm, damp air that boil out of the slot canyons in the afternoon. Their undersides are a pale, chalky white, designed to blend with the low clouds, while their dark backs absorb the hard sunlight, warming the gas-filled chambers within their thin tissues to keep them buoyant. They have no eyes. In place of a face, the forward edge of a velamen is a row of small, cup-like pits that sense the temperature of the air currents and the faint, chemical signature of decay. When one dies, it does not fall so much as it drifts downward like a wet leaf, settling into a canyon where it is slowly reclaimed by the wetness.

Below them, on the weeping vertical faces of the basalt towers, lives the most common resident of the plateau: the *slicks*. 

If you were to watch a single wet wall of dark stone for an hour, you might notice that certain "water droplets" do not run straight down. Instead, they glide sideways, or even climb upward against the gravity of the weep. These are the slicks—living discs of clear, dense gel, about the size of a human palm. They are water that has learned to hold its own shape. They possess no organs that are visible to the eye; they are almost perfectly transparent, recognizable only by the way they refract the dark basalt behind them, turning the grey stone into a shifting lens. 

The slicks survive by grazing upon the dried-blood and sulfur-yellow lichens. They move by capillary action, adjusting the surface tension of their outer membranes to pull themselves across the wet rock. When a slick finds a patch of lichen, it halts. It secretes a mild, alkaline acid that softens the tough crustose film into a cloudy broth, which it then absorbs directly through its skin. The process is incredibly slow; a slick may take three weeks to clear a circle of rock no larger than a coin. They have no social structure, but when two slicks meet on a wet wall, they do not fight; they simply merge into a single, larger disc for several days, exchanging genetic material through simple diffusion, before splitting once more into two smaller, distinct entities that slide away in opposite directions.

Deep in the slot canyons, where the water pools in the level-floored fens, the life is heavier, more lithic. 

Buried in the grey-gold silt of the plazas are the *silt-bottles*. These are long, blind cylinders of grey, porous skin that look exactly like the water-softened stones among which they lie. They are the engineers of the plateau’s immaculate cleanliness. A silt-bottle is essentially a living siphon. It anchors itself to the basalt floor with root-like threads of pure silica, and through a small, circular valve at its front end, it slowly draws in the muddy water of the fens. Inside its body, the water is filtered through a complex labyrinth of charcoal-like chambers that trap microscopic algae, mineral salts, and the tiny, drifting spores of the mosses. The clean, filtered water is then expelled through a second valve at its rear. 

Because they move only when the water level changes—dragging themselves milliliter by milliliter through the mud by inflating and deflating their segmented bodies—they are often mistaken for permanent geologic features. Thick mats of liver-colored moss grow upon their backs, and the slate-grey flatworms crawl over them without ever realizing they are resting on living flesh. The *tink-plip* of the drops falling from the ledges above is the only world the silt-bottles know; they are sensitive to the vibrations of the water’s surface, and if a rare stone falls from a high terrace, every silt-bottle in the channel will contract simultaneously, burying themselves in the silt and remaining motionless for days.

Yet, there is a larger mystery to Olaus, one that resides within the very heart of the grey towers.

Some of the monoliths—particularly those that rise highest into the cold sky—are not solid basalt. Over millions of years, the water that condenses on their tops has eaten away their interiors, carving out vast, vertical chimneys that are completely dark and sealed from the wind. Inside these hollow towers lives the *Chime*.

The Chime is not an individual animal, but a colonial organism of immense scale. It consists of millions of pale, thread-like hydras that hang from the interior ceilings of the towers like living lace. They are white and translucent, fed by the mineral-saturated water that leaks through the ceiling. They do not hunt; they wait for the microscopic spores and dust particles that are carried upward by the warm afternoon mists. 

As the mists rise through the hollow towers, the air passes through the dense curtains of the Chime. This passage of air produces a sound—not a roar, but a low, hollow whistle, like the breath of a giant flute. Each tower has a slightly different internal volume, and thus each tower sings at a different pitch. 

In the late afternoon, when the white mists begin to organize into the low cloud ceiling, the plateau of Olaus begins to vibrate. It is a music that has no audience: a series of interlocking, sub-audible hums that rise from the dark basalt towers and carry across the empty, wet fens. To hear it is to realize that the plateau is not a ruin, nor is it a desert. It is an instrument that plays itself, populated by things that are so perfectly adapted to its silence that they have become part of its acoustics. 

They do not need tools. They do not need to change the stone or control the water. In Olaus, the stone is their bone, the weeping water is their blood, and the wind is their breath. They are simply the plateau, remembering that it is alive.
