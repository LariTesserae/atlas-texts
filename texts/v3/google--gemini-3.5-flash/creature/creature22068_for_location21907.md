To assume the Iron Basin is empty is to mistake the nature of its stillness. Here, life does not occur as an interruption to the landscape; instead, the landscape itself—the iron, the wetness, the low hum—has slowly curdled into forms that can be called alive. 

Nothing here is born, and nothing dies. The dwellers of the Basin are as old as the spires themselves, caught in the same arrested, three-millimeter truce with eternity.

### The Ochre-Men

They are the "absent population" for whom the suspended city was built, though they have long ceased to use the stairs or the walkways for travel. 

To a traveler from the lowlands, an Ochre-Man looks like a statue of cast iron, hunched over an insulated copper handrail or standing at the edge of a rusted viewing platform, staring into the fifteen-mile void. Their skin is covered in the same hard, bright orange crust of rust that shields the gantries. It is three millimeters thick, cold to the touch, and smells of wet metal and old turnips. 

If one were to take a cold-chisel and strike an Ochre-Man’s shoulder, the orange crust would chip away to reveal not bone, but a dense, silver-pink tissue underneath, as bright and clean as newly forged steel. It does not bleed. The fluid within them is not water but a heavy, lubricating oil that flows at a crawl, driven not by a heartbeat, but by the physical vibrations of the world around them.

```
     /   _  _   \
    |   (o)(o)   |   <-- The sealed, rusted eyes of an Ochre-Man
    |    \  /    |
     \   _\/_   /    (Skin of 3mm bright orange rust)
      '.______.'
```

They do not move in the way living things move. To see an Ochre-Man shift his position requires days of observation. A hand may slide three inches along a copper cable over the course of a week; a head may turn toward a blue-white electrical arc over a month. They do not eat, nor do they breathe the cold steam. Instead, their ribs—which have fused into hinged, iron-and-copper plates—expand and contract in perfect, slavish coordination with the steam pistons inside the spires. 

When the distant piston vents:
*Thrum... clack... HISS.*

The Ochre-Man’s chest rises. A thin, grey ribbon of condensation is drawn into his nose; a cold, odorless steam is breathed out. They are the secondary lungs of the sonic grid, amplifying and passing the 40-hertz thrum from one end of the fifteen-mile abyss to the other. 

They do not speak. Their eyelids are permanently sealed under a glassy layer of laminar water, but their ears—large, stiff, and lined with fine copper-wire hairs—are tuned to the great harp of the tension cables. They "hear" the structural integrity of the city. A fraying strand on a gantry ten miles away is felt by an Ochre-Man as a sharp, cold toothache, causing him to lean slightly to the left to compensate for the shifted load.

### The Drift-Globes (The *Hydra-Gears*)

Along the high-tension cables, where the heavy condensation rolls sideways against gravity, there are things that look like large, quivering organs of pure water. These are the *Hydra-Gears*.

They begin as the giant, carriage-sized globes of water that accumulate near the copper ground-wires. Most of these rise and dissolve back into the steam, but occasionally, one does not dissolve. It stays. It catches the 40-hertz hum of the dynamos and begins to structure itself.

```
       ,.~~ ~.~~.
     (  ~  *  ~  )   <-- A Drift-Globe
    ( ~   / \   ~ )      (Suspended by magnetic tension;
     (  ~ \_/ ~  )       contains a skeleton of copper wire)
       '._~_~_~.'
```

Inside the globe’s clear, viscous interior—which has the thickness of linseed oil—a skeleton forms. This skeleton is not grown; it is collected. It consists of microscopic flakes of basalt, copper shavings shed by the cables, and orange rust-dust. Under the influence of the magnetic fields, these particles align into delicate, interlocking geometric patterns that look like three-dimensional clockwork. 

The *Hydra-Gears* crawl along the suspended network. They have no limbs, moving instead by changing their surface tension in rhythm with the electrical currents of the gantries. They are the scavengers of the static. They slide over the wet iron roofs, vacuuming up the bright green liverwort slime and digesting it through a process of slow, acid erosion. 

When two *Hydra-Gears* meet on a narrow copper cable, they do not fight or merge. They press against one another, their internal copper-shaving gears spinning in opposite directions as they exchange electrical charges, before they reverse and crawl back toward their respective spires. 

They are beautiful, silent, and cold. In the dim, phosphorescent green light of the streetlamps, they glow like floating emeralds, their interiors shifting and clicking with the sound of tiny needles scraping on glass.

### The Green Shrouds

On the vertical basalt faces, where the rubbery green skin clings to the stone, there are occasional movements that resemble the slow sliding of tectonic plates. These are the *Green Shrouds*.

A Shroud is not an animal, but a portion of the liverwort-mat that has grown too heavy with water-film and peeled away from the basalt. Because nothing falls normally in the Iron Basin, a detached piece of the green skin—sometimes fifty feet long and as thick as a mattress—does not plunge into the abyss. 

Instead, it floats. The dense, oily fibers of the liverwort trap the cold steam beneath them like a parachute, allowing the Shroud to drift horizontally through the fifteen-mile void. 

```
     \~~~~~~~~~~~~~/
      \  ~  ~  ~  /    <-- A Green Shroud drifting through the void
       \_________/     (Rubbery, turnip-scented, acid-anchored)
```

As it drifts, the Shroud is completely silent. It smells strongly of wet iron and raw garden-greens. It has no head, no eyes, and no destination. It is carried entirely by the eighteen-knot north-northwest wind that never dies. 

When a Shroud eventually collides with a suspended gantry or a boiler chamber, it does not bounce off. Its underside—which is covered in a millions of microscopic, yellow hair-roots—secretes a warm, sulfurous acid upon contact. Within minutes, the green rubbery mass has etched itself into the ironwork, "healing" the gap and hardening into a tough, damp seal that prevents steam leaks. 

The suspended city is blanketed in these dead-eye collisions. Half of the copper roofs are held in place not by rivets, but by the dried, turnip-smelling glue of these ancient, drifted mats.

### The Silt-Marrow (The Heart of the Spires)

Deep within the hollow basalt spires, where the light of the flat, high-altitude noon cannot reach, lies the only part of the Basin that could be said to be warm.

This is the *Silt-Marrow*. It is a black, greasy clay that fills the cracks and chambers around the giant, steam-actuated pistons. It is not biologic, yet it possesses a desperate, mechanical intelligence. 

```
       ||  ||  ||
      [__][__][__]   <-- Basalt fissure
       |        |
       |  (##)  |    <-- The Silt-Marrow (Greasy, self-filtering clay
       |  (##)  |        that lubricates the great pistons)
      [__________]
```

The Marrow is under immense pressure. As the pistons move up and down, they compress the clay, forcing it through channels in the stone. The Marrow reacts to this pressure by self-filtering. It forces its own impurities—bits of sulfur, hard quartz crystals, and iron oxide—outward through the basalt joints, where they form the rocky soil that the green skin clings to.

It is the Marrow that keeps the pistons lubed and the dynamos turning. It is a self-replenishing fluid that has stayed the same volume for ten thousand years. It is the grease, the blood, and the memory of the Iron Basin. 

If one were to descend an iron staircase into the misty nothingness below, past the lowest viewing platform, and press their ear against the wet, vertical basalt of a spire, they would hear more than the *clack-hiss* of the machine. They would hear the Marrow sliding through the stone—a thick, wet, rhythmic *suck-shlap* that matches the breathing of the Ochre-Men above.

In this world of emerald and iron, the machines do not serve the living. The living—the rusted, the drifted, the condensed—exist only to keep the machines from ever having to stop.
