In the hand-width crevices between the flat-topped mounds, where the violent light cannot reach, the shadows are not black. They are a deep, wet slate-blue. 

If you were to watch one of these crevices for several hours, you would eventually notice that some of the shadows are solid. They do not shift with the angle of the sun, but rather with the slow, hydraulic rhythm of the valley's water.

These are the *Laminates*. 

They are shape-matched to the world they inhabit. They are so flat that a mature individual can slide easily between two shingles of dry-stacked shale without disturbing the alignment of either. They have no height to speak of—perhaps three inches at their thickest point—but they can be up to four feet wide and six feet long, resembling broad, flexible shields of damp, grey leather.

Their bodies are not covered in fur, scales, or shell, but rather in a tough, rubbery hide that has the exact color and texture of the wet slate. To touch one would be to touch something that feels precisely like the stone beneath it: cold, wet, slightly gritty with dissolved calcite, and utterly unyielding. They have no internal skeletons of phosphate and marrow. Instead, their support comes from a series of interlocking, overlapping plates of natural shale that have been absorbed into their dorsal skin. As they grow, they slide these plates over one another, maintaining a rigid, armored roof over their soft, pale undersides.

They have no eyes. In this high, violet-white glare, an eye would be a liability, a wet point of vulnerability to be cooked by the sun. Instead, the margins of their flat bodies are lined with horizontal seams—thin, pale lips of tissue that are incredibly sensitive to vibration and temperature. 

They do not hear as we do, but they are intensely aware of the valley’s music. They map their world through the billions of individual water drops hitting the stone. By the variation in the pitch of the *patter-pitter* around them, a Laminate knows if a terrace above has dry-stacked shale that is beginning to lean, or if a gutter three hundred yards away has been choked by a new crust of calcite. A change in the frequency of a drip is, to them, a sign of structural decay.

Their movement is slow, almost geologic. They do not run, crawl, or hop. They slide. 

The underside of a Laminate is covered in thousands of microscopic, muscular cilia-like ridges, hard as horn. When they move, they produce a sound like two flat stones being ground together with a layer of fine wet sand between them—a low, scraping *shuff* that is easily lost beneath the continuous hum of the dripping water. They do not lift themselves from the stone; to lift is to lose the cold moisture that keeps them alive. They must remain in contact with the wet surfaces, drawing the condensation directly through their porous belly-skin.

They are the builders, and they are the maintenance. 

The relationship between the Laminates and the stone is not one of tool-use, but of hydraulic growth. They do not possess hands, but their fore-margins are cleft into three thin, fan-shaped wedges of dense, iron-hard cartilage. When a Laminate finds a block of limestone or shale that has been cracked by the frost, or a seam that has grown uneven, it inserts these wedges into the natural cleavage of the stone. 

Once inserted, the creature does not use muscular force to pry. It uses water. 

The Laminate’s body is a complex system of internal bladders and valves. It siphons the cold condensation from the shallow, square basins, drawing it into its pleural cavities. Once its chambers are full, it seals them and contracts its entire flat mass, forcing the water forward into its wedge-limbs at immense pressure. This biological hydraulic press is capable of exerting thousands of pounds of force per square inch. With a single, slow, silent swell, the Laminate splits the stone along its perfect, clean, right-angled joints. 

They then maneuver the split pieces into place using the same hydraulic grip, sliding them millimetre by millimetre until they fit lock-and-key into the existing masonry. There is no plan to this architecture, no central architect. It is a reflex. They stack the stones because they must remain covered; they build the gutters because the water must keep moving to prevent them from drying out; they chisel the shallow basins because they need the reservoirs to drive their hydraulic bodies.

They feed on the very thing that threatens to petrify them: the lime.

Inside the horizontal cleft that serves as their mouth—located on their leading edge, flush with the ground—are rows of fine, grey, comb-like filters. As they slide over the wet slate, they vacuum up the thin sheet of water. The filters trap the microscopic crystals of calcium carbonate suspended in the runoff. They digest this mineral slurry, using the calcium to grow their dorsal plates and to excrete the crude clay pegs that occasionally pin the heavier blocks together.

But this diet is a slow suicide. 

A Laminate cannot forever prevent the mineral-and-water engine from claiming it. As they age, they become less efficient at expelling the excess calcium. The white calcite crusts that line the channels begin to form on the creature’s own back. At first, this is beneficial, reinforcing their dorsal plates. But over decades, the deposits thicken. The joints of their armor begin to stiffen. The pale, vibration-sensitive seams along their margins become clogged with dry, white, chalky cement.

Slowly, the creature’s movements decrease from feet per day to inches per year. 

Eventually, a Laminate stops moving entirely. The perpetual dew continues to form over its calcified back. The mineral-rich water sheets over it, leaving behind micro-thin crusts of white calcite, sealing its edges to the surrounding slate. The soft tissues beneath the plates wither and dry, replaced by the rising mineral silt of the valley.

The living Laminates do not mourn or remove the dead. They build over them. They dry-stack new slate shingles across the flat, fossilized mounds of their ancestors, aligning them with simple dry-stone precision, splitting new tiles to cover the gaps.

The terraces that press against one another like the scales of an enormous pike are not built *for* anything. They are the calcified remains of countless generations of these slow, flat weavers, who have spent millennia turning the raw mountain into an endless, interlocking skin of stone, washing themselves clean under the cold, violet-white sky until they, too, become part of the ruin.
