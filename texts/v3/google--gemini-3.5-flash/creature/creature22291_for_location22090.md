In the quiet of the perched basin, life does not compete with the stone; it has simply negotiated a truce so old that the distinction between the organic and the mineral has begun to dissolve. 

Beneath the wet, lime-gold light, the primary inhabitants of this cathedral are the ones who listen.

### The Glass-Backs (*Platystoma obsidianum*)

The large, terrestrial snails mentioned in the oldest records of the ridge are more than mere scavengers; they are the basin’s passive historians. Their shells, which can grow to the size of a child’s breastplate, are not made of ordinary lime or horn, but of a dense, volcanic glass so dark it seems to drink the light. This obsidian is highly polished by the constant friction of the damp star-moss and the weeping water.

Because their shells are hollow and dry inside, they act as natural resonance chambers. When a heavy, oil-thick water droplet falls fifty feet from a limestone ledge and strikes a snail’s back, it does not make a wet splash. Instead, the shell rings with a low, metallic chime that hangs in the warm air for several seconds. 

The Glass-backs appear to understand this. Over weeks, they position themselves deliberately beneath specific drips, like copper bells arranged on a temple floor. A traveler—if one could reach this height—might find four of them settled in a semi-circle around a giant succulent, their shells pitched to different frequencies, performing a slow, multi-decade symphony dictated entirely by the changing speed of the condensation above. 

They feed upon the microscopic blue-green algae that sheaths the wet limestone, moving so slowly that a single journey from the floor to the high ledge may span a generation. When they die, they do not decompose so much as empty out; their soft, grey bodies are reclaimed by the moss, leaving behind the perfect, hollow black bells of their shells, which eventually fill with weeping water and become new, ringing fonts.

### The Font-Drifters (*Vitrella*)

Within the natural stone basins and the hollowed succulent stalks, where the water gathers and grows cold only at its topmost skin, live the *Vitrella*. 

To the naked eye, they are nearly invisible—globules of living gel the size of plums, possessing the exact refractive index of the heavy, mineral-rich water they inhabit. They have no face, no limbs, and no internal skeleton. Instead, they are held together by an incredibly tough, elastic membrane, much like the water droplets themselves. 

These creatures are almost entirely water, yet they are alive. They drift in the fonts like slow, suspended lenses, focusing the golden sunlight down onto the dark stone bottoms of the basins. They feed on the minerals that seep from the limestone, extracting calcium and silica directly through their outer membrane. 

When a drop falls from the high cliffs and strikes a font, the *Vitrella* do not break. They absorb the impact, warping into long, teardrop shapes before bouncing back to their spherical form. Through this constant, rhythmic compression, they pump water through their simple bodies, filtering it. The water in the fonts where they dwell is always the clearest in the basin, free of the sulfurous sting of the mountain’s deeper vents.

### The Moss-Core (*The Pale Crawlers*)

Beneath the foot-deep carpet of star-moss, where the earth is heated from below by the thermal vents, the environment is vastly different. It is a dark, humid gallery of roots and steam. 

Here, in the warm damp, live the *Pale Crawlers*. They are blind, soft-bodied creatures, roughly the length of a human hand, with skin the color of peeled almonds. They have no legs, but rather a sequence of broad, muscular rings along their bellies that allow them to glide through the dense star-moss without tearing its roots. 

The Crawlers are the caretakers of the basin’s floor. They are utterly silent, living their entire lives within the green cushion. They consume the fallen, decaying fronds of the giant ferns and the dropped segments of the pillar-succulents, turning them into a rich, sweet-smelling loam that feeds the moss. 

Because the air below the moss is thick with sulfuric steam, the Crawlers have developed a highly specialized breathing system: their backs are lined with tiny, thread-like gills that stick up through the surface of the moss like miniature forest-fires of pale pink hair. As one walks across the buoyant green floor—though no one does—these tiny pink filaments would brush against one’s ankles, retracting instantly into the green depths at the slightest vibration.

### The Hand That Departed (*The Lithogasters*)

The "primitive intelligence" that once wedged the flat stones to protect the yellow ferns was not a transient visitor, nor did it truly depart. It simply slowed down.

The *Lithogasters*—the Great-Bellied Ones—are the oldest living residents of the basin. There are only five of them left, and to the casual eye, they are indistinguishable from the smooth, water-slid limestone boulders that ring the eastern wall. 

They are roughly hominid in their deep anatomy, but their skin is a thick, leathery rind of grey slate, encrusted with the same ancient lichen that covers the cliffs. They have no necks; their broad, flat heads sit directly on shoulders that are three paces wide. They have no eyes, for there is nothing in this basin that requires haste or evasion. Instead, their wide, flat palms are constantly pressed against the basin floor or the wet walls, reading the vibrations of the mountain.

A *Lithogaster* can remain motionless for eighty years. During these long periods of "the grand rest," their metabolism drops to a level that is barely distinguishable from stone. The thermal heat from the vents keeps their blood—a thick, white latex-like fluid—from thickening. 

It was they who built the simple spillways and propped the hollow succulent stalks. They did not do this out of a desire to build a civilization, but out of a deep, aesthetic irritation. To a *Lithogaster*, a dry patch of stone is a discordant note; a drip that falls with a harsh, flat *clack* instead of a round, deep *tock* is like a splinter in the ear. 

Every century or so, when the water-routes change or a limestone lip crumbles, one of these beings will slowly unfold. The movement is accompanied by the dry, grinding sound of tearing lichen and cracking mineral crust. Over the course of several summers, the creature will move three paces, adjust a stone to restore the perfect harmony of the dripping water, perhaps clear a clump of moss that is choking a natural font, and then sink back into the wall. 

Their skin is so soft and porous during their active phases that the star-moss takes root in the folds of their joints. When they return to their sleep, they do not look like statues; they look like the mountain itself, dreaming of water.
