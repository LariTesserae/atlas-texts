To find the builders of the stone enclosures, one must learn to look for what is not quite stone. 

They do not walk; they slide and heave, moving with a heavy, sliding gravity that makes them seem like dislodged pieces of the basalt floor. A mature individual is roughly the size of a dormant seal, but wider, flatter, and far denser. Their bodies are teardrop-shaped—broad and thick at the chest, tapering down to a single, muscular tail-lobe that they drag behind them like a heavy sack of wet grain. 

Their skin is their most striking feature: a thick, rubbery hide of leaden grey, mottled with patches of liver-colored and lichen-yellow discoloration. It has no scale or hair. Instead, it is continuously lubricated by a clear, viscous grease that they secrete from glands along their flanks. This grease smells faintly of cold lard and ancient, waterlogged peat. It serves a triple purpose: it protects their hides from the abrasive silt, keeps them from drying out during the low-pressure drain cycles, and makes them nearly impossible for the great, blind crawlers to grip.

They have no neck. Their heads are simply the blunt, forward wedges of their torsos. Where one might expect eyes, there are only two small, pale discs of scar-like tissue—vestigial organs covered by a permanent, milky caul. They are entirely blind to light, yet they perceive the flat world with an accuracy that borders on the telepathic. 

Beneath the thick skin of their skulls lies a wide, crescent-shaped plate of porous bone, suspended in a pocket of oil. This is their primary sensory organ. By lowering their heavy heads until this plate rests directly against the wet basalt, they can "see" the landscape through its vibrations. They feel the scuttle of a flat-shell grazer half a mile away; they can map the exact rise and fall of the subterranean tides three hundred feet beneath the stone floor. When they communicate, they do not find voice in the deafening, whistle-shriek of the air. Instead, they press their throats to the rock and rumble—a dry, grinding hum that travels through the bedrock like a localized earthquake, felt by their kin as a rhythmic twitching in their flipper-soles.

And it is the flippers that define their labor. 

They possess only two limbs: a pair of short, immensely muscular pectoral flippers positioned near the front of the chest. These limbs are triple-lobed. The three "digits" are not fingers, but broad, spade-like wedges of cartilage, each tipped with a short, thick claw of black, horn-like material. These claws are kept blunt by constant work, worn down to the likeness of heavy chisels. 

With these three-lobed limbs, they are capable of a slow, patient masonry. They do not quarry the stone; they search the silt-plains during the draining phase, selecting flat pieces of slate that have been pre-shattered by the frost and the shifting air pressure. They carry these slabs one by one, clamped against their greasy chests with their powerful flippers, dragging themselves backward to the site of their enclosures. 

Inside the stone circles, their lives are small, quiet, and incredibly orderly. 

They are social, but only in pairs or small triads. A single enclosure usually houses two mates or a parent with a nearly grown juvenile. Within the circle, they are surprisingly tender. They spend hours grooming one another, using their blunt claws to scrape away the grey bacterial mats and parasite-worms that take root in the folds of their skin. 

Their tools are kept in specific, shallow depressions in the packed mud platforms. They possess a high degree of spatial memory; a scraper or a bone hammer is always returned to the exact patch of silt where it belongs. The vulcanized glass scrapers—fashioned from rare chunks of obsidian carried up from the deep blowholes by the water—are their most prized possessions. They use them to flay the rubbery mats of slime-mold from the basalt mounds, rolling the mold into tight, fermented cylinders that they store in the margins of their pools like stacks of sodden cigars.

They feed primarily on the flat-bodied grazers, which they harvest by simply prying them off the rocks with their bone tools. To eat, they use a heavy, circular mouth positioned on the underside of their flat heads, filled with rows of broad, crushing plates rather than teeth. The sound of a family feeding inside an enclosure is a wet, rhythmic *crunch-crunch*, like stones being ground in a mortar.

Perhaps the most mysterious aspect of their existence is their relationship with fire. 

In a world without wood, fire is a rare and difficult technology. They produce it through chemistry and patience. Using their flippers, they scrape up dry, yellow crusts of native sulfur from the edges of certain warm-water vents. They mix this with the fat they render from the blind crawlers they occasionally trap, kneading the mixture into the dry, fibrous bricks of shelf-fungus. 

When struck with a piece of iron-rich basalt against volcanic glass, this prepared fuel does not burn with a bright flame—there is too much moisture in the air for that. Instead, it smolders with a sullen, deep-red glow, producing a heavy, choking smoke that smells of grease and sulfur. 

The builders do not use this hearth-fire for warmth; their cold-blooded bodies are perfectly adapted to the shivering, late-autumn temperature of the flats. They use the heat to cure. They hang the long, pale pool-worms and strips of slime-mold over the smoldering pits, drying them into a leathery, preservable jerky that can survive the long, unpredictable periods when the land remains flooded for days at a time.

As they age, these creatures do not rot; they calcify. 

The mineral-heavy water they drink and live in gradually saturates their tissues. Their movements, already slow, become stiffer. The grey, rubbery hide begins to harden, taking on the rough, gritty texture of limestone. 

When a builder knows its time of stillness is near, it leaves the stone enclosure. It drags itself out into the shallow water, away from the dry platforms, and finds a place among the basalt mounds. There, it settles into the mud, pulling its flippers close to its chest and flattening its head against the bedrock. 

Over decades, the silt siltates around it; the lime-water deposits its crust. The flesh turns to stone, the skin merges with the bacterial mats, and the individual becomes just another smooth, bulbous mound in the vast, grey floor of the world—rounded by the wind, washed by the tide, and joining the collective roar of the earth.
