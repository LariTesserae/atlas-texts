Within the Low-Silt, life does not fight the machinery; it lubricates it. 

The biological and the mechanical have reached an ancient, damp compromise. What lives here does not possess greenness, nor does it know the rapid, hot-blooded cycle of hunting and fleeing. The creatures of the basin are systems of slow, heavy chemistry, designed to survive the pressure of forty-ton iron strokes and to feed on the waste products of a world that runs on steam and friction.

At the lowest, wettest level of this compromise are the **Tallow-Wrecks**. 

These are the largest living things in the basin, though they are rarely recognized as animals by those who first see them. To the untrained eye, a Tallow-Wreck looks like a low, humped mound of greyish-yellow wax, some twelve feet across, resting in the silt near the base of a sliding shaft. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no bony structure. Their bodies are composed of a dense, stearic fat—an organic lard so stable that it does not spoil in the warm air, but merely gathers a fine coat of rust-colored dust.

The Tallow-Wreck moves by a process of localized melting. By routing the heat from the nearby steam vents through a network of internal, copper-rich vessels, it liquefies its own lower margins, allowing its mass to slide inches at a time through the warm clay. It is these creatures that produce the heavy, kitchen-like smell of scorched lard that hangs over the Low-Silt. They feed by scooping the liver-colored mold into their ventral folds, where it is slowly broken down by concentrated sulfurous acids. 

In return for its territory, the Tallow-Wreck performs a crucial service for the great iron shafts. As it crawls over the basalt sleeves, it leaves behind a thick, smeary trail of animal grease. When the blue-grey pistons draw back into their housings, they carry this grease with them, preventing the raw mineral-on-mineral friction that would otherwise seize the machinery within a century. The machine does not crush the Tallow-Wreck; the Tallow-Wreck is too soft, too fluid to be broken, and the pistons simply slide through its yielding contours like a finger through cold butter.

Above the mud, hanging from the undersides of the great, half-buried flywheels, are the **Cable-Strands**. 

These appear to be loops of old, greasy hemp rope, dangling down into the steam plumes. They are actually colonies of blind, worm-like organisms that link themselves tail-to-mouth in chains twenty feet long. Their skin is dry and fibrous, identical in texture to the lithotrophic mold, but their interiors are filled with a cold, clear oil. They hold themselves against the moving metal with thousands of microscopic, hook-like teeth. 

The Cable-Strands are the basin’s filters. As the steam rises from the vents, carrying with it a fine mist of zinc, sulfur, and lead, the Strands swing lazily in the draft, absorption pores along their flanks drinking the mineral vapor. When a strand becomes saturated, it does not die; it simply becomes heavy and stiff, eventually snapping under its own weight. It drops into the silt below, where the solder-lice gather around its mineral-rich carcass like beetles around a fallen branch.

Then there are the **Clonk-Catchers**, the only things in the Low-Silt that move with any speed. 

These are flat, saucer-shaped creatures, the color of wet slate, about the size of a dinner plate. They are entirely deaf to sound but intensely sensitive to seismic vibration. They spend hours pinned flat against the dry, compacted silt, their thin, chitinous rims vibrating in sympathy with the 40-hertz thrum of the earth. 

When a distant piston strikes its basalt sleeve with that deep, tolling *clonk*, the displacement wave travels through the clay like a ripple through gelatin. The Clonk-Catcher uses this specific, heavy shockwave to launch itself. Utilizing a spring-like muscle in its center that is tensed over several minutes, the creature snaps its body into a bowl-shape, flying three or four feet through the thick air. It does this to migrate from pool to pool, pursuing the shifting puddles of lukewarm brine. If you watch them from a distance, they look like flat stones skipping across a dry lakebed, their leaps perfectly synchronized with the industrial heartbeat of the basin.

Deep within the vents themselves, where the steam pressure is high enough to strip human flesh from bone, lives the **Gate-Snail**. 

These creatures have long since given up the need for mobility. They select a small steam vent—one of the thousands of smooth, round holes in the basalt—and cement their shells directly over it. The shell is not made of lime, but of a heavy, lead-zinc alloy that the snail secretes from its mantle over hundreds of years. The shell is spiraled, but porous, designed like a silencer. 

The Gate-Snail lives on the organic matter carried up from the deep biosphere by the steam. As the superheated gas passes through the chambers of its leaden shell, the snail’s gills extract nutrient-bearing chemicals. The high-pitched whistle of the steam is muffled by the snail's body; the *shhh-t-t-t* sound that defines the eastern horizon is actually the collective breathing of millions of these gate-snails, acting as living valves that prevent the steam vents from blowing themselves into jagged craters.

If you sit quiet enough near one of the amber-skinned puddles, you will see how these lives connect. 

A solder-louse will crawl out from beneath a liver-mold ring, its pewter plates clicking softly. It will find the dropped, waxen segment of a Cable-Strand, sodden with lead-dust. It scrapes at the residue, its wire antennae trembling. Nearby, a Tallow-Wreck shifts its weight with a wet, bubbling hiss, leaving a track of golden grease that catches the flat, three-o'clock light. 

There is no competition here. There are no predators to hunt the solder-louse, and there is nothing that could harm the Tallow-Wreck. The only enemy is the cessation of the machine. If the shafts were to stop their slow, ten-minute slide—if the steam were to cool and the 40-hertz hum were to die—the Low-Silt would freeze. The grease would harden into stone; the Cable-Strands would dry and snap; the solder-lice would starve in the silent, cold clay.

But the machine does not stop. And so they remain, the soft, living tissues that keep the iron from grinding itself to dust, running together in the warm, soot-scented dusk.
