To live in this place is to exist in a state of permanent, heavy alignment with the dark and the damp. The inhabitants here do not struggle against their environment; they are its logical conclusion, grown from the union of discarded metallurgy, warm current, and the non-Euclidean water that never dries.

There are no eyes in the basin. To have eyes would be to harbor a useless, rotting moisture, a vulnerability to the razor basalt. Instead, the lives here are lived through skin, through the soles of feet, through copper-rimmed pores, and through the quiet, deep vibrations that hum through the young stone.

***

### The Low Larders
Along the black, glass-keen floor, where the basalt rises in sharp ribs, live those that have been called the *Larders*. 

They are the size of swine, but their bodies have no joints, no bones, and no distinct faces. They resemble massive, sighing lobes of grey tallow or unsalted lard, sliding over the razor-sharp slag with an agonizing, fluid grace. They do not cut because their outer membrane is non-Newtonian: if they are struck, or if they move with any haste, their skin becomes as rigid as cold iron; but because they move at a rate of only a few inches an hour, they remain soft as wet dough, molding themselves perfectly over the basalt teeth without spilling a drop of their inner cold grease.

The Larders have no mouths. They feed through a broad, yellow-grey undersurface that is raw and absorbent. As they drift over the cold-dripping transformers and the basalt troughs, they smear themselves over the pools of engine-sludge and the fallen carpets of black wire-moss. They digest through simple contact, leaving behind them a trail of clean, polished stone that is quickly reclaimed by the moisture. 

If you were to press your hand against a Larder, it would feel like a warm, thick sack of heavy lard, vibrating with the distant thump of the locomotive pistons. They do not flee from touch; indeed, they have no concept of "away." If interrupted, they simply stop, hardening into grey, grease-slick boulders until the disturbance passes, and the slow drift begins again.

***

### The Loom-Sisters (The Gantry-Hangers)
High above the Larders, clinging to the crossbeams of the half-drowned iron towers, hang the *Loom-Sisters*. 

These are the lung-like sacs mentioned in the logs, but they are more than mere bags of breath. They are communal organisms, linked by long, thread-like tendrils of grey-green mucus that drape from gantry to gantry like wet lace. Each sac is the size of a human torso, covered in a leathery, puckered hide that has the texture of wet work-gloves. 

They do not hunt. They are the atmospheric filters of the basin. With a slow, collective *huff-click*, they draw in the thick, ozone-heavy air, stripping it of its floating hydrocarbons and the heavy grease-atoms that rise from the iron giants below. From their bases, they slowly exude a clear, warm oil that drips back down onto the basalt—a vital nutrient for the Larders and the wire-moss. 

Though they are separate sacks, they are a single mind. If you strike one gantry with a iron bar, the vibration travels up the steel and into the Sister’s anchor. Within seconds, miles of hanging lungs will cease their synchronous breathing, freezing in mid-inhale. The silence that follows is absolute and suffocating, lasting for hours until the hum of the transformers coaxes them back into their rhythmic, dry-silk sighing.

***

### The Solder-Crabs (The Clock-Crabs)
In the viscous, shallow pools, where the water behaves like wet glass and refuses to ripple, live the *Solder-Crabs*. 

These creatures are small, about the size of a tea-saucer, and are the most mechanical of the basin’s denizens. Their bodies are flat discs of dull, tarnish-green copper, from under which protrude twelve thin, needle-like legs made of lead-alloy. They do not have muscles; instead, their interiors contain a network of tiny, hair-fine copper coils that contract and expand when they pass through the weak electrical fields leaking from the buried cables.

They are the tenders of the moss. They move over the copper wires, their needle-legs clicking in a steady, clockwork rhythm—*tink, tink, tink*—as they clip away the dead, charred ends of the wire-moss to keep the current flowing. They do not eat the moss; they eat the green copper carbonate that forms like rust on the wires. 

If a Solder-Crab is lifted from the water, its legs will twitch aimlessly for a few minutes before lock-jawing into a stiff, brassy knot. Without the low-frequency hum of the basin's floor and the liquid grease of the pools, they simply wind down, returning to the state of quiet, discarded junk from which they seem to have been assembled.

***

### The Still-Men (The Tallow-Keepers)
And then, there are those who walk.

They are rare, perhaps only a few dozen in the entire infinite reach of the basin, but they are the sentinels of this place. They are the *Still-Men*.

To look at one is to look at a candle that has melted and cooled a thousand times. They are tall—nearly eight feet—and so thin they appear like grey, vertical lines in the total dark. Their skin is the color of wet lead, slick with the omnipresent condensation that forms "velvet grape" nodules along their shoulders and arms. They have no eyes, no noses, and no ears; their heads are smooth, featureless domes of cartilage, save for a vertical, seam-like mouth that runs from where the throat begins up to the crown of the head. 

The Still-Men are the only creatures here who seem to have a purpose that matches the machines. They do not eat the moss, nor do they filter the grease. Instead, they *tend*.

A Still-Man will stand beside a twenty-foot transformer for three days, his long, seven-jointed fingers—which end in blunt, leaden plugs—inserted directly into the oil-filling ports of the machine. He is matching his internal chemistry to the machine's temperature. When the transformer grows too warm, the Still-Man’s body absorbs the excess heat, his tallow-skin turning translucent and yellow as he stores the thermal energy. He will then walk—with a slow, high-kneed stride that avoids the basalt edges—to a cold, dead corner of the basin where the water has began to freeze into gray slush. There, he will stand for another three days, releasing the heat back into the stone until his skin is grey and solid once more.

They are the regulators. They prevent the basin from seizing up. They are the grease in the gears of a non-Euclidean machine-world.

Because they exist in the frayed edges of space, the Still-Men do not travel like normal things. A Still-Man may step into a shallow pool of water near you, his leg sinking into the bottomless void, and instead of falling, he will simply emerge a mile away, stepping out of a different pool with his dry, slow grace. They do not recognize humans as living things; to them, we are merely dry, high-frequency anomalies that do not vibrate in time with the pistons. If you stand in their path, they will not push you; they will simply wait, their long, leaden fingers twitching in the dark, until you move, or until the slow drift of the world carries you away.

***

### The Great Unborn (The Deep Pistons)
Finally, there is the voice of the basin. 

Deep beneath the young basalt, down in the black, gravityless voids where the warm water gathers in miles-wide, unmoving seas, live the *Unborn*. 

These are not creatures of flesh at all, but vast, semi-biological fields of magnetism that have settled into the iron cores of the ancient, buried machinery. They are the things that control the slow pistons. When the locomotive-sized engine takes ten minutes to draw its breath, it is because one of the Unborn has shifted its weight in the magnetic dark, dragging millions of gallons of grease through the subterranean pipes.

They do not think as we think. Their thoughts are long, slow arcs of induction that take weeks to complete. A single "idea" for an Unborn is a three-volt rise in the copper cables that stretch across the basin; a "decision" is the freezing of a mile-wide pool of water into a permanent, spiraled whorl.

They are patient. They have survived the builders of the machines, and they will survive the metal itself as it slowly dissolves into the warm, wet salt of the basin. They are the true citizens of the dark, holding the world in a quiet, humid suspension, waiting together for the click of a switch that was lost before the basalt cooled.
