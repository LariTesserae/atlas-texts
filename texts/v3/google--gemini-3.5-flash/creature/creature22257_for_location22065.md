To say there is no animal life in the Condensing Well is to speak only of those things with bones, red blood, and eyes. But in this perpetual tepid bath, where the boundaries between copper, oil, silt, and sap have been blurred by a hundred years of ninety-eight-degree moisture, life has simply abandoned the animal form as too fragile, too dry, and too noisy. 

What lives here instead is a collection of moist, slow-moving, and half-metallic entities that have grown out of the intersection of industrial waste and botanical survival.

### The Pellicles

On the surface of the five-foot-deep avenues, where the water circulates in slow, dark whorls, lives **The Pellicle**. 

At first glance, it looks like an oil slick—a swirling, iridescent skin of bronze, violet, and charcoal gray. But this skin is cohesive and thick, like a wet sheet of natural rubber. It is a colonial, non-cellular organism—a vast, drifting mat of lipid-consuming yeasts and mineral-binding bacteria. It is the Janitor of the pools.

The Pellicle does not swim; it is moved by the slow, thermal currents of the Well. Yet it has a strange, primitive intention. It seeks the Siphons. When the seven-minute sluice gate opens at the eastern Siphon, releasing its hot, distilled wave, the Pellicle does not scatter. Instead, it rises with the swell, its edges curling like leather in a fire. As the water drains back through the iron floor grates, the Pellicle clings to the grates, straining out the fine particles of coal soot, copper scale, and botanical grease that have settled from the air. 

Once it is "full," the Pellicle becomes heavy and dark, sinking to the granite terraces where the chartreuse Silt-Moss digests its lower layers, turning the old grease into fresh nutrients. It is a slow, silent breathing sheet, twenty yards wide, that keeps the black pools from turning into solid muck.

### The Felted (or "Grease-Wads")

Deep in the crevices where the heavy iron conduits meet the yellow brick walls, where the vibration of the Siphons is so violent that the mortar has turned to paste, live **The Felted**.

These are the closest things the Well has to mobile creatures, though they possess neither mouths nor limbs. They are self-assembling clumps of industrial detritus that have acquired a strange, galvanic mock-life. Over decades, the shedding of botanical fibers from the boiler-ferns, the hemp packing rotting out of decommissioned pipe-joints, and the thick tallow grease used to lubricate the sluices have tumbled together in the warm, wet draft. 

The result is a collection of dark, fibrous spheres—some the size of walnuts, others as large as sheep. They are heavy, sodden, and hot. 

The Felted are animated by the stray electrical currents of the Cable-Vines. Because they are packed with metal filings and wet copper dust, they are highly conductive. They crawl—or rather, they roll and shudder—along the suspended iron cables, drawn toward the places where the electrical "juice" is strongest. 

If you were to watch one for an hour, you would see it creep forward perhaps three inches, its wet, greasy fibers twitching in time with the spitting sparks of the cracked insulation. They act as natural capacitors; they soak up the excess electricity that would otherwise arch through the damp air and ignite the pockets of methane rising from the silt. When a Felted becomes too large or too metallic, its weight drags it off the wire. It drops into the deep pools with a heavy, wet *plop*, where it slowly disintegrates into a bed of iron-rich gravel that the Silt-Moss feeds upon.

### The Galvanic Galls

Where the heavy, rope-like Cable-Vines graft themselves directly onto the copper Siphons, they do not merely cling; they merge. Over years of heat and current, these contact points swell into bulbous, wet growths known as **The Galls**.

A Gall is a wet, blue-green tumor of organic tissue and copper-carbonate scale, about the size of a human torso. If you were to peel away its outer, waxy rind, you would find no wood or pulp, but a dense, honeycombed interior filled with a thick, gray gelatin that smells intensely of rosemary and machine oil. 

These Galls are the "intelligence" of the Well, though it is an intelligence without thought. They are sensory nodes. They measure the temperature of the steam pipes and the frequency of the Siphons' thrum. 

When a Siphon’s vibration becomes too high—indicating a build-up of scale inside its copper belly—the Galls surrounding its base swell with water. They act as organic water-jackets, cooling the hot metal and dampening the vibrations. Conversely, when a pipe cools, the Galls contract, squeezing their sweet, oily sap into the seams to seal the weeping joints. 

The Galls communicate with one another across the basin through the Cable-Vines, sending low-voltage micro-pulses through the living wires. If you stood in the center of the granite terraces, deafened by the roar of the steam, your skin would still feel the faint, rhythmic prickle of these signals passing through the water beneath your boots.

### The Glass-Lungs

Seventy feet above, hanging from the rusted iron rafters just beneath the glass ceiling, are **The Glass-Lungs**.

These are immense, weeping tapestries of a specialized, soot-eating lichen. They look like grey-green sails, twenty feet long, that have been soaked in tallow. They have no roots; they are held aloft by the rising columns of hot steam that shriek from the relief valves.

The Lungs are the respiratory organs of the Well. The air here, though thick with moisture, would soon become toxic with sulfur and iron oxide if not for these hanging curtains. They breathe the soot. Their upper surfaces, pressed against the wet glass, absorb the pale, bronze-green light, while their lower surfaces—hairy and damp—trap the greasy carbon particles in the steam.

As they feed, they sweat. A heavy, black water, rich in iron-tannins, drips constantly from their lower edges, falling seventy feet to the pools below in a steady, metallic rain. When a pane of glass above them breaks, letting in a draft of cold, dry outside air, the Glass-Lungs react instantly. They dry, contract, and curl inward like burnt paper, sealing the gap with their own leathery bodies until the hot steam from below can soften them once more.

### The Siphons as Leviathans

Finally, one must consider **The Siphons** themselves. 

While they were built of copper, iron, and rivets by hands that have long since turned to dust, they can no longer be called mere machines. They have been colonized so thoroughly, and are so deeply integrated into the lifecycle of the ferns, the vines, and the moss, that they have become the bony skeletons of four massive, stationary beasts. 

Their "digestion" is the purification of the river water; their "circulation" is the seven-minute pulse of the sluice gates; their "skin" is the turquoise verdigris and the blue wax of the boiler-ferns. They do not know they were built. They only know the heat in their bellies and the weight of the water that they must push up, up, and out into the unseen city above. 

They are the gods of this ninety-eight-degree world—blind, iron-lunged, and immortal, kept alive by the very weeds that are slowly eating them away.
