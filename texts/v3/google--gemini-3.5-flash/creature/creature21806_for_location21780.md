To understand what lives on the Shelf, one must first understand that life here does not struggle against the metal; it is of a piece with it. The line between the architecture and the organism is thin, clean, and joined with countersunk screws.

At the lowest, most fundamental level of the Shelf’s ecology are the **Vinyl-Lichens**. These yellow and grey hexagons do not grow by cellular division, but by a process resembling stamping. Under a magnifying glass, their edges are not frayed or root-like, but are notched with minute, interlocking teeth, identical to the gears of a microscopic watch. They move across the non-slip surfaces of the stairwells at a rate of three inches a week. As they slide, they do not leave behind organic decay; instead, they act as living buffers. Their bellies are lined with a dry, fibrous felt that polishes the galvanized zinc to a mirror shine, removing the slight, dulling film of oxide that forms from the rare pockets of condensation. Where the lichen has been, the steel is so bright it reflects the overhead plates like water.

Feeding upon these lichens are the **Key-Scuttlers**. They are the size of a hand, constructed of thin, stamped brass plates and tempered steel wire springs that hum with a low, tensioned energy. They have no eyes; instead, they have long, segmented feelers made of typewriter-ribbon guides that trace the seams of the metal floors. They are powered by the static charge that builds in the dry, thin air. When two Scuttlers meet, they do not fight or mate; they bump their flat brass noses together with a dry *clack*, exchanging electrical potential until both are balanced, then part in opposite directions.

The Scuttlers are the ledger-keepers of the plates. If you press your ear to any vertical pillar, you will not hear the wind, but you will hear them. Through the cold-rolled steel, their tiny, spring-loaded feet tap out a continuous, vibrating telegraphy. It is a vast, self-correcting census of the Shelf’s structural integrity. A break in their rhythmic tapping indicates a loose bolt or an out-of-true angle miles away—an occurrence so rare it draws thousands of these creatures to the site like iron filings to a magnet, where they huddle in silent, vibrating heaps until the slow, geologic turning of the flywheels resets the tension and the rhythm resumes.

***

In the vertical drafts of the chasm-like spaces between the terraces live the **Anamorphs**. 

These are the predators of the perspective-slips, though "predator" is a clumsy word for things that hunt not by teeth, but by geometry. An Anamorph has no fixed shape or volume; it exists as a series of nested, silver-plated frames that slide within one another like the brass tubes of a telescope. 

To an observer, an Anamorph might first appear as a distant sky-bridge crossing a chasm three miles away. But as you walk, the rules of distance bend. The sky-bridge does not remain distant; it collapses toward you, its silver frames sliding together with a sound like a closing pocket-knife, until it is a six-inch crab-like shape clinging to the handrail right beside your fingers. 

The Anamorphs hunt the sheared-metal kites. They do so by catching them in these sudden folds of space. A kite, gliding silently on its mirrored pinions, will find the horizon ahead of it suddenly shrinking. The mile-wide gap it was crossing becomes a three-inch vertical slot between two brass ribs; the kite is pinched, its polished wings crumpled, and the Anamorph absorbs its metal-density, expanding its own frames by a few millimeters to accommodate the new mass.

***

Then, there are those who think.

They are called the **Orthogonals**, though they call themselves nothing, as they have no language that relies on air. They are humanoid only in the sense that they are vertical and possess limbs, but their bodies are composed of stacked, sliding plates of dark, oil-tempered lead and bright chrome-cobalt alloy. Their heads are perfect cubes of solid, smoke-grey glass, inside of which three brass plumb-bobs hang from silver threads, swinging in perpetual, silent coordination with the Shelf's gravity.

The Orthogonals do not walk on feet. Their lower limbs terminate in heavy, magnetized sleds that fit perfectly into the T-slots and guide-grooves stamped into the main deck plates. They slide along these pre-determined paths with a smooth, heavy momentum, their movements accompanied by the faint, pleasant smell of warm machine oil.

Because the air is too thin to carry sound, the Orthogonals communicate through **Refraction**. The faces of their cube-heads are highly polished. By tilting their heads at precise angles to the flat, white overhead lights, they project sharp, geometric beams of light across the vast chasms to one another. A single, flash-frozen square of light thrown onto a distant boiler house can mean *“The third flywheel has completed its micro-degree of rotation.”* A series of rapidly shifting octagons projected onto a sky-bridge might mean *“The condensation in Sector 9 is tasting too heavily of iron; the copper pipes are weeping.”*

The Orthogonals have no dwellings, for there is no weather to shelter from, and sleep is unknown to them. Instead, they have "Stations"—vertical clusters of brass brackets where they slide their bodies and lock themselves in place when the great flywheels turn. During these periods of geologic shifting, when the very orientation of the terraces changes and "up" becomes a wall of cold-rolled steel, the Orthogonals remain perfectly horizontal, their glass heads rotating on brass gimbals so that their internal plumb-bobs never touch the sides of their glass chambers.

***

Lastly, in the deep, slow-moving hollows of the pendulum weights—where the silence is so heavy it feels like a physical pressure on the temples—live the **Slipped**.

These are the descendants of those who came from elsewhere, from places of soft earth and rounded corners, who became trapped in the Shelf’s perspective-slippage and could never find the way back down. 

The Slipped have adapted to a world without depth. They have lost their binocular vision; to look at the Shelf with two eyes is to invite a permanent, agonizing vertigo as the brain tries to reconcile a three-mile walk with a three-foot reach. Thus, the Slipped wear heavy, single-lensed masks made of brass plate and dark, smoked mica. They see the world as a flat, exquisite engraving. They do not understand "near" or "far"; they understand only "above," "below," and "overlapping."

Their clothing is made of thin, hammered lead foil, which they stitch together with copper wire. It does not rust, and it doesn't flap in the cold, silent weight of the wind; it simply creases with a stiff, metallic rattle when they move. 

The Slipped survive by harvesting the moisture from the copper pipes. They use long, straight drinking-tubes of rolled zinc, sliding them into the square floor-reservoirs to draw up the oil-tainted water. They eat the yellow vinyl-lichen, which they scrape from the steps with bronze spatulas. They cure the lichen in vats of cold industrial grease until the vinyl texture softens into something resembling salt-cured rind.

Because their bodies are still made of soft, vulnerable flesh, they are terrified of the razor-sharp edges of their world. They move with an incredible, deliberate slowness. A Slipped person will spend twenty minutes negotiating a single step, ensuring their lead-shod sole is perfectly aligned with the non-slip checker-pattern before transferring their weight. They do not run. To fall here is not merely to hurt oneself; to fall is to slide off the edge of a perspective-slip, to become a tiny, silver speck on a vertical pillar three miles away, small enough to be stepped on by a Scuttler, while your shadow remains behind, sixty feet tall, cast permanently across the zinc plates of the terrace.

When a Slipped dies, they do not bury them, for there is no earth. Instead, they carry the body to the edge of a sky-bridge and wait for one of the city-sized flywheels to begin its slow, geologic rotation. As the giant gears mesh, they slide the body between the teeth. There is no sound—the thin air swallows the crunch of bone and lead foil—but for an instant, a tiny smear of red appears on the pristine grey iron, only to be wiped clean a moment later by the passing of a vinyl-lichen, leaving the metal as perfect, cold, and bright as the day it was rolled.
