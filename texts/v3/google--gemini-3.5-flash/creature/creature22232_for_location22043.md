In the damp, hot hush of the fissure, the silence is not empty; it is a waiting vessel. If you were to sit beside the tiny alcove containing those needle-thin tools and remain perfectly still—breathing the thick, sulfuric soup of the air, ignoring the boiling globes that drift past your ears like slow-motion lanterns—you would eventually see the maker of those tools emerge from the impossible depth of the three-inch crack.

They are not humans, nor are they insects, though they possess the delicate, jointed grace of both.

The creatures who live here are the *Scythe-Fingered*, or, as the rhythmic vibration of their own language translates through the glass, *The Tenders of the Seam*. 

An adult Tender is no longer than a dry spruce needle, yet they are built with the exquisite complexity of a watchmaker’s masterpiece. Their bodies are semi-fluid, housed within a segmented carapace of natural, clear silica that they secrete from their own skin. Underneath this transparent armor, they have no red blood; instead, their veins host a slowly pumping, high-boiling-point silicone oil, tinted a faint, bioluminescent violet. When they are calm, their bodies glow with the soft, steady light of a cooling ember. When they are startled, the violet light flashes blue, a silent alarm that ripples through the colony like an electrical current.

They have six limbs, but their division of labor is highly specialized. 

The lower four legs are thin as hair, tipped with minute, suction-cupped pads that allow them to walk with equal ease across the vertical glass walls, the ceiling, or the surface of a stationary bubble of water. Their walking is a sequence of light, crystalline clicks—*tink, tink, tunk*—as their silicate feet touch the obsidian. 

The upper two limbs are their pride and their survival. These are the "needles"—long, jointed appendages of pure, hardened glass-chitin that end in three opposable, thorn-like fingers. These fingers are so sensitive they can feel the individual heat signatures of the bacterial cells they harvest. To watch a Tender work is to watch a jeweler operating on a grain of sand.

They do not have eyes like ours. In this hot, lightless world, eyes are useless. Instead, their heads are topped by a pair of long, sweeping antennae that resemble spun-glass wool. These fibers are incredibly sensitive to infrared radiation and vibrational frequencies. A Tender can "see" the exact geometry of the fissure by detecting the thermal drafts rising from the deep, hot veins of the mountain. They can spot a drifting water-globe from three feet away simply by the tiny temperature differential between the liquid and the sulfur-thick air.

They do not live as individuals so much as a continuous, quiet conversation. 

Because the air is too wet and dense to carry high-frequency sound, they communicate through the stone. They use the obsidian splinters bound to their forelimbs to strike the glass walls. Each strike sends a vibration through the mountain’s black skull. A single, sharp *clink* might mean *“the water here is sweet.”* A rapid, rhythmic drumming means *“the orange mat is ripening; bring the bowls.”* When hundreds of them are harvesting along the deep, impossible miles of the inner labyrinth, the mountain itself seems to hum with a delicate, metallic music, like a thousand wind chimes blowing in a draft.

Their society is small, dense, and intensely cooperative. They are the tenders of this mineral garden.

They do not hunt the other inhabitants of the crack with cruelty; they manage them. They herd the flat, pale grease-worms, guiding them toward the thickest parts of the orange bacterial mats where the worms feed, and then harvesting the clear, fatty secretions the worms leave in their wake. They use this fat, mixed with the ground-up shells of dead glass-mites, to create the grey, cement-like paste found in their tiny bowls. This paste is their medicine and their mortar; when a Tender cracks its outer shell on a sharp edge of basalt, others will gather around it, using their needle-fingers to gently smear the grey paste over the wound, sealing it until the living silica can regrow.

The hovering, gravity-defying globes of boiling water are their pastures and their nurseries. 

A team of three or four Tenders will crawl out onto a glass ridge and wait for a floating orb to drift near. With their long antennae, they calculate its trajectory. As it passes, they cast out incredibly fine, sticky threads of worm-silk, anchoring the bubble in mid-air. Once the bubble is secured, they use it as an incubator. They deposit their tiny, egg-like spheres—no larger than dust motes—into the center of the hot water. 

There, suspended in the weightless, boiling heart of the globe, the young Tenders pass their larval stage, swimming like microscopic, glass-plated tadpoles, feeding on the crimson hydras that also colonize the water. When the young are ready, they pierce the surface tension of their bubble-home, climb out onto the silk tethers, and take their place on the solid stone.

They have lived in this fissure for eras, unchanged and unaffected by the turning of the ages in the valley six thousand meters below. To them, the "Outside" is a mythical, terrifying void. 

On rare occasions, a draft of cold, dry air from the great peaks will pierce the front of the fissure. To the Tenders, this is the "White Death." It causes their thermal antennae to go blind with cold, and the water-globes they rely on to freeze or burst. When the cold air comes, they retreat deep into the three-inch crack, into the impossible, folded miles of the interior where gravity spins in circles and the earth’s blood is always hot.

They are small, patient, and endlessly meticulous. They do not dream of the stars, nor do they care for the vast green world below. Their entire cosmos is a room of wet glass, five yards wide on the outside, infinite on the inside, warmed by the fire of a young world. In this crowded, silent cradle, they continue their work, scraping the orange velvet, herding the grease-worms, and tapping their ancient, metallic songs into the black stone.
