In this static, white-noise world, life does not fight the stone; it has long since concluded that the stone is the only correct way to be. 

To look closely at the coin-shaped creatures in the flat basins—the **Nummulae**—is to realize they are not soft. They are lard-colored, yes, but it is a cold, dense, tallowy fat, more like paraffin than flesh. They do not have organs in any sense that a warm-blooded thing would recognize. Inside their ribbed, circular bodies, there is only a concentric series of water-filled chambers separated by thin, calcified membranes. 

They do not "think," but they are not inert. They are acoustic engines. 

Because the columns above them vibrate with a constant, bone-shaking drone, the water inside the Nummulae is always in motion, dancing in complex Cymatic patterns within their circular chambers. If the pitch of a nearby column rises by a fraction of a vibration—perhaps due to a tiny mineral grain shifting in its throat—the water pattern inside the Nummula shifts with it. This shift in internal pressure is their only sensation. It is their hunger, their sleep, and their direction. They glide across the wet sinter at a rate of an inch a year, propelled not by muscles, but by the minute, rhythmic expansion and contraction of their outer rings in response to the singing of the stone.

***

Above the Nummulae, on the vertical flanks of the pillars themselves, lives a second, stranger order of things: **The Glass-Weeps**.

They look like long, translucent roots or pale, weeping tresses of hair, hanging from the top apertures of the columns where the water first issues. They do not have cell walls of cellulose; instead, they are composed of a flexible, hydrated silica gel—essentially, living opal. 

The Glass-Weeps are entirely dependent on the high-pressure whistle of the escaping water. They hang directly in the throat of the capillary fountains, their gossamer threads vibrating so rapidly in the jet that they appear only as a wet, shivering blur. This vibration is not accidental; it is their mouth. The rapid, high-frequency shaking of the silica threads creates a localized vacuum that draws in the microscopic mineral ions—sulfur, iron, and silica—suspended in the rising water. 

They do not grow longer by cell division, but by accretion. They allow the minerals in the water to deposit directly onto their own bodies, slowly petrifying their older, lower sections while the upper sections remains soft, gelatinous, and vibrating. When a Glass-Weep becomes too heavy with its own mineralized weight, the bottom half snaps off with a clean, glassy *clink*. The broken segment falls into the wet crevices below, where the Nummulae slowly crawl over it, scraping off the soft silica glaze to feed.

***

But the true masters of the crevices—the things that keep this world from sealing itself shut into a solid, silent block of stone—are **The Shales**.

They are the only things here that possess a semblance of scale. A Shale is thirty feet tall, three inches thick, and perhaps six feet wide. They are vertical ribbons of grey, gristly cartilage, perfectly shaped to fit the narrow, wet gaps between the pillars. 

They have no face, no eyes, and no limbs. Instead, their broad, flat sides are covered in thousands of tiny, overlapping scales of slate-like calcite. They look like giant, wet Emery boards sliding through the maze.

The Shales are the maintenance crew of the plateau. Because the water constantly deposits calcium carbonate, the narrow gaps between the columns are always threatened by clog-growth; if left alone, the pillars would eventually fuse together. The Shales prevent this. They are in constant, slow, hydraulic motion. They move by drawing the weeping water into pores on their leading edge and pumping it out through channels in their trailing edge—a slow, piston-like propulsion that forces their flat bodies through the tightest crevices.

As they slide, the rough, calcified scales on their sides grind against the columns. It is a terrible, scraping sound, entirely lost inside the deafening roar of the stone organ pipes. They file away the excess sinter, keeping the fissures open, preserving the path of the water and the space for their own existence. 

They do not "see" the blinding pearl-light of the plateau, but their skin is photoreactive. The outer margin of a Shale—the narrow edge that faces the sky and receives the full, crushing glare of the sun—is a deep, burnt-ochre color, toughened by silver compounds precipitated from the mineral water. The deeper parts of its body, wedged in the perpetual, cold shadow of the columns, are a translucent, milky white. If you were to pull a Shale out of its crevice and lay it flat under the white sky, it would look like a giant, hand-carved photographic plate, its surface etched with the vertical shadows of the forest it inhabited.

***

There is no death here, not in the way of the green world. There is no rot, because there are no bacteria to dismantle the dead. 

When a Shale grows too calcified to pump its hydraulic water—when its joints become too stiff with iron and silica deposits—it simply slows down. It crawls into a narrower crevice, wedging itself tightly between two pillars, and stops. 

The water does not stop. The columns above it continue to weep their mineral-rich sweat over its body. Within a few decades, the minerals fill the microscopic pores of the Shale’s cartilage. Its gristly plates are replaced, molecule by molecule, with calcium carbonate. 

The Shale does not decay; it becomes a new ridge on the column. It becomes part of the flute. The whistle of the pillar changes by a fraction of an octave, adapting to the shape of the creature that was just absorbed into its flank. 

The eternity of this place is built on these petrified transitions. The pillars are not just stone; they are a vertical graveyard of everything that has ever lived there, stacked layer upon layer, singing their silent, mineralized histories into the blinding white light.
