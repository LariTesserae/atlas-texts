To understand what lives in the basin, one must first dismiss the concept of the wild. There are no predators here, because there is no prey; there is no hunger, because there is no metabolism that relies on the destruction of another’s tissue. The life here does not struggle against the machines; it is the machine's wet, self-healing upholstery.

### The Copper-Lice (The Bronze Beetles)

They are called the *shiverers* by the rhythm of their movement, though they have no name for themselves. Each is a perfect, seamless hemisphere of bronze, so highly polished that when they cluster on the sagging copper conduits, they reflect one another’s rounded backs in an infinite, fish-eye mosaic. 

Inside that metallic shell, there is no blood. There is only a core of damp, grey tallow wrapped in a web of hair-thin copper wire. They have no mouths; they do not eat. Instead, their thirty-two needle feet are tipped with minute solder-points that they press against the green-stained insulation of the power lines. They live entirely on the "hum." 

When the current through the thighs of the conduits rises, the beetles swell. The bronze of their backs grows warm to the touch—not the fever-heat of flesh, but the dry, static warmth of an old transformer. When they divide, it is not through mating. A beetle that has sat over a particularly rich leak of electricity will grow heavy and dull-colored. Its shell will split down the middle with a sharp *ping*, like a cooling casting, and two smaller, brighter halves will slide out onto the copper wire, their needle-legs already tapping out their frantic, dry-peas-in-a-can rattle. 

If they fall from the wires to the wet basalt floor, they do not climb back up. Without the magnetic pull of the conduits to orient them, they crawl in diminishing circles until their legs rust together. There they remain, small, dull-golden domes, until the wire-crabs find them.

### The Calcates (The Scraping Crabs)

On the wet floor, where the black magnetite sand pools like oil, live the *calcates*. They are the size of tea-plates, three-legged and jerking. 

Their bodies are not chitin, but a heavy, chalky lime that has grown over frames of twisted iron wire. They look like the fossils of some ancient, three-jawed crab that has been dug up and reanimated by an amateur clockmaker using only rusted wire and boiled leather. They have no heads, only a central, vertical seam that splits their calcified carapace in half. 

They do not hunt. Their entire lives are governed by a single, simple tropism: they are drawn to the green patina of oxidation. When a conduit leaks its pale-green salts, or when the damp slate walls grow a crust of mineral mold, the calcates gather. They scrape the metal with their flat, spatulate front limbs—sounding like dry fingernails dragging across a slate blackboard—until the metal beneath is bare and grey again. 

Once their hollow, lime-walled bellies are full of these green scrapings, they trot with a stiff, clockwork gate toward the nearest circular basin. They stand on the rim, tilt their bodies forward, and vomit the green powder into the still, shimmering water. The water does not turn turbid; the green mineral instantly dissolves into a clear, glowing turquoise solution that remains perfectly flush with the lip. Having emptied themselves, the calcates turn back to the pipes. 

They have no death. If a calcate’s leg snaps, it does not heal; the wire inside simply becomes more exposed to the battery-acid air until it rots through. The remaining parts are slowly ground down by the feet of their fellows into the black sand of the basin floor, their lime bodies returning to the water as calcium, their wire bones returning to the iron mud.

### The Sleeven (The Biological Bellows)

High above, hanging from the gantry-works like rows of cured meat, are the *sleeven*. 

They are the larger, softer kin of the basin. To look at them is to look at something that was once an animal but has been simplified by centuries of single-purpose existence. They are six feet long, pale as lard, and covered in a thick, rubbery sebum that protects them from the sulfurous steam of the chimneys. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no bowels. 

Each sleeven is fused to the iron gantry by its "root"—a mass of black, fibrous muscle that has grown around the rivets and bolts, sealing itself to the metal with a glue so strong it cannot be pried loose without tearing the creature apart. 

They are the lungs of the basin. When the great subterranean pump beneath the basalt floor goes *thug*, a pulse of high-pressure steam is forced through the lead vent-pipes. The sleeven, sensing the pressure wave through their iron anchors, open their single, vertical slit of a mouth. They inhale the hot, greasy vapor. For three seconds, they hold it; their skin turns from lard-white to a faint, bruised lavender as the copper-rich moisture is absorbed into their internal air-sacks. 

Then, as the pump goes *chew*, they contract. The air they expel is cool, dry, and entirely stripped of its acid scent. It is this constant, biological scrubbing that keeps the air in the basin from becoming so corrosive that the iron plates would dissolve within a decade. 

If a sleeven is injured—if a falling bolt punctures its leathery side—it does not bleed red. It leaks a thick, grey starch that hardens upon contact with the air into a seal as tough as vulcanized rubber. They do not seem to feel pain; they only adjust their rhythmic breathing, their pale, blind bodies swaying in unison with the iron beams that hold them.

### The Slip-Skins (The Grease-Tenders)

In the deep shadows behind the two-story power cells, where the oil-seeps are thickest, live the *slip-skins*. 

These are the only things in the basin that have hands. They are roughly the size of a ten-year-old child, but their proportions are long and spider-like. They have no skeleton; their limbs are stiffened instead by high-pressure fluid sacs that act as hydraulic cylinders. Their skin is not skin at all, but a dark, oil-soaked membrane that they shed twice a year in long, greasy ribbons that look like discarded fan-belts.

The slip-skins have no faces. In place of eyes, they have a single, thick disc of bottle-green glass set directly into the gristle of their foreheads. This lens does not see light; it sees *vibration*. Through it, the basin is a landscape of violent, beautiful waves—the deep, red thrum of the pumps, the sharp, violet crackle of the porcelain insulators, the cold, blue hum of the conduits.

The slip-skins are the grease-tenders. They crawl through the narrow spaces between the boiler-plates, their long, grey fingers slick with the cold oil that oozes from the slate. They do not speak. They communicate through *tapping*—striking their wooden-hard knuckles against the iron pipes in complex, rhythmic patterns that can be heard throughout the basin as a low, musical clicking.

They are driven by a singular, physical distress: friction. If a bearing in a pump-station begins to run dry, it emits a high-pitched, screeching vibration that is invisible to the eye but blindingly bright to the slip-skin’s green lens. The sound causes them physical pain; their limbs twitch, and their skin secretes a thin, yellow tallow. 

To stop the pain, they will crawl across the wet slate, locate the dry bearing, and smear it with the cold geological grease they harvest from the rock cracks. They will work for hours, their long fingers working the fat into the dry teeth of the gears, until the screeching subsides into the comfortable, low-frequency *thug-chew* that means peace.

### The Auditor of the Seventh Cell

In the center of the basin, inside the largest of the riveted boiler-plate huts, sits the closest thing the place has to a mind. 

It is called the *Auditor*. It does not move. It has not moved in four hundred years. It is a mass of yellow, tallowy flesh that has grown to fill the interior of a lead terminal box, its body adapting to the shape of the iron walls like dough rising in a pan. 

The Auditor is blind and deaf to the world outside the box. However, its flesh is shot through with thousands of fine silver wires that have been carefully soldered into the copper terminals of the main distribution board. It is literally wired into the basin's nervous system.

Every spark that crawls across the porcelain insulators, every drop of oil that falls from the slate, every rattle of the bronze beetles passes through the Auditor’s wet, grey brain. It does not think in words; it thinks in *potentials*. It balance the load. If the northern wall of the basin becomes too cold, the Auditor’s skin twitches, sending a tiny current down a copper lead that opens a steam valve three hundred paces away. If the water in one of the basalt pools falls by a fraction of an inch, the Auditor’s liver-colored flanks contract, signaling the slip-skins to clear the drain-lines.

The Auditor is not a ruler; it is a slave to the equilibrium. It cannot change the design of the basin, nor can it stop the pumps. It is simply the organ through which the machine feels itself. 

Sometimes, when the condensation is particularly heavy and the violet sparks on the insulators are long and slow, the Auditor will produce a low, bubbling sound from the depths of its lead box—a sound like a man trying to speak through wet flannel. It is the only voice the basin has. It does not complain; it does not pray. It merely reports, to the empty, mist-shrouded air, that the voltage is stable, the grease is thick, and the great wheel is still turning.
