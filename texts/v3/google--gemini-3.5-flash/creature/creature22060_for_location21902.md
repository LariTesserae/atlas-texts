They are called the Silt-Dry, or more simply, the Chapped. 

To look upon one of the inhabitants of the Saddle is to look at someone sheathed in a fine, protective carapace of their own environment. Their skin is not moist like the skin of the lowlanders; it has the texture of heavy, well-oiled vellum. It is a deep, warm copper-brown, stained yellow at the knuckles and elbows from the wax-reed sap they work with daily. They do not possess sweat glands as those from wetter worlds understand them. Instead, their skin secretes a thick, cedar-scented sebum—an organic varnish that seals their bodies against the parching wind, keeping their internal moisture locked deep within their dense, heavy-boned frames. 

Because they do not sweat water, they do not smell of salt or sour brine. Their presence is heralded by the scent of warm wax, bitter almonds, and the faint, chalky sharpness of the pink silt they use for their hygiene.

They are a people of remarkable physical economy. Their chests are extraordinarily broad and barrel-shaped, housing lungs of immense volume that can extract oxygen from the thin, dusty air without forcing them to pant. They move with an almost agonizing slowness that is actually a form of perfect efficiency. To watch a Silt-Dry woman carry a bundle of dry tallow-wood up a five-tiered stone ladder is like watching a shadow crawl across a sundial: there is no jerkiness, no sudden intake of breath, no hitch in the knees. Every step is a deliberate, sliding shift of weight, her broad, flat feet—wrapped in layers of soft, oil-treated fibers—binding silently to the yellow sandstone.

Their eyes are perhaps their most striking feature. To survive the constant, glittering suspension of mineral dust in the copper sky, they have evolved a secondary, semi-transparent eyelid—a nictitating membrane that is amber-colored and clean. When the wind rises and the air becomes thick with floating pink grit, they draw these inner lids closed, giving them the appearance of sleeping birds or blind statues of polished chalcedony, though they can see through the membrane with perfect, amber-tinted clarity.

### The Scrape-Wash

In the late afternoons, when the heat of the canyon walls reaches its peak and the stone is comfortingly hot to the touch, the communal terraces become scenes of quiet, rhythmic movement. This is the hour of the *Scrape-Wash*.

Because water is never touched—indeed, to drop water upon a Silt-Dry’s skin would cause painful cracking and irritation, like hot grease hitting mud—the family group gathers on the flat roofs to clean one another. One person lies prone on the warm sandstone while another stands over them with a scraper made from the curved rib of a mountain-swift or a long, flat flake of polished bone. 

First, they take handfuls of the pink silt, which is mined from the deep crevices at the back of the Saddle. The powder is soft as flour and smells faintly of ancient iron. They rub it vigorously into the skin, where it binds with the old, dust-laden body oils. Then, using the bone scraper with long, certain strokes, they peel the lavender-grey paste away. 

The sound of the scraping—*shrr-clack, shrr-clack*—is one of the fundamental rhythms of the Saddle. It leaves the skin underneath glowing, clean, dry, and smelling of the mountain. The discarded scrapings are not thrown away; they are gathered into small clay pots, where they are used as a base for mortar to seal the gaps in the sandstone houses, or as fuel for the small, smokeless tallow-lamps that burn in their inner chambers.

### The Lizards and the Loom-Keepers

The Silt-Dry share their homes with several other species, none of whom are treated as pets, but rather as roommates in a vertical hive.

The most numerous are the **Blue-Tailed Clickers**. These are heavy-bodied ground lizards, about the length of a man’s forearm, with scales that look like overlapping shingles of blue slate. They live in the hollow spaces between the sandstone blocks of the houses. They are tolerated—and indeed welcomed—because they feed exclusively on the heavy, fat oil-flies that would otherwise spoil the stored wax-sap. 

The clickers communicate through a tongue-clicking mechanism that sounds like two dry pebbles being struck together. When a Silt-Dry family enters a room, they will often click their own tongues against the roofs of their mouths—a polite greeting that reassures the lizards and prevents them from scurrying into the food jars. It is said that a silent house in the Saddle is a dead house; if the clickers leave, it means the sandstone is shifting or a deep vent has opened below, venting toxic sulfuric gases from the earth's veins.

In the higher, more precarious curtains of wax-vine that hang over the mile-deep drop-off live the **Saddlers' Swifts**. These birds are entirely pale grey, matching the color of the dry-moss, and have wings so long and narrow they resemble scythes. They do not know how to walk; their feet are tiny, specialized hooks used only for clinging to the vertical rock faces. 

The Silt-Dry have a symbiotic relationship with these birds. The swifts weave their nests out of their own rubbery saliva and the fine, silvery threads of the dry-moss. The people harvest these nests from the lower ledges. When boiled in the warm hydrocarbon oils drawn from the geothermal fissures, the nests dissolve into a tough, flexible leather-like substance that can be beaten into sandals, straps, and protective aprons.

### Language of the Chasm

Because the Saddle is an acoustic funnel, speech among the inhabitants is completely different from the spoken languages of the lowlands. They do not use loud, open-throated vowels, which would bounce off the canyon walls and create a confusing, deafening echo. 

Instead, they speak in a language of wet sibilants, dry clicks, and low, humming tones that match the resonant frequency of the rock. A mother can call her child from three levels down with a sound that is no louder than a cricket’s rub, yet because of the shape of the stone alleys, the sound travels straight to the child's ear like a physical touch. To a foreigner, a conversation between two Silt-Dry would sound like the wind rustling through a heap of dry leaves, broken occasionally by the deep, throat-click of agreement.

They are a people who have no word for "river" or "rain." In their mythology, the sky is not a source of water, but a vast, dry lung that breathes the mineral dust in and out. They believe that the world below—the white salt desert—is a place of punishment, a flat, silent hell where there are no walls to hold the sound, and where a man’s voice would simply drift away into the nothingness, forever unanswered.

When a Silt-Dry dies, they do not rot. The hot, dry air and the paraffin-rich diet preserve them. They are carried to the **Deep Flue** at the very back of the Saddle—a vertical shaft where the hot, dry air rises from the earth's core. There, they are hung by their feet from copper hooks. Within a few weeks, the wind has dried them into hard, light, mahogany-colored figures. 

These "Ancestors of the Vent" are eventually brought back into the town. They are not worshipped, but they are respected as architectural elements. A particularly sturdy ancestor might be used as a lintel over a doorway, or as a support beam for a new terrace. In this way, the grandparents of the household continue to hold up the roofs of their children, their dry, varnished hands forever gripping the yellow stone, silent and stable as the domes above them.
