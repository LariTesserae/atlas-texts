To look closely at the walls—past the hanging, leathery fronds and beneath the dripping curtains of star-moss—is to discover that this wet, roaring pocket is not merely a garden, but a body. Every surface is occupied by a life that does not merely tolerate the heat and the deafening noise, but uses them as the very scaffolding of its existence.

The most visible inhabitants are the **Verdigris-Drifters**. 

These are the tiny, shell-less snails, though to call them snails is to simplify a creature that has long since discarded the need for armor. In a room where there are no predators, no dry winds, and no sheer drops, a heavy shell is a useless expenditure of calcium. Instead, their bodies are small, oblong dollops of pale-blue jelly, no larger than a rain-soaked bean. Their skin has the matte, chalky texture of dense suede, completely saturated with the copper salts they draw from the walls. 

They do not possess eyes, nor do they need them in the unwavering, flat yellow glare of the ceiling filaments. In place of sight, their entire upper surface is covered in microscopic, stiff hairs that act as acoustic receptors. To a Drifter, the deafening, 100-decibel roar of the turbine and the sharp hiss of the ruptured boiler pipe are not noise; they are a geography. The vibration through the copper plates is a physical map. A high-pitched, tooth-rattling frequency tells them they are approaching the scalding heat of the steam leak, prompting them to turn back; a deep, slow, liquid thrumming indicates a cool, algae-rich hollow where the water pools. They drift across the metal plates with glacial, oil-slick progress, their circular, scraping mouths leaving tiny, bright trails of polished bronze in the blue-green rust.

***

Beneath the Drifters, buried in the thick, dark-red peat of the corners and the rotted under-layers of the star-moss, lives another creature: the **Silt-Strands**.

At first glance, these appear to be discarded pieces of copper wire, left behind by whatever ancient hands assembled the chamber. They are long, stiff, jointless annelids, barely thicker than a sewing needle, pigmented with a dark, metallic red that is indistinguishable from the iron-rust they inhabit. They do not crawl; they flex. They move through the wet silt with a slow, hydraulic logic, bending their copper-hardened bodies in response to the electric currents leaking from the perished rubber gaskets overhead. 

The Silt-Strands are the true janitors of the room. They are lithovores, feeding directly on the flaky iron oxide and the copper filings washed down from the ceiling seams. They process this metallic diet, extracting trace impurities and depositing the remainder as a highly refined, magnetic sludge. This sludge, sticky and dark as axle grease, acts as the perfect mortar for the star-moss, binding the green carpet to the slick, vertical bronze walls so that it is not washed away by the constant weeping of the ceiling. 

***

But the true master of this chamber is not the snail or the worm, but the **Loom-Moss** itself. 

The moss is not a collection of individual plants, but a single, ancient, self-regulating colonial organism that has achieved a kind of slow, vegetative intelligence. It has grown into the very joints of the machinery. Its roots do not seek soil; instead, they have threaded themselves into the screw-threads, the seams of the overlapping plates, and the hollow interiors of the dead copper conduits. 

The Loom-Moss behaves like a living seal. Wherever a draft of the cold, vacant dark from the miles of empty space outside tries to penetrate the seams of the room, the moss grows thicker, denser, and more fibrous. It senses the drop in temperature and the loss of moisture as an injury, and it heals the gap with a thick, springy plug of split leather-fern roots and wet liverwort. It is the moss that keeps the room pressurized, warm, and sealed against the vacuum of the gargantuan structure around it. 

The plants are also electrically alive. By wrapping their moist, iron-rich root-networks around the ancient wiring behind the brass plates, they draw tiny, imperceptible trickles of current from the generator below. They do not use this electricity for light, but for warmth; in the rare moments when the steam-hiss falters, the moss-carpet pulses with a faint, resistive heat of its own, keeping the blood-warm basins from cooling.

***

At the very bottom of the room, in the largest, deepest basin where the floor plates curve upward to meet the iron walls, lies the singular heart of this mini-wilderness. 

It is a shape that has no name, known only by its slow, rhythmic weight. It is the **Accumulator**.

This is an entity the size of a wet, discarded mattress, semi-submerged in the warmest pool of copper-salts and rust-peat. It is a composite being—a slow, dense fusion of thousands of dead Drifter bodies, calcified snail-mouths, decaying fern-stems, and a specialized, pale-blue colonial amoeba that binds them all together into a rubbery, fibrous lump. 

The Accumulator does not move from its basin. It has no head, no limbs, and no senses other than a profound sensitivity to weight and moisture. It is the ultimate sink of the room’s closed loop. As the water drips from the ceiling, carrying down the iron peat and the copper salts, it all eventually flows into the Accumulator's pool. The entity slowly absorbs these minerals, filtering the water like a great, heavy sponge and exhaling a dense, humid vapor that smells of hot lard and sweet peat. 

In its slow, centenary breathing, the Accumulator rises and falls by only a few inches every decade. It is the final stage of the room's metabolism. It has grown so dense with the metallic sediment of a thousand years that it has become a literal part of the room’s architecture—a damp, living flywheel that absorbs the bone-shaking vibration of the external turbine, cushioning the fragile copper walls from the crushing weight of the silence outside.
