In this basin of warm, wet jade, the concept of a "fauna" must be redefined. Here, life does not struggle against its environment; it is a continuation of it, cast from the same green mold, using the same starch-rich fuel, and designed with the same clean, geometric logic. 

The things that live here are fewer than one would expect, and they are beautiful in the way that perfectly balanced plumbing is beautiful. They have no teeth, because there is nothing to tear. They have no weapons, because there is nothing to fear. 

***

### The Milk-Sliders (*The Curds*)

The most common inhabitants of the vast, wet plazas are the white gliders described by the rare travellers who have looked down into the basin. The locals of the lower world, if they knew of them, might call them *The Curds*. 

They are six feet of dense, un-biodegradable milk-white flesh, shaped like a long, thick teardrop. They have no eyes, no ears, and no external mouth. Instead, they are pressurized envelopes of warm, milky starch-water, contained within a skin that feels like cold, wet lard. 

They do not wander. Each Slider is bound to a single, engineered runnel in the jade pavement. They move along these channels with a glacial, magnetic slide, propelled by a single, continuous muscular foot that fits perfectly into the runnel’s U-shaped groove. They are the valley’s vacuum cleaners. As they slide, their porous undersides press against the wet cellulose, drawing in the faint, green film of microscopic yeast-algae that forms where the metronomic drip meets the light.

When two Sliders meet in a channel—an event that may happen only once every several decades—there is no conflict. They cannot turn around, nor can they climb out of their grooves. Instead, they press their blunt noses together. Over the course of three or four days, their skins fuse at the point of contact. They exchange pressure, balancing the volume of their internal starch-fluids until they are equal. Then, with a soft, wet sound like a split melon, they divide again, having swapped chemical memories of the water-flow, and slide backward along the paths they came.

### The Whistling Bladders (*The Melodians*)

High above, suspended in the warm, rising drafts between the monumental arches, drift the *Melodians*. 

These are transparent, bladder-like organisms, some as small as a human head, others the size of a carriage. Their skins are thin as dry onion peel, catching the light like oil on water. They carry no gas of their own making; instead, they are filled with the hot, light vapor that rises from the valley’s high, steaming vents.

The Melodians are the valley’s ears and lungs. Trailing beneath each bladder are dozens of hair-thin, golden threads—not tentacles for hunting, but long, resonant fibers of dry cellulose. As the wind pours through the oval apertures of the high arches, it brushes against these threads, causing them to vibrate like the strings of an eolian harp. The vibration is carried up into the hollow chamber of the bladder, which amplifies the sound. 

Each Melodian is tuned to a slightly different frequency of the valley’s great, bass drone. When the water pressure in the pillars rises, the sound pitches higher, and the Melodians respond, contracting their skins to sink lower into the cooler air. When the valley’s heartbeat slows, they expand and rise toward the canopy. They are the only clock the valley has, rising and falling in a slow, silent ballet that measures the pressure of the great green pump.

***

### The Parchment-Clocks (*The Husk-Walkers*)

Inside the colossal, hollow pillars that dot the plazas, the environment is different. Here, it is dry. The wet, roaring circulation of the vascular tubes is sealed behind feet of thick, green wood; the central chambers of the columns are filled with warm, stagnant air that smells of dry malt and toasted grain.

Here live the *Parchment-Clocks*.

They look like jointed spindles of dried papyrus, six-legged and roughly the size of a dog. Their bodies are completely hollow, constructed of thin, amber-colored bark-plates that rattle when they move. They have no flesh; their joints are moved by the expansion and contraction of dry, air-filled bladders that react to the heat of the walls.

The Clocks are the only things in the valley that make a dry sound. They spend their centuries climbing the interior walls of the pillars, using soft, resinous pads on their feet to cling to the wood. They feed on the dry spores that drift down from the ceiling vents. 

Their movement is entirely rhythmic. They do not walk so much as they tick. One leg moves, then a pause of exactly ten minutes; then the next leg moves. If you were to climb inside a pillar, you would hear thousands of them, scattered across the dark heights, producing a dry, scattering *tick-tack... tick-tack* that sounds like a clockmaker’s workshop left running in an empty warehouse.

***

### The Pith-Tables (*The Registers*)

In the very center of the widest plazas, sitting on the wet, jade-colored rind, are the *Registers*. 

They are easily mistaken for architectural features or dry mounds of peat. They are massive, flat, pentagonal discs of dense, golden-brown pith, thirty or forty feet across and several feet thick. They do not move. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no senses other than a total, exquisite sensitivity to vibration.

The Registers are the memory of the valley. Their flat tops are dry and soft, like cork, but their undersides are rooted directly into the wet cellulose floor of the plaza, fusing with the valley’s nervous pavement. 

Over hundreds of years, the Registers "listen" to the vibrations of the water-pipes and the wind-flutes. This constant hum causes their fibrous bodies to grow in subtle, concentric rings of differing density. A map of their growth is a map of the valley's history: a thicker ring of gold-pith marks a century where the sky-canopy was unusually warm; a dark, dense line marks a moment three thousand years ago when a single, rare bolt of lightning struck the northern cliff.

If one places a hand on the dry, warm surface of a Register, one does not feel a pulse, but a strange, soothing coolness. They absorb the heat of the valley’s roar, converting the endless sound into slow, silent, physical weight.

***

### The Caulkers

The valley is self-repairing, but it does not heal through cells; it heals through the *Caulkers*.

These are small, pearlescent spheres, ranging from the size of a grape to the size of a boulder, that roll along the runnels. They are perfectly round and have no appendages. They are made of a dense, rubbery white latex that is slightly sticky to the touch.

They are driven by gravity and the flow of the water, rolling endlessly through the channels like marbles in a groove. However, they are not entirely passive. When a Caulker rolls over a spot where the jade pavement has been scratched—perhaps by a falling piece of dry wood from the canopy—the drop in hydraulic pressure beneath the rind acts as a chemical signal.

The Caulker stops rolling. It anchors itself over the scratch. Over several hours, the sphere begins to soften, melting into the wound like warm wax. It fills the crack perfectly, its white latex reacting with the green sap of the floor to turn a deep, translucent jade. Within a day, the Caulker is gone; it has become a seamless part of the pavement, and the water flows over it once more, uninterrupted and clean.

In this valley, there is no room for the dead. Every organism, when its time is done, does not rot—it simply dissolves back into the starch-water, or becomes the very floor upon which the others slide. It is a world where to live is to be part of the lease, and to die is to become the house.
