The people who live within the throat of the Flue do not look like those of the sunlit tablelands above, nor do they look like the soft, damp damp-dwellers of the low river valleys. They are creatures of dry friction.

In the Flue, a human body is an assembly of hinges and anchors. Their skin has a dry, matte texture—not the oily sweat of the lowlands, but a powdery, grey-white finish, as though they have been dusted with talc or powdered slate. This is the "drift-skin," a dry-adaptation that prevents the basalt dust and graphite flakes from sticking to their pores and festering. Their eyelashes are unusually long and crosswise, forming a fine, protective thatch over wide, ink-dark irises that have lost the ability to constrict quickly. When they blink, it is not a wet snap, but a slow, dry scrape of the lids that makes a faint, rustling sound, like two moths rubbing their wings together in a drawer.

To live in a city that is constantly sliding requires a different kind of foot. The Flue-folk do not walk so much as they peg. Their heels are thick and yellowed, hardened into horned pads by constant contact with iron rungs and zinc plates. Most wear "latch-boots"—footwear with vulcanized soles split down the middle, allowing them to pinch the copper cables between their great toe and the outer edge of their foot. A seasoned climber can run down a fifty-yard tension-line as easily as a squirrel runs down a pine branch, their hands held outward to balance their short, thick-chested torsos. 

They are small-statured, having evolved or bred down to fit the narrow passages of the suspended hives. Their bones are dense, heavy, and less prone to fracturing under the sudden, bone-snapping jerks of a shifting cable-tier.

### The Social Architecture of the Hitch

In a world where one’s house might drop three feet during breakfast because a basalt shelf has settled, the concept of neighborhood is entirely fluid. The basic unit of society is not the family or the ward, but the *Hitch*. 

A Hitch is a group of twelve to twenty zinc cabins that share a single primary counterweight-train. If the counterweight slips, they all slip; if their corner of the cliff faces a "pinch" (where the chasm walls narrow during a seismic murmur), they must work together with hand-winches to crank their entire cluster of homes up or down the guide-lines. 

Consequently, Flue-folk are intensely collaborative but socially distant. They do not visit one another for idle talk. To walk from one cabin to another requires crossing open mesh-ways where the draft can snatch the breath from your mouth, and every footstep on the iron walkways causes a neighbor’s floor to vibrate. Privacy is maintained through an absolute, sacred silence. To make an unnecessary noise—to drop an iron pan, to slam a zinc latch, or to raise one’s voice above a dry whisper—is considered an act of social violence.

Their children are born into harnesses. A "rigging-pup" is strapped into a canvas sling before it can crawl, suspended from a miniature ceiling-track in the family hut. As the child grows, the sling is lengthened, and they learn the physics of the chasm before they learn the names of things. They learn that a vertical rope is a friend, a horizontal wire is a highway, and a diagonal cable is a hazard that must be crossed with a hook. A toddler’s first game is "swing-tag," played in the three-foot void between the bottom of their home and the roof of the tier below. They rarely fall; those who have the instinct for falling do not survive to breed.

### The Specialized Castes

Among the human population of the Flue, three specific groups have emerged, each shaped by their relationship to the vertical world:

**The Bitter-Skinners (The Larders)**
These are the oil-hunters. They are the only people who do not have the dry, slate-colored skin of the upper tiers. Instead, they are stained a dark, glossy mahogany by the rancid mineral oils they scrape from the basalt runnels. They spend their days suspended in bosun’s chairs over the deepest, darkest drops, using iron trowels to scoop the thick grease into zinc backpacks. 

They smell perpetually of bitter almonds and stale lard. Because they are constantly lubricated, they are the only ones who can squeeze through the "choke-points"—cracks in the basalt so narrow that a normal climber would be wedged fast by their own dry skin. The Bitter-Skinners slide through these black crevices like grease-soaked rags to inspect the anchor-bolts of the deep-well pumps. They are silent, solitary, and have a strangely loose-jointed way of moving, as if their bones were held together by grease rather than tendons.

**The Bite-Talkers (The Tensioners)**
If the Bitter-Skinners are the grease of the Flue, the Tensioners are its nervous system. They are thin, stringy individuals who carry "bit-rods"—short bars of cold-rolled steel with a notched grip on one end. To check the health of a sector, a Tensioner will clamp the notched end of the rod between their teeth and press the other end directly against a main suspension cable. 

By feeling the vibration through their jawbone and skull, they can tell if a cable is "singing" too high (nearing its breaking point) or "slacking" (allowing the houses to sag into each other). They communicate this tension-data across the chasm using a code of sharp, click-like whistled pulses that sound like dry pebbles hitting a windowpane. They are universally deaf to air-conducted sound, their eardrums having been ruined by the constant, high-frequency scream of over-tightened steel, but they can hear a pin drop if it falls on an iron plate three tiers away.

**The Rust-Scrapers**
The lowest and most frantic class, consisting mostly of the old and the very young. Their sole job is to combat the "rust-bloom." Armed with stiff brass wire-brushes and small flasks of tallow, they climb the structural rods, endlessly scrubbing away the orange-red mineral rings. Left unchecked, the rust-bloom behaves like an active cancer; it does not merely coat the iron, but devours it, turning solid steel rods into brittle, orange toothpicks that snap with a dry *tink*. The Rust-Scrapers are easily identified by the bright orange dust that stains their fingers, noses, and the rims of their dark eyes, giving them the appearance of weeping rust.

### The Non-Human Inhabitants

The humans of the Flue share their vertical cage with a strange, dry ecology that has evolved to thrive on iron, grease, and gravity.

**The Brass-Mites (The Cable-Wefts)**
These are tiny, six-jointed arachnids no larger than a grain of sand, with shells that match the dull yellow of tarnished brass. They do not eat organic material; instead, they feeding on the microscopic copper flakes shed by the spinning pulley-lines. They live in long, woolly colonies within the core of the twisted hemp-and-iron ropes. 

As they move through the fibers, they exude a dry, waxy spit that behaves as a natural binding agent, preventing the rope-strands from fraying. The Flue-folk treat these mites with great care; a cable that has a healthy "weft-glow" (a faint, brassy sheen) is known to last three times longer than a "dead" grey line. If a climber finds a section of cable that has gone grey, they will carefully transplant a handful of the brass-mites from an older line, blowing them onto the dry rope like dandelion seeds.

**The Wall-Gliders (The "Grey-Paddlers")**
These are the apex predators of the Flue's mid-levels. They are about the size of a cat, but flat, like a dried leather puddle. They have no eyes—there is nothing to see in the blue-black twilight—but their skin is covered in thousands of tiny, stiff hairs that can detect the slightest change in air pressure. A swinging cabin, a falling bolt, or a human hand reaching for a basalt shelf will cause a glider to flatten itself against the stone, its grey skin mimicking the basalt texture so perfectly it seems to vanish.

They hunt the grease-ticks, but they are also opportunistic. If a small child is left untethered or if a climber is injured and hangs motionless in their harness, the gliders will gather. They do not attack with teeth; instead, they have a wide, circular mouth on their underside lined with hundreds of tiny, rasping ridges. They press themselves against the skin of their prey and "mow" it, scraping away the dry outer layers of skin and fat until the victim bleeds to death from a thousand tiny abrasions. Their move is silent—just that soft, dry *shhh-shhh* of their leathery bodies sliding over the stone, a sound that every Flue-child learns to fear more than the groan of the settling canyon.

**The Deep-Loomers**
At the very bottom of the Flue, where the light of the grease-lanterns does not reach and the oil pools in stagnant, black lakes forty feet deep, lives something larger. No one has ever seen a Deep-Loomer in full, for there are no lights down there that can survive the thick, oxygen-poor air. 

But the workers at the deep tunnels know they are there. They describe them as massive, slow-moving shadows that rise from the oil pools like bubbles of cold tar. They are segmented, slow-pulsing things that feed on the endless rain of detritus—coal-dust, dropped tools, dead grease-ticks, and the occasional human body—that falls from the city above. 

When a Deep-Loomer moves, it does not hiss or click. It produces a very low, heavy *thrum-thrum-thrum* that is felt in the back of the throat rather than heard. It is a sound that matches the natural resonant frequency of the chasm itself. When the Deep-Loomers hum, the tensioners stop their work, hold their bit-rods to their teeth, and listen to the dark, waiting to see if the mountain is going to speak back.
