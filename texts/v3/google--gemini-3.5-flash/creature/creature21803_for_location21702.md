To understand what lives in this pocket of basalt, one must first understand that here, life is not carbon seeking to persist; it is geometry trying to remember itself. 

### The Glass-Gilled Creepers (*The Pleomeris*)

The dozens of translucent, comb-like creatures that slide across the flat basalt faces are the *Pleomeris*. They are not soft, despite their tissue-paper weight. If you were to touch one, it would feel like a cold, dry slide of micro-glass—stiff, unyielding, and sharp-edged. 

They have no organs, no lungs, and no blood. Instead, their clear bodies are shot through with microscopic tubes—parallel vacuums that run the length of their three-inch bodies. They move by a process of rhythmic displacement, creating tiny, perfect seals against the mineral planes and releasing them with a faint, dry hiss. They do not eat the lavender lichen, nor do they hunt. Instead, they "scrape." They are the janitors of the unfinished. 

The basalt, being un-weathered, occasionally attempts to sweat out its remaining impurities—microscopic crystals of silica or salt that rise to the surface of the cleavage planes like white rash. The *Pleomeris* glide over these deposits, their glass-comb legs grinding the salt back into the stone, polishing the basalt until it achieves the oily, friction-free blackness that allows the water droplets to bead. They are the keepers of the flat. If they were to stop, the dust of the world above would eventually settle, the stone would grain, and the pocket would begin to age.

### The Chondri (The Rain-Seekers)

The aggressive, drifting spheres with their hundreds of feathery cilia are known as the *Chondri*. They are the only things in the pocket that possess a hunger, though it is a mechanical one. 

In a world where water behaves like oiled mercury, the *Chondri* are the collectors of liquid state. They drift in straight, slide-rule trajectories through the cold, bright air, their cilia beating with a soundless, frantic blur. They are hunting not for meat, but for *fluidity*. 

When two *Chondri* collide, they do not fight; they solve an equation of volume. The less-dense sphere is instantly "solved" by the denser one. The loser’s structural integrity collapses; its air-cilia snap inward, and its entire substance is reduced to a single, perfect bead of water that drops instantly to the stone below. The victor absorbs the loser’s "light," becoming slightly more grey, slightly more opaque, and slightly more heavy. 

Once a *Chondri* has devoured five or six of its kind, it becomes too heavy to fly. It sinks to the steps of the basalt, its cilia freezing into a hard, crystalline shell. There, it sits for weeks, slowly leaking its stored moisture into the rock-face in those strange, upward-rolling beads, until it is completely hollowed out—a dry, husk-like sphere that eventually splits along five perfect lines to release a dozen new, tiny, transparent hatchlings into the air.

### The Scribe of the Seams (*The Folia*)

The builder of the four-inch dry-stone wall is not a group, but a single, ancient entity that occupies the center of the pocket. It does not live *on* the stone; it lives *between* the laminations of it.

It is called the *Scribe*, though it has no eyes and no hands. To the casual glance, it looks like nothing more than a pile of thin, greasy slate flakes resting in the shadow behind the semi-circular wall. But if you watch it for an hour, the flakes will begin to slide past one another with a sound like a deck of playing cards being slowly shuffled in a small room.

The *Scribe* is flat. It is an entity of two dimensions that has been forced to exist in three. Its "body" is a series of stacked, slate-like leaves, each no thicker than a single red blood cell, composed of a dark, waxy mineral that smells faintly of cold fat and old iron. 

It is fascinated by the "outside." 

The rodent bone in the center of the wall is its library. To the *Scribe*, the bone is an impossible heresy: it is curved. It has interior voids that are not geometric; it has marrow-scars that follow no crystal habit; it is made of calcium that grew by *stretching*, not by cleavage planes. 

Because the *Scribe* cannot comprehend a cylinder, it is trying to translate the bone into a language it understands—the language of the page. Using its razor-thin margin-planes, which can slide into spaces smaller than a wavelength of light, the *Scribe* has been slowly, over generations, peeling the bone. It slides its slate-leaves into the grain of the femur, lifting up layers of calcium a thousandth of an inch thick and laying them down on the basalt like the silvered pages of an open book. 

It is "reading" the rat. It has been reading the rat for three hundred years. 

If you were to reach out to touch the bone, the *Scribe* would not attack you; it has no teeth or venom. But the space around the wall—which is folded and dense—would catch your hand. Your fingers would appear to taper into long, grey needles as they entered the micro-scale of the wall. If you persisted, you would find that the skin of your fingertips would begin to feel dry and papery, as if the *Scribe* were gently trying to find the "seams" in your flesh, curious to see if you, too, could be unrolled into a series of flat, readable sheets.

### The Vagrant Shadows

Perhaps the most active "living" things in the pocket are those that have no mass at all: the shadows of the *Pleomeris*.

In this room, light and mass have different speeds. Because the white glare of the air is so intense and the air itself is so thin, a creature's shadow is not a passive absence of light; it is a physical footprint left behind on the basalt. 

When a glass-comb creature moves from one step to another, its shadow remains behind on the grey-black cleavage plane. These lagging shadows have their own, slow chemistry. They are cold—even colder than the surrounding rock—and they act as anchors. If a water droplet rolls across a "free" shadow, the droplet will freeze instantly into a tiny dome of ice, only to melt back into liquid the moment the shadow finally rushes forward to rejoin its owner.

Sometimes, a shadow loses its creature entirely. 

If a *Pleomeris* is consumed or falls into a dry fissure, its shadow remains printed on the stone, flat and dark, like an inkstain. These dead shadows do not vanish. They migrate. They slide slowly across the steps, searching for anything that casts a shape. 

If you stand in the room long enough, your own shadow will begin to stretch toward them. You will feel a dull, heavy ache in your calves and heels—the physical weight of your shadow trying to peel itself off the floor to join the silent, two-dimensional congregation that crowds the corners of the basalt steps. They are waiting for you to become still enough, for your breath to stop fogging the ceiling, so they can lay their flat, cold shapes over yours and finally solve the equation of your depth.
