Those who live here are not visitors; they are of the same marrow as the Sieve. 

If you were to watch the dark space between two massive brick pillars for long enough, the shadow itself would eventually seem to gather, thicken, and separate from the masonry. This is one of the **Moulders**. 

They are tall, but they rarely stand to their full height. To do so would be to invite a cracked skull against the low-hanging lintels and the jutting stone corbels. Instead, they exist in a state of permanent folding. Their joints do not click; they slide with the wet, grease-smooth silence of damp clay. A Moulder's skeleton is not made of dry, white bone, but of a dense, dark cartilage that remains flexible throughout their long, working lives, only hardening into calcified rigidity in their final years.

Their skin is the matte, gray-livered red of the unfired mud-bricks. It has no hair, no pores, and no sweat glands. Instead, it is constantly damp with a fine mist of condensation, identical to the moisture that weeps from the walls. If you were to press your hands against a Moulder’s ribs, your fingers would leave shallow, distinct indentations that would take several minutes to rise and smooth themselves out again—the very same indentations that mark the hex-tiles beneath your feet. 

They have no eyes. In this world of perpetual cellar-gloom and tallow-smoke, eyes are an unnecessary delicacy, too easily blinded by lime-dust or damaged by flying shards of stone. Where eyes should be, there are only smooth, sloping plates of bone covered by a thick layer of sensitive shroud-skin. They "see" through their feet and their palms. The soles of their wide, flat feet are incredibly thin-skinned and packed with thousands of microscopic nerve endings. Through them, they read the Sieve. They can feel the vibrations of a limestone roller three miles away; they can tell if a basin is filling with water by the subtle change in the ringing pitch of the hex-tiles; they can sense the approach of another Moulder by the rhythmic, heavy thump of their gnarled knees.

Their faces are dominated by their mouths and their nostrils. The nose is not a ridge but two vertical slits, constantly flaring to taste the damp air. They smell the sulfurous rot of the river muck, the fatty sweetness of the smoldering tallow, and, above all, the dry, electric scent of lime. Their mouths are wide and lipless, containing rows of flat, yellow-gray teeth that are not sharp but broad and ridged, like the grinding stones of a hand-mill.

They do not eat as surface creatures eat. There are no kitchens because there is nothing to cook, and no teeth designed for tearing meat. Instead, they are *mineral-feeders*. 

A Moulder will kneel in one of the shallow, three-inch-deep basins where the water gathers. Using their long, spade-like fingers—which have no nails, only hard, horn-like pads at the tips—they scoop up the gray silt that collects in the corners of the hex-tiles. They work this silt in their mouths, mixing it with their thick, starchy saliva, extracting the microscopic iron-deposits, the dissolved sodium, and the spores of the liver-colored lichen. What remains—a small pellet of clean, depleted clay—is spat out and immediately pressed into the seams of the nearest brick wall to serve as mortar. 

They also harvest the "salt-wool." A Moulder will drift along the narrow corridors, their long fingers brushing the dry mortar lines. When they find a curl of the dry, white sodium wool, they pluck it and press it directly into their mouths. It is their only source of salt, and it keeps their internal fluids from turning fresh and sluggish.

Because they do not sleep as we understand it, they have no domestic quarters. When a Moulder is exhausted from pushing the limestone rollers or beating the clay, they do not look for a bed. They simply find a wet corner where two walls meet at an angle. They press their backs into the corner, knees drawn up to their chests, head tucked down. In this position, their bodies perfectly match the contour of the masonry. Their skin dries slightly, turning from gray-red to a dusty, chalky pink. They look like nothing more than an extra buttress or a pile of discarded, sun-dry bricks. While they sleep, their bodies absorb the condensation from the wall behind them. They drink through their skin, pulling the lime-thick moisture directly into their flesh.

There are three distinct "castes" or stages of Moulder life, though they are not born into them; rather, their bodies morph to suit the work they are given to do.

First are the **Paddlers**. These are the young, whose cartilage is still soft and wet. They sit in the muddy basins where the fresh silt is mixed with fish oil and river muck. Their forearms and wrists are monstrously overdeveloped, thick as birch logs and ending in hands that are nearly circular. They do not use tools to shape the clay; they are the tools. They beat the raw silt into the wooden molds with rhythmic, bone-deep thuds—*thunk, thunk, thunk*—their hands acting as shovels and rammers. Their skin is constantly coated in a thick slip of gray clay, making them look like half-risen statues.

Second are the **Rollers**. These are the mature, the heavy-framed. Their chests are broad and plated with calluses of thick, yellowish horn where they press their bodies against the heavy wooden beams of the limestone rollers. They work in teams of six or eight, blind and silent, leaning their entire weight forward into the dark, pushing the massive stone cylinders across the newly laid flats. They do not speak. Their communication is a matter of shared weight; if one stumbles, the others feel the shift in the roller’s timber and adjust their lean instantly. Their legs are bowed and thick, their feet wide as snowshoes to distribute their weight across the unfired tiling.

Lastly, there are the **Groomers**. These are the old ones whose joints have begun to calcify. They can no longer push the rollers or swing the heavy clay-paddles. They also carry the "slick-stones"—flat pieces of river-pebble used to polish the hex-tiles in the high-traffic corridors, wearing down the edges until the stone is glass-smooth.

When a Moulder dies—or rather, when they *dry*—there is no grave. 

As a Groomer reaches their final years, the lime-water they have drunk for decades finally triumphs over their blood. Their joints lock permanently. Their fingers stiffen into stone trowels. Their skin becomes dry, brittle, and indistinguishable from gray-red brick. 

When they feel the petrifaction reaching their throat, the old Moulder will crawl into an unfinished section of wall, or find a doorway that is no longer needed. They will wedge themselves tightly into the gap, arranging their limbs to match the surrounding coursework. The transition is slow. For weeks, they remain there, half-flesh, half-stone, their heartbeat slowing to one thump every ten minutes, then one an hour. 

Other Groomers will arrive with buckets of the thick, fish-oil mortar and trowels of river-stone. They plaster over the gaps between the dying Moulder's fingers, their ribs, and the surrounding masonry. They smooth the clay over the face, filling the nostrils and the mouth-slit until the surface is flush with the wall.

By the time the heart stops, the Moulder is no longer a resident of the Sieve. They are the Sieve itself—another brick in the endless, heavy wall, holding up the miles of dark stone overhead, while below them, the next generation beats the wet clay, *thunk-thunk-thunk*, into the dark.
