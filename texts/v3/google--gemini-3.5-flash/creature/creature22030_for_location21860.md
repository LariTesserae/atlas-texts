Because the water has no teeth, there is no need for shells. Because gravity has grown soft, there is no need for bone. 

The life that has taken root in this heavy, emerald sky is life that has forgotten how to resist. It is an ecology of yielding, where the boundary between a creature and its wet house is as thin as the film on a bubble. 

Here is what drifts, slides, and dreams in the warm, slow deep.

***

### The Mantles

Across the massive, pillow-like domes of grey basalt lie the **Mantles**. To a distant eye, they look like nothing more than discarded cloaks of heavy, dark-green felt, draped over the curves of the stone. But they are alive, operating on a clock so slow that their breathing is felt only as a change in the water’s temperature.

A Mantle is an animal that has become a rug. It has no head, no limbs, and no internal organs to speak of; its body is a single, four-inch-thick sheet of fibrous, blood-warm muscle, up to twenty feet across. The upper side is covered in a dense, mossy pile of purple-grey cilia that constantly combs the water for the glowing copper-green phytoplankton. The underside is slick, secreting a pale, sticky milk that anchors it to the basalt against the gentle drag of the thermoclines. 

If you press your hand into a Mantle, your fingers will sink deep into the warm, doughy flesh. The impression will remain there when you pull your hand away—a perfect, pale mold of your palm. It will take three days for the Mantle’s slow, viscous circulation to push the dent back out, erasing the memory of your touch. 

They do not migrate. They do not seek mates. Once every century, when the Spore-Tides turn the water blue, two Mantles whose edges are touching will slowly run together, their flesh melting into one another at the seam until they are a single, larger cloak, dreaming the same green dream.

### The Ooliums (The Dry-Marrow)

Inside the valleys, suspended within the massive, translucent spheres of the bladder-ferns, live the **Ooliums**. They are the only creatures in this world that are dry. 

An Oolium is no larger than a plum, resembling a tiny, long-limbed gibbon made of dry lint and silver down. They have huge, spherical eyes—completely black and dry—that occupy nearly the entire upper half of their faces. They spend their entire lives clinging to the mossy earth inside the carriage-wheel-sized gas bubbles, looking out through the thin, rubbery walls of their plant-prisons. 

To them, the outside world of wet amber is a terrifying, glowing eternity. They can see the massive, floating kelp-forests and the great, drifting Skeins, but to touch the water would be instant death; their woolly fur is so absorbent that the slightest leak in their fern would fill them like a sponge, drowning their tiny, hot hearts in seconds.

Consequently, their lives are frantic, silent, and hyper-vigilant. They move with nervous, clicking jumps, grooming the moss inside their spheres, catching the microscopic, dry-land mites that live in the ferns' soil, and licking the condensation off the inner walls. 

Because they cannot cross the wet voids between the ferns, they are isolated in their tiny, green bubbles of history. But occasionally, the slow currents or the shifting gravity will bring two bladder-ferns together. For a few days, the spheres will press against each other, their skins flattening into a shared wall. The Ooliums on either side will gather at this window, pressing their tiny, dry palms against the pane, staring into the neighbor-world. If the skin between them wears thin enough to tear, they do not cross; they only whisper to each other through the seam—a high, dry, cricket-like clicking—before the currents drift them apart once more.

### The Weft

Around the melting basalt staircases and beneath the floating jade rings, one finds the **Weft**. These are the descendants of whatever mind built this place, though they have long since surrendered the shape of the builders to the leniency of the deep.

A Weft is a ribbon of pale, semi-translucent cartilage, twelve to fifteen feet in length and only a few inches wide. They look like strips of peeled birch bark or skin that have been left to soften in vinegar. They have no faces, no eyes, and no limbs. At the top of their long, ribboned bodies is a single, circular whorl—like a fingertip print carved in grey wax. This is their sensorium, a delicate, folding knot of nerves that reads the water's currents, its sweetness, and its age.

The Weft do not swim; they drape. You will find them lying across the steps of the giant spirals, three or four of them piled together like wet linens, rising and falling with the tide. If you drift down beside them, they do not slide away. Instead, they will slowly uncoil, their paper-thin edges curling around your arms or ankles with a touch that is cool, smooth, and entirely without malice. 

To be touched by a Weft is to experience the breakdown of the plateau’s physics firsthand. When they wrap around you, the distance between your eyes and your hands seems to double; you may feel your left arm stretching out into a long, amber corridor while your right foot feels as though it is resting on a mountain miles away. They do not hurt; they simply distort. They are living lenses, bending the geometry of the space around them to protect their ancient, static resting places.

### The Mercury-Tethers

Silt does not lie still. Because of its weight and its liquid-like properties, it is constantly moving down the basalt slopes in slow, silver rivers. Living in these rivers are the **Mercury-Tethers**.

These are small, teardrop-shaped creatures that look like droplets of polished chrome, about the size of a walnut. They have no eyes or mouths, but they possess a single, long, feathery antenna that is four times the length of their bodies. 

They use this antenna to anchor themselves to the dark-green serpentine beneath the silt. They do not walk; they "slide." They ride the slow-moving ribbons of silver dust down the slopes, like children sledding down a hill of cold grease. When they reach the bottom of a slope, where the silt pools, they use their feathery antennae to pull themselves slowly, hand-over-hand, back up the naked rock to the summit of the dome, only to slide down again.

If you scoop a handful of silt, you will often find three or four Mercury-Tethers sitting in your palm, perfectly still, cold and heavy as lead. They do not struggle. If you drop them back into the water, they do not swim; they sink like stones, disappearing into the silver grooves with a tiny, metallic *clink* that can only be felt through the teeth.

***

### The Glints

The *Skeins*—the great, three-mile-long ribbon-beings that drift through the water like transparent highways—are not alone. They carry with them their own sky-dwelling parasites, the **Glints**.

The Glints are small, triangular plates of living, copper-colored bone, no larger than a maple leaf. They have hundreds of tiny, hook-like teeth on their undersides, which they use to fasten themselves in neat, geometric rows along the transparent sides of the Skeins. They look like scales, but they are individual, greedy things.

They do not feed on the Skein itself; rather, they are commensal. They ride the great beast through the thermoclines, using its long body as a plow to gather the sweet-smelling kelp-mucilage and the glowing phytoplankton. As they feed, the Glints digest the copper within the water, converting it into a bright, cold blue light that they emit in rhythmic pulses.

When a Skein passes overhead, it is not merely a shadow; it is a three-mile-long zipper of blue-and-violet light, flashing in patterns that match the sub-bass thrumming of the giant’s nervous system. The Glints coordinate their flashes, sending miles-long waves of blue fire down the Skein’s length—a silent, electrical language that can be read by every creature on the plateau, signaling that the great, slow heart of the sky is still beating.

***

### The Pine-Spurs

In the rare, floating "pockets" of old water—those drift-spaces that taste of dry dust and cold pine needles—lives a kind of life that cannot exist in the wet emerald sky. These are the **Pine-Spurs**.

They are tiny, star-shaped crystals of brown-and-gold silica, about the size of a grain of salt. They do not eat, and they do not reproduce in any way that the modern world understands. They are fragments of the old world’s weather, frozen in time and preserved by the peculiar memory of the water.

When you swim through one of these dry, pine-scented pockets, the Pine-Spurs will briefly cling to your eyelashes, your hair, or the skin of your shoulders. Against the warmth of your body, they will begin to sing—a dry, crackling sound like autumn leaves being crushed under boots, or the sound of rain on a tin roof that has been dead for a million years. 

The moment you drift out of the old pocket and back into the thick, warm, amniotic water of the plateau, the Pine-Spurs instantly dissolve. They leave behind only a faint, prickling sensation on the skin, like the memory of a cold wind, and a single, sour drop of amber grease that floats away into the green.
