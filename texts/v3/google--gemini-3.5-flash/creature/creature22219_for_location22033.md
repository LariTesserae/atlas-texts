In the damp, resonant hollows of this endless monument, those who live here have become the soft marrow of the stone. 

They are things of skin, knuckle, and weight, but they are not like any creature that has walked upon wild fields. If they were once the builders of these plazas, they have long since surrendered the sharp-edged thoughts of architects. They have smoothed out. They have grown damp, quiet, and perfectly adapted to the verticality of their world.

They are pale—not the stark white of cave fish, but the translucent, warm pink of wet clay before it is fired, veined with faint lavender and indigo lines where their blood runs close to the surface. They have no hair. Instead, their skin is covered in a microscopic, velvet-like pile, similar to the underside of a peach or the fuzzy moss that carpets the basalt. This texture is not for warmth, for the air rising from the abyss is always thick and humid; it is a sensory array. It traps the constant, falling mist and detects the slightest shifts in the slow updrafts that rise from the dark.

Their bodies are long, low, and heavy. They do not walk upright; to stand erect in a world of narrow ledges and slick, vertical drops is an unnecessary risk. Instead, they flow across the masonry on four broad, flat limbs. Their hands and feet have lost the distinct divisions of fingers and toes; instead, they have split into two wide, fleshy pads, like the cloven hooves of a goat but soft and textured with thousands of tiny, suctioning ridges. With these, they can cling to a vertical wall of fired brick, even when it is slick with algae, holding fast for hours without effort. Their joints are incredibly supple, double-and-triple-hinged, allowing them to turn their heads entirely backward or flatten their chests against the wet stone to squeeze through drainage conduits no wider than a bucket.

They are nearly blind. In the permanent twilight of the high crevices, eyes are of little use, and in the blackness below, they are liability. Their eyes have Receded—they are small, milky spheres, hooded by thick, protective folds of skin that keep out the dripping water and the powdery spores of the giant ferns. They see only the distinction between the faint, gray line of the sky above and the heavy, lightless dark below. 

Instead, they see with their soles, their palms, and their chests. 

The roaring wall of sound—the clicks, the croaks, the whistles of the billions of insects and birds—is not noise to them; it is their landscape. They navigate by tactile resonance. Every surface of the masonry vibrates with the sonic energy of the ecology. A solid basalt pillar hums differently than a hollow brick conduit; a terrace choked with moss absorbs the vibrations, creating a "shadow" of silence that they recognize as a safe bed. 

They do not speak with voices. The air is too noisy for local speech, and their lungs are designed for deep, slow drafts of humid air, not the rapid vibrations of vocal cords. Instead, they speak by shivering. 

When two of these dwellers meet on a narrow stair, they do not look at each other. They press their chests together, or lay their flat palms against each other’s temples. Through their skin, they send rhythmic, muscular tremors—heartbeat-like pulses, rapid flutters, and deep, warm waves of heat. A mother can teach her infant the path to a reliable water basin by shivering the map into the child’s back while they cling to each other in the dark. To an observer, they would seem to be sleeping, molded against one another like sacks of wet dough, but beneath the surface, they are engaged in a silent, high-speed exchange of memory and sensation.

Their life is slow, governed by the same lethargic rhythm as the giant snails they live alongside. They do not hunt the snails; they cultivate them, though "cultivate" is perhaps too active a word. They live in a state of quiet, mutual tolerance. The dwellers climb the buttresses to find the great, dog-sized snails, and they use their wide, horn-like fingernails to gently scrape the yellow lichen from the snail's shell—a delicacy for the dwellers, and a relief for the snail. In return, they harvest the thick, clear slime the snails leave behind. They smear it over their own skin to protect themselves from the abrasive grit of the brickwork, and they drink the clean water that pools in the hollows of the empty shells.

When they are hungry, they eat the starch-rich, corky roots of the ancient figs, or they reach into the hollow friezes to pull out the fat, blind grubs that feed on the bird droppings. They have no teeth of iron or enamel; their jaws are lined with broad, flat plates of hardened cartilage that can grind the toughest ferns into a wet, green paste.

There are two distinct groups of these people, though they are not enemies, for they rarely meet, and when they do, they merge without friction:

There are the **Climbers**, who live in the high terraces where the slate-gray twilight reaches. They are slightly smaller, more compact, and their velvet skin is often patterned with greenish patches where microscopic algae have taken root in their fuzz, giving them the appearance of walking mounds of moss. They are the ones who follow the birds and the snails, moving horizontally along the vast, stepped aqueducts, drinking from the moss-choked basins. They are light-dwellers, and they have a soft, whistling sigh they use to locate one another across the wide plazas—a sound that blends perfectly with the piping of the roosting birds.

Then there are the **Deepers**, who live in the absolute blackness below the terraces, where the air is hot, thick, and smells of ancient, fermenting brick. The Deepers have lost even the memory of the sky. They are larger, longer-limbed, and completely white. Their skin is smooth, lacking the velvet pile of the Climbers, but their fingers have grown long and thread-like, like the antennae of deep-sea prawns. They spend their lives suspended in the massive, vertical elevator shafts and ventilation wells of the old monument, climbing down into the depths where the masonry has begun to soften back into clay, and then climbing back up when the warm, spore-rich updrafts signal that the lower chambers are flooding with condensation.

Neither group knows anything of the world beyond the stone. They do not know that there is an end to the terraces, or that stars exist, or that there was ever a time when the ground did not hum. 

If they have a religion, it is the **Great Hold**. To fall is the only sin, the only death. To fall is to let go of the wet, warm brick, to slide off the edge of a terrace into the warm updraft, and to descend forever into the blackness where the sound eventually swallows you whole. 

Because of this, their dead are not buried; they have no dirt to dig into, and they will not burn wood that is too damp to ignite. Instead, when a dweller feels their grip failing—when their joints grow stiff with the slow, chalky calcification that comes to them in their final decades—they crawl to the oldest, most root-choked corners of the plazas. They climb into the wooden embrace of the great, wax-like fig roots. They wedge themselves into the deep, dark crannies between the root and the basalt, and they simply stop moving. 

Over the course of years, the slow, crawling wooden skin of the tree grows over them, sealing them into the monument. They become a knot in the wood, a slight thickening of the column, a permanent buttress that holds the ancient bricks together for another thousand years. Progress here is not marked by what is built, but by what is slowly, damply absorbed.
