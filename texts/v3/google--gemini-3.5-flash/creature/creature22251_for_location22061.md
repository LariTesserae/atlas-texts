To live in the pocket is to be an element of the machine. The creatures here do not struggle against the cold, eighty-second cycle of the water; they are its valves, its bushings, its gaskets, and its grease. They have no use for soft flesh, wide-spectrum eyes, or the slow, meandering thoughts of the sunlit world above. Their lives are measured in barometric changes and the hard, rhythmic *thrum-shudder* of the basalt floor.

***

### The Slide-Crusts

The most numerous inhabitants are the slate-gray isopods, known in the cold ecology of the cleft as **slide-crusts**. They are about the size of a thumbnail, but they are heavy for their scale, their carapaces reinforced with layers of deposited iron-carbonate and zinc that give them the dull, metallic weight of pewter fishing sinkers.

Unlike their terrestrial relatives, the slide-crusts have no legs designed for walking. Instead, their undersides are lined with twelve pairs of short, hook-tipped tines made of hardened, amber-colored silica. These tines are pitched at an angle that allows them to climb the copper-colored *Equisetum* pipes with incredible speed, but only in one direction: upward. 

When the water-tide begins its rapid, three-inch rise, the slide-crusts do not flee. They climb the six-foot metallic stalks in dense, overlapping sheets, zipping upward like zippers being pulled by invisible threads. As they move, their hard shells clatter against the rigid, petrified silica segments of the plants—a dry, frantic *click-shiver* that sounds like a pocketful of small brass nuts being shaken in a fist. 

At the height of the tide, when the water holds its breath for three seconds and the chamber becomes a dark, flat mirror, the slide-crusts lock themselves together. They slide their hexagonal outer plates under one another’s margins, forming a solid, armored collar around the top of each horsetail pipe. This collective shielding protects the hollow, silica stalks from being snapped by the lateral force of the surging water. They are the plant’s external reinforcement; without them, the heavy, metallic stalks would break under their own mineral weight.

### The Choke-Borers

Inside the hollow chambers of the six-foot copper horsetrails lives a species of blind, segmented larvae called **choke-borers**. They are four inches long, perfectly cylindrical, and as white and dense as nylon bushings. 

The choke-borers do not eat the plant; indeed, the horsetails are too heavily petrified with silica and iron to be digestible. Instead, the larvae feed on the microscopic, iron-oxidizing bacteria that coat the inside of the pipes. 

To move within the plant’s hollow stem, the choke-borer does not crawl. Its white, rubbery body is ringed with spiral ridges like the threads of a screw. By twisting its segments in a slow, corkscrew motion, it rises and falls inside the metallic tube. 

These larvae act as the sliding slide-valves of the natural pipe organ. As the rising water forces cold air up through the horsetail stalks, the positions of the choke-borers within the stems dictate the pitch of the whistling sighs. A reed containing three larvae near the bottom produces a low, mournful drone; a reed where the larvae have screwed themselves toward the top whistles with a sharp, piercing hiss. 

By adjusting their depth to escape the cold water rising into the stems from below, the larvae unconsciously tune the chamber. They are the musicians of the pocket, translating the hydraulic pressure of the glacier’s heart into a complex, shifting chord.

***

### The Lard-Strips

Of the larger inhabitants, the most peculiar are the lungless salamanders, known as **lard-strips**. Ribbon-thin, up to nine inches long, and entirely devoid of bone, they have the consistency of chilled pork fat. 

The lard-strips are blind, possessing only two dark, light-sensitive pits where their eyes should be. They have no ribs; their body shape is maintained by high internal fluid pressure, much like the water-piston that rules the cave. If one is removed from the cold humidity of the cleft, it loses its tension and collapses into a flat, shapeless grease-smear on the dry stone.

Their lungs have been replaced by four pairs of external, feathery gills that sprout from the sides of their necks like bright orange combs. These gills are rich in copper-based blood, which allows them to extract oxygen not only from the icy water but from the thin, ozone-charged air that lingers in the chamber. 

The lard-strips are the system's "grease." They spend the dry forty seconds of the cycle squeezed into the hair-thin gaps between the basalt floor-joints, their soft, mucous-covered bodies acting as seals that prevent the iron-rich water from spraying out too early. 

When the eighty-second *thrum-shudder* begins, the lard-strips feel the vibration through their entire bellies. They slip out of the cracks just ahead of the gray-green surge, sliding across the wet stone like spilled oil. They do not swim; they are carried by the rising tide, their orange gills vibrating like tiny turbines as they drift through the horsetail forest, vacuuming up the loose slide-crusts that failed to lock themselves to the stalks.

***

### The Gaskets

Deep within the dark, finger-wide gaps of the hexagonal pavement, where the ice-cold water first emerges, there is a living layer that never sees the dim sky. It is not an animal, nor is it a plant, but a colonial, rust-colored lichen-hydroid known simply as **the gaskets**.

The gaskets grow in thick, leathery, half-inch ropes that line the edges of the basalt joints. They are composed of a tough, fibrous core of pure cellulose impregnated with iron oxide, covered in a living, slippery skin that secretes a dense, chemical grease. 

When the water-piston rises, the sudden pressure swells the gaskets like hydraulic seals. The fibers absorb the water and expand by nearly thirty percent, narrowing the gaps in the stone and forcing the gray-green water to emerge in high-velocity, pressurized jets rather than a lazy flood. 

When the siphon-phase begins and the water is pulled back down into the mountain, the gaskets release their moisture, shrinking back into the crevices and clearing the path so the debris of the chamber—the shed carapaces of the slide-crusts, the waste of the lard-strips—can be vacuumed down into the deep glacial reservoir below.

***

### The Pale-Glow

When the daylight fails and the pocket is illuminated only by the faint, cold phosphorescence of the mineral crust, another life form becomes visible. This is **the pale-glow**, an iron-eating bacterium that grows in thin, greasy sheets across the sheer basalt walls.

The pale-glow is the source of the ozone smell that fills the cleft. As it metabolizes the iron and nickel salts carried by the water-spray, it releases microscopic bubbles of charged gas that crackle and pop in the humid chill. 

The light it emits is not the warm, yellowish bioluminescence of the forest floor; it is a hard, blue-white, industrial twilight. In this dim illumination, the entire pocket looks like the inside of a massive, wet turbine. 

One can lie on the cold basalt floor—if they can tolerate the eighty-second drenching—and watch the light rhythmically dim and brighten. When the water rises and coats the walls, the phosphorescence is muffled, glowing like light through frosted plate-glass. When the water drains and the ozone gas is released, the light flares up, cold and sharp, casting the long, needle-thin shadows of the whistling copper reeds against the wet, blue-black stone.
