To sit at the edge of the limestone cup and watch the silent thrashing of the water is to realize that the basin is not empty of life, but rather that its biology has long since folded itself into the anatomy of the machine. The division between the metal, the wet stone, and the flesh has been scoured away by centuries of friction. 

What lives here is soft, waxy, and quiet.

If you crawl down onto the first shelf of wet tallow-rock, where the copper lead-pipes run like bundles of veins, you will notice that the "greasy skin of dark green liverwort" is not entirely vegetable. Beneath the mossy green surface, the crust has a slow, peristaltic ripple. This is the **Shudder-Moss**. It is a colonial, lichenous organism that behaves less like a plant and more like a massive, static sponge for kinetic energy. It grows thickest where the vibration of the ironwork is most intense—around the collars of the fast-sliding pistons and the brackets of the screaming, silent copper dials. 

The Moss is the reason for the silence. Its underside is carpeted with billions of microscopic, hollow cilia that press against the metal, drinking the high-frequency shrieks and rattles of the gears before they can escape into the cold air. If you strip a patch of it away with a fingernail, it has the cold, heavy feel of pork fat, and for an instant, a tiny, shrill metallic whistle will escape the exposed metal—a brief, naked sound that is immediately swallowed by the surrounding green skin. Without the Shudder-Moss, the basin would be a deafening, bone-shattering chamber of iron noise; with it, the machines work in the hushed, reverent quiet of a crypt.

In the seams where the iron joins the stone, tucked behind the leaking bronze flanges, live the **Calkers**. 

These are the largest creatures in the basin, though they are difficult to distinguish from the gray, rain-washed limestone. A Calker is roughly the size of a sleeping dog, but its body has no bones, only a series of thick, cartilage rings that rattle softly inside its tallow-skinned torso when it moves. They are the color of lard and have no eyes, no limbs, and no faces. Instead, one end of their body tapers into a ribbed, rubbery suction cup that locked onto the copper tubing decades ago.

The Calkers are the living sealants of the system. They taste the pressure changes in the pipes through their skin. When a gasket begins to weep, or when a seam in the lead piping grows thin under the corrosive rush of the salt-mist, a Calker will slowly, with agonizing patience, drag its heavy, waxy body over the leak. It clamps its mouth-cone over the breach and begins to secrete a thick, yellowish stearin—an organic grease that hardens in the cold water into a patch as tough as vulcanized rubber. They feed upon the lime-scale and the bitter iron rust that collects in the joints, digesting the mineral waste and turning it into the very wax that keeps the boilers airtight. They do not migrate unless the pipe they are tending goes cold; if a boiler dies, the Calkers attached to it will slowly wither, drying into hard, black nodules that look like plugs of old pitch.

In the deep, churning sumps beneath the iron distillation boilers, the water is a milky, turbulent emulsion of oil, rain, and limestone runoff. Here, where the white, heatless boiling is most violent, the **Siphon-Blobs** drift.

These are the larval stages of the basin’s hidden fauna. They look like translucent, pale balloons, no larger than goose eggs, dragging long, thread-like tails of copper-colored fiber behind them. They have no volition; they are swept up by the intense underwater drafts, sucked into the intake ports, and shot upward through the glass-walled tubes in violent, frothing bursts. This is their nursery. The terrifying velocity of the water does not harm them; their skins are incredibly elastic, designed to be stretched and compressed by the frantic pulse of the pumps. As they fly through the glass tubes, they filter-feed on the microscopic iron flakes and sulfur-bacteria suspended in the spray. Sometimes, through the glass, you can see them spinning in the violent gyres of steam, their tiny, pale forms glowing faintly with a cold, phosphorus light under the flat gray sky.

Beneath them all, scuttling through the deep, oil-slick grooves of the basalt floor, are the **Marrow-Crabs**—the white, lard-like mud-crabs that are the only creatures here with legs. 

They are the janitors of this frantic equilibrium. Their shells are not hard chitin, but have the greasy consistency of bar soap. They have no claws for fighting; instead, their front limbs end in small, spatula-like scraper plates. They follow the Calkers, scraping up the excess yellow wax that drips from the seals, and they graze on the dead, water-logged fragments of Shudder-Moss that peel off the pistons. They are entirely blind, guided only by the deep, low-frequency hum that rises from the bedrock. When a Marrow-Crab dies, it does not rot; it has so little organic protein in its body that it simply dissolves into the lubricating grease of the sumps, its waxy shell melting down to grease the very tracks its descendants will scuttle across.

There is no competition here. There are no predators, no prey, and no struggle for dominance. Every living thing in the basin is a part of the plumbing. The machinery provides the pressure, the cold, and the mineral-rich water; the creatures provide the seals, the silence, and the grease. It is an ecosystem that has forgotten the sun, existing solely to maintain the frantic, silent panic of a machine that is running only to keep itself from stopping.
