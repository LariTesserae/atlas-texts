In the spaces where the water does not crush but merely sheets, where the underside of a five-mile-wide cantilever offers a dripping, iron-scented ceiling, there is a soft and rubbery kind of life. These are the **Sluice-Born**—though they have no name for themselves, having long since discarded the mouth-noises of their ancestors in favor of the deeper, low-frequency language of the stone.

They are the distant, soggy remnants of the builders. In a world where the air is a warm, constant steam-bath, they have lost their hair, their nails, and the dry, flaky skin of terrestrial mammals. Instead, their bodies are covered in a thick, slate-grey integument that looks and feels like wet neoprene. It is slick to the touch, constantly secreting a heavy, lard-like sebum that smells faintly of mutton fat and cold oil. This grease is their primary defense; it makes them hydrophobic, causing the endless warm drizzle to bead and roll off them in perfect, shivering pearls, preventing their flesh from water-logging and sloughing away in the perpetual humidity.

The Sluice-Born are built for a world of angles, wet pipes, and vertical climbs. Their fingers are long and double-jointed, ending not in nails but in broad, ribbed pads of calloused, yellow cartilage that function like suction cups on the wet, vitrified basalt. They do not walk upright; they crawl with a low-slung, lizard-like gait, their bellies nearly scraping the dark masonry of the aqueduct rims. 

They are deaf to the air, but they hear through their bones. The constant, thunderous *shhh-wesshh* of the water and the *thung-thung* of the turbines would have ruptured dry eardrums generations ago. Instead, their auditory canals have grown shut, filled with a dense, fibrous wax. They receive the world through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands, which are pressed flat against the vibrating girders. They can "hear" a transit sled screaming through a pneumatic tube three miles away as a fine, high-pitched itch in their wrists; they can feel the slow, heavy settling of a basalt plaza as a deep, resonant rumble in their ribcages.

They spend their lives in the "dry" undersides—the moist, horizontal crevices where the hanging conduits bundle together like the roots of some colossal iron mangrove. Here, they weave their nests. They do not use thread or straw, but the **Sulfide-Weft**, a tough, felt-like fungus that grows in thick, greasy blankets wherever high-voltage cables leak their magnetic fields into the damp air. The Sluice-Born tear this felt away, chew it into a sticky pulp, and plaster it against the bronze housings of the filtration modules, shaping deep, warm pouches where they sleep in heaps, five or ten together, sharing their greasy warmth.

They are not hunters, for there is nothing to hunt but the metallic fauna, which are too hard to bite and too greasy to digest. Instead, they are gleaners. They follow the migration of the Silt-striders, scraping the thick, orange yeast-muds that build up behind the creatures' jointed legs. They harvest the **Glandular-Wort**—a pale, blind weed that isn't a plant at all, but a colony of sulfur-eating bacteria that grows in long, gelatinous ribbons inside the dark interior of the drainage grates. The Sluice-Born pull these ribbons out like tripe, washing them in the warm, grey runoff until the mineral bitterness is gone, leaving a soft, salty marrow that tastes of tin and tallow.

They do not speak, but they "tap." Each Sluice-Born carries a **Pestle**—a short, heavy cylinder made of self-repairing silicate block that they have fished from the debris of the lower ruins. By striking these pestles against the hollow, resonant ribs of the massive iron aqueducts, they send signals through the iron. 
*   *Tink-tink-chink* means the water in the eastern conduit is rising, hot and sour with grease.
*   *Thrum-thrum-thrum*, delivered with the weight of the shoulder, is a warning that a hydraulic piston below is about to drop, a shift that will crush anything caught in the guide-channels.

Below the Sluice-Born, down in the vertical heart of the great well-shafts where the water falls in continuous, roaring columns toward the subterranean oceans, lives something much larger and far less human. 

These are the **Turbine-Minds** or **Lobe-Trees**. They are stationary, half-biological intelligences that have grown into the very machinery of the world. From a distance, they look like immense, pale tumors of fibrous gristle, fifty or sixty feet across, that have swelled around the rotating bronze shafts of the dynamos. Over the centuries, their tissues have integrated with the copper wiring under the casing. Their nerves—thick, translucent tubes filled with a salty, conductive fluid—run alongside the electrical conduits, tapping directly into the thermal currents generated by the city's friction.

The Lobe-Trees do not have eyes, mouths, or thoughts as we understand them. They do not wonder about the sky or the stars beyond the white-gold glare. Their consciousness is entirely hydro-dynamic. They think in terms of *head*, *fall*, *viscosity*, and *drag*. To a Lobe-Tree, a "good thought" is a smooth, laminar flow through the bypass valves; a "nightmare" is a blockage of silt and Filter-clinker shells that causes the water to cavitate and pit the bronze blades of their host turbines.

They work in silent partnership with the Sluice-Born. When a Lobe-Tree feels its turbine beginning to shudder from a buildup of debris, it will alter the electrical resistance in the nearby conduits, causing the high-tension wires to hum with a specific, irritating frequency. The Sluice-Born, feeling this itch in their nests, will climb down into the wet, howling dark of the shaft, carrying heavy bronze scrapers. They will work for hours in the blinding spray, clearing away the mineral crusts and the dead clinkers from the intake screens. In return, the Lobe-Tree gently warms the surrounding metal plates, creating a cozy, dry haven where the Sluice-Born can rest without freezing in the draft of the falling water.

And then there are the **Glass-Pearls**. 

They are not alive in any cellular sense, nor are they machines. They are the drifting memories of the city itself. They are spheres of pure, heavy quartz-glass, ranging from the size of a plum to the size of a man's head, that have been cast by the millions into the rushing waters of the high aqueducts. Inside each pearl is a hair-fine coil of gold-leaf that has been etched with microscopic patterns of light and dark.

As these pearls tumble down the channels, washed along by the grey runoff, they roll over the magnetic sensor-beds laid into the basalt troughs. Each sensor-bed writes a tiny scratch of data onto the pearl's gold heart—the temperature of the water, the concentration of copper-salts, the vibration of the nearest transit line. 

For hundreds of years, these pearls have drifted through the system: down the towers, through the filtration lagoons, into the deep wells, and back up through the high-pressure steam-pipes to condense once more at the top of the world. They are polished to a mirror-sheen by their endless journey, worn so smooth that they make no sound as they click against one another in the shallows.

The Sluice-Born treat these pearls with a reverence that borders on worship. They do not understand the data etched inside them, but they know that the pearls are the "seeds" of the great stone tables. When they find a particularly large, clear pearl stranded in a drainage grate, they do not leave it. They bring it to their nests. They rub it with their sebum until it gleams with a dull, yellow light under the gold sky. 

They will press these pearls against their foreheads, lying still for hours in the humid dark, listening to the tiny, microscopic hum of the gold coil inside as it reacts to the magnetic fields of their nests. To them, this is the voice of the mother-rock, telling them that the water is still running, that the columns are still healing, and that the great, wet, roaring world is, for now, still whole.
