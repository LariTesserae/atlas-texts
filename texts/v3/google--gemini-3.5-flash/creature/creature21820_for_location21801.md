To understand what lives on the Terrace of the Needle-Spurs, one must first discard the biological definitions of the lowlands. Down there, life is wet, soft, and hurried, characterized by the quick heat of blood and the soft rot of leaves. 

Here, at nine thousand meters, life does not run; it *settles*. It is a process of slow, cold crystallization that has, over millions of cycles of the sun, learned how to move.

They are called **the Tenoned**, though they have no name for themselves, possessing no lungs with which to shape a word. 

To a traveler struggling through the labyrinth of lead-colored dolomite, they are initially invisible. They are the thirteenth pillar in every dozen. Their bodies are perfectly lateral—scarcely three inches thick from chest to spine, but rising nearly three meters high, matching the exact taper and coloration of the surrounding stone. They stand shoulder-to-shoulder with the dead dolomite, wedged so tightly into the crevasses that they appear to be part of the mountain's geological skeletal system.

#### The Anatomy of the Lead-Sheathed
A Tenoned is not made of flesh, but of a dense, living laminate. 

*   **The Outer Rind:** Their exterior is a dry, leaden skin composed of highly compressed calcium plates, overlapping like shingles. This "skin" is completely dry, cold to the touch, and chemically identical to the slate flags below their feet. It does not feel pain, but it is highly sensitive to barometric pressure and the weight of the air.
*   **The Suture-Core:** Beneath the shingled rind lies a network of tough, black, fibrous cords. This is the "sinew" found in the wind-shelter—a biological polymer of dried mountain-grasses and calcified protein that connects their flat, jointed plates. It is incredibly strong, possessing the tensile strength of steel wire but remaining flexible enough to bend when the wind reaches hurricane force.
*   **The Turquoise Humour:** They have no heart, no stomach, and no lungs. Instead of blood, their flat bodies are irrigated by a slow, capillary-driven crawl of the *turquoise brine*—the heavy, copper-saturated water that seeps from the mountain’s fissures. This fluid moves through their interior plates not by pumping, but by the physical laws of capillary action and thermal expansion. It is the green brine that keeps their sinew-cords from dry-rotting, acting as a natural antifreeze and seal.

#### The Pulse of the Phase-Change
Because they have no metabolic heat, the Tenoned are entirely dependent on the sun’s "dissection." 

During the freezing, indigo night, they are indistinguishable from the stone pillars. They are totally dormant, frozen solid, their internal brine locked in a state of glassy suspension. If you were to strike one with a hammer in the dark, it would ring like lead and chip like flint.

Their "waking" is a sudden, violent event. As the sun clears the eastern peaks and its flat, raw light strikes the terrace, the temperature of the stone jumps twenty degrees in a matter of seconds. The ice inside the Tenoned’s outer rind does not melt; it sublimates. 

As the frost turns directly to steam, it creates a sudden, localized pressure-drop inside their porous joints. This "puffing" of steam behaves like a pneumatic system. The Tenoned hiss. A low, dry whisper runs through the grove as hundreds of flat bodies absorb the vapor, their jointed plates expanding and contracting with a series of sharp, metallic *clinks*. 

This is their movement. They do not walk as animals do; they *inch*. They use the expansion of the morning steam to lift their flat feet—which are shaped like heavy, dull wedges—and slide them several millimeters across the frozen shingle. They move only when the light transition is active. Once the sun is high and the temperature stabilizes, they freeze in place once more, locked into their new positions until the shadow of the western ridge brings the next phase-change.

#### The Purpose of the Hands
The ancient copper bands and slate pendulums are not the work of a vanished race. They are the Tenoned’s own clothes, their armor, and their anchors.

Because they are so tall and thin, the violent winter gales of the high peaks—winds that can rip the feathers from a high-flying vulture—threaten to snap them from their bases. To prevent this "shatter-death," the Tenoned work together during the brief, fluid hours of the spring thaws.

Using their heavy, three-fingered hands—which terminate in dense, greenstone claws that grow directly from their mineralized wrists—they chip grooves into themselves and into the sturdiest of the dead dolomite pillars. They find deposits of native copper in the deeper clefts, beating the cold metal into flat bands using their own heavy forearms as hammers. 

They bind themselves to the mountain. A Tenoned will wrap a copper collar around its chest and anchor it to the pillar behind it, using braided cords of dry sinew. 

The slate pendulums are hum-dampers. When the wind blows through the narrow channels of the terrace, the pillars begin to vibrate at a high, destructive frequency that can shatter dolomite. The slate plates, pinned to the pillars by small copper pegs, absorb this kinetic energy. They are silent stabilizers, keeping the Tenoned's living houses from shaking themselves to dust.

#### The Sanctuary of the Shedding
The small, dry-stacked wind-shelter in the center of the terrace is not a home; it is a **moulting-house**.

Every century or so, the mineralization of a Tenoned’s outer rind becomes too absolute. The *gypsum-curls*—the snowy-white crystal threads—begin to sprout from their breathing pores, choking off the flow of the turquoise brine. If left unchecked, the Tenoned will turn entirely to stone, becoming just another dead pillar in the grove.

When this begins, the affected individual crawls into the shelter, away from the drying wind and the direct, violent rays of the sun. Inside, in the cool, humid shade of the stacked flags, they begin the slow process of *scraping*.

Using the greenstone hand-axes found on the shelf, they patiently shave the calcified crust from their own bodies. It is a dry, dusty, agonizingly slow process that can take years. They scrape until they reach the soft, slate-grey under-layer. 

The "unworked slate tiles" in the shelter are their discarded back-plates—heavy, mineral shields that have been peeled off to allow new, porous layers to form beneath. The "resinous grease" in the stone bowl is a heavy lipid they exude from their joints during this period, used to paint their raw, newly exposed plates to protect them from the frost before they can harden into leaden stone.

#### Their Song
They are completely mute, but they are not quiet.

When the afternoon wind rises, squeezing through the vertical slits of the needle-spurs, it plays the Tenoned like an organ. The wind catches the copper bands and the tight sinew cords, producing a long, droning chord that can be heard for miles down-valley—a cold, metallic *hum* that rises and falls with the pressure of the air.

To the few lowlanders who have seen the terrace from a distance, it is said that the mountain is singing. But it is not the mountain. It is the Tenoned, standing shoulder-to-shoulder in the hard, blue vacuum, holding onto their stone pillars with green-stained fingers, waiting for the shadow to fall so they may freeze, and rest, and begin again.
