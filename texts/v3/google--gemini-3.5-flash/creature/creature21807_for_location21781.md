In the warm, green-tinted twilight of this subterranean sanctuary, life does not merely survive; it has folded itself into the very rhythm of the moisture and the stone. Because there is no wind to carry seeds, no sun to mark the passage of seasons, and no sound to warn of predators, the inhabitants have evolved sensory languages of touch, light, and chemical drift. 

Here is what lives in the damp, heavy dark of the dome:

### The Amber-Wells
They sit upon the rims of the calcium carbonate basins like drops of pale wax themselves. These are the small, soft-bodied frogs that claim the still water. Their skin is so thin and pigment-free that they appear nearly blue-gray, their internal organs visible as dark, coiled shadows beneath their ribs. 

They have no vocal cords; to croak here would be a useless expenditure of energy where no ears are tuned to receive it. Instead, they communicate through their large, balloon-like throat sacs, which are a deep, saturated amber. When a frog wishes to speak, its throat swells and pulses with a slow, lucent rhythm—a tiny, warm lantern flickering in the dimness. 

When one frog begins to pulse, others around the basin mirror the frequency, then shift it, creating a silent, wave-like chorus of amber light that travels from pool to pool across the grotto floor. They feed on the heavy gnats that dance in the emerald light shaft, catching them not with the snap of a wet tongue, but by sitting perfectly still beneath the low-hanging leaves and letting the insects land on their sticky, mucus-covered backs, which they then scrape clean with their hind legs.

### The Slate-Backs
Slow, heavy, and ancient, the Slate-Backs are the quiet caretakers of the central basalt ruins. Looking like a cross between a giant trilobite and a polished river stone, these flat, shield-shaped crustaceans are about the size of a man’s hand. Their shells are a dark, matte basalt-black, perfectly matching the blocks of the terrace.

The Slate-Backs are blind, possessing instead a row of incredibly sensitive chemical pits along the margins of their carapaces. They spend their decades-long lives crawling over the basalt blocks, driven by a singular, instinctual compulsion: they keep the channels clear. With tiny, brush-like front appendages, they sweep the silt, decaying leaves, and encroaching moss away from the concentric rings of the terrace, ensuring that the water dripping from the ceiling continues its uninterrupted flow toward the central depression. 

In exchange for this labor, they feed on the black film of cyanobacteria that grows in the running channels. They move so slowly that a carpet of liverwort may take root on the back of an elder Slate-Back before it reaches the other side of the platform.

### The Ripple-Watchers
Where the Slate-Backs are slow and armored, the Ripple-Watchers are light and wire-thin. These are arachnids of immense delicacy, with translucent bodies no larger than a pea, but with eight jointed legs that span nearly ten inches. 

They do not spin webs; in this humidity, silk would sag and lose its stickiness within hours. Instead, they hunt by touch upon the surface of the calcium carbonate pools. A Ripple-Watcher will anchor its rear legs to a wet limestone edge and suspend its body over the water, resting its four forward legs lightly upon the surface tension. 

They are the ultimate listeners of the dome. Through the water's surface, they can read the tiny, concentric vibrations of a drowning midge, the entry of a fungal spore, or the footfall of a frog twenty feet away. When an insect touches the water, the Ripple-Watcher glides across the liquid ice like a shadow, its movements so light they do not even disturb the reflection of the emerald light beam above.

### The Felt-Wings
These are the heavy, quiet lepidopterans that inhabit the upper canopy of strangling figs and pink roots. With wingspans the size of a human palm, they look like dry, water-logged birch leaves when at rest. 

The Felt-Wings are covered in a dense, velvet-like down that repels the constant mist. Their wings are completely silent; even if one were to fly directly past your ear, you would feel only a sudden, cool draft of air and hear nothing. They have no mouthparts and do not feed; their adult lives are brief—lasting only a few days—fueled by the fat stored during their long, slow life as pale, root-eating grubs in the loam below.

Once a cycle, when the angle of the sun far above aligns perfectly with the fissure, the emerald-tinted light shaft grows briefly bright and warm. At this moment, hundreds of Felt-Wings detach themselves from the leaves and rise into the column of light. They do not flap their wings so much as drift upward on the rising, warm air currents of the dome, a silent, swirling cylinder of silver-grey felt, finding mates in the only light they will ever know before descending back into the dark to lay their eggs on the rotting logs.

### The Weave (The White Mycelium)
Perhaps the true master of the dome is not an animal at all, but the vast, pale fungal network that blankets the forest floor. It is a single, continuous organism that acts as the valley’s stomach and its memory.

Because decay in this sweltering heat is so rapid, the Weave is always active. It presents itself as a fine, glowing lace of white threads that covers every patch of rich black earth, climbing several inches up the trunks of the ferns and the stems of the monocots. It is highly sensitive to pressure; if a Slate-Back crawls over a section of the floor, the Weave beneath it dimples and withdraws, sending chemical signals through its network to prepare the surrounding area for the nutrients that will be left in the creature's wake.

The Weave is also responsible for the peculiar green light. As it digests the fallen leaves and wood, it emits a low, cold bioluminescence—not enough to light the cavern, but enough to give the undersides of the broad, translucent leaves a faint, pale-green counter-glow that mimics the light from the sky.

### The Ghost-Lungs
Deep within the crevices of the wet marble walls, where the condensation runs down in steady, silent sheets, live the Ghost-Lungs. They are large, shell-less terrestrial snails, completely transparent save for a single, pulsing pink mass in their centers—their rudimentary lung. 

They slide along the wax-smooth marble, grazing on the film of microscopic algae. Because they have no shells to protect them from drying out, they are entirely dependent on the sweltering, 100% humidity of the dome. If they venture even slightly too close to the draft of the upper fissure, they begin to dissolve. 

They are the most fragile things in this world, spending their lives in a slow, wet drift, leaving behind trails of shimmering silver mucus that catch the emerald light and turn the marble walls into a glittering map of paths taken and forgotten.
