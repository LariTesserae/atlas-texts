In a world where light is a rare, copper smudge and the ground is a dull, vibrating iron-sheet, life does not look up. It listens. It presses itself flat against the stone, feeling for the slow, thrumming heartbeat of the Great Striders leagues away. To live here is to be a creature of vibration and grease, molded by the endless, warm dampness and the scent of scorched tallow.

At the smallest, most persistent scale, there are the **Grease-Leeches**. They are not true annelids, but flat, rubbery ribbons of grey muscle, as long as a man’s forearm, that cling to the weeping joints of the Striders. They have no teeth, only wide, heated leathery suction-mouths that sew themselves directly over the oil-ports and weld-seams. They drink the black, oxidized tallow and the sulfurous lubricants that weep from the machines’ pistons, processing these heavy hydrocarbons into a thick, milky lard. When a Strider takes a particularly violent step, hundreds of these gorged leeches shake free, falling to the basalt below like heavy, dark fruit. There, they rupture, spilling their processed fats into the dry iron filings—creating tiny, short-lived oases of nutrient-rich grease where the smaller, scurrying things of the plateau gather to feed.

Among these scavengers are the **Silt-Ticks**. They are the size of tea-saucers, flat and hexagonal, with legs made of hardened, horn-like chitin that clack against the basalt like dry pebbles. They are entirely blind, navigating by the thermal plumes rising from the fresh slag-trails. They consume the grease-spills and the dead leeches, their carapaces coated in a thick, velvety layer of volcanic soot that makes them virtually invisible against the basalt. When the wind rises, they hunker down, locking their joints and tilting their shells into the gale like small, dark slates, letting the ash drift over them until they are indistinguishable from the stones.

Further up, in the cold-iron scaffolds of the Striders’ high backs—where the heat of the lower engines dissipates into the damp, charcoal sky—lives the **Iron-Weaver**. These are delicate, spider-like organisms with leg-spans as wide as carriage wheels, though their bodies are no larger than a fist. They do not spin silk from glands; instead, they harvest the long, glassy fibers of the wire-reed from the slag-trails, carrying them up into the Strider’s iron-beamed skeleton. With their mandibles, they secrete an acidic, binding saliva that melts the silica fibers, welding them together to spin vast, structural webs across the machine’s open ventilation vents. These webs do not catch prey by stickiness, but by filtration; as the Strider moves, its warm exhaust blows through the webs, leaving behind a thick crust of soot-moss spores, oil-moths, and drifting organic dust, which the Weaver slowly scrapes off and consumes at its leisure.

But there are larger things here, too—creatures that have climbed out of the wide, wet chasms to follow the iron giants across the endless flats.

The **Hush-Cutter** is the silent apex of this dust-world. It is a long, low hunter, built like a greyhound but covered in overlapping plates of soft, fibrous leather that absorb sound. It has no claws; its feet are wide, spongy pads that muffle its movement entirely, allowing it to glide over the basalt dust without making a sound. It hunts the slide-grubs. Because the grubs rely on detecting the vibrations of approaching danger, the Hush-Cutter has evolved a bizarre, mechanical mimicry. Inside its deep chest cavity, it possesses a massive, muscular air-sack that it compresses and releases in perfect synchronicity with the Striders’ distant piston-strokes. It moves only when the ground shakes. To a slide-grub, the approaching hunter is nothing more than the background rhythm of the world—until the Hush-Cutter’s heavy, spade-like head strikes down, slicing through its chitinous armor to reach the warm, mineral-rich fat within.

And then, there are those who are not beasts. 

They are called the **Echo-Children**, though there is nothing young about them; they are as old as the rust on the Striders’ flanks. They are bipedal, standing half again as tall as a man, but they are so thin, so stretched and reed-like, that they seem more like shadows than flesh. Their skin is the color of wet slate, perpetually smeared with grease and black dust, which they rub into their bodies as a sealant against the abrasive iron winds. 

They have no eyes. Where a face should be, there is only a broad, concave disc of sensitive, fibrous skin, ringed with delicate, twitching cilia that can detect the thermal draft of a steam vent from five miles away. They do not speak in words. They communicate by tapping upon the stones with long, iron-wood staffs—stiff, fossilized stalks of giant wire-reeds that they harvest from the deep chasms. The clicks are dry and sharp, cutting through the heavy air, carrying information about water-pools, approaching wind-storms, or the direction of the nearest Strider.

The Echo-Children do not build. They do not possess the concept of a wall or a roof. To them, the sky is too vast and the earth too hard to be divided. Instead, they live *with* the Striders. 

Each small band of Echo-Children—numbering no more than a dozen—is bonded to a single, specific giant. They call it by the unique pitch of its main drive-shaft, a deep, grinding musical fifth that they can feel through the soles of their feet even when the machine is lost beyond the horizon. They follow its slag-trail for months, living off the soot-moss and the grubs that swarm in its wake. 

When the high-altitude winds blow cold and the black paraffin pools freeze over, the Echo-Children climb. They use their long, double-jointed fingers—tipped with hard, spade-like nails—to wedge themselves into the rivet-lines and safety-ladders of their Strider. They crawl into the warm, oily recesses near the boiler-houses, nesting in the dry, soot-choked spaces beneath the steam-manifolds. 

Here, in the humming, roaring heart of the machine, they sleep. They do not fear the grinding gears or the crushing weight of the pistons; they know the rhythm of every tooth and lever. They know when a gear is about to shift, when a valve is about to blast its scalding steam, and they move with the effortless grace of dancers through a burning forest, never touching the moving metal, always nested in the quiet, vibrating voids.

There is a strange, silent pact between them and the irons. When a Strider’s lubricant-port becomes clogged with oxidized slag, the Echo-Children use their long staffs to clear the obstruction, letting the black tallow flow freely once more. In return, the machine provides them with warmth, water from its hot condensation-drips, and passage across the endless, hostile basalt.

When one of the Echo-Children dies, there is no grave. The basin is too hard for digging, and the wind would only blow the ash away. Instead, they carry the body to the Strider’s front, where the massive, grinding undersides meet the basalt. They place the body gently upon the dust, directly in the path of the colossal iron legs. 

As the machine steps forward, grinding the bone and flesh into the mineral dust along with the basalt, the band stands on the high scaffolding above. They do not weep, for they have no eyes for tears. But they press their hands flat against the vibrating iron skin of their giant, feeling the extra, momentary shudder as the machine absorbs their companion into its path, turning them, at last, into the very road they have walked for a thousand years.
