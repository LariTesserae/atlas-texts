To look upon the people of these peaks is to see the mountain’s own harsh masonry rendered in flesh. 

They are a short, barrel-chested folk, their lungs wide and deep as bellows to wring what little sweetness they can from the thin, biting air. Their shoulders are abnormally broad, slightly hunched from a lifetime of leaning into the vertical world, and their thighs are thick, knotty bundles of muscle that never quite relax. They do not walk with the swinging, easy stride of the valley-dwellers; instead, they have a heavy, flat-footed plod, planting each foot as if they intend to root it into the stone before lifting the next.

Their skin is the color of smoked lard—pale where the heavy, grease-soaked wool coats cover them, but wind-scabbed, leathery, and dark as cured meat on their faces and hands. To protect themselves from the dry, splitting cold and the blinding glare of the high sun, they smear their exposed skin with *chara*—a thick, yellow paste made of ox-tallow, blue-shrub berry oil, and pulverized slate dust. It dries into a greyish, water-resistant lacquer that smells of old fat and cold earth. When they smile, the dried paste cracks around their mouths in tiny, dark gray fissures, revealing teeth that are short and hard, worn down by the stone-grit that seasons every meal.

Their eyes are small, set remarkably deep beneath heavy, hair-tufted brow ridges that act as natural visors against the falling sleet and the white glare of the glaciers. Many wear goggles carved from the dense hip-bones of the great condors, with nothing but a hair-thin horizontal slit cut into the bone to let in a sliver of light. Through these slits, they scan the vertical world with an icy, unwavering focus. They rarely look up or down with their necks; instead, they pivot their entire torsos, a habit born from the absolute necessity of keeping one's balance centered over the feet.

Because the cliffs offer no mercy to the clumsy, the very concept of childhood in this city is tethered. 

From the day a infant begins to crawl, they are buckled into a harness of braided goat-hair rope. The other end of this line is secured to a heavy, diorite iron-pin driven deep into the bedrock of the family’s hearth-stone. For the first six or seven years of life, a child’s world is a semi-circle exactly nine paces wide—the edge of the terrace roof. They learn to play, sleep, and eat within this radius, the soft, dry *clink-clack* of their bone toys sliding across the slate accompanied always by the taut hum of their tether. As they grow, the rope is lengthened in increments, but a child is not "untethered" until their twelfth winter, after they have completed the *Climb of the Five Crevices* without a guide. Those who do not pass are not spoken of; the mountain is a silent ledger that keeps no record of its debts.

The elderly do not die of old age so much as they dissolve into the stone. When a person’s knees finally refuse the stairs, or when their lungs fill with the grey "slate-cough" that claims many stone-cutters, they do not remain in the crowded upper rooms. Space is too dear. Instead, they descend to the *Roots*—the lowest, dampest tier of the city, located directly beneath the great ox-vaults. 

Here, in the perpetual twilight of the lower alleys where the condensation runs down the walls like sweat, the old ones live among the beasts. They sit in the warm, dung-scented straw, their bodies wrapped in thick, unwashed hides, and perform the work that requires no legs. They spin the coarse goat-hair into yards of greasy twine; they polish the dark green jade axes with water and river-sand until the stone glows like frozen moss; they scrape the fat from bird-skins with bone knives. They are the memory of the city. Because there is no paper and wood is too precious to burn for light, history is kept in the *Syllable-Knots*—long, heavy ropes of goat-hair where every knot, splice, and shade of grease represents a famine, a birth, a landslide, or a winter that did not end.

The language of these people is a low, guttural click-tongue, spoken almost entirely through the nose and throat to keep the lips closed against the freezing wind. It is a language of short, heavy sounds, devoid of the flowing vowels of the lowlands. They have forty-three distinct words for different states of ice: *glis* is the hard, blue ice that can hold an ox; *krak* is the rotten, grey ice that look solid but will drop a man into a crevice; *vorn* is the greasy condensation that makes the stone steps as slick as lard. 

They do not sing with their voices. Instead, they "sing" with their tools. At dusk, when the wind drops for a brief, freezing hour, the stone-masons across three peaks will play the *Rock-Chime*. Using small, hammer-stones of varying density, they strike the protruding slate-flanges of their terraces. The sound—a cold, ringing *ting-clink-tong*—travels incredibly far in the thin air, a polyphonic stone-song that tells the neighboring summits that the day’s work is done and that the cliffs still hold.

Domestic life is an exercise in shared, heavy warmth. Inside the low, windowless stone huts, there is no furniture. Floors are covered in thick layers of dried moss, goat-hair mats, and the hides of beasts. At night, the family does not sleep in separate beds, nor even under separate blankets. They sleep in the *Heep*—a single, massive nest of fur and wool in the center of the room. The children are placed in the middle, followed by the women and the old, with the young men on the outer edge where the frost creeps under the heavy door-flaps. 

In the center of this human pile, they often tuck two or three of the white, woolly dogs. These dogs, known as *Sentry-Warmers*, are breed specifically for their body heat and quiet nature; they do not bark—a barking dog wastes breath and can trigger a snow-slip—but they possess a deep, rhythmic purr that calms the children and keeps the blood moving in frozen toes.

Their diet is monotonous, oily, and incredibly energy-dense. Its cornerstone is *tsampa-grease*, a thick porridge made from ground blue-shrub berries, dried mountain-clover roots, and hot ox-tallow. It is served in communal bowls carved from goat skulls and eaten with the fingers. It is incredibly heavy, coating the mouth with a layer of fat that protects against the dry-cracking of the cold. 

Once a season, a goat is slaughtered, an event of solemn gravity. Not a drop of blood is lost; it is caught in stone vessels and churned with sour goat’s milk to make *black-curd*, a sour, iron-rich cheese that is buried in the ice-chimneys for months until it turns the color of slate and has the hardness of dried wood. This cheese is the travelers' food; a hunter or messenger will keep a lump of it under their tongue for three days, letting the freezing saliva slowly dissolve the fat and salt into their throat as they climb.

And above them all, hovering on the thin, violet thermal currents, are the *Bone-Gatherers*—the great black condors. They are more than birds to the city; they are the final stage of life. 

The people do not bury their dead, for there is no soil to dig, nor do they burn them, for wood is more precious than silver. Instead, when an inhabitant dies, their body is carried by the family up to the *High Spire*—the highest, sharpest needle of diorite on each summit. There, the *Sky-Steward*, an old man who has lost his toes to the frost, cuts the body into precise segments with a green-jade cleaver. He calls the birds with a hollow whistle made from a wing-bone. 

The condors descend in a flurry of heavy, grease-shining black feathers. Within hours, the flesh is gone, carried up into the thin, clean sky. A week later, the family climbs back up to collect the bones. The skull is taken back to the home to serve as a oil-lamp or a drinking cup; the long leg-bones are carved into looms and awls; the ribs are used as braces for the slate roofs. 

In this way, no one ever truly leaves the mountain. The ancestors are in the roofs that keep out the snow; they are in the needles that sew the winter coats; they are in the cups that hold the tooth-cracking water of the seeps. They have become part of the very masonry, clinging to the edge of the sky, hard and silent as the young rock beneath them.
