To inhabit this vertical machine of basalt and cold steam is to be an extension of its circuitry. The creatures here are not visitors to the stone; they are its rust, its lubricants, and its slow-thinking valves.

At the lowest level of scale, nestled directly into the greasy, wet margins where the copper-lichen meets the raw basalt, are the **Volt-Gleams**. These are tiny, shell-less molluscs, no larger than a grain of coarse salt, with bodies made of dark, iron-rich gel. They do not travel by crawling; instead, they align themselves along the hair-like copper threads. When a static charge pulses down a pillar, it passes through their bodies. The Volt-Gleams absorb the heat of the current, using the charge to catalyze the stone’s mineral salts into food. To watch a pillar from a distance is to see thousands of these tiny creatures flare like dying embers, glittering in sequence as the blue electricity rolls over them in silent, slow-descending rings.

Beneath the velvet-furred beasts that harvest the lichen are **The Lubrists**—the heavy, multi-jointed climbers. These creatures, known to themselves only through the cold pressure of the damp stone against their chests, are called *Kret* in the low, rattling vibration they produce when they strike the columns. A *Kret* is shaped like a flattened cask of lard, supported by eight sprawling, double-jointed legs. They have no necks; their broad, hard heads are fused directly to their chests, ending in a wide, horizontal slit that contains three rows of self-sharpening, iron-hard teeth. 

The *Kret* do not walk so much as they adhere. Their leg-joints are powered not by dry muscle, but by hydraulic chambers filled with a thick, grease-like tallow. At the tip of each leg is a fleshy disk that secretes a dense, sulfurous fat. This fat interacts with the mineral-water film of the pillars, creating a temporary, high-viscosity vacuum seal. When a *Kret* lifts a foot, the sound is a wet, heavy *thwip*, like a boot being pulled from lake-mud. They spend their decades-long lives moving in spiraling paths up and down the columns, grazing upon the copper-lichen. They are the regulators of the electric system: if the lichen grows too thick, the current disperses and the pillars grow cold; if the lichen is grazed bare, the *Kret* starve. They move with an ancient, instinctual calculus, leaving behind them paths of clean, glistening stone that allow the static charges to ground safely into the depths.

In the immense, hollow voids between the pillars, where the cold air moves in frozen, mile-wide rivers, live **The Great Sails** or **Vellum-Whales**. These are the silent gliders of the chasm. They are gargantuan—often seventy feet from tip to tip—but they weigh less than a young land-beast, for their skeletons are not bone, but hollow, pressurized reeds of glass-like cartilage. Their flesh is a pale, translucent rubber, through which one can see the slow, rhythmic pumping of their three hearts—flat, dark organs that beat only twice a minute.

The Sails have no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the indigo gloom but the cold-blue sparks of the pillars. Instead, their wide, flat heads are fringed with dozens of whip-like antennae, some thirty feet long, that drag behind them through the air like the lines of a drift-net. These antennae are extremely sensitive to electromagnetic fields. By reading the electric pulse-rate of the distant pillars, a Sail can map the entire chasm in its mind, perceiving the basalt columns as towering, humming cylinders of light. 

The Sails feed upon the steam-vents of the bellows-vines. When a vine contracts and vents its sulfurous breath, the Sail drifts over the plume, opening its colossal, bottom-facing mouth to swallow the warm, spore-rich fog. They do not reproduce through contact; instead, when two Sails pass close to one another in the dark, their antennae tangle for a brief, crackling moment, exchanging genetic information via micro-electrical discharges. Years later, a Sail will drift to the very bottom of the chasm—the hot, lightless floor where the bases of the pillars sit in burning silt—and drop a single, leathery egg that resembles a deflated balloon, before rising once more into the cold, clean air of the high galleries.

But there is another presence here—creatures that do not graze or drift, but who watch over the rhythm of the pillars with a slow, solemn reverence. They are **The Scribes of the Valve**.

To an outsider, a Scribe might easily be mistaken for a dead bellows-vine or a peculiar outcrop of basalt. They are tall, excessively thin beings, standing twelve cubits high, with bodies like grey, petrified wood. Their limbs are long and triple-jointed, ending in fingers that are like iron pliers. They have no eyes, no noses, and no mouths; where their face should be, there is only a flat, circular plate of polished obsidian, black and reflective as stagnant oil.

The Scribes are so slow of habit that they seem to belong to the geological time of the basalt rather than the biological time of the animals. A Scribe may stand in a single niche for fifty years, its long fingers wrapped around a valve of a bellows-vine, its body covered in a silver shawl of moss. They do not eat; instead, they have a deep, tap-root limb that bores through the lichen directly into the basalt, drawing up the faint, warm electricity of the column to sustain their stone-like hearts.

They are the tenders of the machine. When a bellows-vine becomes choked with mineral deposits or when its segments crack in a sudden freeze, a Scribe will awaken. The movement is agonizingly slow—a single lift of the arm taking several days—but it is incredibly precise. With their plier-like fingers, they pinch the damaged segments of the vine, clearing the salt-crust with their breath, which is hot and smells of vinegar. They will bind split vines with ropes woven from their own fibrous hair, guiding the plants’ growth so that they do not block the flowing channels of mineral water.

Sometimes, when the wind falls completely silent and the blue light-rings crawl down the pillars in perfect, unbroken intervals, the Scribes will speak. The sound is not a voice, but a resonant, mechanical hum produced by vibrating the obsidian plates of their faces against the bare stone of the pillars. The sound travels through the basalt, miles deep and miles high, a low, metallic *gonging* that coordinates the contraction of the bellows-vines. One pillar begins; another answers miles away; then another, until the entire valley is vibrating with a single, slow, industrial heartbeat.

In this world, nothing is wasted, and nothing is lonely. Even death is a functional process. When a Sail grows too heavy with mineral deposits to fly, or when a Scribe’s tap-root finally petrifies into solid stone, they do not rot. They are slowly dismantled by the *Kret*, who scrape their remains down to the bone, using the calcium to strengthen their own carapaces. What remains falls into the abyss, where the roots of the bellows-vines drink it in, turning the dead back into the steam that feeds the sky. 

It is a cathedral that maintains its own stones, a kingdom of cold grease and blue light, where the only duty of life is to keep the great wet wheel turning.
