To understand what lives in this wet stone throat, one must understand the hierarchy of the tiles. In this basin, existence is not measured in years, but in the slow, grinding wear of terracotta against terracotta, and every creature here has been shaped by the heavy, rhythmic chime of the dripping vault.

### The Tile-Turners

The largest inhabitants of the basin are the *Tile-Turners*. There are never more than three or four of them at any time, and they rarely acknowledge one another, moving through the grey fog like heavy, wet sacks of lard dragged across the masonry. 

To look at a Tile-Turner is to look at something halfway between an elephant seal and a mound of river-silt. They have no legs to speak of; instead, their lower bodies taper into a broad, muscular disk—a single, massive foot like that of an land-snail, but leathery and gray-white, the color of turnip flesh. Their skin is thick, hairless, and perpetually cold, smelling strongly of river-mud and iron rust. It is so soft that it takes on the impression of whatever it rests against; when a Turner has slept for several hours in a hollow, its flank bears the perfect, diamond-scored herringbone pattern of the tiles beneath it.

The Turners are blind. Where their eyes should be are two deep, horizontal slits that weep a thick, sterile mucus. They navigate entirely by touch and the deep-frequency rumble of the floor. Their hands—four of them, arranged in a small, crowded cluster beneath their chin—are delicate and surprisingly small, like the hands of human children, though the skin is yellowed and the fingernails are as hard and flat as slate chisels. 

With these hands, the Turners work. They are the tenders of the floor. They move across the basin with an agonizing, shuffling crawl, their flat bellies making a wet, sucking sound against the wet clay. When they encounter a tile that has buckled too high—one that threatens to tip and break under the weight of the water-floods—they stop. For hours, they will scrape at the seams with their iron-hard nails, clearing away the build-up of calcified snail husks and brick-dust, until the tile settles back into its groove. 

They do not speak, but they make a sound: a low, wet *soughing* through their nostrils that sounds like wet leather being wrung out. Every hour, when the great copper float-valve clinks and the torrent of icy water gushes from the wall, the Turners do not run. They flatten themselves against the floor, their gray rinds turning dark and slick as the water foams over them. 

### The Shingle-Backs

When the flood comes, the Turners do not drown alone. They are the lifeboats of the basin.

The flat, gray woodlice—the *Shingle-backs*—which grow as large as a man's hand, have a symbiotic dread of the deep pools. While their smaller cousins might survive in the damp seams, these giants will drown if submerged for more than a few minutes. Thus, they have learned the schedule of the copper valve. 

Ten minutes before the *clink* sounds, when the water in the unseen drains beneath the floor begins to sigh and gurgle, the Shingle-backs begin their migration. They scramble toward the Turners. They climb the soft, damp flanks of the great beasts, their jointed, horny legs tickling the Turners' gray skin. The Turners do not mind; they lie still, allowing their backs to be covered in a thick, overlapping armor of living slate. Thousands of the woodlice will pile three or four deep on a single Turner, their gray shells dryly clicking against one another like shingles on a roof during a storm. 

Once the flood recedes and the water whispers away through the weep-holes, the Shingle-backs disperse, returning to the dry-ish margins of the walls to scrape at the black bacterial slime with their tiny mandibles.

### The Gutter-Snipes

Nesting in the deep, water-hollowed joints of the granite walls, thirty feet up where the cold fog drifts, are the *Gutter-Snipes*. 

They are birds that have forgotten how to fly, or perhaps beasts that never quite completed the transition from the water. They are the size of herons, but they are entirely featherless. Their skin is a translucent, pinkish-white, so thin that one can see the dark, pulsing thread of their jugular veins and the slow, rhythmic expansion of their lungs through their ribs. Their wings have withered into three-jointed, leathery claws which they use to cling to the wet granite, climbing the vertical walls with the silent, jerky precision of geckos.

The Gutter-Snipes have long, needle-thin beaks, yellow and slightly curved like bone bodkins. They hunt by sound. They will hang upside down from the ruined stone arches overhead, or cling to the side of a granite block, utterly motionless for hours. Their heads twitch in time with the dripping water. 

In this basin, the sound of a falling drop is a constant. But if a drop is *interrupted*—if its metallic *ring* is cut short because it struck the back of a moving crayfish or the soft head of a snail—the Gutter-Snipe knows. It drops from the wall without a sound, its long, leathery body falling like a plumb-bob. It strikes the water with its beak, pinches its prey, and is back on the wall in a single, wet scramble before the ripples have reached the edge of the pool.

### The Sump-Spindle

Underneath everything—beneath the tiles, beneath the wet silt, deep in the dark throat where the water drains—lives the *Sump-Spindle*.

It is not an animal, nor is it truly a plant. It is a single, massive, fibrous organism that has filled the wet void beneath the herringbone floor. If one could lift twenty tiles at once, one would see its pale, woody flesh: a tangled, pulsing mattress of yellow root-tubes, some as thick as a man's thigh, others as fine as hair. 

The Sump-Spindle is what causes the low-frequency groaning that vibrates through the floor. It is the lungs of the basin. When the water drains through the weep-holes, the Spindle drinks. Its woody fibers swell, absorbing thousands of gallons of icy water, growing turgid and heavy. As it swells, it pushes upward against the underside of the floor, causing the terracotta tiles to buckle, warp, and crack. 

Then, over the course of the hour, the Spindle slowly digests the minerals in the water—the lime, the iron, the dissolved tile-dust—and breathes the water out into even deeper channels below the earth. As it empties, it shrinks. The floor tiles sink back down, groaning as they settle into new, slightly different configurations. 

Without the Spindle, the basin would have choked on its own water centuries ago. Without the Spindle, the Turners would have no work, the snails would have no pools, and the copper valve would have nothing to fill. It is the slow, wet heart of this stone puzzle, beating once every sixty minutes, washed clean by the cold, and buried forever in the dark.
