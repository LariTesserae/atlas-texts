To live in the Sump-Ward is to live in a state of perpetual, lubricated grace. 

The people who dwell here—known to themselves as the *Sumpers* or simply the *Parishioners*—are as much a product of this warm, vertical green womb as the giant gourds they inhabit. They are a human cooperative, but one that has been softened, oiled, and water-logged by generations of life under the cedar canopy until their very biology has taken on the patient, non-friction quality of their machinery.

In appearance, they are long-limbed and remarkably smooth-skinned. The concept of "goosebumps" or dry, flaky flesh is foreign here; the 100% humidity keeps their skin in a state of constant, plump hydration, giving them a waxy, polished luster similar to the peel of an apple or the cured rind of their gourd-homes. Under the emerald-tinted light, their skin appears pale-bronze or a delicate, lichen-gray, though their hands and forearms are almost always stained a deep, indelible blue-black. This is the "tannin-brand," a permanent dye earned from years of handling the bark of the swamp-cedars and working the raw skins in the tannery vats, where the wood-acid reacts with the iron-bearing grease of the engines.

They wear very little, for clothes in this atmosphere quickly become heavy, cold sponges. A Sump-dweller’s wardrobe consists mostly of aprons and loin-wraps made from "gut-skin"—the cured, translucent bladders of giant river eels, oiled until they are as supple as silk and totally waterproof—girded with harness-straps of braided hemp. 

Their feet are their most distinctive feature. They do not wear boots, which would rot within a week in the wet peat. Instead, they go barefoot, their soles having developed a thick, spongy, and deeply ridged pad of orange callousing. This "moss-tread" allows them to grip the wet, fibrous cobblestones of liverwort and the slippery, bracket-fungus walkways with the security of a tree-frog. They walk with a peculiar, sliding shuffle—the "Gourd-Gait." To lift one's feet high is to risk tearing the delicate root-skins of the floor and causing the peat beneath to wash away into the Sump; they slide their soles across the moss, feeling for the underlying structural roots with their toes.

Because the air is thick with steam and the heavy *chug-thump* of the geothermal wood pistons vibrates through the peat-moss floor every six seconds, the Parishioners do not speak like people of the dry world. They have adapted their language to the rhythm of the ward. They speak in low, breathy sibilants and clicks that carry easily through the damp mist without requiring them to draw the hot, thick air deep into their chest cavities. More than that, they "speak in the gaps." A conversation in the Sump-Ward is a rhythmic affair: a speaker waits for the heavy, swallowing *chug* of the redwood flywheel, delivers a short, three-syllable phrase during the silent interval, and hushes on the *thump*. To an outsider, their speech sounds like a hesitant, syncopated song, perfectly timed to the heartbeat of the basin.

The social structure of the ward is organized around the maintenance of the great machinery, which they treat not as cold metal ironwork, but as a giant, iron-lunged pet that must be fed, petted, and kept clean. 

Most esteemed among them are the **Anointers**. These are usually the elder women and men whose joints have begun to stiffen from the dampness. Armed with long-necked copper cans and pots of thick, grey tallow rendered from vegetable oils and fat-candles, they spend their days sliding along the copper pipes and crawling inside the wooden housings of the gears. They are incredibly sensitive to friction. An Anointer can lay a calloused palm against a sixty-foot cedar root and feel, through the wood, if a single wooden gear-tooth on the far side of the ward is swelling or running dry. They treat dry friction as a moral failing, a sin against the quietude of the cooperative.

Then there are the **Gourd-Wrights**, who possess a surgeon’s touch. They spend their lives suspended from hemp ropes sixty feet in the air, tending to the young, green storage gourds that sprout from the structural vines. Using bone splints, leather bindings, and small, steady applications of warm pine-tar, they shape the living gourds as they grow, coaxing them into perfect, carriage-sized globes, training their necks to form natural chimneys, and grafting them to the cedar-roots so they can tap directly into the trees' sap-veins for warmth. 

The children of the Sump are small, sleek creatures who look like otters. Because the walkways have no railings—which would rot too quickly—the children are tethered. From the moment they can crawl, they wear a harness of soft reed-fiber tied to a long, braided green-shoot cord that is anchored to the center of their family’s gourd-home. They spend their childhoods dangling over the emerald gloom, swinging from fungus-shelf to fungus-shelf, their skin slick with the condensation of the steam-vents. It is their job to manage the giant, pale-gray garden snails. With small willow switches dipped in mint-oil, the children herd the slow, primeval mollusks along the walkways. The snails love the smell of the mint and follow it hungrily, their rough, rasping tongues licking the green-crusted fungus-planks clean of the slippery, invasive black algae that would otherwise make the walkways deadly to walk upon.

The people of the Sump do not have "meals" in the traditional sense; they graze. The heat and humidity do not invite heavy eating. Instead, they keep small, wicker baskets hanging inside the steam-vents where they slow-steam "moss-bread"—a dense, sweet cake made from dried duckweed flour, wild yeast, and sugarcane syrup. This they eat alongside boiled snail-foot (which tastes of earth and mint) and "well-milk," a thick, white, sweet fluid tapped from the roots of the swamp-cedars, which they drink warm, straight from the copper tubes. 

At the end of the day, when the low-pressure engines vent their final, heavy sighs and the world disappears into that complete, milky white blankness, the Parishioners retreat into the amber bellies of their gourds. There are no beds here, only deep hammocks woven from the hair-like rootlets of the cedar trees. They climb into these wet hammocks, rub their skin with a paste of crushed mint and tallow to ward off the midnight mold, and sleep. 

They sleep to the sound of the water. They do not know silence. For them, peace is the sound of the Sump—the water weeping down the cedar bark, the drip of the canopy upon the moss, the lazy, hot gas bubble breaking the surface of the green duckweed pool, and the deep, heavy, and eternal *chug-thump* of the redwood wheel, turning slow and warm in the dark.
