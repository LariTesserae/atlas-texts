To observe the inhabitants of the deep basin, one must first unlearn the associations of the upper world. There is no blood here that is warm, nor is there any eye that looks back with recognition. 

The swarming builders of this valley—which a traveler might call the Slate-Turners, though they have no name for themselves—possess the heavy, greasy paleness of lard or peeled tubers. In size, they are substantial; even a young one has the mass of a hound, while the draft-sized elders are as broad as oxen, their segmented plates overlapping like wet, grey shingles. Beneath these heavy shields, their bodies are surprisingly soft, composed of a dense, pale muscle that smells faintly of old cisterns and cold oil.

They move without the clicking rattle of common crabs. The entirety of their underside is a single, continuous sole of dense, thick-piled pile—a grey, water-resistant felt that pads their joints and deadens their weight against the basalt floor. When a million of them pass through an avenue, they produce only the sound of a velvet glove sliding over wet glass. It is a mass of silent weight, a heavy, sliding murmur.

They are blind. The bright, flat pillars of light that descend from the rifts miles above are not seen by them, but felt. To a Slate-Turner, the searchlight beams are regions of dry, skin-tightening heat. They avoid the center of these light-shafts during the peak of the high day, crowding into the cool, grey margins where the dampness remains thickest. 

In place of eyes, their heads are crowned with a fringe of pale, translucent tentacles that drift through the humid air like sea anemones. These organs do not feel the wind so much as they map the acoustics of the basin. The slow, rhythmic *doop... doop* of the dripping ceiling is their grid. By measuring the intervals between the drops and the tiny, vibrating echoes they produce as they hit the circular pools, a Turner knows exactly where the nearest tower stands, where the basalt floor dips, and where another of its kind is approaching. They walk through a world composed entirely of percussion and vibration.

The mystery of their technology—the presence of the plaited cords and the great, man-sized clay jars—is solved not by the discovery of workshops, but through the observation of their death.

The Slate-Turners do not have graves, nor do they rot as soft-bodied things do. When an individual reaches the end of its long, circular life, its joints begin to stiffen. Its felted sole loses its grip on the polished basalt, and it drifts toward the margins of the stone avenues, settling near the walls. There, it ceases to move. 

Within days, the mineral-rich dew of the valley begins to settle upon its stationary back. The creature’s chitinous shell absorbs the lime from the damp air, calcifying and thickening until it is as hard as unglazed earthenware. The younger Turners approach this dead elder. With their specialized, chisel-like mandibles, they do not destroy it; they harvest it. 

They peel the outer layers of its flanks, which have softened through a final, chemical sloughing into long, gray ribbons of tough, fibrous hide. This is the "leather" used to bind the joints of the taller towers. Next, they hollow out the calcified interior of the beast, clearing away the dry, chalky marrow which they consume for its salt. What remains is the massive, heavy "jar"—the hollow, mineralized husk of their ancestor, set at the corner of a stone avenue to catch the drips from above. Nothing is brought into this valley from the outside; the city is built of the stone, and the tools to build it are grown from their own bodies.

One might watch them for weeks and believe their endless dismantling and rebuilding of the dry-slate towers is a form of madness or a collective chore without an end. But there is a slow, cold intelligence behind the labor.

The Slate-Turners are farmers of wind and moisture.

Underneath the flat plates of slate, in the dark, pressurized seams where stone presses against stone, a microscopic, starch-white fungus grows. This is not a plant, but a lithic moss that feeds upon the dampness and the lime-dust carried by the drafts. It cannot survive in the open, where the searchlight glare dries it, nor can it grow in the stagnant air of the valley's deepest depressions. It requires a continuous, gentle movement of cool, wet air.

By constructing the towers, the Turners create wind-troughs. 

When they stack thirty feet of slate in a crescent, they draw the basement drafts together, forcing a cold, whistling current through the cracks of the stone. In these dark cracks, the white fungus blooms. After a few days, when the crop is mature, a swarm of Turners will arrive. They dismantle the tower stone by stone, licking the wet, starchy mold from the face of each slate with their broad, raspy tongues. 

Once the stones are clean, they carry them half a mile away to a new plaza where the drafts have grown sluggish, stacking them once more to seed a new crop. 

The city is not a place of habitation; it is an orchard. The towers are its trees, the stone dikes are its irrigation channels, and the whistling wind is the hand that waters them.

When two vast columns of these creatures meet in the great plazas—one carrying slate to the north, the other to the west—there is no confusion. They do not possess the aggressive territory-sense of insects. Instead of colliding, they perform a slow, drifting filtration. The smaller ones slide beneath the bellies of the draft-beasts; the laden ones yield the crown of the basalt road to those carrying the heaviest slates. Their soft, pale sides rub together with a sound like wind in a dry pine forest. 

For a few minutes, the plaza becomes a single, undulating carpet of pale, segmented armor, a living floor that moves in two directions at once. Then, as quietly as they came together, the two currents untangle. The towers in the south begin to rise, and the towers in the north begin to fall, while the stone-bearing wind continues its low, indifferent sigh through the joints of the valley.
