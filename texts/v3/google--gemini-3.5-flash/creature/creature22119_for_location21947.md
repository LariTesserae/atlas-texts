Inside this chamber, life does not exist as an inhabitant of the space; it exists as part of the plumbing. Nothing here has a name for itself, and nothing here has ever seen the sun, but every moving part in this hot, wet valve is alive, shaped by the pressure of the great entity’s blood.

To look closely at the walls is to see the **Pulse-Tappers**. They are small, oblong creatures, the size of a human fist and the color of peeled garlic. They are hairless, blind, and possess no limbs—only a flat, rubbery suction pad where a face should be. They stick themselves directly to the polished walnut wood over the channels where the vascular conduits are loudest. There they remain for decades, motionless, their translucent skins vibrating so violently with the *shuck-shuck* of the sap-pistons that their outlines appear blurred. They do not eat in any traditional sense; their suction pads are porous, absorbing the trace sugars and hot vinegar-starch right through the wood-skin under the sheer force of the plant's internal pressure. They are living rivets. When one dies, it does not rot; the high antiseptic content of the sap cures its body where it hangs, turning it into a hard, golden nodule that eventually becomes part of the wall itself, a tiny, fossilized knot in the grain.

Above them, clutching the long, copper-colored vines that drone with static electricity, are the **Spark-Gleaners**. These are thin, jointed things made of dry, segmented reed-fiber, looking like starved stick-insects that have been dipped in liquid copper. They have dozens of tiny, hair-like legs that end in split copper needles, which they use to tap directly into the low-voltage lines. They move with an agonized, hydraulic slowness, dragging themselves along the ceiling to find the junctions where the current is hottest. They do not have blood; their hollow bodies are filled with a thick, conductive oil that smells of burnt insulation. When the bio-electric cords crackle with static, the Spark-Gleaners flinch, drawing the electricity into their own shells where it glows briefly with a dry, amber light. They are the system's capacitors. If a surge occurs, they absorb the excess, bloating slightly and turning a dark, scorched brown, slowly releasing the heat back into the mist so the delicate copper-vines do not melt.

The air itself is processed by the **Lobe-Breathers**. These are the largest things in the room, three or four great, slate-grey sacks hanging from the ceiling like waterlogged bags of heavy canvas. They have no skeleton, only a thick, leathery husk that expands and contracts in a slow, counter-rhythm to the walls. When the room's pressure rises too high—when the *shuck-shuck* behind the walnut grain grows too frenzied—the Lobe-Breathers open their wide, muscular circular mouths with a wet *shluck* and draw in the mineral-scented mist. Inside their bellies, the mist is cooled, distilled, and squeezed out of their lower pores as cold, clear water that falls in thin, steady pencils to the floor. They are natural pressure-valves, keeping the chamber’s atmosphere from becoming thick enough to cook the smaller residents. They have a primitive, communal nervous system; if you were to touch one, all three would contract at once, their leather sides groaning as they squeezed their air out in a single, pressurized whistle.

And down in the deepest, most ancient drainage-groove, where the hot, acidic runoff is sucked back into the fiber of the world, lives the **Sieve-Skin**. 

It is a single creature, though it has no center. It is a flat, three-foot-long ribbon of wet, black leather that has grown so perfectly into the curve of the groove that it appears to be nothing more than a strip of dark, decaying bark. But it is not decaying. Its back is covered in millions of stiff, microscopic comb-teeth that catch the debris of the room—the discarded brass legs of the cleaning beetles, the dried scales of the hose-worms, the tiny flakes of walnut-wood worn away by the fast-moving fluids. 

The Sieve-Skin does not move. It is a filter, but it is also a ledger. Because it has sat in the same drain for centuries, receiving everything the room sheds, its body has become a geological record of the greater entity's life. The thickness of its leather, the density of its comb-teeth, the very color of its dark skin change according to the quality of the sap. It is the only thing in this chamber that knows of the world three miles above. When there is a dry summer in the upper leaves, the sap runs thick and bitter, and the Sieve-Skin grows thin and hard as horn. When a great storm shakes the root-bases, the runoff carries down a fine, white silt of crushed cellulose, and the Sieve-Skin turns pale and soft, absorbing the stress of the world in its fiber. 

It has no brain to understand these things, but its flesh remembers them. It lies there in the dark grease of the drain, vibrating with the roar of the engines, a tongue that tastes the health of a giant, singing its silent, ancient song of survival to the empty, illuminated room.
