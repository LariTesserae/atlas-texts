In the vast, leaden quiet of the Great Slag-Sill, life does not struggle against the cold; it forms a partnership with the heavy, metallic silence. Every living thing on this continental terrace is a variation on the same physical theme: they are creatures of weight, oil, and mineral.

To understand what lives here, one must first look at the skin of the world.

### The Joint-Keepers and Valve-Worms
A *Slag-Mother* or *Piston-Bull* is not merely an animal; it is a traveling mountain range, a self-sustaining industrial unit that hosts its own internal and external biota. 

In the deep, recessed folds of their four-foot-thick iron hides—specifically where the massive, fifty-ton leg-joints meet the central chassis—the skin must remain soft enough to flex. Here, where the self-lubricating, graphite-rich "sill-oil" seeps from the giant's lymphatic pores, live the **Gasket-Worms** (*Siphonostoma plumbum*). 

These are finger-thick, segmented annelids made of tough, vulcanized cartilage. They have no eyes or mouths in the traditional sense; instead, they possess flat, rasping scraper-plates at either end. They spend their entire lives within the dark grease-channels of the giants, cleaning out basalt dust and iron scale that would otherwise grind the joints to a halt. If a Slag-Mother loses its Gasket-Worms, its movements grow stiff; within a decade, the joint will "cold-weld" itself under the beast's own immense weight, anchoring the giant to the spot until it starves and oxidizes.

Further up, near the warm exhaust-vents along the creature’s spine, are the nesting colonies of **Soot-Moths** (*Lithopterix carbonaria*). These are flat, triangular organisms with wings like thin sheets of mica. They do not fly so much as they drift on the hot, sulfurous updrafts of the respiration bellows. They feed on the carbonaceous crust that forms around the steam-ports, their charcoal-colored wings vibrating in a silent, high-frequency flutter that helps shed the damp iron-ash that would otherwise plug the giant’s breathing-valves.

### The Scavengers of the Slag-Casts
The giant beasts move across the horizontal landscape like glacier-slow ironworks, periodically dropping their waste: the **Slag-Casts**. These are perfect, heavy cylinders of purified, non-magnetic iron-silicate, still warm from the beast’s internal chemical smelting.

These cylinders are the primary resource for the **Corrosion-Lice** (*Ferriclastes vorax*). 

A Corrosion-Louse is the size of an anvil. It resembles a flat, many-legged dome of dark, pitted slag, practically indistinguishable from the stones around it. Its mouthparts consist of a rotating, diamond-hard grinding drill. Specialized glands in its fore-carapace secrete a highly concentrated, greenish sulfuric acid that softens the slag-casts.

When a fresh cylinder is dropped, dozens of these lice will gather around it, moving at a speed of mere inches per day. They drill into the core, extracting the trace minerals—manganese, cobalt, and nickel—that the Slag-Mother’s digestive hearth did not absorb. As they feed, they exude a cold, white salt-crust that accumulates in the cracks of the basalt like dry frost.

### The Life of the Brine-Basins
In the shallow, motion-resistant pools of grease-like condensation that dot the high plateau, life takes on a simpler, denser form.

Here, where the water is too heavy to ripple, live the **Solder-Skiffs**. 

These are flat, leaf-like organisms that float upon the surface tension of the brine. They have no legs; they move by slowly secreting a surfactant oil from their trailing edges, sliding across the dark metallic pools like drops of water on a hot griddle. Their bodies are made of a dense, spongy lead-protein matrix that shields them from the freezing wind. 

Below them, in the black, oxygen-less mud at the bottom of the basins, are the **Graphite-Drifters**. These are soft-bodied, slug-like creatures that look like tubes of wet charcoal. They plow through the heavy silt, digesting the fine iron dust that precipitates out of the air. Their waste is pure carbon, which they compress into hard, glossy flakes that line the basin floors like pieces of broken slate.

### The Drift-Sieves of the Low Sky
Even the wet, heavy twilight above the plateau is inhabited.

Suspended in the iron-colored mist are the **Drift-Sieves** (*Aeroventer gravis*). These are bladder-like organisms, some as large as carriage-houses, which drift through the freezing fog. They have no active means of propulsion; they are buoyant because their interior cavities are filled with hot, biologically generated hydrogen gas, kept warm by the slow decay of sulfur-eating bacteria in their central sacs.

As they drift, they trail long, hair-thin filaments of copper-rich fiber that drag along the basalt plateau below. These filaments act as anchors and grounding lines. When a Drift-Sieve drifts through a region of high electrostatic tension between the clouds and the ground, the current travels up these cooper threads. This charge is utilized by the beast’s internal organs to electrolize the water it absorbs from the mist, recharging its chemical batteries.

From a distance, in the dim pewter light, one might see a dozen of these dark, silent spheres hovering twenty feet above the ground, their long, metallic tendrils brushing smoothly through the wire-grass, leaving tiny, blue static sparks in their wake.

### The Process of the "Great Weathering"
Death on the Slag-Sill is not a matter of rot, but of *decommissioning*.

When a Piston-Bull finally reaches the end of its centuries-long life, its internal chemical fires cool. The great bellows falter, gasp a final, freezing plume of white steam, and stop. The four-foot-thick plates of biogenic steel begin to lose their electrical charge.

This is when the landscape claims them. 

The death of a giant is a quiet, non-violent event that lasts for fifty years. Once the internal heat is gone, the Gasket-Worms die, and their bodies dissolve into a black lubricant that runs down the legs, staining the basalt. Without the animal's active biological field to resist oxidation, the wet, iron-heavy mist begins to bite into the charcoal-gray plates.

The giant begins to rust.

First, the **Corrosion-Lice** descend in their hundreds, their acid-secreting drill-mouths working day and night, carving the massive chassis into smaller, manageable fragments. Over decades, the great rib-frames of silicate bone and steel plates are planed down by the freezing wind and the acid-feeders. 

Eventually, nothing is left but a long, low mound of reddish-brown oxide—a rust-ridge. On this ridge, the copper-colored wire-grass will grow thicker than elsewhere, its roots drinking the concentrated iron-salts, its tips reaching up into the cold, heavy fog, waiting for the next electrostatic storm to channel the sky's energy back down into the iron heart of the world.
