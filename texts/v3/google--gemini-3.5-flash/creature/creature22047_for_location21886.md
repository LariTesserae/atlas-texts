The life of this chamber does not struggle against the dark; it has digested it. 

In the absolute absence of light, the color of life has retreated into the interior. Here, things are the color of things that have never been seen: oily purples that verge on blue-black, charcoal-gray, and the pale, translucent pink of blood seen through wet membranes. Without the sun to divide the day, there is no morning or evening, only the long, wet *now* of the mountain's heart.

### The Velvet Soughers

The air of the upper chamber is inhabited by the **Velvet Soughers**. They are the keepers of the high, frantic noise that fills the wet vault. 

Each Sougher is small, no larger than a pear, with a body that is surprisingly heavy and warm to the touch. They possess no true fur; instead, their skin is covered in a dense, water-repellent pile like heavy moleskin or the velvet of an elk’s antler. This felted skin is always hot, radiating a feverish warmth that they maintain by constantly feeding on the rich, spore-laden air. 

To look upon a Sougher—if there were light to do so—would be to see a creature consisting almost entirely of ears and chest. Their ears are large, ribbed sails of pale cartilage, so thin that the network of their hot veins is visible in the dark. These ears do not merely listen; they feel the air. They can detect the passage of a single falling water-drop three ledges away, adjusting their flight path to avoid its tiny momentum. 

Where their eyes should be, there are only two deep, hairless depressions, lined with grey, heat-sensitive pits. To a Sougher, the chamber is not dark. The central engineering ring, with its low 40-hertz hum, appears to them as a massive, burning sun of pure infrared comfort, suspended in a cool void. The wet basalt columns are faint, dark-purple outlines; the mosses are warm, glowing carpets; and their fellow Soughers are bright, leaping sparks of gold and crimson.

They do not roost like birds or bats. Instead, they gather in the hanging rigging of the purple air-plants, their small, three-toed feet locking around the ropy vines by reflex. When thousand-strong colonies of them sleep, they drape their ribbed ears over their faces like cowls, forming great, quiet clusters that resemble giant, dark grapes hanging from the ceiling. When they wake, they begin to click. The sound is not intellectual; it is cooperative. Each click is a tiny *tack*—like wood on stone—that joins with millions of others to build a continuous, real-time map of the void. If a vine snaps, or if one of the slick ledges crumbles, the rhythm of the clicking shifts instantly, a ripple of adjustments running through the colony like wind through grass.

### The Basalt-Scrapers

Below the flyers, on the slick, black ledges where the moss is thick enough to swallow a footstep, live the **Basalt-Scrapers**. 

These are the slow-movers, the heavy, ancient crust of the chamber’s life. They are flat-bodied, shaped like wide, segmented shields, some as small as a hand, others as broad as a tavern shield. Their backs are covered in a soft, leathery chitin that feels like wet horn, colored the deep gray of the stone they inhabit. They have no eyes, no ears, and no wings. Their entire existence is defined by the surface of the basalt.

Underneath their heavy shields, they possess thirty or forty pairs of short, stubby legs, each ending in a muscular pad that secretes a sticky, clear mucus. This mucus allows them to walk upside down on the wet, water-worn ceilings or cling to the vertical faces of the great central pillar even as sheets of water rush over them.

As they move, their heavy, ribbed bellies rub against the basalt with a dry, rhythmic hiss: *shhh-t... shhh-t... shhh-t*. They are the vacuum cleaners of the ledges. They do not eat the moss; rather, they graze upon the thin, jelly-like layer of blue-grey slime that grows between the moss roots and the stone. This slime is rich in minerals and ancient bacteria that feed on the raw elements of the basalt itself. 

The Scrapers live at a pace that matches the mountain. A single Scraper may take forty years to crawl the length of a single ledge. When two of them meet, they do not fight or flee; they simply halt. They press their flat, blunt heads together, exchanging chemical signals through their antennae—sharing the flavor of the mosses they have eaten, the temperature of the water they have crossed—before slowly, over the course of several days, sliding over and around each other’s shields to continue their journey. They are so slow, and so silent in their growth, that the purple mosses often take root on their backs, turning them into small, crawling islands of vegetation that carry their own miniature gardens through the dark.

### The Drift-Bells

Suspended in the empty air between the ledges and the central ring are the **Drift-Bells**. These are perhaps the most mysterious inhabitants of the chamber, for they never touch the stone, nor do they ever land.

A Drift-Bell is a living balloon. Its body is a vast, thin-walled sphere of purple-black tissue, three or four feet across, filled with a light, warm gas produced by its own slow metabolism. They are almost weightless, perfectly balanced against the density of the warm, humid air. 

At the bottom of this living sphere hangs a long, delicate fringe of purple tentacles—fine as spider silk—that trails twenty feet into the empty space below. These threads are covered in a sticky, sweet-smelling fluid that attracts the Soughers. As a Drift-Bell floats on the warm convection currents rising from the 40-hertz ring, its threads comb the dark. A Sougher, flying too close or distracted by its own echolocation, may brush against a thread; the thread instantly clings, and the Drift-Bell slowly draws the small flyer up into its soft, bell-shaped mouth.

The Drift-Bells have no brains as we understand them, but their entire skins are sensitive to vibration. They are governed by the 40-hertz hum of the central ring. This vibration acts as a tether; it keeps them centered in the warm air column of the void. If a Drift-Bell drifts too far toward the cold, wet walls of the chamber, the hum fades in its skin, and its muscles contract, squeezing its gas airbag to lower it back down into the warmer, louder currents. They are like slow, dark moons orbiting the artificial hearth, rising and falling in silence, their long tentacles whispering through the dark like the strings of a harp.

### The Sieve-Mouth at the Base

And finally, at the very bottom of the void, where the basalt pillar disappears into the wet, unseen floor, there is **The Sieve-Mouth**.

It is difficult to say if this is one creature or many. It appears as a vast, continuous carpet of wrinkled, leathery flesh, black as pitch, that covers the floor of the abyss. It has survived here for thousands of years, fed by the constant, dripping rain of the chamber. 

Every drop of water that falls from the ceiling, every dead Sougher that falls from the vines, every discarded piece of moss or shell from a Scraper eventually lands upon the Sieve-Mouth. Its surface is covered in millions of tiny, cup-like depressions, each lined with microscopic hairs. When a water-drop hits one of these cups with a soft *clock*, the cup closes, filtering out the organic dust, the minerals, and the spores, before allowing the clean, filtered water to seep through its body and down into the deep, subterranean channels below.

The Sieve-Mouth is the great heart of the room's economy. It is completely still, save for the opening and closing of its millions of mouths. It is the thing that hears the entire room; every click of the Soughers, every scrape of the basalt-dwellers, and every slow shift of the drifting ring is recorded in the tension of its vast, leathery skin. 

Here, in the warm, wet dark, life has found its perfect circle. The ring warms the air; the air grows the moss; the moss feeds the crawlers; the crawlers drop their waste; the flyers carry the seeds; and the Sieve-Mouth catches it all, turning the mountain's slow, weeping decay into a soft, eternal hum.
