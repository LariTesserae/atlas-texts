To understand those who live in the vertical basalt wound, one must first understand that to them, the concept of a flat surface is a myth, an absurd fantasy of a wild and boundless world they will never see. 

They are a people who have grown thin and long, stretched by the verticality of their home. If you were to see one standing—though they rarely stand in the way a lowlander does—they would appear like pale, jointed reeds. They are narrow in the hip and broad in the shoulder, with ribs that flare wide and shallow under skin the color of wet slate. This barrel-chested frame is an evolutionary necessity; their lungs are immense, designed to squeeze every scrap of oxygen from the cold, metallic air. They breathe with a slow, deliberate rhythm that matches the rising drafts of the canyon.

Their hands and feet are their most remarkable feature. They do not have the soft, fleshy heels of valley-dwellers. Instead, the palms of their hands and the soles of their long, split-toed feet are covered in a dense, charcoal-grey leather, patterned with microscopic ridges similar to the whorls on a gecko’s toe. When they press a hand against the sharp black basalt, the air is expelled from beneath these ridges, creating a silent, pneumatic seal. They do not climb so much as they flow across the rock, their movements liquid and certain, requiring no more effort than an idle stroll.

They do not speak with drafty, vibrating vocal cords. In the absolute silence of the rift, a loud voice would be a violence—shattering the fragile, floating water-globes, startling the cloud-moth drifts, and perhaps vibrating the unstable shale until it wept stone-shingle down upon the lower huts. 

Instead, they speak in three distinct tonalities of silence:

First, there is the **Somatic Gesture**, a complex syntax of finger-flicks, shoulder-tilts, and the spreading of their long, pale fingers. In the dim lavender light, these hand-shapes look like the opening and closing of the hand-ferns that grow beside them.

Second, there is the **Basalt-Tap**. Every inhabitant carries a small, polished stylus of fossilized bone, needle-sharp at one end and blunt at the other. By touching this stylus to the rock and tapping with a fingernail, they send vibrations directly through the stone. To a stranger, the rock would seem dead, but to these people, the basalt is a library of gossip, warning, and history. A mother three tiers down can press her temple to the wall of her slate hut and feel the rhythmic, comforting scratch of her child’s stylus four levels above, telling her they have successfully harvested a cluster of mist-plums.

Third, they use the **Bioluminescent Shutter**. Like the flat-bodied lizards that share their walls, the people have developed a symbiotic relationship with a species of light-emitting algae that they culture in shallow, scarred tracks along their forearms and throats. By flexing their skin-folds, they can expose or conceal these glowing blue patches, sending quiet, stuttering signals across the chasm from hut to hut when the mists are too thick for gestures.

***

They call themselves the *Oru*, which is not a spoken word but a soft, double-inhale through the teeth, mimicking the sound of the wind-sigh in the chimneys.

The *Oru* do not sew with thread; they weave with the pale, sticky silk harvested from the cocoons of the cloud-moths. Their garments are gossamer-thin but layered ten or twelve times, trapping pockets of air that are warmed by their own skin. These clothes are dyed a deep, dusty violet using the spores of the velvet moss, rendering them nearly invisible against the damp basalt walls.

Their society is organized around the maintenance of the **Muscle-Vines**. These thick, fibrous creepers are the true veins of the village. They are not merely plants; they are semi-sentient, muscle-like tendons that anchor the slate huts to the mountain. Twice a day, at the peaks of the cold-tides, the *Oru* must "grease" the vines. Specialized tenders—the *Viners*—crawl along the suspension bridges with pots of fat harvested from the geothermal vents. They rub this tallow into the joints of the vines to keep them pliant, whispering to them through the vibration of their fingers. If a vine dies, the hut it supports will drift outward, catching the cold updrafts, swinging wildly until the slate shingles scatter into the nothingness below.

The children of the *Oru* are born with an instinctive lack of vertigo. Before they can walk, they are strapped to their mothers' backs with moth-silk harnesses. A baby's first independent movement is not a step, but a "cling"—learning to lock their fingers into the basalt fissures while their parents tend the mist-gutters. 

By the age of five, these children are given their first **Water-Cup**. This is a shallow, translucent bowl carved from the sternum of a mountain bird. The children sit on the edges of the suspension bridges, their legs dangling over the three-hundred-yard drop, waiting for the floating water-globes to drift past. With infinite patience and impossibly gentle hands, they slide their bowls beneath the shivering, plum-sized spheres, capturing them whole. A child who can bring home three unbroken water-globes in a single afternoon is considered ready to learn the craft of the stone-masons.

***

Beside the *Oru*, other strange lives share this sanctuary:

Deep within the dark crevices behind the huts live the **Lanth-Lizards**. These are flat, boneless-looking creatures that can squeeze into cracks no wider than a coin. They are the domestic cats of the rift. They are tolerated—even loved—because they hunt the tiny, invasive mites that burrow into the muscle-vines. The *Oru* often carry a lizard or two in the wide sleeves of their coats. The lizards enjoy the warmth of human skin, and in return, their throat-sacs pulse with a soft, cool-blue light that helps the *Oru* read the slate-texts in the dark interiors of their huts.

Then there are the **Shiver-Foxes**, though they are foxes only in their alert, pointed ears. These tiny, puff-furred mammals are the true acrobats of the chasm. Their fur is so incredibly soft and dense that they look like tufts of grey dust blown from the stones. They have no claws; instead, their paws are cushioned with millions of microscopic, hair-like cilia that allow them to run upside down on the roofs of the slate huts. They are entirely silent. When they hunt the cloud-moths, they do not leap; they *launch*, trusting the thin air to carry them. They can flatten their bodies mid-air, using their wide, skin-flaps to glide from one side of the gorge to the other. To the *Oru*, the shiver-foxes are weather-tellers: when the foxes retreat deep into the basalt cracks and press themselves flat against the geothermal vents, it means a "frost-shear" is coming—a sudden drop in temperature so severe it will freeze the floating water-globes solid mid-air, turning them into falling showers of ice-glass.

At the very bottom of the fissure—a place where the violet light never reaches and the air is thick with the sulfurous stink of the steam-vents—lives the **Grave-Moss**. It is not a plant, but an animal colony of blind, thread-like worms that have knitted themselves into a single, vast carpet. When an *Oru* dies, or when a shiver-fox falls from the bridges, they do not bury them. There is no earth to dig. Instead, they gently lower the body on a long, braided moss-rope down into the dark chimney until it touches the Grave-Moss. Within hours, the colony dissolves the organic matter, returning the precious minerals to the chasm’s cycle, leaving behind only the clean white bones, which the *Oru* retrieve to carve into styluses, picks, and water-cups.

In this way, nothing is ever lost to the void. The stone remains young, sharp, and hungry; but the life that clings to its shoulders is soft, silent, and endlessly resourceful, suspended in a cold, eternal dawn.
