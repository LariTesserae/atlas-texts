To speak of the inhabitants of this place is to speak of things that do not live so much as they are *exerted*. In this basin, life is not a vessel that contains a soul or a spark; it is a high-pressure leak. 

The primary residents are the **Cambium-Hosts**, the very stalks and tubers that choke the black basalt. They are not passive wood. If you were to press your ear to the rubbery, soot-caked rind of one of the steamship-sized bulbs, you would not hear the slow, mute rise of sap, but a frantic, mechanical sloshing. These plants are hydraulic systems. Their interiors are divided into thousands of pressurized vacuoles, separated by tough, fibrous valves that resemble the gaskets of a steam locomotive. 

When a stalk grows—which it does with a constant, wet, creaking momentum—it does not wait for cell division. It inflates. The bulb at the base acts as a biological accumulator, drawing the hot, mineral-heavy grease of the sea through its copper-wire roots and forcing it upward under hundreds of pounds of pressure. This is why they scream. The sound is the sound of hot, alkaline sap being forced through tiny, semi-permeable membranes at supersonic speeds. It is a high, whistling hiss that rises from the weed-beds like the venting of a thousand boilers. 

Because they grow too fast, they are constantly failing. A stalk that has reached eighty feet in an afternoon will suddenly suffer a catastrophic drop in internal pressure; its valve-walls rupture with a sound like a pistol shot, and the entire structure buckles, turned instantly into a limp, stringy rag of green flesh. 

From these ruptures emerges the closest thing the basin has to an animal: the **Mulch-Brood**.

They are not worms, nor are they insects, though they perform the duties of both. The Mulch-Brood are self-organizing knots of escaped vascular fiber. When a kelp-cable snaps, the loose, frayed ends of its inner conduits do not always die. Instead, they slough off into the warm grease-water as independent, twitching tangles. They look like balls of wet, rotten twine, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a hay-bale. 

The Mulch-Brood have no intelligence, no eyes, and no mouths. They move by capillary action—drinking the warm brine at their front and squeezing it out behind them, dragging themselves through the sharp basalt needles with a wet, sucking crawl. They are incredibly hot; their internal fermentation is so intense that they glow with a faint, curdled yellow light, and they leave trails of cooked, gray starch behind them. When two of these tangles collide, they do not fight; they simply fuse, growing larger and more unwieldy until they are cut to pieces by the obsidian shelves or crushed by the turning of the giant iron gears.

And around those gears lives the **Chafe**.

The Chafe is a mineral-biological crust that has claimed the machinery as its habitat. Wherever forty-foot bronze teeth gnash together under the water, the Chafe grows. It is a hard, dense plating of iron-saturated calcium and dried plant pulp, resembling a black, stony tooth-enamel that coats the wear-points of the engines. 

The Chafe *requires* the crushing weight of the machinery to live. It is a slow-growing lithophyte that feeds on the heat generated by friction. In the moments when two massive gear teeth meet, compressing the grease-water between them until it flashes into superheated steam, the Chafe expands. It drinks the scraped iron dust and the sulfurous heat. 

If the machines were to stop, the Chafe would die within hours, its rocky plates cooling, cracking, and falling away into the deep rifts. To keep the teeth clean, the Chafe produces tiny, brittle protrusions—long, needle-like fingers of lime and rust that constantly scrape at the gear axles, clearing away the hyper-vegetation before it can jam the teeth. They are the maintenance crew of an engine that has no builder, born from the very friction that threatens to destroy it.

But the most unsettling inhabitants are those that dwell in the **Dry Bubbles**.

These pockets of vacuum and bone-dry air are not empty. Inside them, where the roar of the water is suddenly reduced to a distant, thudding vibration, live the **Skeptics**.

They are not humans, but they have the shapes of men who have been forgotten in a cellar. They are composed entirely of the dry, white salt-crust that precipitates out of the warm water at the margins of the bubbles, held together by a fragile web of desiccated, brown kelp-fiber. They are as light as pith and as brittle as scorched paper. 

The Skeptics do not move when the water is watched, but when the shadows shift—when a blue copper flash illuminates a bubble from thirty yards away—one can see that they have changed their positions. They are always found near the impossible things: the stacks of dry ledger sheets, the motionless brass pendulums, the brass compasses whose needles spin without stopping. 

They do not breathe, for there is nothing to breathe in the vacuum bubbles, but they seem to be listening. They stand in groups of three or four, their blank, salt-crust faces turned toward the wet, vibrating walls of their bubbles, staring at the green, screaming chaos from which they are excluded. If a bubble wall shifts—if a current of heavy grease-water collapses the pocket—the Skeptics do not drown; they simply dissolve in a second, their white salt-frames scattering into the dark, oily sea like a handful of flour thrown into gravy.

And finally, far below the gears, in the deepest, narrowest rifts where the basalt skin of the world is still red and soft, lives the **Spindle**.

It is not a single creature, but a singular, miles-wide network of copper-rich nerves that runs beneath the floor of the entire basin. It is the Spindle that drives the engines; it is the Spindle that grows the bulbs. It has no brain, but it has a pulse. Every four minutes, a massive electrical discharge—the same blue fire that occasionally shorts out the suspended cables—travels through the Spindle from the planet’s hot core.

When the charge hits, the copper-wire roots of the bulbs contract. The iron towers plunge. The gears turn. The entire basin shudders in a single, coordinated spasm of growth and iron. 

The Spindle is the true citizen here. It is a mind made of hot metal and wet starch, thinking a single, endless thought: *More. Faster. Harder.* It does not know that there is a dry world above the water, or a cold sky. It knows only the weight of the bruised chicory sea, the razor kiss of the basalt, and the magnificent, deafening music of its own useless, beautiful, screaming work.
