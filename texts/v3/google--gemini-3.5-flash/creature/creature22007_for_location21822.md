### The Wet-Born

To survive in Oru, the human body has made its peace with the water. The people who tend the great boilers and scrape the terraces are known to outsiders as the Wet-Born, though they call themselves simply *the Operators*. 

They are small-statured and limber-jointed, with bones that seem to yield rather than break when they slip on the wet schist. Because hair cannot survive the perpetual warmth and humidity without rotting at the follicle, the Operators are entirely bald. They have neither eyebrows nor eyelashes; in their place, they have developed thick, hooded eye-orbits and a pronounced, pale nictitating membrane—a semi-transparent second eyelid that clicks shut horizontally to clear the condensation from their corneas without their having to blink. Their skin is the color of skimmed milk, but it is not soft; it is tough and rubbery, smelling faintly of the copper-slick and grease-mat fat they rub into their joints to prevent the "creeping itch"—a fungus that thrives in the warmth of the lower tiers.

The most striking adaptation of the Operators is sensory. In a world where the air is a constant, deafening roar of falling water, iron-shriek, and the clicking of billions of shingle-crusts, spoken language is useless. To talk, an Operator does not use their throat. 

Instead, they communicate using *The Knotted Chord*—a language of rapid, percussive taps produced by heavy copper rings worn on their thumbs. They strike these rings against their leather aprons, their iron tools, or the copper-clad plates of their vests. Two Operators standing on opposite sides of a boiling sluicing-run do not look at each other's faces; they look at each other’s hands, listening to the sharp, metallic *clink-clack* that cuts through the low-frequency rumble of the world like shears through silk. When they must speak in the dark of their windowless stone homes, they rest their hands on the same wooden table, feeling the vibrations of the tap-tongue through their fingertips.

### The Silt-Suckers

Because there are no draft animals that could keep their footing on the three-second pulse of the shingle-crusts, the heavy work of excavation and clearing is shared with the *Silt-Suckers*. 

These are massive, blind annelids, as thick as whiskey barrels and up to forty feet long, that live in the silt-choked channels of the lower terraces. They are grey-pink, segmented, and entirely covered in a thick, defensive mucus that tastes of bitter zinc. The Operators have domesticated them, not with whips or reins, but with steam-prods and copper rings pierced through their heavy, muscular head-flaps.

A Silt-Sucker’s mouth is a three-lobed ring of black, chitinous teeth that can grind through soft schist like sand. They are set to work in the aqueducts, crawling slowly upstream against the torrent, their massive bodies vacuuming up the mineral sediment and crushed shingle-shells that would otherwise choke the iron intake valves. 

Once full, a Sucker is led to a dry-docking pen, where its belly is massaged by three or four Operators using heavy wooden levers. The beast regurgitates a thick, chalky slurry of calcium carbonate. This "cement-spit" is the primary building material of Oru; when mixed with ground basalt and copper-slag, it hardens under water into a dark, indestructible mortar that bolts the city to the mountain’s wet ribs.

### The Siphon-Hounds

Though the plates of Oru are rich with life, they are also home to the *Siphon-Hounds*, which serve the Operators as both watch-beasts and clean-up crews. 

A Siphon-Hound looks less like a dog and more like a land-dwelling lungfish that has grown four squat, pillar-like legs ending in broad, fleshy suction cups. They have no fur; their skin is loose and folded, like wet velvet, and colored a mottled slate-purple that matches the shingle-crusts. They have no eyes, but their snout is dominated by a pair of long, prehensile nostrils that can stretch and bend like small hoses.

The Hounds are kept in the lower residences and the valve-chambers. They are trained to hunt the *Rust-Lice*—crayfish-sized isopods that chew through the lead gaskets and tar-paint of the steam lines. A Hound will crawl along the pipes, head low, its nostrils snuffling into every seam. When it detects the chemical scent of a louse or a leak, it seals its mouth over the spot and creates a powerful vacuum, sucking the pest out of its crevice with a wet *sloop*. 

In the evenings, the Hounds are allowed to sleep on the stone thresholds of the houses. They make a sound like a wet bellows—a rhythmic, bubbling sigh that the Operators find comforting, signaling that the seals of their home are dry and clear.

### The Luffers

In the air above the towers, where the steam meets the cold sky glass, live the *Luffers*. 

These are the great gliders of the Heights, creatures that have rejected both the stone and the water. They are flat and disc-shaped, like giant, fleshy shields five feet across, made of a thin, translucent cartilage that glows a pale, bioluminescent green in the dark. They have no wings to flap; instead, their bodies are concave, acting as small parachutes that catch the rising heat of the boiler-flues.

On their undersides, the Luffers have hundreds of tiny, thread-like cilia that hook into the drift-midges. They do not hunt; they simply hang in the warm updrafts, drifting from one chimney-cluster to another like great, living kites. 

The Operators value the Luffers for their oil. Once a season, when the wind turns from the west and the mist thickens, the Luffers drift too low, weighed down by the condensation on their backs. The Operators catch them with long, hooked copper poles, harvesting the sweet, clear fat from their perimeter rings. This oil is the only substance that can keep the copper steam-valves from seizing up in the wet heat; it smells of ozone and vinegar, and when burned in the windowless stone houses, it casts a cold, flickering green light that does not produce soot.

### The Dry-Box Dwellers

For all their adaptations, the human residents of Oru cannot remain in the wet forever. The constant damp is a slow killer; it softens the nails, rots the skin between the toes, and fills the lungs with a cold, heavy fluid that the Operators call "the silt-cough." 

To survive, every home in Oru is built around a *Dry-Box*. 

The Dry-Box is a small, vault-like room constructed of double-walled basalt blocks, with steam-pipes running through the hollow space between them. Inside, the temperature is kept at a parched, roasting one hundred and ten degrees Fahrenheit. There is no water allowed inside; the air is so dry that it makes the eyes sting and the throat crack.

Here, the family strips off their wet leathers and spends eight hours of every twenty-four in absolute dryness. It is in these boxes that the true domestic life of Oru occurs. 

Because traditional paper would turn to mush in the outer rooms, the Operators keep their records here, scratching their genealogies and boiler-receipts into thin plates of dried grease-mat hide using copper needles. Here, too, they raise the *Dry-Moths*—the only non-aquatic insects on the Heights. These are fat, white, silk-spinning caterpillars that feed on the dried lichen-pastes imported from the dry western plains three hundred miles away. The silk they spin is coarse and golden; it is the only fabric the Operators wear next to their skin beneath their heavy, greased leathers, providing a breathable, dry layer that saves them from the rot.

To enter a Dry-Box after a twelve-hour shift on the wet copper scaffolding is said to be like being born again. The Operators lie on shelves of woven wire, their skin crackling as it yields its moisture to the parched air, listening to the iron pulse of the city outside through the thick basalt walls—a world of steam and water that they have conquered, one dry breath at a time.
