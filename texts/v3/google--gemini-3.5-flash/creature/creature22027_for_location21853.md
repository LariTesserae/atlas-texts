To think of the Oru-Vatta as a desert is to misunderstand the nature of abundance. It is a desert only if one defines life by its franticness—by the green of chlorophyll, the snap of jaws, and the hurry of migrations. Here, life does not run; it settles, thick as grease, over a world that has discarded the need for change.

The silent crowd that covers the plateau exists in a state of perfect, frictionles symbiosis. At the bottom of this quiet machine are the **Pellicles**—those millions of dinner-plate-sized, wet-slate discs that tile the granite. 

If you were to gently peel one of these discs from the stone, it would not fight you. It has no teeth or claws, and its grip is maintained only by a weak hydraulic suction. In your hand, a Pellicle would feel heavy, cool, and surprisingly thick, like a slab of fresh, salt-cured lard. Its top surface is matte and slightly rough, mimicking the texture of the granite so closely that when dried by the noon sun, it is indistinguishable from the rock. Its underside, however, is a pale, pearlescent lavender, ribboned with a network of thin, exposed veins that pulse with a cold, copper-based blue blood. 

The Pellicles do not have brains; instead, they possess a "nerve-sheet"—a decentralized, web-like grid that covers their entire lower surface. When they lie tiled together, their edges overlap, allowing these nerve-sheets to touch. In this way, the millions of discs across a single dome form a single, continuous sensory organ. They feel the planetary curve as a collective; they feel the approach of the evening mist hours before it rises, their edges curling slightly in anticipation like the leaves of a sensitive plant. They feed on the invisible films of prehistoric yeast and blue-green mineral-molds that grow in the microscopic pores of the granite, licking the stone clean with thousands of tiny, microscopic tongues that cover their bellies like the pile of a carpet.

Above them, the air belongs to the **Aero-Skins**, the translucent drift-globes. 

These floating bladders are the color of diluted milk, their skins so thin that the sun shines through them, casting pale, circular shadows that drift across the Pellicles below. Inside their spherical bodies, one can see a hanging basket of dark, emerald-green threads. This is their lung and leaf; they do not eat, but rather sustain themselves on a perpetual internal cycle. The green threads use the bright, yellow sunlight of the midday to process the biological hydrogen that keeps the globe aloft, turning their own waste gases back into nutrients. 

Perhaps the most beautiful moment on the Oru-Vatta occurs at dusk. As the air cools and the hydrogen inside the Aero-Skins contracts, they lose their buoyancy. They do not fall; they sink with a majestic, dream-like slowness, landing softly upon the backs of the Pellicles. The Pellicles, which have spent the entire day absorbing the heat of the sun, act as living hot-water bottles. The globes nestle against them, drawing the day’s stored warmth from the gray discs. In return, as the moisture of the evening mist condenses on the cold, balloon-like skins of the Aero-Skins, it doesn't run off onto the dry stone. Instead, it drips from their lavender cilia directly into the margins of the Pellicles, watering them through the night. 

It is a silent, ancient contract: warmth for water, shelter for sweat.

In the endless cracks that split the ancient granite—crevices no wider than a finger—live the **Sieve-Spindles**. These are the velvet-skinned cylinders, grey-white and blind, that fill the darkness below the stones. 

If the Pellicles are the skin of the plateau and the Aero-Skins are its breath, the Sieve-Spindles are its chemistry. They have no mouths, only a contractile pore at either end of their cylindrical bodies. Every four seconds, they draw in the thick, starch-scented mist of the plateau, filter it through their fibrous, spongy interiors to extract micro-pollen, mineral dust, and nitrogen, and expel it as dry, warm air. A single crevice may contain ten thousand of these tiny, pulsing cylinders, all breathing in unison. It is their collective exhalations that give the air of the Oru-Vatta its peculiar smell of toasted starch and old lime—the comforting, warm scent of a giant, clean bakery that has been left empty for a thousand years.

But it is in the "Hollows"—the ancient, hand-scooped sandstone pillars—where the mystery of the Oru-Vatta’s past resides. Here live the **Loom-Snails**, the shell-less, fist-sized creatures that clog the spiral wind-flutes of the mounds.

The Loom-Snails are completely transparent, save for a small, hard nucleus in their center that glows with a faint, cold, greenish bioluminescence when the sun goes down. They do not move during the day; they pile six or seven deep inside the vertical flutes, their soft, gelatinous bodies conforming to the inner curves of the sandstone like poured wax.

These snails are almost certainly the degenerate, shrunken descendants of the **Silt-Tongues**—the legendary builders who shaped these pillars millions of years ago, before the plateau was lifted three miles into the sky. 

In that ancient epoch, when the Oru-Vatta was still a low, wet delta of red mud and river-silt, the Silt-Tongues were massive, multi-limbed creatures of soft cartilage. They had no tools, but their saliva was a powerful, quick-setting mineral glue. Using only their broad, muscular tongues and their own mucus, they scooped the river-silt and piled it into these massive, porous towers to escape the rising tides. They carved the spiral flutes with their bodies, allowing the coastal winds to blow through the mud and cool their immense, wet hulls.

As the land rose and the water retreated, the Silt-Tongues did not die; they simply adapted by letting go. 

Mind, on a finished world, is an unnecessary burden. It is an organ of friction, useful only when there are problems to solve, predators to outsmart, or food to hunt. In the perfect, slow-motion balance of the Oru-Vatta, there were no problems left. And so, over ten thousand centuries, the Silt-Tongues let their minds dissolve. They shed their size; they shed their bones; they shed their memories. They became small, simple, and silent. They stopped building because the towers were already built, and they moved into the hollows their ancestors had carved, content to spend eternity huddled in the dark, warm flutes, listening to the wind they no longer had the ears to hear.

Even death on the Oru-Vatta has been smoothed of its jagged edges. There are no scavengers, because there is no violence. Instead, there are the **Mantle-Beasts**.

The Mantle-Beasts are the largest and rarest residents of the domes. They look like great, low-slung cushions of gray-pink felt, some three to four feet across, which move so slowly that their progress is measured in weeks rather than hours. They are the cleaners. 

When an Aero-Skin finally reaches the end of its life—perhaps after forty years of floating—its biological gas-integrity fails. It does not burst; it simply deflates, its skin settling onto the granite like a yellowed, wet sheet of parchment. The nearest Mantle-Beast, sensing the chemical change from half a mile away, begins its long, slow march. 

When it arrives, it creeps over the deflated globe, covering it entirely with its broad, warm underbelly. Underneath its body, the Mantle-Beast secretes a liquid enzyme that is entirely acid-free and sweet-smelling. Over the course of three days, it dissolves the dead skin, turning it into a sterile, nutrient-rich jelly which it absorbs directly through its own skin. The leftovers of this process are left behind as a clear, sweet glaze on the stone. The Pellicles follow this trail like sheep following a salt-lick, consuming every trace until the granite is bare and clean once more.

And when a Mantle-Beast itself dies, it does not rot. It has no teeth, no bones, and very little liquid in its flesh. Instead, as its life-force departs, its outer felt-skin begins to absorb the mineral salts from the nightly mist. Over a decade, the dead beast slowly mineralizes, its soft, gray-pink tissues converting molecule by molecule into hard, pinkish-gray granite. 

It becomes a hummock. It becomes the very stone it spent its life cleaning.

To stand on the summit of the Oru-Vatta is to look down at a mountain that is not made of dead rock, but of the fossilized, quiet bodies of a trillion ancestors who have simply completed their work and become the earth itself. It is a world of biological completion—a warm, wet, stone-slow clock, ticking its way toward an eternity of perfect peace.
