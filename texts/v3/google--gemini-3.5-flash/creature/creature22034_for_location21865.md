To find the inhabitants of this place, you must first unlearn what it means to be a creature.

Look at the floating globes of water. They do not drift aimlessly; they are being steered. If you watch one closely as it glides between two carbon towers, you will notice that its surface is not entirely smooth. It ripples against the wind of its own movement, and at its center, where the light of the lavender conduits refractorily pools, there is a thickening.

These are the tenants of the suspended city. They do not walk the walkways because they have no use for feet, nor do they fly through the air like birds. They are liminal, half-solid things—beings of liquid crystalline structures, colloidal silica, and soft, self-healing polymers. They are the *Vitreous*.

To a human eye, a single citizen looks like a ribbon of heavy, dark oil suspended inside a capsule of its own personal gravity. They are roughly the length of a tall man but have no fixed shape. They can stretch themselves into thin, shivering threads that wind through the hollow carbon lattices, or they can condense into compact, dense spheres of dark glass to rest inside the dwelling modules. 

They are as warm as the air, smelling of the same freshly sheared basalt and hot silicon. Indeed, they are made of it. 

They do not have faces, but one end of their ribboned bodies terminates in a dense, frilled crown of silica fibers. These fibers are incredibly sensitive, acting as eyes, ears, and hands all at once. They glow with a faint, internal lavender luminescence that matches the city's power grids. When two of these beings meet in the humid void, they do not speak; they touch their crowns together. In that brief contact, there is no sound, only a sudden, silent flare of ultraviolet light as they exchange cold, dense packets of thought, memory, and geometry.

The city’s apparent desertion is an illusion of scale and speed. The Vitreous move differently than we do. When they must travel long distances across the hanging metropolis, they do not use the walkway platforms. Instead, they approach the glowing channels of lavender light. A Vitreous will press its liquid body against the conduit, and in a fraction of a second, its material form is translated into a wave-front of coherent energy. They travel *as* the light, a purple pulse sliding along the glass tracks at the speed of electricity, only to condense back into wet, dark oil when they reach their destination. What looks to you like an empty transit system is actually a highway crowded with thousands of people traveling as pure illumination.

They live in the hollowed-out modules, but these are not homes in our sense. They are chambers of absolute stillness. Inside, there is no furniture, only a gentle, localized gravity field that suspends a single, large pool of warm, mineral-rich water. A Vitreous will submerge themselves in this water for days, dissolving their outer membranes to mingle with the silicon-rich runoff. This is how they sleep, and it is how they feed—by absorbing the raw minerals of the earth that have been dissolved by the constant, hot rain.

Between them and the aggressive greenery, there is a deep, mute contract. The Vitreous do not fear the moss and the ferns; they require them. The plants are the city's lungs and processors. The broad-leafed, translucent ferns scrub the sulfurous vents, transforming the hot volcanic gas into breathable, humid oxygen. 

More extraordinary still is the long, root-like ivy that drapes between the towers. These vines, which feed on the electromagnetic hum of the city, are the library of the Vitreous. They do not write books or store data in digital clouds. They write their histories, their sciences, and their genealogies into the genetic code of the ivy. When a young Vitreous wishes to learn of the city's founding—of the day they silent-split this basalt cavern open from the mantle—it does not read. It glides to a hanging vine, wraps its silica crown around a wet, glowing leaf, and drinks the history directly into its neural network.

They are a young people in an old world, or perhaps an incredibly old people in a brand-new cradle. The cavern is newly split because they are still actively carving it, expanding their territory by using silent, high-frequency sound waves to fracture the deep rock. 

And at the very bottom, in the roar and hiss of the deep, black-water sump, is where they begin. The sump is not merely a drain; it is a womb. The excess runoff of water, raw basalt dust, and artificial polymers collects there, churning in the dark. In that violent, hot soup, new life precipitates. Small, oily globules form in the foam, growing denser, absorbing the ambient lavender light from the towers above, until they are heavy enough to drag themselves up the wet green moss of the towers—new, silent children of glass and steam, climbing up to join the perfect, humming hive.
