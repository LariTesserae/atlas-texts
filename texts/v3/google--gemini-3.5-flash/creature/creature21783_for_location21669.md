To understand the life that occupies this high-altitude abyss, one must first discard the concept of the soft, wet interior. Here, there are no bellies of warm blood, no damp membranes, and no yielding skins. In a world locked at one degree above freezing, where the pressure is immense but the gravity is weak, life is a branch of crystallography. 

The inhabitants do not swim so much as they align. They do not hunt so much as they conduct.

***

### The Glass-Shrimp (*Scyphozell*)
The most numerous inhabitants are the needle-like glass-shrimp. They do not resemble the jointed, twitching crustaceans of shallow seas; they are more like fragments of broken chandeliers, drifted into long, fluid arrays. 

Each shrimp is a three-inch spindle of hollow quartz, perfectly clear, with twelve pairs of stiff, hair-like silica tines radiating from its midsection. They have no eyes—light is irrelevant to their survival—but their entire length is etched with micro-grooves that act as diffraction gratings for sound. When the three-mile-wide turbines thrum, these grooves vibrate in sympathy. 

A single glass-shrimp is practically inert, a tiny crystal floating in the blue. But in their millions, they form cohesive sheets that can stretch for three miles in any direction. These sheets behave like a single, liquid mirror. They do not swim by muscular contraction; instead, they utilize the piezo-electric properties of their own shells. By flexing their crystalline bodies under the influence of the turbines' electromagnetic fields, they warp the water around them, gliding in shimmering, geometric curves toward the basalt spires.

There, they graze. They do not eat plants, for there are none. Instead, they cling to the razor edges of the young basalt, where the mineral concentration is richest. They use microscopic, diamond-hard mandibles to scrape the microscopic flakes of silica and iron-rich dust that sheers off the spires. When they have filled their hollow interiors with mineral grit, they glow with a faint, scattering amber light—the only warm color in the entire blue universe.

***

### The Chalcophiles (*The Patina-Gleaners*)
On the green-patinaed copper housings of the great machines themselves lives a specialized class of sessile life known as the *Chalcophiles*. 

To the casual observer, they look like part of the machinery: heavy, pewter-colored wedges and flat plates, bolted to the copper plates by their own calcareous secretions. They are slow beyond human comprehension. A Chalcophile may take three centuries to move the length of a single rivet.

These creatures have bypassed the need for organic food entirely. Their bodies are composed of layers of lead, tin, and a dense, self-healing plastic-like oil. They are galvanic. By anchoring one end of their bodies to the iron rivets and the other to the copper sheathing, they create a biological battery, drawing a micro-current directly from the galvanic corrosion of the machine itself. 

They are the tenders of the engines. As they slowly scrape across the metal, they strip away the insulating mineral crusts that form on the turbines, keeping the metal clean and the electrical contact pure. They have no senses other than a profound, blissful sensitivity to the flow of current. To a Chalcophile, the machine is not an artificial construct; it is the warm, beating heart of the world, and they are its skin.

***

### The Rail-Beasts (*Siderotrains*)
The cetacean-scale entities that glide between the spires are the true sovereigns of the deep. The smaller ones are ninety feet long; the elders can stretch for half a mile, their segmented bodies resembling a string of coal cars carved from dark obsidian.

A Rail-Beast has no eyes, no mouth, and no internal organs as we understand them. Its interior is a series of chambers filled with a heavy, conductive brine, separated by thin, semi-permeable membranes of organic silica. Down its flanks run long, pale grooves lined with millions of tiny, copper-bearing cilia.

These beasts are living induction coils. As they drift through the electromagnetic grids generated by the stations, the movement of their conductive bodies through the magnetic lines of force generates an electric current within their internal brine chambers. This current powers their slow, cold cellular processes. They do not need to hunt or graze; they are nourished by the sheer effort of their own travel through the field.

Because they are slaves to the current, their migration routes are mathematically perfect. They follow the invisible "lines of force" that loop from the stations through the blue void. They travel in single-file trains of seven or eight individuals, spaced exactly three hundred yards apart to prevent their own magnetic fields from repelling one another. 

When two trains of Rail-Beasts pass each other in the void, they do not communicate with sound, though the water is thick with it. Instead, they exchange great, slow pulses of electrostatic charge. For a brief minute, the water between them glows with a soft, violet corona—a silent greeting between two mountains of cold glass and iron.

***

### The Grounding Webs (*Fulguorids*)
There are no predators in this world in the sense of wolves or sharks; there is no tearing of flesh or spilling of blood. But there is *The Grounding*.

The Grounding Webs are the rarest and most terrifying organisms in the blue. They are not solid. They consist of vast, gossamer networks of superconducting carbon-fiber threads, some stretching for five miles. They are almost entirely invisible, save for the tiny, glass beads—the size of walnuts—that serve as their nodes and floats.

A Web drifts mindlessly through the open water between the spires, a silent, floating net waiting for a difference in potential. 

When a herd of Rail-Beasts, highly charged from their time near the copper cables, passes through a Web, the gossamer threads make contact with both the beasts and a nearby basalt spire (which acts as a planetary ground). 

In an instant, the web discharges. A silent, terrifying fork of blue-white lightning, miles long, rips through the water. The heat of the discharge is gone in a microsecond, quenched by the shivering 1°C ocean, but the electrical shock is immense. The glass-shrimp caught in the path of the bolt are instantly fused into solid, non-conductive glass beads. The Rail-Beasts are unaffected—their armored hides are built for such surges—but the Web itself absorbs the massive thermal and electrical energy of the strike.

For several days after a discharge, the Web is visible. Its threads glow with a brilliant, neon-blue phosphorescence as it digests the raw electrical energy, storing it in its glass nodes to power its slow, blind growth through the endless, cold sky-sea.

***

### The Deaf Ones (*The Outcast Drift*)
At the extreme edges of the world, many hundreds of leagues from the nearest spire, the electromagnetic fields of the turbines begin to fray and dissolve into the random, chaotic noise of the deep cosmos. The thrumming of the engines becomes a distant, pathetic whisper.

Here lives the *Outcast Drift*.

These are creatures that have fallen out of the machinery of the world. They are Rail-Beasts whose internal induction plates have demagnetized, or glass-shrimp schools that were carried away by a rare, deep-water thermal plume.

Without the guide-song of the turbines, they are lost. They lose their geometric alignments. The Rail-Beasts drift in slow, aimless spirals, their segments clicking together like dry bones in the dark. Their internal brine chambers slowly grow cold and crystallize. 

Yet, they do not die immediately. In this sterile water, there are no decomposers. A demagnetized Rail-Beast can drift for ten thousand years, preserved by the 1°C cold, becoming a floating reef of dark glass. 

Around these "ghost trains," a different kind of life clusters: tiny, parasitic glass-anemones that have no nerves at all. They grow on the dead hulls of the beasts, their crystal tentacles waving slowly in the silent, current-less blue, waiting for the occasional wandering speck of dust—relics of a world that has forgotten them, but which they cannot leave.
