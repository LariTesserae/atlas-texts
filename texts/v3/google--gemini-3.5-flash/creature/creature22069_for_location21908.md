### The Ash-Pinions (The Stills)

The wingless, three-legged moths that float two inches above the slate are not individual beasts so much as they are structural pins. The locals—if the other things that wander here could be said to have names for them—call them the *Stills*, or the *Ashen Pegs*. 

If you attempt to touch one, your fingers will meet a pocket of intense, rubbery resistance just before you reach its dry, grey-white body. It is impossible to force the creature down to touch the basalt; the two-inch gap between its hair-thin legs and the stone is an absolute physical threshold, a seam where the weak gravity of the Shelf has simply ceased to apply. Similarly, you cannot lift them. No matter how much strength is applied, a Still cannot be raised three inches from the ground; it behaves as though it is anchored to the bedrock by an invisible, solid-iron bolt.

They do not rot, because there are no bacteria on the Shelf to consume them, and they do not eat, for they have no inner hollows. If one is broken apart by a heavy blow—which requires the force of an iron mallet—it does not bleed. Instead, its dry, chalky husk splits open to reveal nothing but a small pocket of the same heavy, grey-violet air that fills the sky, trapped under pressure. When this air escapes, it makes a tiny *tink* like a cracking glass bead, and the pieces of the Still fall to the stone, instantly losing their buoyancy and becoming indistinguishable from the surrounding slate debris.

They are sparse, but they are not random. If one were to map their locations across the three-mile plain, they would find that the Stills are arranged in immense, spiraling lines that wind inward toward the single, mammoth-sized boulder at the center. They are the nails holding the thin carpet of this twilight world down upon the silence beneath it.

### The Scale-Keepers (The Slender Ones)

Because distance and perspective are broken on the Shelf, the things that walk here do not possess fixed dimensions. 

These are the *Scale-Keepers*, or the *Linearists*. To look at one from a distance of what seems like a hundred yards, it appears as a thin, dark line—hardly thicker than a pencil—leaning slightly against the violet horizon. It might resemble a discarded surveyor’s rod or a vertical splinter of slate.

But as you draw near, the Keeper does not grow larger in the ordinary manner of approaching an object. Instead, the ground beneath your feet seems to contract, and the Keeper remains precisely the same size until you are "beside" it. Only then do you realize that the Keeper is either forty feet tall and yards thick, or that you have shrunk to the size of a beetle and are standing before a tiny shard of stone that is barely three inches high. 

The Keepers are composed of thin, dry-stacked shingles of slate that slide over one another with a dry, clicking sound as they move. They have no faces, only a terminal stone at their crest that is licked smooth and sloped at the same gravity-defying angle as the pillars in the roofless cells. Their movement is not a walk, but a slow, rhythmic tilting: they lean forward until their balance should fail, but instead of falling, the space ahead of them simply bends to receive them, and they are suddenly ten yards further than they were, without having made a stride.

They are the ones who built the dry-stacked ruins. They do not use tools because they do not need to lift the basalt blocks. To a Keeper, a three-ton block of slate is weightless if approached from the correct angle of perspective; they can slide it into place with the tip of a finger-stone, provided they are standing in the specific spot where the block and the sky are the same size.

They are entirely indifferent to travelers. If you stand in their path, they will not go around you. You will simply find yourself momentarily folded into their perspective—a terrifying instant in which you feel yourself stretched until you are as thin as a single thread of cotton, before being deposited behind them as they click past.

### The Lead-Swells (The Lurchers of the Slate)

The humpbacked mountains that borders the Shelf are not entirely inanimate. They are the oldest things at the edge of the world, and their life is so slow that it is measured in the crystallization of stone rather than the beating of hearts.

These are the *Lead-Swells*. They are massive, low-slung domes of basalt, indistinguishable from the hills except for two things: the greasy trail they leave behind them, and their breath.

A Lead-Swell moves perhaps three inches every century. As it crawls, its flat, soft underside—composed of a dense, semi-solid clay that smells of wet sulphur and ground iron—grinds against the slate plain. This grinding is the source of the "deep, structural groan" that contributes to the Great Roar. When the Swells shift, the very bedrock of the Shelf bends under their weight, sending those dry, metallic vibrations through the soles of anyone standing on the shale.

They are the source of the heavy water. The oil-like moisture that clings to the ledges is the sweat of their passage, squeezed out of the deep basalt by the immense pressure of their slow-motion crawl. 

On their broad, rounded backs grows the black concentric lichen. To the Lead-Swells, this lichen is not a parasite but a system of nerves. The rings act as delicate acoustic receivers; they feel the vibration of the Great Roar as it strikes the stone, allowing the Swells to orient themselves. They crawl endlessly toward the north, attracted by the pale, cold smudge of light that never sets, though they will never reach the horizon before the world itself runs down.

### The Tremblers of the Drop

Inside the heavy, mercury-like droplets of water that cling to the undersides of the basalt ledges, another kind of life exists. It is tiny, frantic, and entirely self-contained.

If you peel one of these heavy drops from the stone and hold it in your palm, you will see inside its clear, cohesive body a couple of minute, silver threads. These are *The Tremblers*. They are no longer than a hair-clipping and as bright as polished tin.

They cannot exist outside the high-tension water of the drop. If the droplet is forced to split—which can only be done with a dry slate needle—the silver threads instantly oxidize upon contact with the open air, turning into tiny streaks of dry, red rust that blow away in the motionless twilight.

Inside their liquid domes, however, they are in constant, violent motion. They do not swim; they vibrate. They are tuned to the Great Roar. When the metallic hiss of the sky rises in pitch, the Tremblers coil and uncoil like tiny springs, striking the inner walls of the droplet. This keeps the drop from sliding or falling; the vibrations of the Tremblers create a micro-current that locks the heavy water to the stone, fighting the weak gravity of the Shelf with sheer kinetic persistence.

If you bring your ear close to a droplet containing Tremblers, you will hear, beneath the deafening roar of the plateau, a tiny, high-pitched *zipp-zipp-zipp*, like the winding of a watch. It is the only fast thing in this slow, heavy world.

### The Heart-Keepers (The Vessel-Born)

Within the three-sided, roofless ruins where the Great Roar drops by half its volume, there is a small reprieve from the cold grease of the plateau. Here, the air smells less of iron filings and more of dry dust and old wool.

In the corners of these cells, where the slate walls meet at sharp angles, you may find the *Heart-Keepers*. Unlike the other residents of the Shelf, they are soft-bodied. They resemble small, folded bundles of grey, oil-tanned leather, about the size of a sleeping dog. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no mouths; they are simply sacks of thick skin, warm to the touch—the only warm things on the Shelf.

Inside their leather skins is nothing but the heavy water, held under such high tension that it has become thick and jelly-like. 

The Heart-Keepers survive by anchoring themselves to the floor of the ruins, directly adjacent to the basalt pillars where the gravity is stable. When the Great Roar enters the cell and softens into a wet, rhythmic pulse, the Keepers’ leather bodies expand and contract in perfect time with that pulse. They are "breathing" the sound.

If you sit inside the cell with them, their rhythmic expansion will gradually match your own heartbeat. Your lungs will want to empty when they contract; your blood will want to push when they expand. To stay too long in a cell with a Heart-Keeper is to have your own biological rhythm overtaken by the slow, wet heartbeat of the ruins. Many travelers who have reached the Shelf have not died of cold or starvation, but have simply sat down in the roofless cells, their hearts slowing to match the five-beats-per-minute pulse of the stone, until their skin turned the grey of oxidized lead and their joints stiffened into dry basalt.
