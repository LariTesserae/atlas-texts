To find them, you must first unlearn the habit of looking for movement. 

In this light that never changes, where the shadow of a basalt column remains the same three-foot pool of charcoal-grey from one century to the next, the eye is drawn to the silver weeping of the rocks. You might look at a stepped plaza for what feels like hours, watching the shallow sheets of water glide over the blue-black flagstones, before you realize that three of the dark, smooth shapes rising from the terrace are not pillars or decorative boundary stones. 

They are the people of the seep.

### The Clay and the Glass

They are tall, though they rarely stand at their full height, preferring a low, crouched ease that brings their center of gravity close to the wet earth. 

Their skin is the color of the basalt after a heavy cloudburst: a deep, mineral blue-black that catches the amber light in long, soft smears. It has no hair, no pores, and no wrinkles. Instead, it is slick and rubbery, like the skin of a dark plum or the belly of a river salamander. To touch them would be to feel something cool but warming quickly, like a stone left in the sun, always coated in a micro-thin glaze of clean moisture that they secrete from their shoulders and temples. This moisture does not run; it clings, giving them a permanent, quiet luster.

They have no bones as we understand them. Their skeletal structure is a system of cartilage as dense and flexible as cured wood, allowing them to bend their limbs in long, jointless curves. Because of this, their movements are silent and liquid. They do not step so much as slide, their broad, flat feet—devoid of separate toes but marked by deep, leaf-like ridges on the soles—clinging to the lime-green moss of the plazas without tearing a single fiber.

Their faces are broad and flat, with features that seem only half-emerged from the clay. They have no noses, only two vertical slits between their eyes that open and close in slow, rhythmic pulses to pull in the heavy, pressurized air. Their eyes are the most striking thing about them: they have no whites, no pupils, and no lids. They are large, perfectly round lenses of clear, amber-tinted water, held in place by rings of dark muscle. When they look at you, they do not blink. Instead, a thin film of moisture rises from the bottom of the eye, washes over the lens, and drains away into the seam of the cheek.

### The Language of the Well

They do not speak with voices. The air is too thick, and the silence of the mountain is too ancient to be broken by breath. 

Instead, their communication is hydraulic. 

Throughout the settlement, the thousands of stone basins are not merely water storage; they are a nervous system. Two of these people will approach a basin from opposite sides, dip their broad, webbed hands into the perfectly still water, and begin to move their fingers. They do not splash. They make tiny, precise, rhythmic pulses—pinpoint waves that travel across the surface to meet in the middle. 

By reading the interference patterns of these ripples with the pads of their fingers, they exchange thoughts, histories, and directions. A conversation looks like two people gently kneading the surface of a mirror.

Because every basin in the town is connected by the log flumes and channels, an elder sitting at the very highest terrace near the weeping rock faces can dip a finger into the source-pool and "speak" to a child sitting three miles away near the lowest steps. The message travels as a specific chord of vibrations through the cedar conduits, a low, thrumming hum that the recipient reads by resting their palm against the wet wood of the flume. 

Their names are not sounds, but specific rhythms of water. One might be named *Three-Drops-Slowing*; another, *The-Basin-That-Does-Not-Ripple*.

### The Habits of the Standing Noon

Because time does not run forward, they do not have days or nights. They do not have seasons. Their lives are divided into "dry" and "wet" phases, though these terms are relative.

During the wet phase, which is their state of activity, they maintain the town. Their technology is entirely based on the guiding of water. They do not use hammers or saws. To shape the massive basalt blocks, they place a small, incredibly hard river pebble into a groove and let a steady trickle of water drop onto it for what might be several decades. The pebble, spun by the water, drills a perfect, smooth socket into the basalt. 

They spend immense spans of time in contemplation. One of them might stand on a ramp for several of our weeks, watching a broad-leafed lily float in a canal. They are not asleep; they are simply participating in the stasis of the place. To them, the speed of human life would look like a frantic, self-consuming fire.

When they are tired, they enter the houses. 

Inside the squat, timber-roofed dwellings, there are no beds, no chairs, and no hearths. The center of each house is a shallow, circular pit filled with warm, mineral-rich mud that never dries. They slide into these pits, sinking until only their amber eyes and the breathing-slits of their faces remain above the surface. 

In this state, they can rest for centuries. Their bodies soften, their margins blur slightly with the clay, and they of the mud become one entity. A house that appears empty is often home to five or six individuals, sleeping in the floor, waiting for the mountain to shift, or for a change in the light that never comes.

### The Origin

They do not die of age, for there is no age here; the basalt is young, but it is preserved in its youth, and so are they. Yet, they are not eternal. 

Very rarely, a basin at the edge of the town—one that has been neglected or sits in a corner where the mist is too thick—will begin to grow cold. The water inside it loses its tension; it no longer remains a finger-breadth below the lip, but begins to spill over in an untidy, chaotic drip. 

When this happens, the people gather. They do not mourn, for they have no concept of loss. Instead, they stand around the basin and place their hands in the water, sending a fast, synchronized vibration through the pool until it begins to glow with the pale, amber daylight of the sky.

From the bottom of the basin, the silt begins to gather. It draws itself together, layer by layer, matching the pattern of the ripples. Over several of the slow, unmeasured cycles of the afternoon, the silt hardens into cartilage; the cartilage skins over with the blue-black basalt-flesh; and a new inhabitant rises from the water. 

The new one is small, the color of a wet slate shingle, and entirely silent. It does not need to be taught how to walk on the moss or how to read the flumes. It simply takes its place at the edge of the basin, dips its hand into the water, and joins the conversation that has been happening since the mountain first broke the sky.
