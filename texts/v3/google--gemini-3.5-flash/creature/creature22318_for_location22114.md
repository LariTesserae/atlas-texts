### The Lung-Keepers

To inhabit a world of vertical glass and iron, the human body has had to remake its relationship with gravity and breath. 

The people who live here—the *Needle-born*—are physically distinct from any race that might have walked on horizontal soil. They are long-limbed and spare, with chests that are deep and barrel-shaped, designed to milk the thin, high air of what little oxygen it possesses. Their lungs are highly vascularized, their blood thick and dark with hemoglobin. They do not pant when they climb; they have learned a rhythmic, slow-pulsing respiration that matches the heavy, sub-audible *thump-thump* of the distant valley flywheels.

Their skin is the color of wet slate, tanned by the unfiltered, blinding sun and stained by the greasy soot that rises from the coal-burning hearths below. The most striking feature of a Needle-born is their eyes: they have developed a thick, semi-translucent nictitating membrane—a "glare-lid"—that they can draw across their vision. It protects them from the agonizing glare of the afternoon sun reflecting off miles of black, wet obsidian mirrors, filtering the light into a soft, amber haze.

They move with a slow, joint-saving economy. In their lexicon, there is no word for "run." To run is to lose your footing, and to lose your footing is to become an unanchored object in a mile of empty space. Instead, they use a gait called the *Tether-step*, a triple-pointed movement where two limbs are always anchored to a rung, a cable, or a harness line before the third moves. 

Their feet are rarely bare. They are wrapped in thick, multi-layered soles of boiled felt and cured glider-skin, bound tightly to the ankle with brass wire. These boots do not have heels; they are flat and supple, allowing the toes to curl slightly around the edges of iron grates or find purchase in the microscopic pits of the basalt.

Historically, they do not speak in loud voices. The thin air does not carry high-frequency sounds well, and shouting is considered an offensive waste of moisture and breath. Instead, they communicate in a language composed of low-frequency clicks made in the back of the throat—which mimic the creak of the copper pipes—and *Knot-sign*, a silent, one-handed gestured language that can be signed even while hanging from a horizontal guide-rope.

### The Castes of the Cable

Society is organized not by wealth or bloodline, but by one’s relationship to the tension of the city.

At the apex of this vertical hierarchy are the **Tensioners**. These are the engineers of the tilt. They live in small, circular brass cabins suspended directly from the main stay-lines—the great, multi-strand iron cables that prevent the peaks from shearing under the weight of the masonry. The Tensioners are a monastic, watchful breed. They spend their days hanging in mid-air with long brass plumb-levels and listening-rods pressed against the iron. 

They can hear the mountain shifting before the stone itself knows it is going to move. When a peak begins to lean, it is the Tensioners who organize the crews of the **Turnstile-Men**. These are the physical giants of the city, men and women with over-developed shoulders and backs like slate slabs. Together, working in teams of twelve, they slide long, heavy timber bars into the slots of the bronze gears and walk in slow, grinding circles, winding the cables until the iron groans and the sector settles back into a level plane.

Below them are the **Grease-Keepers**, who live in the dark, hot, dripping spaces beneath the residential platforms. Their entire lives are spent in the mist of the steam pipes and the spray of oil. It is their duty to keep the giant flywheels and the reciprocating pump-rods lubricated with a thick paste made of mineral grease and pulverized graphite. They are almost entirely deaf, their ears permanently deadened by the deep, resonant *thud* of the iron pistons, but they are incredibly sensitive to vibration; they can feel a dry bearing or a cracked axle through the soles of their feet from three hundred yards away.

### Domestic Life on the Slant

Because there is no level ground, the interior of a needle-house is a marvel of gimbaled design. 

A citizen's home is typically a narrow tower of dark-fired brick, three or four stories high but only twelve feet wide, clamped to the vertical basalt. Inside, nothing is fixed to the floor. Tables, cooking stoves, and water-crocks hang from the ceiling on short copper chains. When the mountain leans, the furniture remains level, swinging gently like silent pendulums. A homeowner can tell how many degrees their district has drifted by looking at the angle between their dining table and the wall.

Sleeping is done in *slings*—heavy, pocketed hammocks made of woven iron-wire and lined with felt. These slings are suspended in the corners of the rooms, keeping the sleeper safely cupped even during the sudden, sharp shudder of a cable adjustment.

Water is the great currency. It is not scarce, but it is raw and acidic, rich in heavy metals and carbonic acid. The **Cistern-Keepers** filter this wetness through massive, multi-tiered gravity vats filled with crushed basalt, limestone dust, and charcoal. This processed water—flat, cold, and tasting faintly of sulfur—is metered out through the city’s rusted veins. To waste oil is a crime, but to split a water pipe is an act of treason.

The diet is predictably mineral. Without soil or sun-warmed valleys, agriculture is impossible. Instead, the citizens cultivate **Slime-Gardens** in the dark, warm crevices of the basalt where the sulfur steam pipes leak. These are cultures of subterranean, chemotrophic yeasts and black fungi that feed on sulfur and heat. It is a dry, fibrous paste when harvested, tasting strongly of iron, but when mixed with hot water and salted, it forms the dense, rubbery grey loaf that sustains all life in the city.

### The Companions of the Steam

In this mineral waste, the creatures that survived are those that found a way to turn the inorganic city to their own advantage.

#### The Rust-Mites (*Ferro-sarcus*)
These are the scavengers of the ironwork. Ranging from the size of a beetle to the size of a domestic dog, they are not insects but a highly specialized branch of crustacean. Their shells are plates of dull, brownish-red iron-oxide, grown through the metabolic processing of the city's pipe-rust. They do not have legs so much as three pairs of heavy, magnetized copper claws that allow them to scuttle along the undersides of the iron beams without any risk of falling. They are slow, blind, and functionally deaf, moving by sensing the magnetic fields of the great iron structures. They feed on the thick grease used to lubricate the pulleys and the salt-frost that crystallizes on the mortar-joins. To the citizens, they are both a pest—as they occasionally chew through copper wire insulation—and a blessing, as they clean the narrow crevices where dangerous mineral deposits build up.

#### The Mute-Sails (*Vapor-drifters*)
These are the great, silent shadows of the high air. With wingspans that can reach thirty feet, they are leather-skinned, rib-winged gliders that look like ancient, toothless bats. They have no lungs; instead, they breathe through a series of spiracles along their flanks, which absorb oxygen directly from the steam-heavy moisture of the air as they fly. They are entirely silent, possessing no vocal cords. They spend years without touching the stone, riding the hot, volcanic updrafts that rise from the deep rifts. 

The Mute-Sails feed on **Soot-Plankton**—microscopic flakes of carbon, ash, and organic spores that are carried up from the lower industrial levels by the heat. When they do land, they do so on the highest, unswept needles of basalt, anchoring themselves with long, curved brass claws that have grown to match the shape of the volcanic crags. Their skin is highly prized by the citizens for making safety harnesses, but they are difficult to hunt; a dead Mute-Sail simply drops, falling into the bottomless rifts below the city, lost to the heat.

#### The Salt-Spiders (*Silica-weavers*)
In the shadowed spaces between the iron towers and the black glass cliffs, one finds the webs of the Salt-Spiders. These are not organic threads, but delicate, glassy filaments of pure silica, spun from the spider’s own specialized mineral glands. The webs do not catch insects; they catch the drifting, needle-fine crystals of salt and gypsum that are carried by the wind. Once the web is fully laden with mineral crust, the spider—a creature of cold, calcified stone and clear, liquid-filled joints—harvests the white frost and digests it, extracting the trace minerals. The empty webs, shining like spun glass in the late afternoon sun, rattle against the basalt with a metallic hiss when the wind rises.

### The Consignment to the Drop

Because there is no earth, there can be no burials. And because there is no wood, there can be no pyres. 

When a citizen dies, they undergo the **Consignment**. The body is wrapped in heavy, grease-soaked felt and bound tightly with iron wire to a heavy basalt weight. The family carries the bundle to the lowest galleries of the city, where the great iron flywheels turn and the heat from the rift is a physical pressure against the chest.

Without prayers—for prayers require long vowels and loud voices, which are not the way of this place—the body is slid over the rim of the lowest iron platform. 

It drops. It does not hit the rocks; the rifts are too deep, their bottoms lost in the rising, white-hot haze of the earth's open throat. Long before the body reaches the magma below, the moisture is sucked from its tissues by the extreme, dry heat of the deep rifts. The body mummifies in seconds, becoming a leather-hard, black cylinder of bone and salt, rising and falling on the thermal updrafts for decades like a silent, dry log, joining the Mute-Sails in their eternal, noiseless drift between the iron peaks.
