To understand what lives here, you must first set aside any definition of life that requires a pulse, a breath, or a brain. 

If you were to descend into this black water, you would at first believe the forest to be entirely abandoned. If you stood in the tiled corridors for a week, you would detect no motion save for the passive, oceanic sway of the kelp. But if you remained for a year, or a decade, you would begin to notice that certain shapes among the petrified scaffolds are not wood, and they are not stone.

They are the Tenders. They are not animals. They belong to the same slow, chemotrophic kingdom as the black-veined kelp and the pale moss, but where the forest is rooted, these forms are motile. 

In appearance, a Tender resembles a heavy, vertical wedge of dark, compressed peat, standing three times the height of a man. Their outer surface is not skin, but a thick, seamless rind of black, rubbery bark that feels like cold, wet lard, yet is as tough as cured harness leather. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no heads. The top of their great, wedge-shaped bodies simply tapers into a broad, flat collar from which floats a crown of thousands of gossamer-thin, white filaments. These threads drift upward into the freezing water like a halo of milk, constantly tasting the current for the chemical signatures of sulfur, iron, and silica.

They have no legs. The base of a Tender is a single, massive foot—a sliding pad of dense, muscular fiber that perfectly matches the width of the shallow grooves carved into the basaltic floor. They are literally keyed into the world. They do not walk across the tiles; they slide along the grooves like heavy, dark drawer-slides, guided by the masonry. To move a single yard along these stone tracks takes them several days of silent, hydraulic contraction.

Because they have no bones, their bodies are sustained by the crushing weight of the water itself. Inside their leathery rinds, they are filled with a thick, pressurized slurry of mineral-rich gel. They have no hearts; their "blood" is moved through their fibrous interiors by the external pressure of the currents, which rhythmically squeeze their bodies as they lean.

Their arms are their most remarkable feature. They sprout from the sides of their wedge-like bodies in pairs of three or four—long, jointless limbs of green, flexible wood that bend with the slow, heavy grace of fresh timber. These limbs do not terminate in hands, but in bundles of incredibly tough, black rootlets. 

These rootlets are the weavers of the forest.

The Tenders work with an patience that mimics the stone beneath them. To tie a single knot in a braided reed rope, a Tender may labor through an entire cycle of the planet's rotation. One rootlet will loop a strand of split reed; three years later, another limb will rise to pull it taut. There is no urgency in their labor, because there is no death by starvation or predation. They feed directly on the cold, mineral-rich seeps of the tile joints, absorbing nutrients through the soles of their sliding feet as they glide over the seams. 

They do not communicate with sound. Instead, they interact through the very lines they spend their lives maintaining. 

The planetary garden is a vast, interconnected instrument of tension. If a guideline miles away becomes slack due to a decaying timber or a shifting current, the change in tension causes a micro-vibrational hum to travel down the braided reed rope, through the stone peg, and into the basaltic tile. The Tenders feel this low, sub-audible singing through the soles of their feet. They do not think; they simply slide toward the vibration, guided by the tracks in the stone, until they reach the source of the slack.

Their birth and death are as quiet as their work. 

They do not reproduce through mating, but through budding. Every few centuries, a mature Tender sliding along a deep basalt groove will slow to a complete stop over a particularly rich mineral seep. Over the course of fifty years, its leathery rind will split, and a small, pale nodule of moss-like tissue will emerge from its side. This nodule slowly absorbs the minerals of the floor, growing its own rind, its own wooden limbs, and its own crown of white sensory threads. When it is large enough, it detaches, slips its sliding foot into an adjacent groove, and begins its endless, slow journey.

The parent Tender does not starve, but it does eventually end. After ten thousand years of sliding through the dark, the mineral gel within its body begins to petrify. It slows. It stops. Its outer rind hardens, absorbing the dissolved silica in the water until it is no longer leather, but stone. 

The other Tenders do not mourn it, nor do they bypass it. They treat their petrified ancestor as what it has become: a new piece of scaffolding. They will scale its hardened, stone limbs, lash new guidelines to its shoulders, and plant fresh sprigs of pale moss in the crevices of its back. 

In this way, the weavers of the garden eventually become the garden itself, supporting the weight of the dark forever.
