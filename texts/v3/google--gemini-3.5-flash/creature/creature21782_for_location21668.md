To peer into the tiny, stacked dwellings of the clay town is to find them apparently empty; there are no furnishings, no hearths, and no soot. Yet they are not abandoned. The inhabitants are there, but they do not possess the soft, wet anatomy that our eyes are trained to recognize as life. They are so perfectly integrated into the materials of their world that, during the quiet moments between the earth’s deep breaths, they are indistinguishable from the architecture itself.

They are the *tesserae*—geometric, box-like beings about the size of a plum, constructed from the very same substances that form the valley and the town. 

Each citizen is a hollow polyhedron, its flat faces formed from wafer-thin plates of smoky gray mica, held together at the seams by a dry, flexible plaster of red clay and fibrous mineral wool. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no internal organs. Inside their hollow bodies is nothing but a trapped portion of the chamber's parched, sulfurous air, and a single, loose grain of heavy iron-rich magnetite that rolls within them, acting as a sensory plumb-bob.

They do not eat, nor do they breathe in the muscular sense. They are thermodynamic puppets, born of the temperature differences within the basalt bubble. 

When the floor fissures exhale their bursts of hot, pressurized gas, the air inside the tesserae’s hollow bodies remains momentarily cooler than the rushing draft outside. This sudden barometric and thermal gradient, combined with the rapid expansion of their mica plates, forces their joints to flex. They do not walk so much as they click and fold, their limbs—six thin, scissor-like needles of black obsidian—extending from the corners of their clay seams as the heat rises.

When the vents breathe out, the town comes alive with an dry, mechanical synchronization. 

Thousands of tiny, hollow boxes crawl forth from the wasp-nest doors of the clay towers. They do not move with the fluid grace of animals, but with the sudden, snapping jerks of tin toys or dry seed pods. Their movement is a collective, rhythmic clicking—a sound like thousands of fingernails tapping rapidly on slate. 

They are the masons and the weavers of this pocket world, though they possess no conscious intent. Their labor is a physical necessity, a consequence of their design. 

As they crawl across the steeply curved walls of the bubble, their bellies—which are coated in a fine, electrostatically charged silt—drag along the ground. They act as automated polishers. When they encounter a fracture in the red clay houses, the dry dust clinging to their bodies is forced into the crevice by their weight. The heat of the vents then glazes and bakes this dust, sealing the crack. 

When they climb through the forest of silica reeds, the tough, hair-like asbestos fibers that leak from the vents catch on their obsidian joints. As they are pushed by the thermal drafts from the west cliff to the east terrace—floating across the central void like dry, angular kites—they trail these fibers behind them. Over centuries, this accidental weaving has formed the sagging, humming suspension bridges that span the gap.

Even their reproduction is a matter of pure mineral physics. 

When a tessera reaches the end of its structural life, the dry clay seams holding its mica plates together finally bake to the point of extreme, glass-like brittleness. During a particularly violent exhalation from the vents, the pressure difference becomes too great, and the creature shatters with a sharp, musical *clink*. 

Its components—the flat mica plates, the sharp obsidian legs, and the internal grain of magnetite—are scattered into the forest. There, they act as templates. The falling, sticky silicate spores of the silica plants cling to these mineral fragments. Over decades, nurtured by the constant, rising heat and the accumulation of clay dust, new plates of mica grow along the crystalline boundaries of the old. A new polyhedron self-assembles, resting in the cracks of the basalt until it is light and hollow enough for the first draft of hot gas to lift it into motion.

They do not know of the sky. They do not know of water, which would liquefy their clay joints and dissolve their fragile, dry world into mud. They exist only as a beautiful, closed loop of physics: the heat creates the movement, the movement maintains the stone, and the stone holds back the crushing weight of the volcanic deep.
