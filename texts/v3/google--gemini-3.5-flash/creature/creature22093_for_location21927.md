To live here is to be made of the water, and the water here is more metal than rain. 

The first and most populous inhabitants are not creatures of flesh and bone at all, but form a slow, mineral-based domain that blurs the line between the wet stone and the living cell. Clinging to the lime-scab of the massive boilers are the **Limonite-Lice**. They are small, the size of iron carriage bolts, and indeed they look very much like them. Their carapaces are not made of chitin, but of heavy, layered iron-crust and copper carbonate, harvested atom by atom from the dripping grease and dissolved scale of the pumping district. They possess no eyes—there is nothing to see—but their undersides are lined with thousands of microscopic, chemical-sensitive pits. They do not walk; they slide with a glacial, scraping persistence, leaving trails of bright, clean brass behind them where their rasping, silicon-tipped tongues have licked away the green patina. To a Limonite-Louse, the great boiler is not a dead monument, but an endless, sweet pasture of galvanic salts and iron-rich grease.

Higher up, where the warm, pencil-thin streams of geothermal water rise in shimmering threads, lives the **Tallow-Skin**. 

These are soft, shape-shifting masses of gristle and cold fat, some no larger than a fist, others as wide as a rowboat. They are the color of mutton tallow, translucent at their feathered edges and dirty yellow at their dense, heavy cores. The Tallow-Skins have no lungs, no hearts, and no gills. They survive by absorbing the slow-release lubricants that have seeped from the old machinery for a century. They drift through the bronze-green fog with a perfect, sickening neutrality, neither rising nor settling, hanging like clumps of lard suspended in syrup. When they occasionally bump into a copper steam conduit, they do not flinch; instead, they slowly mold their bodies to the curve of the pipe, warming their greasy tissue until it runs liquid, allowing them to drink the ancient, petroleum-sweet paraffin that still oozes from the packing glands.

Among the purple, ribbon-like ferns, there are things that mimic the drowning vegetation so perfectly that they are only revealed when the great siphon-piston behind the brickwork makes its rare, heavy *thrum*. 

These are the **Silt-Gills**. They are long, segmented ribbon-worms, three or four feet in length, that hang vertically from the cast-iron balustrades like peeled willow bark. Their skin is the precise, bruised-plum color of the ferns, and they possess hundreds of fine, hair-like appendages that drift in the water, indistinguishable from the plant-fronds. But when the vibration of the piston rolls through the cavern, the Silt-Gills react to the change in pressure. In unison, they draw themselves upward into tight, spring-like coils, their bodies making a soft, wet *fwick* as they contract against the iron railings. Once the liquid *gargle* of the displaced air pocket dies away, they slowly, inch by inch, unspool themselves back into the current, their blind, spade-shaped heads pointing downward into the dark silt, waiting for the microscopic flakes of rust and dead moss that the vibration has shaken loose.

In the deepest, most stagnant corners—where the brick stairs have melted into waxen tiers—lives the **Solder-Mother**.

She is singular, or perhaps there are three or four of her kind in the entire flooded district, tucked into the hollows of the collapsed distillery stills. She is a creature of immense, passive age. Her body is a great, bulbous sack of thick, rubbery hide, gray-blue and scarred with patches of orange crust, resembling a discarded boiler tank that has grown a wet, pulsing throat. She has grown around a cluster of ancient, copper geothermal pipes, her flesh having swallowed the metal until the pipes seem to emerge directly from her ribs like a bypass graft. 

The Solder-Mother does not hunt. Indeed, she produces no heat of her own; she is entirely dependent on the slow, insulated ticking of the pipes she embraces. She breathes by drawing the heavy, bronze broth through a series of wide, circular siphons that line her sides. The water enters her carrying tannins, grease, and sulfur; it exits her pale and cleared of its metals, leaving her interior lined with a thick, glittering sludge of lead and copper mud which she uses to strengthen her massive, leathery hide. 

On her back, a miniature wilderness thrives. The blind white snails crawl over her in slow, spiral patterns, grazing on the emerald mosses that feed on her weak, galvanic skin-currents. When she shifts—which happens perhaps once every week, a movement of only a few inches—the snails do not fall off. They are glued to her by a thick, mucus-grease that protects them from the acid water.

Occasionally, the slate-gray salamanders, blind and soft as sodden birch bark, will nest in the warm folds of her neck. They lay their eggs there in small, grape-like clusters that are perfectly transparent, each egg containing a tiny, needle-like embryo that twitches occasionally in response to the deep, low-frequency hum of the distant water-piston.

There is no hunger here as the surface world knows it. There is only the slow, chemical breakdown of things that were built to last forever, and the patient, blind things that have learned to turn those collapsing metals into flesh. Nothing screams. Nothing dies in a hurry. When a Limonite-Louse finally grows too heavy with iron, it simply stops moving. Its soft underside rots away, and its metal shell remains fused to the brass boiler, becoming one more knuckle-like bump on the machine, waiting for the next generation to scrape it clean.
