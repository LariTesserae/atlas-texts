To exist in the Obsidian Teeth is to be shaped by the rule of the slot. 

Because the hexagonal columns of basalt are packed so tightly together—forming a vast, vertical honeycomb that stretches for hundreds of leagues—life does not move horizontally across a plane. It moves up and down, or it slides laterally through cracks no wider than a man’s forearm. Every living thing here has been squeezed flat, elongated, or rendered silent by the crushing weight of the columns and the acoustic deadness of the stone.

### The Hex-Walkers (The Hushed Ones)

The most complex inhabitants of the vertical labyrinth are the *Hex-Walkers*, though they do not think of themselves by this name; to them, there is no other way for a body to be shapewise. They are humanoid, but only in the most distant, skeletal sense. 

A Hex-Walker is nearly seven feet tall, yet their torso is no thicker than a fence post. Their ribcage is not a rigid cage but a series of overlapping, hinged slats of cartilage that can collapse inward when they exhale, allowing them to squeeze through crevices that are only eight inches wide. They have no shoulders to speak of; their collarbones are jointed like a pair of folded wings, allowing their arms to rotate in complete circles both forward and backward. 

Their skin is the matte, purple-gray of basalt in shadow. It is slick, tough, and entirely hairless, constantly secreting a thin, odorless oil. This oil is their primary survival mechanism: it prevents their skin from tearing against the abrasive, weathered edges of the pillars, and it causes the heavy, mineral-laden condensation of the morning mists to bead up and slide off them, preventing rot.

Having lived for generations in a world where sound dies three meters from its source, the Hex-Walkers have no vocal cords. Their mouths are small, round openings used only for sipping the stagnant water from the pillar-top cups and swallowing the steam-beetles they pluck from the stone. Instead of speech, they communicate through a complex tactile language called *The Tap*. 

They carry small, polished basalt cubes—"clicking-stones"—tethered to their wrists by cords of braided air-plant fiber. By pressing their chests or palms directly against a column and striking the stone with their clickers, they send vibrations through the elastic basalt. To a Hex-Walker, the entire plateau is a single, massive telephone wire. A mother can feel the rhythmic, reassuring *thrum-thrum* of her child’s location two hundred pillars away, translated through the soles of her broad, leathery feet.

They do not build houses. There is nowhere to set a foundation. Instead, they sleep suspended. They weave complex hammock-nets from the tough, orange inner thread of the air-plants, wedging them into the vertical joints between columns thirty meters above the dark floor. Here, they hang like cocooned moths, swaying gently in the hot thermals that rise from the depths.

### The Steam-Chafers (The Iron-Eaters)

Beneath the Hex-Walkers, crawling along the wet flutes of the pillars, are the *Vapor-Chafers*. These are beetles the size of flat loaves of bread. 

Their carapaces are not made of chitin, but of a dense, organic compound heavily impregnated with iron and silica harvested from the basalt. They look like ambulatory chunks of the pillars themselves, hexagonal and dull gray. They do not have legs in the traditional sense; instead, their undersides are lined with hundreds of tiny, rhythmic cilia that grip the polished rock like the tracks of a heavy tank.

Vapor-Chafers do not eat vegetation. Their mouths are hard, abrasive drills that grind away the weathered skin of the basalt to reach the mineral-rich veins of iron beneath. As they digest the stone, their internal chambers operate like tiny, biological boilers. They absorb the morning moisture, heat it using the ambient warmth of the sun-baked pillar tops, and use the pressurized steam to clear their breathing spiracles and eject waste. 

To encounter a drift of Chafers in the morning fog is to see hundreds of tiny geysers. Every few seconds, a beetle will emit a sharp, silent jet of white steam from its side, clearing its internal chambers with a soft *plup* that is instantly lost to the stone. 

### The Slate-Skins (The Shadow-Gliders)

Among the columns, the predators must be silent and fluid. The most common of these is the *Slate-Skin*. 

A Slate-Skin is a creature of pure surface. It has no discernible skeleton; its body is a broad, flat sheet of cartilaginous muscle, three feet wide but less than an inch thick. The top of its body is colored in irregular hexagonal patterns of black and ash, mimicking the mosaic of the pillar-forest from above. Its belly, however, is a pale, milky yellow, covered in thousands of microscopic, mucus-filled cups.

A Slate-Skin does not climb; it clings. It slides over the basalt faces like a drop of heavy grease, molding its body perfectly to the hexagonal corners of the stone. It hunts by waiting at the edge of a crevice. When a small flyer or a young Hex-Walker passes beneath, the Slate-Skin releases its grip on the stone and falls. 

It does not drop like a stone, however; it expands its body to catch the rising thermals, gliding horizontally through the narrow gaps with terrifying, silent grace. It wraps around its prey like a hot, wet blanket, sealing the victim’s breathing passages with its mucus-covered belly until the struggle stops. Because it has no teeth, it digests its food externally, secreting a highly acidic fluid that turns the prey to mush before sucking it up through a small valve in the center of its chest.

### The Glass-Shrimp of the Cups

If one climbs to the very top of the columns—where the sun sits like an iron iron and the heat reaches three on the scale—one finds the *Plate-Pools*. These are the dinner-plate-sized depressions worn into the pillar-tops by ancient water. 

In these pools, which are often ninety degrees Fahrenheit and thick with green-black algae, lives the *Glass-Shrimp*. 

These creatures are no larger than a fingernail and are completely transparent. They have adapted to a life of extreme, localized velocity. Because the pools are tiny and isolated, separated from one another by yards of dry, blistering basalt, the shrimp have a life cycle that matches the fog.

In the morning, when the fog condenses and the pools fill to brim-full, the shrimp hatch from desiccated eggs that have baked in the sun. They grow, mate, and lay eggs within the span of four hours. They have no eyes; instead, they have two long, feathery antennae that are sensitive to the slight vibrations of the wind. 

By noon, as the sun burns away the fog and the water in the plate-pools begins to boil and dry up, the adult shrimp die. Their bodies dissolve into a thin, gelatinous glue that covers their eggs, cementing them to the very bottom of the stony cup. This glue hardens into a glassy, protective lacquer that can withstand temperatures hot enough to scorch wood. There the eggs sleep, baked and dry, until the next morning’s mist arrives to melt the lacquer and start the cycle anew.

### The Condenser-Gulls (The Rust-Birds)

Though the copper condenser stations are unmanned, they are not uninhabited. The constant *shuck-hiss* of steam and the warmth of the heavy, bronze turbines have created an entirely artificial ecosystem around the machines.

Chief among these inhabitants is the *Condenser-Gull*. These are not true birds; they are pale, membranous flyers resembling small pterosaurs, with wingspans of about two feet. Their bones are hollow and filled with warm air, helping them float effortlessly on the thermal drafts created by the hot machinery.

The Gulls have developed a symbiotic relationship with the copper boilers. They nest in the joint-spaces where the station legs are bolted into the basalt. The birds are completely blind, but their long, scissor-like beaks are tipped with copper-sensitive nerves. They spend their days clinging to the massive brass vats, using their long tongues to lick the green copper-salt (verdigris) that accumulates on the valves. 

This verdigris is highly toxic to most life, but the Gulls use it to synthesize a thick, foul-smelling grease which they secrete from glands near their tails. They preen themselves with this grease, coat their nests with it, and even feed it to their young. The result is a colony of rancid-smelling, green-tinged flyers that are entirely immune to the heat of the steam vents.

When a turbine begins to foul with mineral scale or lichen, the Gulls descend upon it in a silent, frantic mass. They peck away the crust, keeping the machine's joints clear and white. In return, the machine provides them with a perpetual source of boiling water and absolute warmth, its low, subsonic hum acting as a heartbeat for the creatures that have tethered their lives to its iron frame.

### The Deep-Silt Moss

At the very bottom of the columns, thirty to fifty meters down, there is no sun. There is only a perpetual, dripping twilight where the air is heavy with the smell of wet sulfur and dead beetles.

Here, covering the floor in a carpet two feet deep, is the *Deep-Silt Moss*. 

This is not a single plant, but a collective organism—a vast, pale network of fungal threads and translucent liverworts that feeds on everything that falls from above. It has no color; it is a ghostly, wet white that seems to absorb what little light manages to filter down through the dense columns.

The Moss is the garbage collector of the Obsidian Teeth. It digests the cast-off carapaces of the steam-beetles, the bones of dead Hex-Walkers, and the grease that drips from the copper cables. It is so efficient that the bottom of the labyrinth has no smell of rot—only the clean, sharp scent of wet stone and starch.

If a Hex-Walker dies, their people do not bury them in the stone, for there is no soil. Instead, they drop the body down into the deepest crevice they can find. Within three days, the Deep-Silt Moss has claimed it, leaving behind only a few clean, white bones that are slowly cemented to the floor by the mineral-thick drip of the columns, becoming part of the plateau’s foundation itself.
