In Sluice 12-B, where the boundary between engine part and organism has been worn smooth by the passage of ages, life does not merely inhabit the machinery—it has become the machinery’s soft, wet lining. 

To crawl or drift here is to be part of a closed, vertical digestion.

### The Lowly: Rivet-Lichens and the Galvanic Scuttlers

At the microscopic and insectoid scale, the Sluice is dominated by a slow, anaerobic metabolism that eats the engine itself. 

The most common lifeform is the **Rivet-Rust**, an iron-oxidizing bacterium that does not look like slime but rather like thick, orange-furred felt. It grows in dense, circular rings around the rounded bronze rivets, feeding on the trace currents of galvanic electricity that pass between the dissimilar metals of the sleeve plates. When a steam bubble rises and strikes these rings, they release a faint, chemical scent of scorched cinnamon and battery acid.

Grazing upon these orange rings are the **Wire-Crabs** (properly called *Anode-Scuttlers*). They are no larger than a keyhole. Their bodies are flat, shield-shaped discs of dark, leaden grey, from which protrude six legs that are virtually indistinguishable from bits of discarded copper wire. They do not swim; they can only walk where there is grip, using tiny, hook-like claws to anchor themselves against the constant upward tug of the brine. They have no eyes—only two long, whipping antennae that they keep pressed flat against the metal plates, "listening" for the vibration of the great piston below. When the piston thuds, the crabs freeze in unison, their legs locking into the pitted brass like pins in a socket.

### The Mid-Water: The Sprocket-Eels and the Bellows-Skins

In the open four-meter column, where the water is a thick, warm soup of grease-silt, the **Sprocket-Eels** drift. These are the glass-eels of the Sluice, but here they have adapted to the physical geometry of the hum. They are long, translucent ribbons of cold white light, almost invisible save for the dark, reddish-black cluster of their internal organs. 

Within their transparent bellies, their digestive track and circulatory pumps have taken on a bizarre, mechanical regularity. They possess a series of hardened, cartilage-like discs that resemble miniature escape-wheels and cogs. These discs do not actually rotate like metal gears, but they pulse in a rhythmic, interlocking sequence to move blood and food through their high-pressure bodies. They feed by hanging head-down in the lee of the kelp-ribbons, their bodies swaying like white pendulums in the current, waiting to snap up the wire-crabs that lose their grip on the rivets.

Stitched directly into the seams where the brass plates meet are the **Bellows-Skins**. These are colonial organisms that have taken the place of gaskets. They look like thick, rubbery purses of black-purple hide, about the size of a man’s forearm, wedged tight into the horizontal cracks. Whenever the pressure in the Sluice fluctuates—which happens each time a valve opens in the distance—the Bellows-Skins compress or expand, drawing brine through their porous bodies and expelling it in tiny, jet-like squirts. They are the filters of the column, trapping the grease-silt and the sloughed-off orange bacteria. They are so integrated into the joints of the Sluice that to pull one free would require a chisel; their roots are made of fine, calcified threads that have grown deep into the micro-fissures of the ancient bronze.

### The Master of the Seams: The Oiler

The closest thing to a "who" in this vertical chimney is a creature the color of wet tallow, known to the depths as the **Oiler** (or *The Shunt-Dweller*). 

There are never more than three or four Oilers in Sluice 12-B at any one time. They are semi-intelligent, solitary things that seem to have descended from some air-breathing ancestral diver, though they have long since traded lungs for the heavy, iron-threaded gill-slits that scar their necks.

An Oiler is long and rubbery, about two meters from its blunt, featureless head to its tapering, finned tail, but it is incredibly narrow—barely wider than a thigh. It has no legs, but its forelimbs are long, double-jointed arms that end in three flat, blackened fingers tipped with iron-plated claws. Its skin is completely hairless, greasy to the touch, and patterned with faint, pale-green swirls that mimic the patina of the bronze walls. 

The Oiler is blind. In place of eyes, its face is dominated by a flat, disc-shaped plate of cartilage that acts as an acoustic lens. By pressing this disk flat against the brass walls, the Oiler can "see" through the metal, mapping the entire miles-long conduit by the pitch of the steam bubbles, the vibration of the valves, and the distant, rhythmic roar of the piston.

The Oilers are the self-appointed tenders of Sluice 12-B. They spend their long, slow lives climbing up and down the vertical seams, using their fingers to scrape the thick, amber grease that leaks from the piston seals. They do not eat this grease; instead, they store it in a large, sack-like throat-gland. 

Whenever they find a seam where the brass plates are grinding together under the tectonic pressure of the engine, or where a valve is beginning to rust shut, the Oiler will press its mouth to the crack and regurgitate a thick, waxy glob of the refined oil, packing it into the joint with its broad fingers. They do this without thought or instruction, driven by a deep, ancient instinct that equates the smooth running of the Sluice with their own survival.

When two Oilers meet in the narrow column, they do not fight. They press their chests together, their gill-slits interocking, and hang suspended in the warm current for hours, vibrating in harmony with the piston's low-frequency thrum, sharing the heat of the water before one slides down into the blue-black depths and the other climbs toward the amber light above.
