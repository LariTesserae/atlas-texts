In this world of mineral-dry majesty, the creatures are not soft bags of water waiting to spill. They are structures of dry-cellulose, horn, silica, and graphite. They do not have blood; they have currents of static electricity, pressurized nitrogen, and sliding joints of polished carbon.

Below is an record of the life that drifts, slides, and sings within the great, dry silence.

***

### The Sieve-Lanterns (*Phosvelum grandis*)

These are the gossamer kites of the high altitude, the rarest and largest of the true fauna. To see one is to watch a mountain-shadow bend, for they are immense—often forty feet from wing-tip to wing-tip—yet they weigh less than a handful of dust.

*   **Structure:** A Sieve-Lantern’s body is built like a skeletal umbrella. The spars are made of hollow, black silicate straws, incredibly stiff and light, over which is stretched a skin of translucent, amber-colored vellum. It has no head, no tail, and no eyes in the terrestrial sense. Instead, its upper surface is a curved, polished dome of natural quartz plates that focuses the blinding sunlight downward into its hollow interior, warming the nitrogen gas within. This solar heating provides lift, allowing it to float effortlessly on the thermal plumes rising from the sun-baked basalt.
*   **Feeding:** From its underbelly hang hundreds of fine, copper-colored threads, some sixty feet long. These threads are highly electrostatic. As the lantern drifts across the dry basins, the wind drags these threads through the air, where they act as combs, attracting the golden pollen-spores and dry, flour-like dust of the ribbon-trees.
*   **Sensation:** The Sieve-Lantern does not hear sounds, but it is deeply sensitive to the electric tensions of the atmosphere. When a pollen-front approaches, the creature’s trailing threads begin to glow with a faint, purple corona—St. Elmo’s fire of the dry sky. It uses this charge to steer, contracting the margins of its vellum sails with tiny, dry-fiber muscles that click like snapping twigs.

### The Flint-Skaters (*Chondrus calx*)

On the flat-topped tablelands, where the wind-polished basalt is so smooth it shines like glass, lives a creature that has surrendered the concept of legs.

*   **Movement:** The Flint-Skater is a flat, disk-shaped animal, roughly the size of a shield. Its underside is lined with plates of a self-lubricating, graphite-like mineral. To move, it does not walk; it raises two large, sail-like fins of stiff, orange wax into the path of the wind. It sails across the twenty-mile tables, tacking against the dry gales like a yacht on a frozen sea. 
*   **Survival:** Its body is heavily armored in overlapping scales of white calcium, protecting it from the intense ultraviolet radiation of the indigo sky. When the wind drops to a dead calm, the Skater anchors itself by dropping small, magnetic stone weights from its central chassis into the natural fissures of the basalt, remaining perfectly still for weeks until the air stirs again.
*   **The Silt-Scraper:** Its mouthparts are not jaws, but a pair of rapidly vibrating quartz chisels. It skitters across the stone, scraping up the micro-thin layer of dead ribbon-tree powder and mineral salts that the wind has packed into the basalt’s grain. The sound of a hundred Skates feeding in a basin is a dry, rhythmic *scritch-scritch-scritch* that carries across the silence like the turning of a million book pages.

### The Chime-Weavers (*Scypha vitrea*)

In the deep, fluted canyons where the wind-music is loudest, the rock walls are colonised by communities of stationary, semi-intelligent colonial organisms called Chime-Weavers. 

*   **Form:** They look like clusters of delicate, hollow glass organ pipes, varying in height from a few inches to ten feet. They grow by absorbing the vaporised silica carried by the abrasive dust-winds, slowly depositing it in perfect, concentric rings of green and pale blue glass.
*   **Mechanism:** Inside each glass tube hangs a delicate, copper-wire tongue, weighted with a heavy grain of ironstone. When the low-frequency *huff* of the wind blows through the canyon, it causes the tubes to vibrate at their resonant frequencies. The ironstone tongues strike the inner walls of the tubes, producing a crystalline, metallic music—a shimmering, endless chord that changes key as the air pressure drops or rises.
*   **Purpose:** This music is not incidental; it is how the colony feeds. The specific sound vibrations create miniature electrostatic vortices inside the tubes. This vacuum-like effect sucks the airborne pollen directly into the Weaver’s hollow interior, where dry, chemical glands lining the glass walls absorb the nitrogenous proteins of the pollen grains.

### The Drift-Cysts (*Semina dustica*)

The life-cycle of this world is incredibly slow, and nothing illustrates this better than the Drift-Cysts—the reproductive stages of almost all the flora and fauna.

*   **The Shell:** Because there is no water to keep a cell membrane plump, life is packaged in hard, geometric containers of black carbon-glass. These cysts are faceted like cut obsidian, reflecting the blinding sun as they lie scattered in the deep, dry moss-carpets.
*   **The Spark:** A cyst can lie on the basalt for a thousand years without changing. It requires no moisture to trigger it, only the specific electrical voltage of a major pollen-front. When a gold-storm rolls over a plateau, the intense static charge in the air accumulates in the cyst’s sharp corners. Once the charge reaches a critical threshold, the cyst snaps open with a sharp, dry *ping*, releasing a tiny, thread-like crawler that immediately begins to weave its own sail of wax.

### The Great Loom-Moths (*Veloforma*)

If one is fortunate, once in a season, they might observe the descent of a Loom-Moth into the deep basins. 

*   **Appearance:** These are the apex organisms of the dry upper air. They are long, segmented cylinders of grey, fibrous bark, up to sixty feet in length, held aloft by three pairs of rigid, triangular wings that do not flap. They look less like insects and more like ancient, wind-drifted ship wreckage.
*   **The Gathering:** They spend their centuries-long lives in the highest strata of the thin atmosphere, near the edge of the indigo void, where they are nearly invisible. They descend to the plateaus only to die or to shed their skins. 
*   **The Shedding:** When a Loom-Moth sheds, its old skin—a massive, sixty-foot sleeve of silver-grey, paper-dry fiber—is caught by the basin winds. It does not fall as a heap; it is so light and stiff that it floats for miles, a phantom sail drifting over the ribbon-trees. Eventually, the wind drafts tear it against the sharp glass-ferns. The silver fragments fall into the golden mosses, where they are slowly ground into the dry, mineral powder of the soil, completing the clean, friction-less circle of this waterless world.
