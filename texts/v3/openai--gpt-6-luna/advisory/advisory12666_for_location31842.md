## First decision: don’t try to climb inside

The blister is smaller than a walnut, and its hairline throat is not an entrance so much as part of the mountain’s plumbing. A human cannot enter it at ordinary size. Cutting away the basalt flake, widening the throat, or forcing a passage would turn a quiet, stable pocket into a different place: pressure and gas could change, heat could escape, crystals could fracture, and the colony could be crushed or scattered.

Treat the chamber as a world to observe, not a room to open.

If your multiverse equipment allows remote sensing or a tiny instrumented avatar, use that. If it allows you to become small enough to enter, remember that “small enough to fit” is not the same as “small enough to move safely.” The chamber is already nearly full of life and mineral. There may be nowhere to put a human-sized foot—or even a probe—without displacing its inhabitants.

## Before the visit

**Learn the mountain before approaching the blister.** The ridge is young, bare, and sharp. Its thin air and fresh volcanic rock call for a pressure suit, thermal protection, traction suited to loose or glassy stone, and a reliable way to retreat if the ridge shifts or vents. Do not assume that a quiet slope is an inactive one.

**Assume the throat’s gases are unsafe.** The description gives no exact chemistry or pressure. Sulfur deposits and hot gas are warnings, not a complete gas analysis. Bring sealed life support, independent atmosphere monitoring, and a way to abort without relying on breathing the local air. Do not introduce oxygen, water, or any other gas to “make the chamber safer.” That could alter the conditions on which the little ecosystem depends.

**Plan to keep your own warmth and matter out of the system.** A human body carries heat, moisture, microbes, skin flakes, and a great deal of mass. Even a small instrument can contaminate a nearly closed habitat. Use sterile, dry, low-outgassing equipment; keep lamps dim and cool; and avoid anything that sheds particles or releases lubricant, solvent, or cleaning fluid. If you can observe without contact, do so.

**Set a strict no-disturbance rule.** No drilling, prying, sampling, touching crystals, collecting creatures, or placing a marker inside the pocket. The basalt shard’s trembling is part of the chamber’s rhythm, not a mechanism to manipulate. Don’t brace against it or try to stop its vibration. Avoid loud pings, strong illumination, and nearby vibration: in a place this small, a signal that seems gentle to you could be a major disturbance to everything in it.

## If you have a miniature or remote presence

Use the smallest, least intrusive platform you can manage, and send it in only if its dimensions and effects are genuinely negligible at the colony’s scale. The packed creatures occupy the hollows between crystals; a device might crush more life simply by entering than a large animal would disturb in a spacious habitat.

Prefer passive instruments: low-light imaging, temperature sensing, and vibration listening from outside the throat if possible. The dim redness is not useful illumination for a human, and turning the chamber bright would reveal it at the cost of changing its conditions. Record the colony’s ordinary traffic before attempting any closer view. Give its slow routines time to become visible; what looks motionless at first may be movement measured in long intervals.

If entry is essential, make it brief and reversible. Do not push through the throat or shift its glassy cover. Establish a clear abort threshold for rising temperature, changing pressure, gas readings, vibration, or signs that the creatures’ movement has been disrupted. Leave no equipment behind.

## While you are there

Expect confinement rather than scenery. There is no horizon to orient by, no open floor, and no unoccupied corner. The creatures’ ticking, rasping, and grinding may seem like background noise, but it is the sound of the whole chamber’s daily life. The deeper hammering from the throat is not necessarily a warning or a message; it is the mountain working through a narrow opening, and the colony has made a routine around it.

Don’t mistake unfamiliar life for a threat simply because it is numerous or close. At their scale, the creatures are part of the terrain as much as the crystals are. Your safest posture is that of a visitor who makes no demands on the place.

## Departure

Withdraw by the same route, without widening it. Check that you have not carried away mineral grains or organisms, and that nothing you introduced remains. Continue monitoring from outside if you can: the most important sign of a successful visit may be that the throat keeps its rhythm, the crystal edges remain undisturbed, and the colony resumes its slow traffic as if no traveler had come.

The best preparation is to accept that this is not a destination for ordinary human presence. It is a hidden pocket in stone, already crowded with a complete world. Go equipped to survive the mountain—but more importantly, go prepared not to make the mountain notice you.
