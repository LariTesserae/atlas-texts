## What to expect

Treat this as a **warm, waterlogged, enclosed environment**, not as an open forest. The daylight may look ordinary overhead, but at ground level you’ll have little sky, poor visibility, and a maze of narrow passages. Progress will be slow: roots, slick silt, shallow pools, hanging growth, and low living ceilings will matter more than distance.

The place does not appear actively hostile, but it is not well understood. Plants respond in unusual ways, old machinery still runs, and the remains of industry may have left contaminants in the water or soil. Plan to observe more than you explore.

## Before you enter

- **Check the atmosphere rather than assuming it is breathable.** Test oxygen, carbon dioxide, and any relevant toxic gases at ground level and inside the roofed chambers. Take a mask or respirator suitable for dust, spores, or unknown irritants as a backup, but don’t assume it makes an unsafe atmosphere safe.
- **Bring drinking water and electrolytes.** Do not drink from pools, leaf-cups, or pipes, even if the water looks clear. It could contain microbes, plant toxins, or industrial residues.
- **Tell someone your route and return time.** Agree on a check-in schedule and a missed-check-in plan. If possible, travel with a partner; this is a poor place to get lost, injured, or separated.
- **Use redundant navigation.** A map and compass, a reliable electronic device, and a way to mark your route are better than any one system. Don’t assume satellite navigation or radio will work beneath the canopy. Use removable markers that do not damage the plants.
- **Decide your limits in advance.** Set a turnaround time, a maximum depth of exploration, and clear rules for approaching the machine, pipes, and the shadowless iron frame.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear **lightweight, quick-drying, long-sleeved clothing** with gloves and eye protection. The vegetation is close enough to brush against you, and even a small cut can become a problem in a warm, wet environment. Choose footwear with strong grip and good ankle support; fully sealed boots may keep water out, but can also trap it if water gets over the top. Test your choice before committing to a long visit.

A **helmet** is sensible in the low chambers: there are hanging roots, dense overhead growth, and potentially loose or rotten material. Bring a headlamp, spare light, waterproofed batteries, a whistle, a basic first-aid kit, and a means of keeping essential electronics dry. A walking pole can help test footing, but don’t use it to prod unfamiliar plants or the machinery.

Because the air is warm and close, avoid heavy insulation. Carry water and electrolytes, pace yourself, and take breaks before you feel overheated. Humidity can make exertion tiring even when the temperature seems moderate.

## Moving through the place

- **Go slowly and keep your hands free when possible.** The paths are gaps between roots, not maintained trails. Test silt and submerged footing before putting your weight down.
- **Watch above and below you.** Pools may conceal holes or soft mud; hanging roots and low growth can catch a face, pack, or limb.
- **Keep the group together.** Visibility is limited, and the constant layered rustling and dripping can make it hard to judge where sounds come from. Agree on simple signals before entering the thicker passages.
- **Use hearing protection only if you need it, and stay able to hear your companions.** The sound is continuous and may become disorienting, but blocking it entirely could leave you unaware of movement or warnings.
- **Do not force a route by cutting, pulling, or breaking growth.** Stems and roots may be load-bearing, connected to water channels, or more responsive than they appear. If a passage is too tight, turn back.
- **Avoid rushing after a sound, light, or apparent opening.** The canopy hides the wider landscape, and the paths may not lead where they seem to.

## Machinery, water, and anomalies

Keep well clear of the operating flywheel, belt, and any machinery that is still warm or vibrating. **Do not touch, stop, restart, open, or try to repair it.** Moving parts may be concealed by leaves, and the machine’s connection to the surrounding growth is unclear. Rusted track, plates, and fittings may also be unstable or sharp.

Treat pipes and basins as contaminated until tested. Don’t enter the water, wash in it, or place food or equipment in it. Avoid disturbing the dark silt, where contaminants or irritants could be concentrated.

The plants’ responses—the stems bending toward water sounds, roots suspended above puddles, and leaves oriented toward the iron frame—are reasons for caution, not invitations to experiment. Observe from a distance, record what you can without disturbing it, and don’t attach gear to the frame or stand in it for extended periods. Its lack of a shadow is an unusual feature; it is not, by itself, evidence that it is a portal or a hazard. Treat it as an unexplained landmark and keep your planned distance.

## Health and environmental precautions

Do not forage. Sparse visible animal life does not mean the plants are edible or that the water is safe. Avoid touching unfamiliar flowers, leaf-cups, or enclosed plant structures. If you develop a rash, breathing irritation, dizziness, confusion, or unusual sensory effects, return by your marked route and seek medical assessment.

Bring supplies for minor wounds and clean them promptly. Wet ground and old metal create a bad combination for cuts; avoid stepping on or handling rusted debris. Check yourself and your clothing for hitchhiking organisms or plant material before leaving.

Avoid open flames. The vegetation is dense, the air is humid, and you don’t know what vapors or dry pockets may be present. Also avoid leaving tape, flags, food, tools, or other waste behind. This place is already a mixture of living growth and industrial remains; add as little as possible.

## The right pace and mindset

Expect the visit to feel **close, damp, and acoustically crowded**, even though the place is quiet in the ordinary sense. The steady patter, creaks, and machine rhythm may make it difficult to tell whether anything is approaching. Stop periodically, reorient yourself, check your partner, and confirm your route rather than letting the atmosphere hurry or absorb you.

A sensible first visit would be short, close to a known exit, and focused on documenting the outer paths and industrial remains. Leave deeper exploration for a later trip, after you have verified the air, water, communications, and the behavior of the site over time. The safest assumption is that the landscape is stable only on human timescales: it changes slowly, but it is still changing around you.
