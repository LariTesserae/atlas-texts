Treat this as a place that looks safe because nothing is happening. That is the trap. The plateau offers no obvious storm, predator, or poison—but it gives you almost no reliable way to tell where you are, what is alive, or whether you are making progress. Prepare for an expedition through a quiet, crowded anomaly, not a barren landscape.

### Before you go

- **Assume there is no usable water, food, or shelter.** Bring all drinking water in sealed containers; do not expect dew, condensation, streams, or moisture in the hollows. Bring food you can eat without relying on local plants or animals. Nothing here looks like a trustworthy resource.
- **Do not assume the air is breathable.** The description gives no evidence either way. Test it remotely if possible; otherwise bring independent breathing equipment, an atmosphere monitor, and enough reserve for an immediate return. A cool breeze—or the absence of one—would tell you very little.
- **Plan a short first visit.** Bring redundant clocks and record your departure time, but do not assume local time, biological rhythms, or elapsed distance will behave normally. Set a firm turnaround time before crossing over.
- **Arrange a dependable return method.** Keep the arrival point marked and within reach. If your multiverse travel method depends on a beacon, anchor, or retrieval team, test it before exploring and carry a backup. Do not make the first experiment on a far shelf.
- **Go with a partner, not a group that can easily scatter.** Agree on simple contact signals and a rule that nobody follows a sound, animal, or apparent landmark alone. Radios may help you talk, but don’t trust them to tell you where a sound came from.

### Dress for hard ground and bad edges

Wear a helmet, eye protection, sturdy boots with reliable grip, and clothing that protects against scrapes. Use a harness and a line near any rim. The cliffs are immense, their bottoms obscured, and the strange distances make visual estimates especially untrustworthy. A seemingly level walk could still end at a drop you did not recognize as approaching.

The stone appears smooth and dustless, but that does not guarantee good footing. Test each step. Do not climb on the animals, even if their backs look solid enough to serve as ledges. The plateau is crowded with life disguised as terrain.

### Navigate as if your eyes can mislead you

Do not use the horizon as a map. A ridge may remain the same apparent distance away as you walk toward it; turning around may not make your starting point seem any closer. Pick a nearby, visible patch of safe ground instead of navigating toward distant features.

Use a marked route, a physical guide line, or another method that does not depend solely on remembering the view. Markers should be distinctive and checked by touch as well as sight: repeated strata and apparently repeated animals could make one place resemble another with unnerving precision. Keep a written or recorded log, but treat it as an aid, not proof that the landscape has been traversed normally.

Move slowly enough to watch the ground, and keep your partner in physical contact or on a short line where practical. The sounds here seem to arrive from the wrong distance, so do not turn toward a click or scrape and assume its source is nearby.

### Give the doorways a wide berth

The little structures are not ordinary shelters or landmarks. A doorway can make a distant place seem close, and crossing while looking at the doorway can leave you beside it again. For a first visit, **do not cross any threshold**. Do not lean through one, send a partner through to “test” it, or use it as a shortcut.

If a later expedition studies them, do so as a separate, controlled experiment: one doorway, a secured line, a recorder, a designated observer, and a prepared return plan. The account suggests that looking at the door while crossing matters, but it does not provide a dependable procedure for avoiding the effect. Do not improvise one while standing in the threshold.

### Respect the creatures, even when they seem motionless

They may not hunt or flee in any familiar way, but their stillness is not evidence that they are harmless or inert. Avoid touching, feeding, collecting, stepping on, or trying to clear them from a route. They are packed into hollows, cracks, rims, and even one another’s bodies; choose your footing as carefully as you would in a dense animal colony.

Do not eat the plants or animals. Do not assume that apparent repetition means a creature is a copy, a statue, or the same individual in a way you can safely exploit. Keep equipment and exposed skin clean, and treat samples as unknown biological material. The absence of visible decay is not a reason to relax ordinary contamination precautions.

### Prepare for the quiet

The unnerving part may be how little changes. There is no wind to mark time, no moving shadow to orient by, and almost no sound you can trust. The animals occupy nearly every patch of ground without seeming to move. A traveler can start to feel that the whole plateau is watching simply because there is nowhere truly empty to look.

Agree on regular check-ins, scheduled rests, and clear conditions for turning back: lost line, uncertain return point, a partner becoming disoriented, or any unexplained change in bodily condition. Keep notes about what you have actually observed rather than what the landscape seems to imply. If you begin to feel that you have seen the same creature, ridge, or shelf before, do not spend hours trying to prove the pattern by wandering farther.

The safest first journey is deliberately modest: establish a secure arrival point, examine one small area, avoid the cliffs and doorways, and return while you still know exactly how to get back. This place has room to walk, but not much that can be trusted as empty ground.
