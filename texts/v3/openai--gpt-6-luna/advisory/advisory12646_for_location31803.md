Treat it as a **working, unfinished life-support system**, not as a natural garden or a safe ruin. It may feel peaceful enough to invite wandering, but nearly every pleasant thing here—water, warmth, greenery, even the ground—is part of a system whose limits are not fully known.

### Before you go

- **Confirm the basics:** breathable air, safe pressure and gravity, tolerable temperature, and a known way back to your point of arrival. “Ordinary daylight” is reassuring, but it does not prove the atmosphere is safe for you.
- **Arrange an entry and exit plan.** Tell someone outside where you are going and when to expect you back. If possible, visit with a local guide or maintenance worker. Do not assume every stair, passage, or apparent opening is a route.
- **Ask what is safe to touch, drink, or breathe near.** The water is part of an irrigation and drainage system, not a water supply. Do not drink it or use it to wash cuts. Ask about the plants, growing mats, construction material, and any maintenance chemicals.
- **Bring only what you can carry securely:** a small first-aid kit, drinking water from a known source, a charged light, a reliable way to communicate, and a map or route notes. A respirator may be prudent if you have asthma, allergies, or no information about spores and aerosols; do not treat it as a substitute for checking the air.
- **Wear boots or shoes with strong wet-surface grip, and clothing that dries easily.** A light waterproof outer layer can help, but avoid bulky gear that could snag on vines, grates, or pipework. Cover cuts and scrapes. Remove or secure dangling straps and jewelry.
- **Plan for heat and damp.** Metal sweats, surfaces stay wet, and the air may feel close even when the temperature seems comfortable. Hydrate before and during the visit, and take breaks if you feel overheated, chilled by evaporation, dizzy, or unusually short of breath.

### While you’re there

- **Stay on clearly supported paths and terraces.** The mountain is being built, and the walls and floor are not ordinary rock. A smooth surface may be newly cast; a ridge may conceal a seam; a stair may simply stop. Do not climb unfinished walls or test a bulge, tile, grate, or ledge with your weight.
- **Treat every outer edge as a serious drop.** The pale walls are retaining walls, not necessarily guardrails. Keep a generous distance from them, especially where plants obscure the footing or mist makes the edge hard to read. Never lean over a wall for a better view.
- **Mind the water.** Tiles, grates, moss, and mineral deposits can be slick. Watch where water disappears under the floor, and avoid the drain at the central stair: it follows a route that is not on the diagrams. Keep hands and feet out of troughs and basins.
- **Do not operate, block, or climb the machinery.** Pumps, valves, pipes, vents, and maintenance fixtures may be controlling water or supporting the garden. Don’t pull vines out of vents, turn a valve, cover a grate, or attach a tether to anything unless someone has identified it as a safe anchor.
- **Leave the plants undisturbed.** They are living in a controlled system even where they look wild. Don’t pick, eat, or pocket leaves, seeds, insects, or moss. Avoid reaching into thickets or lifting leaves to investigate. If you see an animal, give it space and report it rather than trying to catch it.
- **Keep track of your route.** The place is small, but its unfinished stairs, dense growth, and similar-looking terraces could make the way back less obvious than expected. Use a map, notes, or a guide; don’t mark the walls or plants.
- **Listen for changes.** The usual sounds are water, pumps, valves, and occasional settling clicks. A sudden change in pump rhythm, a new flow of water, a cracking or repeated shifting sound, or a change in the air is reason to stop exploring and return by your known route. Don’t investigate a strange noise alone.

### Afterward

Wash your hands, clean wet footwear and equipment, and check for cuts, bites, rashes, or breathing irritation. Report any new leaks, damaged grates, unusual plant growth, or changes in the construction to whoever maintains the site. Don’t carry specimens away: even a small amount of plant matter or mineral material could belong to a system you don’t understand.

Expect the visit to be quieter than the scenery suggests. The garden may look exuberant, but it is still young, dependent on pumps and scheduled water, and only partly in control of its own growth. The safest way to experience it is slowly: stay on the paths, keep back from the walls, and let the place remain a little unfinished rather than trying to find out what happens when you push it.
