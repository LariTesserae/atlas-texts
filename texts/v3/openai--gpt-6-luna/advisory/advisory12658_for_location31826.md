**Treat the Sill as an active, partly unknown environment—not as an ordinary mountain shelf.** It may take twenty minutes to cross, but the hidden drop, changing springs, and devices that seem to alter distance make “short walk” a poor measure of risk.

### Before you go

- **Secure your return route before setting out.** Agree on a turnaround time, a rendezvous point beyond the arrival route, and a signal for help. Don’t assume the route’s apparent length—or the time it takes to cross—will stay consistent.
- **Go with a partner or guide.** Stay together and in sight; don’t split up to explore.
- **Plan for warm, wet conditions.** Saturated air can make exertion uncomfortable even without strong sun. Anyone prone to overheating, breathing problems, faintness, or severe vertigo should be especially cautious.
- If you have them, bring basic instruments such as a thermometer, humidity meter, compass, and camera. Treat their readings as useful clues, not proof that conditions are safe.

### What to wear and carry

Wear breathable clothing with a **cut-resistant outer layer**, sturdy gloves, eye protection, and grippy boots that cover the ankles. The new basalt is sharp, and the wet surfaces may be slippery. Secure loose fabric and straps so they cannot snag.

Carry your own drinking water, electrolytes, a first-aid kit with dressings for cuts, a light, and an agreed signaling device. Keep electronics in sealed, water-resistant cases. Don’t drink from the springs or pools: clear water is not necessarily safe, and its temperature or composition may be unfamiliar.

### While you’re there

- **Stay well back from the rim.** Cloud conceals the drop; the absence of wind does not make the edge safe. Don’t lean over or throw anything down to judge the depth.
- Walk slowly on ground you can see clearly. Avoid fresh shards, glassy seams, steaming cracks, wet slopes, and unstable ledges. The stone is still changing.
- Treat every spring and pool as potentially hot or chemically unknown. Don’t touch the water, test its strange flow, or put equipment into it.
- Give the plants and small creatures space. Don’t pick, feed, or carry anything away.
- Keep a wide distance from the black arch, floating post, metal frame, and anything similar. Their effects may be subtle rather than spectacular. **Don’t touch them, walk through apparent gaps or rectangles, or use them as shortcuts.** Never trust the apparent distance to a cliff or opening near one of these structures.
- Check in with your partner at fixed points. If a landmark shifts, vanishes, or no longer seems to be where you expect, stop and regroup rather than improvising a new route.

Pay attention to your own balance and sense of direction. If the vibration changes sharply, distances begin to seem wrong, the mist obscures your companions or footing, the heat rises, rock begins shedding, or you feel confused or unwell, turn back promptly if the route remains clear. Don’t push on just to complete the crossing.

The Sill sounds quiet, but quiet is not the same as settled. Make a short, conservative visit: observe from stable ground, disturb as little as possible, and leave while the return still feels straightforward.
