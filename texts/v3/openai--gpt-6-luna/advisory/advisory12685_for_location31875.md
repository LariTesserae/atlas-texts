Prepare for a **wet industrial basin with a changing shoreline**, not for a conventional city or a wilderness trek. The biggest mistake would be trusting the map—or assuming that a quiet, empty-looking stretch is safe.

### Before you go

- **Get local tide, rainfall, and sluice information.** Ask the people who operate the pumps or manage the basin which paths flood, when gates move, and which channels can change level quickly. A map may show where the water usually is, not where it will be during your visit.
- **Arrange access and a way to check in.** Many of the most tempting routes pass beside active works, locked compounds, or structures that look abandoned but may still be in use. Tell someone your route and return time. Carry a charged phone or radio, plus an offline map and a locator that works in the basin.
- **Check local air and water advisories.** Warm outflows, industrial sites, and standing water warrant caution, but don’t assume every canal is poisonous—or that any is safe. Follow site signs and local monitoring. Don’t drink the water, swim, or eat fish or shellfish unless the relevant local authorities say it is safe.
- **If you are arriving from another universe, handle the usual interworld checks first:** confirm that the atmosphere is breathable, gravity and pressure are tolerable, and your return link or equivalent is reliable. The damp air may make a place feel gentle; that is not a substitute for verifying conditions.

### What to wear and carry

Choose **waterproof clothing that can cope with persistent damp**, not just a brief shower. A breathable waterproof shell, warm insulating layer, spare socks, and dry clothes sealed in a waterproof bag will matter more than elaborate cold-weather gear. The air may feel warm and clammy while wet stone and wind steadily chill you.

Wear **sturdy, grippy, ankle-supporting boots**. Expect slick concrete, mud, salt crust, rust-stained surfaces, and hidden changes in footing. Avoid relying on the apparent dryness of a path. Bring a headlamp, spare batteries, and a small light that can signal your position; the dim, cloud-flattened daylight may make it hard to judge distance and time.

For any walk near an open channel or exposed edge, carry a **personal flotation device** and know how to use it. Do not treat waders as a substitute: they can become dangerous in moving water or deep mud. Keep your hands free where possible, and carry a basic first-aid kit, drinking water, and food in sealed bags.

### Moving through the city

Stay on authorized paths and raised causeways. Keep well back from canal edges, lock gates, sluices, pump intakes, and warm discharge outlets. Water can move or rise quickly when machinery or gates change state, and the sound of that change may be faint in the broad, damp quiet. Don’t climb pipe bridges or enter tanks, filter beds, flooded basements, or shuttered buildings. An empty structure may still have live equipment, unstable floors, trapped water, or a sudden route to the tide.

Walk with a companion if you can. The city’s long empty intervals can make a minor injury or a missed turn a serious problem. Check in at agreed times, keep your phone protected from moisture, and don’t wear headphones: you’ll want to hear pumps, alarms, approaching vehicles, and changes in the water.

If fog, heavy rain, or rising water obscures your route, turn back early rather than trying to push through. A familiar path can become hard to recognize when every surface shines black and the channels reach their rims.

### The animals

The wildlife may be the most memorable part of the visit, but it is also the part least amenable to a fixed itinerary. Fish, crabs, shrimp, predators, and seabirds gather in pulses around tides, feeding seasons, and the operating schedule of the works. A channel that seemed empty an hour ago may become crowded with moving animals; another may remain bare.

Watch from firm, elevated ground. Don’t wade into a shoal, walk through a mass of crabs, block a drainage route, or crowd the birds when they gather on the flats. Keep food secured, don’t feed wildlife, and don’t handle animals—even apparently stranded ones. Sudden movements can send birds up in a dense flight or drive animals toward gates and machinery.

Clean and dry your footwear and equipment before moving on to another world or ecosystem. Even a small amount of mud or water can carry organisms you didn’t intend to transport.

### What the visit may feel like

Expect the place to be **larger and lonelier than it first appears**. The industrial buildings are spread apart; there may be long stretches with no one in sight, while distant machinery continues to thrum through the damp air. Bring enough supplies to avoid needing to improvise, and don’t mistake quiet for abandonment. Some works run continuously; others are merely between floods.

The uncanny part is likely to arrive after rain, when water covers the low ground and the city seems reduced to a set of narrow dry ledges. Leave yourself time to stop and watch the tide—but plan your route so that watching it never means betting your safety on how far it *looks* from the road.
