Treat the Cup as a **brief, exposed high-mountain visit**, not a place to camp or explore casually. Its small size may make it look manageable, but the thin air, hard light, cold, narrow footing, and lack of shelter leave little margin for mistakes.

### Before you go

- **Check that the world is physically survivable.** Confirm the air’s composition and pressure, gravity, temperature range, and radiation levels before stepping out unprotected. The description suggests cold, high-altitude conditions, but it does not establish that the atmosphere is breathable or safe.
- **Approach gradually if the air is thin.** Allow time to acclimatize at lower elevations; bring supplemental oxygen if measurements or your medical needs call for it. Do not treat dizziness, headache, nausea, confusion, or unusual breathlessness as ordinary exertion.
- **Go with a partner, but keep the party small.** The stone fins leave little room to pass. Travel single file, agree on a turnaround time, and make sure someone outside the Cup knows your route and return deadline. Carry a way to call for help that works in this universe.
- **Plan for no refuge.** There is no remaining roof, wall, firepit, or obvious protected place. If the weather worsens, you may have nowhere to wait it out.

### What to bring

- **Insulating layers and wind protection**, including warm gloves, a hat, and dry spare layers. Mist can wet clothing and stone, then quickly turn to ice.
- **Eye and skin protection.** The light is intensely bright and sharply reflected by pale stone and ice. Use suitable sunglasses or goggles, and cover exposed skin; at altitude, assume harmful ultraviolet exposure unless proven otherwise.
- **Sturdy boots with reliable grip**, plus a walking pole if it helps your balance. A helmet is sensible around fractured rock. Gloves will also protect against sharp flakes and cold surfaces.
- **Water and food**, even though condensation gathers in the Cup. The pools are shallow, fleeting, and potentially contaminated; collect only if you have a safe method to treat it. Do not count on the groove or depression as a dependable source.
- **Navigation and emergency gear:** a map or terrain model, compass or equivalent, light, first-aid kit, communication device, and enough backup power. Mist may erase distant landmarks, and the many stone fins can make a short crossing confusing.
- **A way to monitor conditions**, especially temperature, wind, visibility, and pressure or oxygen availability if relevant. Set clear limits in advance for turning back.

### Moving through the Cup

Keep well back from the rim and give narrow ledges and fresh-looking fractures a wide berth. The old, rounded stone may appear settled, but some slabs can shift; a crack that holds clear ice may also conceal a weak edge or a deeper void. Test footing before committing your weight, avoid climbing the fins, and do not reach across gaps for a better view.

Move slowly and one at a time through constrictions. Wet stone can become slick, and a brief freeze can change the footing while you are there. If mist thickens, visibility drops, or you hear rock shifting, stop the exploration and retrace your route rather than improvising a new one.

### Leave the place as you found it

The small stone stacks and the groove leading to the depression are the only surviving signs of human presence. Their purpose is unknown, but their age and care deserve respect. Photograph or record them without touching, rebuilding, or adding to them. Keep out of the groove and depression where practical; do not collect crystals, ice, or stone, and carry out everything you bring in.

A good visit would be short and quiet: arrive prepared, observe the Cup’s small cycle of mist, water, ice, and light, and leave before changing conditions make the return uncertain. Its emptiness is not evidence that it is safe—or that nothing there is worth protecting.
