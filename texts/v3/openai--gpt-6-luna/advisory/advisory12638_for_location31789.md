Treat this as a **hazardous, living industrial process**, not an empty landscape with some strange wildlife in it. An ordinary traveler should not plan to explore it on foot. The safest visit is from a sealed, stationary shelter—or by remote probes—until the basin’s air, water, and local physical rules have been tested.

### Before arrival

- **Bring everything you need.** There is no vegetation or obvious source of food, clean water, fuel, shelter, or dry material. Carry a generous reserve of food, water, power, medical supplies, and a way to return that does not depend on local conditions.
- **Use a sealed environmental suit.** The air is cold and saturated with fine wetness; its chemistry and biological content are unknown. Protect your lungs, eyes, skin, joints, and suit seals from damp, cold, mineral aerosols, and possible corrosive deposits. Do not assume the water is safe to touch or drink.
- **Make the suit and equipment tolerant of wet mineral grime.** Keep connectors and tools enclosed, bring spares, and assume exposed mechanisms may be fouled or abraded. Include independent heating, breathing, power, and communications systems.
- **Plan for disorientation.** Bring inertial navigation, multiple clocks, marked physical reference points, and redundant positioning systems. Do not trust the horizon, apparent distance, echoes, reflections, or a single clock or compass. The basin may distort the order or apparent direction of events.
- **Prepare for sound and vibration.** Use hearing protection, and let instruments—not your ears—judge direction and approach. The deep thrum may be felt before it is heard, and the basin’s echoes may return misleadingly.
- **Have a retreat plan before landing.** It should include a return route, a reserve route, and a means of calling for extraction that does not rely on a clear view across the basin. Keep the exit vehicle or shelter in a confirmed, physically marked position.

### On arrival

First observe from a distance. Use remote cameras, atmospheric samplers, water probes, and ground sensors before opening a hatch or sending anyone onto the flats. A place that looks vacant may be densely occupied just beneath the film of water. Even the ridges host active colonies.

If you must go outside, travel only in a small, tethered group. Keep a continuous physical line back to shelter; radio contact and apparent line of sight may not be dependable. Use broad, stable load-distributing platforms rather than assuming that a ridge, crust, or patch of shallow water can bear your weight. The surface may conceal animals, channels, or soft ground, and a footprint is not a reliable record of what happened there.

Choose a position only after monitoring it through several cycles. Avoid obvious migration channels, freshly disturbed flats, vibrating ground, and areas where the water begins to flow differently. The enormous bodies beneath the basin are part of the machinery of the place: their movement can change water levels and currents over a wide area. A quiet patch may be quiet only because a migration has passed through.

### While exploring

- **Keep your exposure short and your profile low.** Changes in air pressure or disturbance may matter to animals that sense the world differently from humans. Move slowly; avoid loud equipment, unnecessary lights, and repeated impacts.
- **Do not approach, feed, touch, or block the animals.** Small creatures may swarm or reorganize when disturbed; larger ones may be concealed under water or difficult to interpret. A shape that seems to have passed could leave a second, misleading image—or be much larger than it looked.
- **Do not enter the water.** It is both habitat and working fluid for the basin. Treat every pool, film, curtain, and crust as occupied, chemically unknown, and potentially connected to a moving channel.
- **Do not collect samples casually.** Colonies and animals may release swarms when disturbed. Any sample should be taken remotely, sealed immediately, and kept separate from your suit and life-support system. Assume that biological or mineral material could contaminate equipment even when it appears inert.
- **Record several kinds of evidence.** Log what you see, what instruments measure, and when each measurement was taken. Note direction and time independently. If a sound, ripple, or visual landmark seems impossible, do not use it alone to make a navigation decision.
- **Turn back at the first large change.** A rising vibration, a sudden redirection of water, a new vapor front, or animals moving in synchronized ranks may indicate that the basin’s cycle is shifting. Do not wait to see what follows.

Most importantly, don’t mistake the basin’s ancient stillness for safety. It is in motion everywhere: water, animals, stone, and distance all seem to participate in the same slow, immense process. A traveler should arrive as a quiet, self-contained observer, leave as little disturbance as possible, and regard a short visit from secure shelter as a success.
