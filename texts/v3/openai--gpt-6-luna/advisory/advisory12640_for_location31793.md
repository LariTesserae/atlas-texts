## Before you go

Treat this as a working industrial city built on terrain that is still changing—not as a ruin, a wilderness, or a place you can safely understand by looking at a map.

Bring sturdy waterproof boots with excellent grip, warm layers, gloves, and a shell that can handle cold, wind-driven rain. The damp is the important part: even when it is not raining, walls, rails, stairs, and sheltered walkways may be wet. Pack dry socks, sealed bags for electronics and papers, and a small towel. Rust-resistant hardware is useful; untreated metal may bead with moisture or corrode faster than you expect.

Wear a helmet wherever you are near construction, cableways, retaining walls, or overhead conveyors. Add eye protection and a high-visibility outer layer around foundries and rail lines. The city’s industrial sounds can be misleading, and fog may hide moving equipment until it is close. Do not assume that a quiet stretch of track is inactive.

The height and weather vary across the settlement, and the highest districts vanish into cloud. Arrive rested, take the climb gradually, and ask local guides which routes are open before you set out. Do not use visibility as a measure of distance: fog can make the next shelf seem near when it is separated from you by a gulf.

## Moving around

Use a local guide for your first journeys between districts. Streets are identified by elevation as well as direction, and the paths may not lead where older maps say they do. Get a current route description from someone who has traveled it recently, and check it again at each major lift station or stair landing.

Carry both a local map and a written list of landmarks, but treat neither as authoritative. Record where you have actually been rather than trying to reconcile every discrepancy. If a ridge appears in two places, or a familiar route seems to lengthen, do not press onward just to prove your instruments right. Turn back to a known, occupied place and ask for help.

Allow extra time for stairs, lifts, fog, and delays. Some staircases are longer on the way down than they were on the way up. At the West Lift, wait for local instructions rather than boarding the first car that seems to arrive; reports of cars arriving from the wrong direction are treated here as routine. Keep a companion in sight, and agree on a simple meeting point before separating. In dense fog, use a tether in hazardous areas—but do not tether yourself to machinery, rails, or a structure whose position may change.

Bridges and platforms may have adjustable joints. Do not climb over barriers, loosen fittings, or treat construction zones as shortcuts. A support shifting by a finger’s width over several months may be normal here; that does not make it safe to stand beneath it.

## Rules worth following

- **Do not enter the sealed tunnel at the end of the last rail spur.** The warning is specific, old, and serious: **DO NOT MEASURE BEYOND THIS POINT.** Do not breach the masonry, send a probe through it, or use a device to estimate what lies beyond. Take the sign literally.
- **Ask before surveying, mapping, or recording unusual terrain.** The city keeps careful records, but it does not try to correct every discrepancy. A traveler’s “helpful” measurement may be unwanted, misleading, or dangerous.
- **Do not assume that the ground between neighborhoods is open public land.** Some spaces are named; some are blank on the map; some are not yet agreed to be ground. Ask who is responsible for an area before crossing it.
- **Keep clear of cable baskets, rails, cranes, and foundry traffic.** Loads move slowly, but their paths and stopping distances may be hard to judge in mist.
- **Follow local signals.** Whistles, bells, lamps, and warning lights have practical meanings. Learn them before traveling independently.
- **Do not chase animals or try to drive them off a ledge.** Goats, birds, and small mammals are part of everyday life here, but their routes and behavior may not obey the boundaries you expect.
- **Use handrails, but do not trust them as fixed points.** They may be wet, newly installed, or attached to ground that is not yet settled.

## What to pack

A sensible day kit includes:

- Waterproof, high-grip boots; warm layers; gloves; and a windproof waterproof shell.
- Helmet, eye protection, and a visible outer layer for industrial districts.
- A headlamp with spare batteries. Blue-glass street lamps and dim daylight make ordinary vision unreliable in some alleys.
- A whistle, a paper copy of your lodging address, and a way to contact your host or local guide.
- Food and water for longer than the planned trip. There may be long gaps between inhabited shelves.
- A basic first-aid kit, including supplies for scrapes and blisters.
- A waterproof notebook or sealed phone case, if you need to keep records.
- Spare socks and a dry layer sealed separately from the rest of your pack.

Do not rely solely on a compass, GPS, watch, or perfectly synchronized clocks. Use them as aids, not authorities. Keep a written itinerary with your host, and check in when you reach a new district.

## What to expect

The city may feel less like a place perched on a mountain than a place being negotiated with one. Its buildings are deliberate and sturdy, but the stone beneath them is not finished becoming familiar. You will see doors that open onto rock, stairs that end at walls, new ridges beside old paths, and carefully maintained infrastructure that appears to have been planned with these contradictions in mind.

The damp will seem to come from the structures themselves. Water may gather under bridges or on a wall facing away from the clouds, while nearby gutters remain dry. Wear clothes that can tolerate lingering moisture, and do not drink from pools, pipes, or condensation collectors unless a resident confirms they are safe.

Sound and distance are unreliable companions. A hammer may seem nearby when the workshop is far below; a bell may be heard before anyone strikes it. Keep your bearings by landmarks and local directions rather than by sound alone. At night, do not walk an unfamiliar route just because the lamps make it look straightforward. Beyond the lit windows and warning beacons, the open air and the sky may be difficult to distinguish.

The place is not simply dangerous, and it is not in a state of collapse. People work, garden, travel, and live among its oddities with practiced care. Follow that care. Move slowly, ask specific questions, and accept that some discrepancies are documented rather than solved. A good visit here is not one in which you prove how the mountain works. It is one in which you return with your footing, your companions, and your respect for the parts of it no one has agreed to measure.
