Treat this less like a hike and more like a visit to a living, unfinished laboratory—one whose walls happen to be a furnace.

**Before you go**

- **Do not approach an operating furnace on your own.** Arrange access with the people responsible for the equipment. The furnace’s normal heat, pressure, atmosphere, and moving parts are hazards even before the seam is considered. Use a controlled shutdown, isolation, and verification procedure; don’t improvise by opening plates or reaching into machinery.
- **Make sure you can actually fit.** The whole landscape is hand-sized, and its entrance is a narrow seam. You’ll need a reliable way to reduce your scale or send a suitably small remote presence through it. Do not force the opening to accommodate you: the description gives no reason to think the plates, or the pocket beyond them, will tolerate that.
- **Plan for an environment you cannot fully characterize.** The pocket has no water, very little air, unusual minerals, and rules that may still be settling into place. Use a sealed, low-leak, low-outgassing suit or remote probe rather than assuming the air is breathable. Keep your heat protection suited to the *measured* conditions on both sides of the seam; the furnace’s ambient temperature may not be the temperature inside the pocket.
- **Have an outside operator and a dependable extraction plan.** The seam may shift, and the plates may close. Monitor the opening continuously, agree on a clear abort signal, and don’t make your return depend on finding the same tiny entry point by eye. The pocket’s habit of relocating may preserve it, but it does not promise to preserve your route home.
- **Bring instruments, not souvenirs.** Use non-contact temperature, atmosphere, distance, and imaging tools. Choose lights and equipment that produce as little heat and contamination as possible. Leave adhesives, flags, dust, food, water, and loose equipment behind.

**While you’re there**

Move slowly and keep to the bare mineral where possible. The plants are the only known life, and they appear to draw what they need from the rock and trace gases; a breath, spilled liquid, hot lamp, or shed fragment could be a large disturbance at this scale. Don’t touch the leaves or bulbs, harvest mineral, or try to “help” the terrain finish forming.

Treat the high shelf as genuinely steep, despite its small size. Its slopes are still taking shape; a surface that looks firm may be loose grains in the act of becoming stone. Avoid the edges, the pale material that seems to be gathering itself, and the floating pinnacle. Its tiny gap above the base is not an invitation to test gravity. A stone that lifts or a shadow that points the wrong way is a reason to pause and observe, not to experiment.

Keep your own position and the furnace’s cycle logged independently. Heat arrives there as a slow change in light, and ordinary time may not be a dependable guide: a plant’s movement might take a day, a ridge’s change months, and the shelf itself may shift when nobody is watching. Use external clocks, fixed camera views, and a companion outside the seam. Don’t leave markers; the landscape may move, and the markers would become part of it.

**Most of all, be willing to turn back without having “done” anything.** This place is quiet, sparse, and still becoming. A successful visit might consist of a few careful observations, no contact, and a safe return. It isn’t a miniature wilderness to conquer. It’s a beginning that happens to have a narrow, dangerous doorway.
