Treat this as an **active industrial interior**, not a cavern or an abandoned building. It may look empty, but its water, air, floors, doors, and machinery are all in motion—and the landscape itself is still being built.

### Before entering

- **Check the atmosphere and basic physics.** Confirm that the air is breathable, pressure and gravity are tolerable, and there are no harmful gases, aerosols, or unusual electrical fields. The metallic smell is not proof that the air is safe. Carry an independent air monitor and enough sealed breathing supply to retreat if conditions change.
- **Tell someone your route and return time.** Use a tracked entry point, carry a reliable beacon, and agree on a check-in schedule. Distances are hard to judge through the haze, and the industrial noise could drown out calls for help.
- **Bring warm, waterproof layers and sturdy, non-slip boots.** Cold condensation and shallow floods can chill you quickly. Choose gear that won’t snag on grating or catch easily on sharp plate edges.
- **Protect your head, eyes, and hearing.** A helmet, sealed eye protection, and hearing protection are sensible around overhead walkways, shifting panels, cranes, and loud machinery. Keep some way to receive alarms or communicate while wearing ear protection.
- **Carry redundant lights, navigation, and power.** Mark your route with approved beacons or a tether where possible. Don’t rely on reflections, distant towers, or apparent floor markings to judge distance; the repeated lines and mist make the space deceptive.
- **Pack basic medical and emergency supplies**, including a first-aid kit, thermal wrap, spare light, and a way to signal for help. Assume rescue may be slow in a place this large and loud.

### While crossing the terraces

- **Learn the machine’s rhythm before committing to a route.** Watch the fans, lights, water channels, and vibration for a while from a sheltered spot. The description suggests that walkways tremble before heavy transfers, fans reverse, pumps change the flow, and doors may remain sealed for days. A route that is safe now may not stay safe.
- **Cross only when conditions are clear and stable.** In particular, avoid exposed flats when the fans blow outward: mist can become blinding, and you may not hear vehicles or machinery approaching. If visibility or sound awareness collapses, stop in a protected place rather than trying to hurry through.
- **Stay on known, load-bearing paths.** The plates are bolted to a framework, but their sharp edges and black seams suggest movement or gaps. Don’t step on shifted panels, cross open seams, climb beneath suspended loads, or approach fresh, wet-looking construction. Treat unfinished ribs and covered panel stacks as unstable work areas.
- **Keep clear of water and metalwork.** Don’t drink from channels or basins, and avoid putting your hands or equipment into the water. It may be contaminated, chemically treated, electrically hazardous, or simply much deeper than it looks. Don’t touch pipes, cables, rails, or exposed machinery to steady yourself.
- **Treat the doors and drains as operating equipment.** Never force a pressure door, enter a duct, descend into a sump, or stand in front of a nozzle. The system can reverse or pressurize without warning. Don’t assume water is flowing “downhill”: pumps may send it uphill or suddenly redirect it.
- **Watch the floor as well as the ceiling.** Condensation makes plates slick, water can spread overnight, and seams may start leaking after a pressure change. Look for new wet patches, fresh drips, or changes in the sound of a nearby pump.
- **Don’t disturb the life you find.** The dark growths and pale animals may be harmless, but their biology is unknown, and they may occupy niches the machinery doesn’t. Avoid touching, collecting, feeding, or blocking the routes they use.

### Have a retreat rule

Turn back if the fans change direction unexpectedly, the floor begins to tremble, water starts moving against its usual course, a door cycles, visibility drops, or your air monitor gives an uncertain reading. Don’t wait for the machinery to become quiet: even when the pumps stop, water and structure continue to move.

The best way to visit is patiently. Pause, listen, and watch before each crossing. This place is not hostile in the manner of a creature, but it has the indifference of a very large machine—and its habits are easier to survive once you have learned them.
