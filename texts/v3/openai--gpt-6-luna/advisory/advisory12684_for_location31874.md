Treat this as an expedition into a moving geological system, not a hike across a quiet plateau. The greatest danger is not something hunting you; it is the ground changing its mind on a scale too large and too slowly for human instincts to recognize.

## Before you go

- **Do not travel alone.** Go with a trained team, a dedicated safety officer, and remote support. Have an independent extraction plan; do not assume a rescue craft can reach you once a dust or gravity event begins.
- **Reconnoitre remotely first.** Use orbital and ground instruments to map cracks, slopes, dust deposits, tremors, and the condition of any nearby structures. A map may become obsolete while you are using it, so favor live measurements over printed routes.
- **Treat the atmosphere as unbreathable until proven otherwise.** The air is thin, and its composition is unknown. Do not rely on acclimatization. Use a pressure-capable suit with an independent oxygen supply, redundant life support, and a way to monitor suit pressure and external conditions.
- **Keep the first visit short and local.** Choose a landing or staging site only after surveying it. Set a strict turnaround time, and never let a distant-looking ridge persuade you to extend the trip. Here, the eye is a poor measure of distance.
- **Agree on abort rules in advance.** A new tremor, a changing pressure reading, the wind stopping or reversing, dust beginning to climb slopes, or a distant escarpment shedding grit should mean retreat to shelter—not a closer look.

## What to bring

Wear a sealed, pressure-capable suit with thermal control, dust-resistant joints and seals, and a visor that protects against bright high-altitude light. Carry backup oxygen, power, communications, navigation, and medical supplies; do not let one damaged component become a fatal single point of failure.

Bring equipment for a landscape that may alter beneath you:

- Live terrain and seismic monitoring, plus inertial navigation that does not depend on a fixed landmark or uninterrupted satellite guidance.
- Multiple independent beacons. A route marker may be buried, displaced, or left on the wrong side of a new crack.
- A shelter or vehicle rated for abrasive dust and sudden ground movement. It should have redundant communications and a way to remain locatable if visibility disappears.
- More water, food, power, and oxygen than the planned outing requires. There is no local water to replenish anything.
- Dust-control measures for entering and leaving the shelter. Fine mineral powder can get into seals, optics, filters, and moving parts; assume it is an equipment hazard as well as a breathing hazard.

Do not expect a conventional tent, a shallow cave, or a vehicle parked on loose dust to provide dependable refuge. The surface itself is the unstable part of the environment. Select shelter sites by measurement, not by how sheltered or solid they look.

## While you are there

Travel by surveyed segments and keep your group within reliable communication range. Use buddy checks for suit condition and orientation, but do not tie people together with a line that could drag them into a fissure or under moving material. Keep vehicles and instruments out of dust channels, basin floors, cliff bases, and the edges of chasms. The chasms are too deep to judge by sight, and a dust-covered valley may conceal a drop or a newly opened crack.

Watch instruments as closely as the horizon. Changes may begin far away or too slowly to feel, then become dangerous all at once. If a broad dust front is approaching, **do not try to outrun it**. It advances at walking pace, but it spans a region, carries grit, and may coincide with ground movement or abnormal pressure. Get to the preselected refuge while the route is still sound, seal up, and wait for measurements—not appearances—to show that conditions have settled.

Expect distance and scale to mislead you. A nearby ridge may be days away. Plan by instrumented travel time and verified landmarks, not by what seems walkable. Avoid night travel: the stars may be spectacular, but the darkness is profound, and the slow distortions occasionally seen in the sky are not reliable navigation cues.

## The structures and the life

Do not touch, enter, excavate around, or camp immediately beside the black tower or the pale rings. The rings appear to distribute crustal strain, but their operation is not understood; even a device that makes nearby ground quieter is not necessarily safe to disturb or use as shelter. Treat these objects as protected, active infrastructure. Observe from a planned distance and leave them as you found them.

The pale animals and mineral growths are rare. Do not approach, collect, feed, or bring them aboard. Their biology is unknown, and the environment has little life to spare. Bring out all waste and equipment, and follow contamination-control procedures on return.

## After a disturbance

A region may look settled soon after it has shifted. That does not mean it is stable. Re-survey the return route, check the vehicle and suit seals for dust damage, and compare current terrain measurements with the outbound record. If the ground has changed, do not try to reconstruct the old route by memory. Use live data and be prepared to wait or choose another exit.

The right mindset is patient and conservative. This country can seem finished—smooth, empty, almost peaceful—because its violence is usually too large and too slow to read as an immediate threat. If you go, let instruments set the pace, keep your refuge close, and regard every apparently uneventful hour as a reason to continue being careful, not a reason to take a chance.
