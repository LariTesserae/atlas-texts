## The first preparation: do not assume you can fit

The room is washbasin-sized. An ordinary human body cannot safely enter it unless your travel method can reduce or represent you at that scale. Do not try to squeeze through the pale rock, enlarge the cavity, or “make space” by force. Those would be interventions in a place that is still forming.

Use a **scale-adapted remote presence or small, non-invasive avatar** if you have one. If you do not, remain outside and observe through the skin of rock, if that can be done without cutting or peeling it back. Treat “the passage into the earth” as an uncertain impression, not an invitation to crawl in: it may be a crease when unattended, and the room does not actually grow to accommodate you.

## Pack for restraint, not exploration

Bring as little as possible:

- A small, quiet means of keeping track of your route out. Keep the reference outside the cavity if you can; do not plant a marker in the room.
- Passive instruments for recording light, temperature, humidity, and position. Prefer dim displays and no active beams, flashes, pings, or scanning fields.
- A way to monitor your own breathing and orientation without relying on the room’s reflections.
- A sterile barrier or remote sampling system—but plan not to collect anything. The seams, water, grit, plants, and animals are all part of a system whose boundaries are not yet clear.

Do not count on ordinary assumptions about air, gravity, distance, time, or the reliability of instruments. A drop may rise; a passage may vanish from view; something may be present most clearly when you are not looking directly at it. Your equipment can document these discrepancies, but it cannot make the room behave consistently.

## How to behave inside

Move slowly and make no unnecessary contact. The stone is damp, the floor is partly grit and partly something softer, and even a light touch could alter a surface that has not finished becoming itself. Don’t test the water, press the dark sediment, shift the upright stone, or follow the shallow marks into the wall.

Avoid staring at the animals. In this place, direct attention seems to make them less distinct, while averted attention lets them appear sharply. Watch gently from the edge of your vision, and accept that you may not be able to verify what you saw. Do not chase a creature, shine a light at one, or try to make it reappear. Watch the floor as well as the animal: the dark creature’s reflection arrives first, and the mites react to that advance image.

Expect your own reflection and instruments to be unreliable witnesses. The fronds’ reflections move after the fronds stop; an insect may become only the place where it was about to be. Record what happens without deciding too quickly which version is real.

Keep your voice low, or remain silent. If you hear a breath from the far side of the rock, do not answer it. It may be a sound, a feature of the room, or the beginning of something that should not be encouraged. Likewise, avoid sudden movement: even without a shadow, you can apparently interrupt the light.

## Leave gently

Set a short visit limit before arriving, and leave on schedule rather than staying to resolve the room’s mysteries. Before departure, check your exit reference rather than trusting a passage that seems to continue into the earth. Do not leave equipment, labels, samples, or a new mark behind. The existing traces may be animals’ paths, early shelters, or something not yet settled into either meaning.

The safest stance is that of a careful guest at the beginning of a world: observe, take notes, disturb as little as possible, and let unanswered things remain unanswered. If your scale, route, or sense of what is present becomes uncertain, retreat to your external reference instead of exploring farther. This place is small, but it may hold more distance than it can safely explain.
