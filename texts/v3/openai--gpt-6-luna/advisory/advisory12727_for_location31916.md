## Prepare for it as a real dive, not a shallow-water swim

The clear light and warm sea would make the drowned highland feel welcoming, but neither tells you how deep a slope falls, how far away a ridge is, or whether you can safely reach the surface from it. Treat the place as an unmapped underwater wilderness with potentially deceptive depth.

### Before entering

- **Check that your body and equipment are compatible with the local sea.** Test the water for unfamiliar chemistry, microbes, toxins, and unusual pressure or temperature gradients. Use a clean, self-contained breathing system rather than relying on the surrounding water.
- **Go with a trained dive partner and a surface support team.** Agree on time, depth, route, and turn-back limits before descending. Carry redundant breathing gas, a cutting tool, emergency signaling, and a way to locate and recover one another.
- **Bring instruments, not just good eyesight.** A depth gauge, compass, sonar or mapping device, and reliable lights will help when the blue distance makes a nearby ridge look far away—or a distant basin look temptingly close. Use a computer and plan conservatively for pressure exposure and ascent.
- **Set a fixed, modest depth limit.** The site’s apparent shallowness is no guarantee: the description suggests slopes and basins whose depth may be difficult to judge, perhaps even anomalous. Do not follow a valley downward just to see where it goes.
- **Arrange surface support without anchoring on the highland.** A vessel holding position, or a temporary reference line kept clear of the bottom, is preferable to driving an anchor into a landscape with living ledges and delicate growth.

### While exploring

Follow the ridgelines and broad, open saddles. They offer the clearest orientation and the easiest route back. Treat dense groves as places to look into, not to push through: long blades and branching fronds could snag hoses, obscure your partner, or conceal a sharp drop. Keep enough distance from the rock to avoid cutting yourself on fresh volcanic edges, and avoid loose pumice and ash pockets that could cloud the water or shift beneath you.

Stay clear of the warm vents and their quiet pools. They sound gentle, but hot or mineral-rich water can still be hazardous, and shimmer or drifting dust may make nearby features hard to read. Don’t enter cracks, swim under slabs, or descend into any basin whose bottom you cannot see and map.

Move slowly. The forest’s patient sway will make a steady, unhurried pace feel natural; fast swimming will stir sediment, startle the occasional animal, and make it harder to notice changes in depth or current. Keep your group close and in sight, and turn around before fatigue, cold, or disorientation becomes an issue—even in warm water.

### Leave it as you found it

There are no marks of human passage here, and that should remain true after your visit. Don’t collect mineral seams, plants, or animals; don’t stand on living mats or roots; and don’t attach lines to the rock or growth. Keep lights modest, avoid chasing the rare creatures, and take all equipment and waste back to the surface. Clean and quarantine your gear afterward so you don’t carry organisms between worlds.

### What the visit may feel like

Expect to lose ordinary measures of distance. The sun will be present overhead as a pale, wavering disk, while ridges and basins unfold into shades of green and blue. A slope that looks simple may fall away farther than your eyes can tell. In the groves, the view may narrow to layered leaves moving almost too slowly to notice; then a passage may open onto a vast saddle and reveal the whole highland at once.

The quiet will probably feel spacious rather than empty. You may hear water moving over stone, fronds brushing together, and little else. Let that stillness set the pace—but let your instruments, limits, and dive partner make the decisions.
