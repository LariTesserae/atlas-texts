## Before you go

Treat this as a **living industrial system**, not an empty ruin or a wetland. The machinery is operating, the water is everywhere, and the creatures occupy even the places that look uninhabited. Nothing in the description proves that the air, water, or surfaces are safe for a human—or that the animals will react predictably to one.

If you have a choice, make the first visit a **remote survey**. Send a drone or tethered probe to sample the air and water, map the surface, check currents and temperatures, and inspect a small number of routes. In particular, do not assume that a dark channel is shallow, that a grate is load-bearing, or that an apparently empty basin is actually empty.

Before entering, establish a clear return plan: a known arrival point, a mapped route to it, regular check-ins, and a hard turnaround time. In a place with no trustworthy horizon and miles of repetitive construction, “I can see the way back” is not a navigation system.

## Clothing and life support

The atmosphere sounds warm and persistently saturated. That can make exertion dangerous in a deceptively quiet way: sweat will not evaporate efficiently, so a person can overheat without feeling as though they are working hard. Wear a lightweight protective suit that can be ventilated or actively cooled, and monitor your temperature and hydration. Avoid heavy insulation unless measurements show it is needed.

Do not rely on the air being breathable. Use a monitored respirator or a sealed, pressure-appropriate suit until you have reliable readings for oxygen, pressure, and contaminants. Condensation can fog visors, cameras, and connectors, so use anti-fog equipment and keep backups protected. Every seal, zipper, and exposed connector should be rated for continuous wet conditions.

Wear boots with excellent grip and ankle support. The surfaces are likely slick, and the water may hide seams, drops, grilles, or sudden changes in depth. Use nonconductive outer equipment where practical, and keep batteries, power connections, and communications hardware sealed. Even if there is no obvious electrical hazard, an active complex of pumps, metal structures, and water is a poor place to discover one.

Bring a flotation aid and a tether. The water is described as warm and still, but that says nothing about its depth, what lies beneath it, or whether a channel leads into a pump intake or enclosed conduit. A tether should attach to your harness—not to a loose piece of the environment—and should be managed so it cannot snag on grilles, animals, or machinery.

## Moving around

Move slowly and deliberately. The plain’s repeated arches, lights, pipes, and basins may defeat visual estimates of distance. Use inertial navigation, a detailed map, and your own marked route. Place removable, non-toxic markers at important junctions, or use tracked beacons; do not rely on footprints, reflections, or remembered landmarks in a landscape where every surface is wet and many structures repeat.

Stay on broad, clearly load-bearing platforms. Avoid:

- Walking through groups of animals, even if they appear to be resting.
- Putting hands, feet, or equipment through grilles.
- Entering channels, basins, pipe mouths, cisterns, or windowless cuts.
- Leaning on pressure chambers, valves, outlets, or warm housings.
- Climbing machinery or stepping onto a surface before checking that it is fixed.
- Blocking drains, slots, vents, or other openings.

Water movement may be extremely slow and difficult to see. A quiet outlet may still have suction, pressure, or a flow that becomes dangerous when disturbed. Never test a channel by entering it yourself. Use a probe or remote instrument.

Keep a measured pace and take frequent cooling breaks somewhere dry, stable, and pre-checked. The world’s quiet can make it tempting to walk for a long time without noticing fatigue or heat stress. Carry more drinking water than you expect to need, in sealed containers. **Do not drink the local water**, even if it looks clean: it may contain microorganisms, dissolved chemicals, or biological material that neither you nor your suit can easily detect.

## The animals

Assume that every surface or opening may contain life. The bodies may be motionless, but motionless does not mean inert, tame, sleeping, or harmless. A small withdrawal of feelers or a slow contraction could be a response to your approach; it is not necessarily an invitation or a warning you can interpret.

Give animals a wide berth. Do not touch, feed, collect, shine intense lights into their eyes, or deliberately provoke them to see what they do. Avoid stepping on apparently empty ground beside a cluster: animals may be layered under the water or beneath grates. Do not remove a creature that has attached itself to your suit until you are in a controlled area; pulling it off could injure you, the animal, or both. Photograph and observe from a distance instead.

Use smooth outer surfaces with minimal loops, dangling straps, or exposed cables. In a place where animals occupy pipes, seams, and grilles, anything that catches can become difficult to retrieve. Keep your hands covered and your suit closed. A quiet, crowded ecosystem may be far more vulnerable to accidental disturbance than it looks.

Treat the whole plain as a protected biological site unless you have convincing evidence otherwise. Do not introduce food, waste, soil, seeds, or unsterilized gear. Plan for decontamination and quarantine when you leave. If you must take a sample, do so only under a deliberate containment protocol; for an ordinary visit, there is little reason to take one.

## Interacting with the machinery

The system may be maintaining the conditions that allow all this life to exist. Do not turn off, open, adjust, block, or disconnect anything. Do not assume a valve is abandoned because its gauge is steady, or that a pipe is safe because it only emits a soft breath of steam. A small intervention could alter water levels, pressure, temperature, or the movement of nutrients over a very large area.

Pay attention to changes. A new vibration, a shift in water level, a pump that stops, an outlet that changes rate, or an unusual increase in animal movement should be treated as a reason to withdraw along your mapped route—not as an opportunity to investigate more closely. The machinery’s calm may be a stable operating state, and you cannot know what lies beyond it.

## What to carry

A sensible kit would include:

- A cooled, humidity-tolerant protective suit with verified air monitoring.
- Anti-fog eye protection and backup lights.
- High-grip boots, gloves, flotation, and a secure personal tether.
- Redundant navigation, communications, and location beacons.
- A portable air and water sensor suite.
- A waterproof medical kit and heat-stress monitoring.
- Sealed drinking water and energy supplies.
- A remote camera or small survey drone.
- Spare dry layers and sealed bags for contaminated equipment.
- A clear extraction signal and a companion who tracks your condition and route.

Avoid unnecessary tools that could puncture, scrape, snag, or shed material into the environment. A weapon is unlikely to make this safer; distance, observation, and a prompt exit are better protections.

## The right attitude

Expect the visit to feel less like exploring a deserted factory than walking through a machine whose parts are alive. Its stillness is not proof of safety. Its order is not proof that it was built for you. And the animals’ unhurried behavior does not mean your presence is insignificant.

Keep your route short, your contact with the place minimal, and your attention on the ground, the water, and the functioning machinery. If the landscape begins to seem familiar, remember that it may simply be repeating itself. In a plain with no reliable horizon, the safest traveler is the one who leaves while the way home is still certain.
