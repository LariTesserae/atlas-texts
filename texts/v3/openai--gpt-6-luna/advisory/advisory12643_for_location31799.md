## Preparing to visit the Upper Reservoir

Treat this as a **submerged industrial site**, not as a garden you can simply swim through. It may feel calm and welcoming—the water is warm, the light is clear, and the machinery is quiet—but the calm does not make it safe for an unprotected person. The place was built to contain water, not to accommodate visitors.

### Before you enter

- **Find out the pressure and the permitted route.** The exact depth and pressure are not given, and a gentle-looking reservoir can still exert dangerous force. Do not attempt entry until your suit, transport capsule, and return route are rated for the measured conditions. Don’t assume you can swim back to an ordinary surface: the bright space above may not be an accessible surface at all.
- **Use a pressure-rated, fully sealed suit or transfer capsule.** The district contains water rather than breathable air. Ordinary clothing, a snorkel, or a conventional open-circuit scuba setup would be inadequate. A closed-circuit breathing system is preferable if it is compatible with local rules, because it limits bubbles and keeps exhaled gases contained.
- **Keep your life support independent of the district.** Don’t plug into a pump, borrow a line, or assume a gauge or pipe is meant for you. The machinery is part of a closed system; changing its loads or opening a connection could affect more than your immediate surroundings.
- **Make your gear clean, sealed, and non-shedding.** No loose fibers, uncontained lubricants, exposed chemicals, soil, seeds, spores, or small objects. The plants and nutrient lines appear deliberately managed, and the rule against unsealed material is best taken literally.
- **Plan a short visit with a clear turnaround point.** Bring a reliable tether or another independent way to find your return route, a pressure and life-support monitor, and a way to communicate through the suit. Go with a buddy if possible. Agree in advance on what counts as a problem and when you both turn back.

If the entry system or a hatch is unfamiliar, **do not force it or improvise a passage through the outer wall**. The retaining ring is holding back a great deal of water. Use only a known access route and leave every seal and latch as you found it.

### While inside

Move slowly. The water has little current, but the passages are narrow and crowded. Plants overhang rails and bridges, and the space often leaves only enough room to turn around. Keep your buoyancy controlled; a casual kick or sudden turn could stir up deposits, damage stems, or send a loose tool into machinery.

Use the rails and fixed structure for orientation, not the foliage. Avoid gripping, pruning, bending, or pushing aside plants. They may look like ordinary greenery, but their roots run through conduits and around pipes. Don’t touch gauges, valves, grilles, or the filtration tower, even if something looks accessible or in need of adjustment. The pumps and relays are quiet because the system is operating—not because it is abandoned.

The light may make it hard to judge depth or direction. There is no clear horizon, and the apparent brightness above is not a dependable way home. Follow your tether, route markers, and instruments instead. A tick from a relay, a low vibration through a rail, or the small repeated spill into a catch basin sounds like part of the place’s ordinary rhythm; don’t mistake every mechanical noise for a call to intervene. Do, however, take any change in pressure, water movement, or machinery sound seriously.

### What to avoid

- Don’t release bubbles or leave anything behind if you can help it.
- Don’t drink the water or assume it is clean merely because it is clear.
- Don’t collect leaves, deposits, or pieces of machinery.
- Don’t feed, seed, or introduce any organism.
- Don’t climb toward the apparent brightness or assume there is an air pocket overhead.
- Don’t open a route to the reservoir, even briefly, to make entry or exit easier.

### Leaving

Return by the same verified route, without rushing. Follow whatever pressure-equalization procedure the access system requires; don’t guess at decompression or pressure changes from how comfortable you feel. Check yourself and your equipment for leaks or trapped debris before opening your suit or capsule anywhere outside the designated safe area.

The Upper Reservoir seems less threatening than many underwater places because nothing lunges, chases, or visibly struggles. Its danger is quieter: pressure, close machinery, delicate infrastructure, and a system that may be functioning exactly as designed. Visit as a careful guest. Keep your footprint small, your equipment sealed, and your hands to yourself.
