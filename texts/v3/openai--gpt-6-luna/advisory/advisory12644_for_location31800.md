Treat this as a **fragile, unstable underground site**, not as an abandoned room you can safely wander through. It feels quiet, but quiet is not the same as safe: the fresh fractures, loose rubble, and old pipes all suggest a place still changing.

### Before you go in
- **Go with a trained cave or industrial-site guide**, and tell someone outside your party your route and return time. Agree on a firm turnaround time.
- Have the air and structure assessed first. Bring monitors for **oxygen deficiency and hazardous gases**; old infrastructure and enclosed underground spaces can hold invisible dangers. A regular face mask will not make bad air safe.
- Wear a **helmet, sturdy high-grip boots, gloves, and protective clothing** suited to heat and damp. The ceiling is low, and the floor is uneven; expect to crouch, brace yourself, and move slowly.
- Carry at least two independent lights per person, with spare batteries. The dead lamps are not useful guides, and the faint greenish water is too dim to show where you’re putting your feet.
- Establish a reliable way to communicate with the surface. Underground devices may fail. Mark your route without damaging the site, and make sure everyone knows how to retreat.

### In the chamber
Move as if every foothold might shift. The rubble is new, the mineral crust may be slick, and wet seams can conceal hollows. Keep clear of fresh fractures and do not climb on the machinery or use it as a handhold. The pipes may still be carrying heat or pressure: **don’t touch them, open them, or stand beneath joints where condensate gathers**. A small drop can still be hot, and a corroded fitting can be less harmless than it looks.

Don’t drink from the pools or collect water without testing it. Don’t taste, break, or handle the plants; their appearance tells you little about their chemistry, and the damp surfaces may be delicate. The room’s lack of animals is not permission to disturb it. Its life seems slow enough that a careless boot or snapped stem could undo years of growth.

The machinery should be treated as active until qualified people establish otherwise. The trembling gauge, faint vibration, and occasional clicks are clues, not assurances that the system is safe or functioning normally. Avoid switching, turning, or repairing anything.

### What it may feel like
Prepare for the darkness to take time. At first you may see almost nothing; then a leaf edge, a wet patch, or a reflection will emerge, and the chamber may seem larger—or stranger—than it did on entry. Give your eyes a chance to adjust, but don’t mistake familiarity for visibility. Use your lights sparingly if you want to experience the place, not so sparingly that you lose track of your footing.

The silence may also make small sounds feel significant: a drip, a pipe’s click, a vibration you feel more than hear. That is part of the chamber’s character, not necessarily a message meant for you. Resist the urge to fill the quiet by talking loudly, testing echoes, or making the machinery respond. A patient visit may be mostly standing still and noticing how little changes.

Leave the place as you found it. Take nothing, leave nothing, and turn back well before fatigue, heat, or uncertainty begins to affect your judgment. This is a chamber to observe carefully—and to let keep its own pace.
