## Before you go

Treat Vey as a place where the **ground, routes, and even the apparent layout of the city may change**, but not necessarily in a dramatic or predictable way. Its main hazards seem less like sudden attacks and more like quiet misjudgments: stepping onto a newly shifted edge, trusting an outdated map, or following a route that has become unreliable.

- **Arrange a local guide or contact before arrival.** Ask which districts are currently accessible, whether any transit lines are suspended, and what the latest ground survey says. Vey’s maps are explicitly provisional; a year-old route may no longer meet the river, stairs may have changed, and bridges may no longer lead where you expect.
- **Set a firm check-in and return plan.** Carry a working communication device, but don’t make it your only lifeline. Agree on physical meeting points and a fallback route with someone who knows the city. If your multiverse crossing requires a return anchor, place it in a designated, stable location and confirm that local authorities approve of it.
- **Keep your first visit modest.** Stay within an inhabited district, travel in daylight—or its local equivalent—and avoid trying to cross the open plain alone. Vey’s spaciousness can make destinations look nearby while intervening hollows, ridges, or wetland make them hard to reach.

## What to bring

Wear **sturdy, waterproof footwear with good grip**. The black clay is warm and newly exposed, cut by sharp ridges and glassy mineral seams; hollows may conceal water or soft ground. Choose shoes that can cope with both damp surfaces and patches dry enough to dust. Gloves are useful if you expect to handle rails or rough ground.

Bring:

- A compact first-aid kit, including dressings for cuts and blisters.
- Water and a modest supply of food, even if you expect to stay in town.
- A reliable light for covered walks and dim streets. Don’t expect the windows or public lighting to illuminate much.
- A **nonreflective physical compass or orientation aid**, plus an offline map and a written description of your route. Check these against a local guide rather than assuming they are authoritative.
- A way to record landmarks and your route without relying entirely on network service. Take notes on what you see, not just where your device says you are.
- Weather protection suited to persistent damp and sudden rain: a shell, a small towel, and a bag or case for electronics.
- Any medication you need, carried on your person rather than left in a distant lodging.

You probably don’t need expedition gear for the central city. You do need enough equipment to stay comfortable if a short walk takes longer than expected.

## Moving around

**Use marked, currently approved routes**, and confirm your destination at each major crossing. Transit is quiet and may be difficult to notice except by the moving shadows cast beneath it. The lines can appear to cross or share space without actually meeting, so don’t infer a route from sight alone. Use stations, signs, or local advice—and obey service closures, especially around the southern plain.

Bridges deserve particular caution. Some span open space for long distances; some appear to end at buildings that are no longer there; others arrive across a canal without obvious support. Use only bridges that locals identify as open. Don’t improvise across a gap, climb onto a transit line, or assume that a structure is safe simply because it looks precise.

In the open ground:

- Keep to known paths and give canal banks, cut edges, and sharp shelves a wide berth.
- Don’t walk across a dark hollow just because it looks shallow. Rain can fill grooves quickly, and water may drain away again into porous ground.
- Avoid walking close to a changing waterline. The canals may be empty one day and carrying water the next.
- If your route becomes unclear, **stop and ask** rather than pressing on by guesswork. The plain’s small changes can make familiar-looking terrain misleading.

The rain is not a dependable guide to local conditions. It can approach from far away without falling where expected, or wet the city while the apparent storm remains beyond the horizon. Check the ground and local reports, not just the sky.

## Water, plants, and wildlife

The city is often damp rather than rainy. Condensation gathers on cables, leaves, and bridge undersides; shaded channels and mossy hollows may hold standing water. Avoid drinking from canals or ground pools unless a local source confirms they are safe.

Leave the vegetation alone. Plants grow through buildings, vents, and pavement cracks, and residents often train vines aside rather than remove them. Some species are believed to help stabilize the ground. Don’t cut, uproot, or step through growth to make a shortcut. If a plant crosses your path, find the customary way around it or ask someone how it is handled.

The small long-legged creatures beneath the bridges are best observed from a distance. Don’t feed, approach, trap, or try to identify them by handling them. Their behavior is poorly understood, and Vey is not the place to assume that an unfamiliar animal is harmless—or that it needs your intervention. The same caution applies to the tiny insects around lower-district lights.

## Reflections and the “second city”

The most important local precaution concerns still water. Reflections in Vey may show a second, unfamiliar skyline, and that image can persist under unusual conditions. **Do not treat a reflection as a doorway, a map, or a reliable view of the surrounding area.** Don’t step toward reflected structures, attempt to navigate by them, or conduct experiments over a pool.

If you see the second skyline:

1. Step back from the water without disturbing it.
2. Tell your local contact or the relevant city authority.
3. Avoid nearby crossings and transit routes until you know whether they are affected.
4. Follow any closure or diversion notice, even if the route looks physically clear.

The suspended southern service during a prolonged reflection event is a good reason to take such notices seriously. This may be a navigational issue rather than a direct danger, but you don’t need to know which before keeping clear.

## Local manners and communication

Vey is quiet. Its residents move through plazas and covered walks with little conversation, doors close silently, and public notices may only become visible at an angle. Don’t mistake quiet for hostility, but avoid shouting, playing loud audio, or drawing unnecessary attention.

Approach someone calmly and give them room to respond. If you don’t understand a notice, ask rather than touching or scraping the wall surface. The writing or display may be built into the material. Keep your communications simple and concrete: where you are, where you’re going, and what help you need.

Be especially considerate around infrastructure, canals, and living walls. Don’t tamper with the silver transit threads, investigate the buried power structures, or leave equipment attached to bridges or plants. A low ring sometimes passes through the foundations, making puddles ripple together. If this happens, pause and follow local guidance; don’t assume it is an alarm, but don’t ignore it either.

## How to pace the visit

Expect Vey to feel **spacious, subdued, and difficult to measure**. The dim sky gives little sense of time passing. Distant sounds may arrive late or not at all, and there may be few familiar cues—birds, traffic noise, or a bright sun—to help you judge distance and orientation. Build in more time than you think you need, take breaks somewhere sheltered, and avoid scheduling tightly linked trips across the city.

The best first itinerary is a short one: arrive, confirm current conditions, visit a nearby public district with a local guide, and return by the same verified route. Save the open plain, distant towers, and remote canals for a later visit.

Vey is not presented as a place in immediate crisis. It is more like a city carefully inhabiting a landscape that continues to adjust beneath it. The sensible traveler is curious but not entitled: keep to known routes, preserve what is growing, respect closures, and let local people decide which parts of the city are safe to cross today.
