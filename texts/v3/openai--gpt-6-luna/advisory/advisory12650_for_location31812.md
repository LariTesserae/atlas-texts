## Prepare for a quiet place that may not let time behave normally

Treat the plateau as **physically real but temporally uncertain**. It looks habitable, gentle, and orderly—not immediately threatening—but its unchanging stream, suspended droplet, and unweathered paths make it unwise to assume that ordinary processes are occurring. You may be able to move through it while the rest of the place remains fixed; or you may be mistaking a strange, stable pattern for stillness. Prepare for either possibility.

### Before you enter

- **Set a firm visit limit and a clear return plan.** Agree on a check-in schedule with someone outside, and decide in advance what will trigger your departure. Don’t rely on the sky, shadows, thirst, or fatigue to tell you how long you have been there.
- **Carry two independent ways to measure time**, preferably using different mechanisms, and check them against each other regularly. Bring a charged light and spare power as well: the daylight appears generous, but it may not change, and workshops or shaded lanes will still be dim.
- **Bring everything you need to remain self-sufficient.** Take drinking water, food, personal medication, and a small first-aid kit. The cistern and stream look inviting, but water that appears to run without changing may not be renewing itself in an ordinary way. Don’t drink it untreated.
- **Dress for cool damp air and rough ground.** Wear sturdy boots with good grip, layers you can adjust, and gloves for handling stone or old timber. A waterproof outer layer is sensible even without signs of rain: moisture is already in the air and on the rock.
- **Make your navigation plan simple.** The town’s paths follow the slopes, and the plateau edge is broken and steep. Carry a map or record your route, keep a companion in sight, and choose a recognizable meeting point near your entry route.

### While you’re there

Move slowly, especially on the pale fractured rock and around the unfinished slopes. The land is newly risen and sharp-edged; it may not have settled into stable scree, and narrow cuts can lead toward abrupt drops without becoming obvious ravines. Stay on marked paths and stepped lanes where possible. Avoid climbing ledges or taking shortcuts down the slope.

The town’s construction looks soundly made but hand-repaired. Watch for loose shutters, weighted roofs, uneven paving, drainage channels, and open doors. A dim workshop may contain tools, cords, or other objects left exactly where someone last placed them. **Treat these as belonging to someone**, even if no one appears to be present.

Give animals room. Birds, lizards, mice, and the grazers may be the only living inhabitants you encounter—or they may simply be the most visible ones. Don’t touch, feed, corner, or carry them. If an animal seems unnaturally motionless, leave it undisturbed rather than trying to test or “help” it.

### Be especially careful about changing things

The stillness is the central uncertainty here. A droplet that never falls and a stream that never changes may be harmless peculiarities, signs of a repeating moment, or evidence that ordinary cause and effect works differently. You do not need to solve that mystery to visit safely.

Avoid experiments that could alter the place: don’t dam the stream, move objects from doorways, disturb the pools, or mark stone permanently. If you want to test whether time is passing, do so with **your own equipment**, in a small and reversible way—such as recording a short interval on a camera while checking your independent clocks. Keep any test away from animals, homes, and water. If your instruments disagree or behave strangely, stop testing and follow your return plan.

The same caution applies to people. The open door, cup, basket, and half-coiled cord suggest interrupted life, not necessarily abandonment. Don’t enter private rooms or move belongings to see what happens. If you encounter someone, approach openly, keep a respectful distance, and don’t assume that silence means consent—or that a still figure is safe to touch.

### Look after your own sense of time

The place may feel peaceful at first and disorienting later. Its quiet is not empty: water ticks into stone, a shutter taps, and a bird calls once. But those sounds may recur without any larger change to anchor your sense of passing time. Check your watch, eat and drink by schedule, and take breaks with your companion. If you begin to feel detached, confused, or compelled to keep investigating, that is a good time to leave.

Most of all, don’t let the landscape’s apparent balance persuade you to stay indefinitely. The plateau is beautiful and welcoming in a restrained way: green in its sheltered folds, bright with fresh stone, and arranged with remarkable calm. Visit it as a guest, leave its small motions and possessions alone, and return while you still have a reliable route back.
