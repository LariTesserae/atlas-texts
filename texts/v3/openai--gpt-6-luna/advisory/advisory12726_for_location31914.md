Treat this as a wetland wilderness in which **you cannot see, cannot trust the ground, and may be heard by things you cannot locate**. A casual walkabout would be a poor plan. Go only with a local guide or a team that has surveyed the route, and make your first visit short, close to a known safe point, and easy to abort.

### Before you go

- **Confirm that you can breathe there.** Do not infer a safe atmosphere from the presence of animals or working machinery. Have the air sampled for oxygen, toxic gases, and irritating aerosols, and check pressure and temperature. If the atmosphere is uncertain, use suitable life-support equipment rather than an ordinary respirator; a respirator does not supply oxygen.
- **Learn the local hazards from people who work there.** Ask which sounds indicate dangerous water, machinery, animals, or weather, and which routes are actually used. A pump house or cable may be a landmark, but that does not make it safe to approach or use.
- **Set a hard turnaround time and extraction plan.** Tell someone outside the plateau your route and check-in schedule. Carry a locator or communications device appropriate to the site, but do not assume radio, satellite navigation, or any other familiar system will work.
- **Do not go alone.** Agree on simple tactile signals and a lost-contact procedure before entering. Shouting may not carry reliably, and it may also announce your position to wildlife. A guide’s familiarity with the place is more valuable than elaborate equipment.

### Clothing and equipment

- Wear **waterproof, abrasion-resistant outer clothing** with insulating layers underneath. Constant damp can chill you even in warm air; high humidity can also make heavy clothing dangerously hot. Test your clothing in similar conditions if possible.
- Use sturdy boots with good grip, gaiters, and gloves. Avoid assuming that tall waders are protective: if they fill, or if you sink, they can become a serious liability. Take flotation equipment for any unavoidable water crossing, but treat it as backup, not permission to cross.
- Carry a **probe pole**, a helmet for wooded or industrial areas, first aid supplies, and enough water and food for an unexpected delay. Protect electronics and dry essentials in sealed containers.
- Bring a physical guide line or another reliable way to retrace your route. It must be secured so it cannot trail loosely into machinery, snag vegetation, or endanger animals. Plan its placement with someone who knows the site.

### Moving through the dark

Do not plan around seeing. A lamp or night-vision device may be useless, misleading, or simply irrelevant in a place with no visible light. Your main tools are a hand on a guide line, a pole probing the ground, and careful attention to sound, airflow, and smell.

Move slowly and probe every unfamiliar step. The ground may be a root mat over water, not solid earth; pools may be much deeper than they sound. Do not wade merely because a route looks level, and do not test a pool by putting a hand or foot into it. Water may carry vibration farther than air, so a small disturbance can travel a long way.

Do not treat “quiet” as safe or “noisy” as dangerous. Sounds can arrive from odd directions or vanish unexpectedly. The industrial hum and animal calls may help you keep your bearings, but they can change with weather, migration, or machinery. Record landmarks and changes as you go, but never follow an unfamiliar sound off your surveyed route. Use a guide’s interpretation rather than trying to decode the plateau yourself.

### Around wildlife, plants, and machinery

Assume unfamiliar plants can irritate or poison you, and unfamiliar animals may react to scent, movement, or vibration. Do not touch, taste, feed, or collect anything. Avoid placing equipment in pools or striking the ground to “listen” unless a local guide has told you that it is safe.

Treat every industrial structure as active until proven otherwise. Pipes, cables, softened pit edges, moving machinery, and hidden water channels can all be hazardous. Do not enter a building, climb a tower, cross a conveyor, or use a handrail as a route without the crew’s approval. A stopped machine may still conceal a flooded interior or unstable ground.

### The most important rule

Keep the visit small. Establish a safe base, make a short guided excursion, and return while everyone is still warm, alert, and oriented. In this place, distance is not something you can glance at: it has to be measured by route, sound, and memory. The plateau is not merely dark and wet. It is a living, shifting environment that can make a confident stranger lose track of where they are long before they realize they are lost.
