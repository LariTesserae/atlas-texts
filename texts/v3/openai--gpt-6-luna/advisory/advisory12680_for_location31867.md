## Before you go

Treat this as a **small, enclosed waterworks that happens to be alive**, not as a cave or an abandoned building. It may take only minutes to cross on a map, but its tightly joined rooms, low arches, and echoing side passages could make it surprisingly easy to lose your bearings.

- **Go with a local guide or a companion, and tell someone outside when you expect to return.** Agree on a turnaround time and a simple signal for “stop” or “help.” Don’t explore alone.
- **Check that the environment is human-compatible.** Confirm the air is breathable, the pressure and temperature are tolerable, and there are no unfamiliar airborne irritants or biological hazards. The place’s age and dampness make it unwise to assume the air is harmless.
- **Bring two independent lights.** A headlamp keeps your hands free; carry a backup and spare power. Keep the beam modest around roosting bats and nesting birds.
- **Wear grippy, closed-toe footwear** with good traction on wet tile. Use a compact helmet where ceilings are low, and bring clothing that tolerates humidity and can dry quickly. A light shell may help against drips; bulky equipment will be awkward in the passages.
- **Protect essentials from water.** Put communications, maps, medication, and spare batteries in waterproof bags. Keep a physical route record: electronics may fail, and the architecture’s echoes can make “just follow the sound” a poor plan.
- **Bring drinking water and a small first-aid kit.** Don’t drink from the channels or cisterns, however domestic they look. Their water is part of a living, interconnected system, not necessarily treated for visitors.

## While inside

Move slowly and keep one hand free. The brick and tile are worn smooth in places, and a shallow-looking channel may have a slick edge or a deeper drop than the dim light suggests. Watch for low beams, grates, wet steps, and sudden changes in floor level. Stay on established walkways; **don’t climb into vents, drains, tanks, or openings beneath grates**.

Water flow may change while you are there. A gate opened somewhere out of sight could send a brief surge through a channel, so don’t stand in a drain or put hands and feet into unseen water. Likewise, don’t lift grates, redirect trickles, remove objects, or “test” how the system works. A small alteration could affect several rooms and the animals relying on them.

Expect to be disoriented by sound. Voices and footsteps may arrive doubled or from the wrong direction, while water can seem close when it is not. Keep your group together and use agreed visual signals as well as speech. Mark your route only with removable, non-damaging markers, and retrieve them on the way out.

The wildlife is part of the structure’s present balance. Give bats, swifts, frogs, fish, and insects room; don’t handle them, feed them, shine bright lights into roosts, or block their routes. Avoid touching water, algae, mineral deposits, guano, or crumbling plaster. Damp old masonry can carry irritants or pathogens, and disturbing dust or animal waste is an unnecessary risk. Wash your hands after the visit.

Use **no flame, smoke, pesticides, or strong fragrances**. Besides the fire risk in a confined space, fumes and chemicals could harm both you and the resident life.

## Leave if

Turn back if anyone feels faint, unusually short of breath, confused, chilled, or suddenly unwell; if the air changes; if water begins rising or rushing; or if your lights, route markers, or communications become unreliable. Don’t press on merely because the destination seems close.

A good visit here should be quiet and light-footed. The place may feel almost domestic in its pools of filtered daylight, but its real map is made of low seams, old repairs, moving water, and creatures that have learned to live within them. Go prepared to observe rather than rearrange—and leave it functioning as you found it.
