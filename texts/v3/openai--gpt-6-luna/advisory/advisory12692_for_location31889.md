## Before you go

- **Confirm where you will arrive.** Do not step through a portal directly into open water. Establish a verified dry staging point or arrive inside a pressure-rated vehicle. The pale surface may be far above, and the description gives no reliable depth or pressure.
- **Survey before sending people.** Use a remote vehicle to measure depth, temperature, current, water chemistry, visibility, and the condition of the structures. Map a small initial area and identify a return route. Do not assume that a seemingly shallow district connects safely to the next one.
- **Bring a team and a recovery plan.** At minimum, travel with a trained dive or submersible crew, a surface or gateway watch, and redundant communications and life-support equipment. Agree in advance on turnaround limits and what happens if contact is lost.
- **Use the right mode of travel.** For distances that could take days to cross, an ordinary diver is the wrong tool. Prefer a suitably rated submersible with independent life support, navigation, lights, and recovery capability. Any diving should be done only by people trained and equipped for the measured depth and conditions—not with improvised gear or casual snorkeling equipment.

## Equipment and personal safety

- **Plan for sustained cold.** Use an exposure suit appropriate to the measured temperature, plus spare thermal protection and a way to warm and dry yourself afterward. Cold can impair judgment and coordination before it feels dramatic.
- **Carry redundant life support.** Air supply, power, lights, navigation, and communications should have backups. If diving, use equipment and procedures matched to the actual depth, with a qualified team; do not guess at gas needs or decompression from appearances.
- **Expect darkness to erase landmarks.** Bring multiple independent lights, compass or inertial navigation, and a route-marking system. The plants’ faint glow may help distinguish shapes, but it is not dependable illumination.
- **Stay attached to a known route.** Use a reel, tether, or vehicle navigation log, and mark junctions. The avenues and arches may look regular enough to invite confidence, but silt and darkness can make neighboring passages hard to tell apart.
- **Protect yourself from the water and growth.** Keep skin and eyes covered until the water has been assessed. Do not drink it, eat the plants, or handle unfamiliar growth. Ribbonlike fronds and mats may snag equipment even if they are not dangerous in themselves.
- **Bring first aid and a recovery method.** Plan for cold injury, equipment failure, entanglement, and a lost or incapacitated traveler. The nearest safe exit may be far away.

## How to move through the place

Go slowly. Fine silt can settle over the paving and rise again if disturbed, turning an already dim passage opaque. Avoid kicking up sediment, pulling on fronds, or using old walls and pillars as handholds. Some structures may appear solid while slowly yielding beneath the water; a falling slab or collapsing arch could trap a route without much warning.

Keep clear of roofs, under-arches, and rooms whose supports cannot be inspected. Do not enter a confined passage unless you know it is flooded throughout, structurally stable, and has a mapped exit. A route that narrows gradually may become impassable before it looks dangerous.

The current is described as slow, but it crosses enormous distances. Check it locally, especially around breached walls and channels. A quiet flow can still carry you away from your planned route, and long, plant-filled corridors can make it hard to notice that you are drifting.

## Pace and expectations

Do not plan to “see the whole place.” Its scale is beyond a normal excursion, and slow conditions can make even a short route demanding. Set a modest objective—one basin, one mapped avenue, one return—and leave ample time for delays. The silence, limited light, repeated architecture, and lack of visible animal life may also be disorienting. Travel with a companion, check in regularly, and turn back while you are still comfortable and oriented.

The description gives no reason to expect hostile animals or sudden violence. It gives every reason to respect the water. The safest visitor is equipped to leave, careful not to disturb what they pass, and willing to treat each quiet stone corridor as an unknown environment rather than a familiar ruin.
