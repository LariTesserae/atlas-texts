## Before you go

Treat this as a **cold, wet, dark, biologically crowded world**, not as a rugged city break. The city is habitable, but its safety depends on continuous maintenance and on people respecting routes and rhythms that may be older than the buildings. Arrange a local host or licensed guide before arrival. Do not assume that a map, a marked street, or a familiar-looking machine will remain reliable: the terrain shifts, passages change, and some routes are deliberately shared with migrating animals.

Ask your host about current conditions immediately before departure:

- Which districts and walkways are open?
- Where are animals breeding, feeding, or migrating?
- Are any corridors temporarily reserved for them?
- Which surfaces, rails, or pipes are safe to touch?
- What signals mean “stop,” “leave the passage,” or “stay indoors”?
- How are local emergency messages sent—and how can a visitor receive them?

Learn the local alarms before you set out. Public warnings may travel as vibrations through rails rather than as sirens or spoken announcements. A translation device that only listens for voices could miss the most important instructions.

## Pack for water, cold, and poor footing

Bring layered, insulating clothing that remains warm when damp, plus a fully waterproof outer shell with sealed seams. A hood is more useful than an umbrella in narrow, windy walkways and heavy mist. Pack dry spare layers in waterproof bags; even when it is not visibly raining, water collects on nearly everything, and cloud can soak you simply by moving through it.

Wear grippy, ankle-supporting footwear with soles suited to slick stone and metal. Avoid smooth soles, delicate shoes, and anything you cannot afford to lose in a dark channel. Gloves should be warm and waterproof, but allow you to handle rails and fastenings securely. A compact tether or handline may be useful on approved routes; never attach one to a pipe, cable, or structural fitting without permission.

Bring:

- A headlamp with a low setting, spare batteries, and a way to shield its beam.
- A local navigation device or printed route information in a waterproof sleeve.
- A personal locator or emergency beacon compatible with the city’s systems.
- Ear protection that still lets you hear or feel local alerts.
- Any personal medication, in waterproof packaging, and a way to keep it within safe temperature limits.
- A small supply of familiar, sealed food and drinking water for delays—not as a substitute for local guidance.

Do not assume that a filter makes local water safe. The water may contain unfamiliar organisms or chemical products of the plateau’s geology. Drink only water that your host identifies as treated and potable. Likewise, do not touch or taste the chemical films around hot cracks, even if they look harmless or are harvested by the city.

## On arrival: slow down and orient yourself

The cloud and layered walkways can make distance and direction difficult to judge. Give yourself time to acclimate before attempting to explore independently. Ask which level you are on, where the nearest enclosed refuge is, and how to return there if visibility drops. Keep your guide or host in sight; agree on a meeting point in case a span closes or you are separated.

Expect a constant chill, damp surfaces, muted light, and sounds that are hard to locate. A noise may travel through a bridge or the rock rather than through the air. Do not follow a call, click, or vibration to investigate. It might be animal communication, a structural warning, or both.

Keep lights dim and directed downward. Bright beams can disturb animals, obscure other people’s low-light cues, or draw attention to a route that is meant to stay dark. Use local guide strips and doorway markings rather than trying to illuminate the whole street.

## Move as a guest in a shared habitat

The animals are not background scenery. They occupy the city’s ledges, cavities, channels, and undersides of bridges, and some migration routes pass through built areas. A passage that appears empty may be in use above, below, or inside its walls.

Follow local instructions on where to walk, when to wait, and when to retreat indoors. Give animals generous space. Never block a corridor, reach toward a creature, feed one, flush one out of a cavity, or shine a bright light into a mass of them. Do not cross a bridge, drainage channel, or open basin just because it looks passable; it may be an active route or structurally unsafe.

Some buildings use flexible skins and other features to let animals pass without entering inhabited rooms. Leave these intact. Do not close, cut, lean on, or test a barrier unless a local person tells you it is meant for that purpose. If an animal enters a human space, do not try to capture or drive it out. Move away, secure yourself in the designated refuge, and notify your host.

A good rule is simple: **if local residents pause, listen, or yield the way, do the same**.

## Respect the city’s machinery—and its unfinished ground

The city is held in place by adjustable supports, deep anchors, cables, water systems, and machinery whose function may not be obvious. A dark panel could be a sensor or a device affecting fog; a pipe may be part of a water, heat, or pressure system. Do not operate controls, climb supports, attach equipment to structures, or treat exposed infrastructure as a convenient handhold.

Use only maintained routes. The ground is actively forming and eroding: a familiar-looking shelf can end at a fresh fracture, and a street may cross terrain that is still rising beneath it. Keep behind barriers around raw rock faces, hot cracks, open water, and unfinished districts. If you see water emerging where it was not flowing before, hear a new crack or groan, or feel a walkway shift, report it and leave by the route your guide specifies. Do not wait to see whether it happens again.

Changes may be subtle rather than dramatic. A walkway’s incline, the height of a doorway, or the sound of a support can alter over time. Trust current local instructions over old maps, travel accounts, or your own sense that a route “looks the same.”

## Stay healthy and keep a refuge in mind

The combination of damp, cold, low visibility, unfamiliar organisms, and long delays can make small problems serious. Keep warm and dry where possible, take breaks indoors, and tell your host early if you become chilled, unsteady, short of breath, or disoriented. Ask in advance what medical facilities are available and how an injured visitor is moved through the city.

Avoid eating local biological material or collecting water, film, shells, or animal remains. Do not bring foreign food or organic material into shared spaces unless your host has approved it; unfamiliar substances may affect the local ecology. Follow local hygiene procedures even if they seem unusual. In a place where water and animals pass through the built environment, ordinary assumptions about what is clean, sealed, or separate may not apply.

Know the nearest enclosed room or refuge at all times. If visibility collapses, an animal movement begins, or an alarm sounds, do not press on to reach your destination. Go where your host has told you to go, keep with your group, and wait for instructions. Do not make a solo return journey through fog because the route seems familiar.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the city to feel less like a place humans have conquered than a place they have learned to inhabit cautiously. The air will seem close and cold; surfaces will be wet; the cloud will erase the far end of streets. At night, the animals’ rasping, clicking, and deep calls may make the whole settlement seem to vibrate. Human speech can feel strangely small.

That is not necessarily a sign that you are in danger. It is a sign that the city is shared, and that its ordinary sounds are not all made by people or machines. Travel slowly, keep your light low, follow local rhythms, and let the place tell you where not to go. The safest visitor is not the one who explores the most, but the one who can recognize when the city is asking them to wait.
