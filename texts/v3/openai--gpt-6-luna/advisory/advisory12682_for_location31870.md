Treat this place as an active geological event, not as a wilderness. The mountains are not simply dangerous terrain; they are still being made, and the ground beneath you may not keep the shape it had when you arrived.

**Do not land a person first.** Send a remote probe to measure pressure, atmosphere, gravity, temperature, radiation, and the rate of surface movement. The description tells you that the rock is warm and the air carries dust, but not whether a human can breathe there—or whether the heat, pressure, or radiation is survivable. If the measurements are uncertain, use a sealed, independently supplied pressure suit with thermal protection. A filter mask is no substitute for a sealed life-support system. Bring enough oxygen, cooling, and power to return with a generous reserve; there may be nowhere to shelter or replenish them.

Pack for a place with almost no usable natural cues. Carry redundant lamps, a headlamp, and a way to preserve night vision; the faint glows in the rock will not illuminate a route. Use lidar or another active mapping system, an inertial navigator, and your own portable beacons. Do not rely on stars, a visible horizon, or yesterday’s map. In that darkness, distance is deceptive—and a newly opened cleft can make a familiar route meaningless.

Move slowly and keep the excursion short. Scout each section with a drone or remote sensor before crossing it. Prefer low, broad ground over knife-edged ridges, steep ramps, and tilted slabs, but do not mistake any shelf for permanent ground. Keep well away from cliff edges, overhangs, and the fall line beneath pinnacles. Avoid fissures even if they look narrow and quiet: they may vent heat, shed unstable rock, or widen without warning. The warm surface and wavering air are reasons to use a thermal camera, not invitations to test the stone by hand.

A tether and a return beacon are useful, but neither makes the terrain safe. An anchor set into rock that is itself shifting may pull free; use tested, redundant anchors only where a probe has confirmed stable material. Mark your route with beacons that you can retrieve or replace, and assume that surface markers may be buried, tilted, or lost. Keep a remote vehicle nearby if possible, but do not trust it to cross ground that has not been surveyed.

Expect movement without a dramatic warning. A vibration may arrive through your boots before you hear it; a crack or settling slab may make little sound. Keep your visor down and protect your head. If the ground begins to move, do not run across unknown shelves. Get away from edges and falling stone if you can do so safely, then crouch in the clearest low area you can reach, shielded from debris but not beneath an overhang. Wait for the motion to stop, reassess by instruments, and be prepared to abort. A route that was safe minutes ago may no longer be safe.

Bring all water, food, medical supplies, and waste containers. There is no sign of water, breathable resources, or dependable shelter. The branching mineral growths should be treated as brittle and potentially hazardous: do not touch, crush, or collect them without a reason and proper containment. Likewise, do not enter or lean on the small stone constructions. Their purpose is unknown, and their age and stability are impossible to judge from appearance. They may be artifacts, natural accumulations, or something else entirely; in any case, they are not trustworthy shelters or anchors.

Before setting out, establish a strict turnaround time, continuous communications, and an automatic emergency beacon. Decide in advance what readings, ground movement, suit damage, or navigation failure will end the visit. Keep a partner or remote support team tracking your position and life-support reserve. If communications fail, the plan should be to return—not to continue exploring.

The most important preparation may be psychological. The place offers few landmarks, little sound, and almost no reassurance that anything is still. A pinnacle can lean and break with such slowness that you may not notice the danger until the terrain has changed. Do not let the quiet persuade you that the ground is settled. Visit briefly, observe from a distance, and leave before curiosity turns into a commitment.
