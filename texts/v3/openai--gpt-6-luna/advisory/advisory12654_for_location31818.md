## Prepare for an ocean, not a landscape

This is a submerged plateau with no reliable shore, no obvious settlement, and no promise that the high ground stays within a human-safe depth. Treat the entire visit as a deep-ocean expedition. The fact that daylight reaches the ridges does **not** mean a person can survive there without life support.

### Before departure

- **Survey the local physics and chemistry.** Confirm water pressure, temperature, salinity, acidity, dissolved gases, and any unusual properties of this universe’s ocean. Do not assume your home-world equipment, medicines, or materials will behave normally.
- **Establish a pressure plan.** Use a pressure-rated submersible or habitat appropriate to the deepest point you might encounter. A suit alone is not a substitute for a vehicle or shelter rated for the environment. Map safe limits in advance, and do not approach the plateau’s flanks without a clear depth and ascent plan.
- **Plan for persistent cold.** Bring redundant thermal control, insulated emergency shelter, and a way to keep batteries, seals, and medical supplies within their operating ranges. The rock and machinery will be cold too; assume nothing is warm merely because it is powered.
- **Bring independent life-support and power reserves.** Carry backup oxygen or breathing supply, carbon-dioxide removal, heat, lighting, navigation, and communications. Allow for delays: currents, equipment trouble, or a lost route could turn a short excursion into a long one.
- **Arrange a return route before you leave.** Use multiple navigation methods—acoustic beacons, inertial navigation, mapped ridgelines, and tether where practical. In exceptionally clear water, distant features may still be hard to judge for scale; visibility is not the same as easy navigation.
- **Check the installations from a distance first.** Their lights and engines may make them seem welcoming, but the description gives no reason to assume they are inhabited, safe, or still under human control. Learn which equipment is active, what it does, and whether approaching it could expose you to moving cables, machinery, electrical hazards, or unstable structures.

### Equipment worth bringing

A sturdy pressure-rated vehicle with excellent thrusters and precise low-speed handling is more useful than a fast one. The plateau is cut by sharp ridges, shelves, basins, and narrow passes; a vehicle that cannot hover, turn carefully, or retreat against a current is a liability. Protect its underside and intakes from fractured stone and debris.

Bring **directional lights**, but use them sparingly. The upper ridges receive diluted daylight, while lower slopes disappear into blue and then black. Strong lights can help reveal cracks and ledges, but they can also startle or disorient animals and flatten depth cues. A low-light camera, sonar, and a depth sounder will often tell you more than a brighter lamp.

For communication, carry acoustic equipment and expect delay, distortion, and confusing echoes. Sound travels through water and rock from directions that may be difficult to locate. Agree on short, unmistakable signals with your team; do not rely on ordinary speech or on being able to hear an alarm clearly over the surrounding noise.

Take a compact medical kit suited to pressure-related emergencies, cold exposure, and confinement. If your expedition includes any transition between different pressures, make the ascent and decompression schedule part of the mission plan rather than an afterthought.

### While exploring

Keep to the mapped high ground unless you have a specific reason and equipment to descend. The plateau’s broad shelves may feel spacious, but its edges fall away into a depth that can become difficult to interpret long before it becomes visible. Avoid crossing unfamiliar chasms or entering narrow cuts in strong flow. Currents concentrate around the pinnacles and through the passes; a place that looks calm from a distance may have powerful water moving through it.

Watch the water as well as the terrain. Shoals may arrive like a moving wall, dimming the light over an entire valley. Larger animals may pass below or beside you without warning, and small creatures can emerge suddenly from cracks. Give wildlife room and keep your vehicle out of their routes and gathering places. The clusters of shells, mats, and animals on the rocks are sparse but meaningful: they appear to mark currents and favored ledges, so avoid scraping or dislodging them.

The soundscape will take getting used to. Current over a ridge may be felt through the vehicle as a steady roar. Narrow passages can whistle or boom; shoals may produce a dense crackle of clicks; calls from large animals may seem to come through the rock itself. Old machinery adds another layer: pulses, turbines, and cables under strain. Do not assume that a noise is an approaching animal—or that a familiar mechanical rhythm means a machine is safe.

Keep a written or recorded track of your route, depth, current direction, and landmarks. The terrain is severe enough that a small navigation error could put you against a sharp face, into a dead-end basin, or too close to the plateau’s drop-off. Set strict turn-around times and return thresholds for power, life support, and visibility.

### What it may feel like

The first impression may be that the place is empty: cold blue water, pale stone, and machines scattered too far apart to form a community. Resist that impression. Life may be absent from one stretch of shelf and suddenly present in enormous numbers over the next. A shoal passing overhead could change the light and sound of the whole landscape at once.

There is little here that seems to hurry. Storms above arrive as pressure and distant sound; currents repeatedly follow the same stone channels; the machinery runs on without apparent interest in anyone visiting. That steadiness can be calming, but it can also make distance and danger feel abstract. A plateau that looks unchanged from one moment to the next may still contain strong currents, a lethal drop, or an active installation just beyond the light.

Travel with enough supplies to remain patient, but not so much confidence that you stop checking your instruments. Stay on the high ground, respect the water’s routes, and treat every return journey as part of the expedition.
