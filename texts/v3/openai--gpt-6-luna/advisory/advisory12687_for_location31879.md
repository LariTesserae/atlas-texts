Treat this as an **anomalous flooded cave inside an active, changing mountain**, not as a small underwater room. The chamber is cramped; the water is violent; the walls and water may not obey ordinary rules. If you cannot establish a reliable way in, a reliable way out, and a way to retrieve someone who gets stranded, **do not enter**. Send a remote instrument first—or admire it from the safe side of the arrival point.

### Before you go

- **Survey the approach and exit.** Identify the actual route into the pocket and the route back out. Do not assume the bright crack overhead is an air hole or an exit: bubbles vanish there, and the description gives no sign that it leads anywhere a person can breathe. Do not enter through an untested crack in newly forming rock.
- **Measure conditions remotely.** Use a tethered probe to check pressure, temperature, water chemistry, current strength, vibration, and whether instruments keep reliable time and position. Map the chamber more than once; a single survey may not describe a space that is still making and unmaking its seams.
- **Bring a specialist team.** This is not a solo dive. Keep a competent support person outside the pocket, with a proven retrieval plan. Agree on short time limits and simple abort signals before anyone enters. Acoustic communication may be useless in that roar, so bring a communication method that does not depend on sound.
- **Use equipment suited to unknown water and pressure.** A sealed, pressure-rated immersion suit and redundant breathing supply are essential. The water’s chemistry and temperature are unknown, and the pressure cannot be inferred from the chamber’s apparent size or altitude. Do not go until those conditions are measured and the equipment is approved for them. Hearing protection must be compatible with the suit and pressure; ordinary earplugs will not protect you from strong pressure pulses.
- **Keep everything compact and snag-resistant.** There is barely room to turn around. Avoid projecting gear, loose straps, bulky tools, and powerful thrusters that could stir grit or drive you into a wall. Secure every instrument to yourself. Do not count on a dropped object coming back promptly—or taking an ordinary path if it does.

### While inside

Enter slowly, one person at a time, and stay close to the tested route. Maintain a conservative connection to your support team, but do not lay a line across the chamber or assume it will remain a trustworthy guide: currents, shifting stone, and the pocket’s strange return paths could make a line snag or lead somewhere unexpected. Use independent position and time checks as well.

Keep your buoyancy controlled. A bubble that disappears into the upper fissure is not evidence that you can follow it safely. The water surges without an obvious source, and grit can turn a small movement into a sudden cloud that hides the room. Don’t swim hard against a current or chase anything through a gap. If your position or the exit becomes uncertain, stop, signal, and follow the agreed recovery procedure rather than improvising.

Expect the sound to be physically exhausting and disorienting. The chamber’s roar may drown out speech and mask changes in the water; its pulses and shudders may feel alarming even when nothing visibly moves. Use visual signals and watch your team’s prearranged time limits. Do not let the chamber’s apparent stillness between surges persuade you to stay longer.

Do not touch the crystal threads, the pale vegetation, or the translucent grazers. Avoid the warm plume and do not handle unfamiliar deposits. The mountain is actively changing, and loose grains, sharp fresh seams, and mineral crusts may be unstable or irritating. Most importantly, **leave the three stacked stones, the braided fiber, and the clay cup exactly as you found them**. They may be evidence of previous visitors or inhabitants; their purpose is unknown, and the currents conspicuously do not move them.

### Leave early

Turn back at the first sign of a stronger surge, a changed route, unexpected heat, loss of communication, equipment trouble, unusual timing, or a tremor that makes the walls move. Don’t collect souvenirs, and don’t test the pocket’s strange behavior with a person, a breathing line, or an essential piece of equipment. Objects may return to a hand after seconds—or after a day. That is a curiosity for an expendable probe, not a recovery plan.

The light may make the whole little chamber look invitingly clear. Don’t mistake visibility for safety. This is a small space with the force and noise of a much larger one, held inside a mountain that has not finished becoming.
