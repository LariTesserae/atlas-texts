Treat the basin as a living surface, not as empty ground. Its animals occupy nearly every hollow, and their migrations have worn the terrain into a network of routes you may not be able to recognize. The safest visit would begin at a distance.

**Before descending**

- **Survey the atmosphere and water first.** Do not assume the air is breathable or that the rain is safe. Test for gases, pressure, corrosive aerosols, and biological particles; keep your suit sealed until the results are clear. Do not drink or wash in the basin’s water.
- **Send quiet, remote scouts ahead.** Map the hollows, cracks, seepage, animal channels, and sheltered cuts. Look for a place to land that does not lie across a migration route or a known gathering hollow. If the ground is too crowded to approach without crushing life, observe from above or remain in a sealed, elevated vehicle.
- **Prepare for biological contact even if you never touch anything.** Use a sealed suit with independent air, protected joints, and redundant filtration. Bring a clean inner layer and a strict decontamination plan for the return journey. Nothing from the basin—water, specimens, dust, equipment—should leave quarantine casually.

**What to wear and carry**

- A **fully sealed, abrasion-resistant suit** with a smooth exterior. Fine crawlers and winged animals may investigate seams, vents, and straps; don’t give them easy footholds or entry points.
- **Low-light vision and passive mapping**, such as lidar or a low-power depth sensor. The dimness is part of the place’s normal condition. Bright lamps may disorient animals, provoke a mass flight, or draw attention to you. If a lamp is necessary, use the weakest shielded beam that works and point it at the ground.
- **Serious hearing protection** with reliable communication and an external sound monitor. The chorus may be more than unpleasant: in enclosed parts of the basin, reflected sound could make it difficult to hear warnings, judge direction, or stay oriented. Agree on hand signals before you enter.
- **Stable footing and fall protection.** Wet stone, salt, polished grooves, submerged cracks, and bodies moving underfoot make ordinary walking hazardous. Use a tether, a route marker, and a partner who can keep you in sight. Avoid stepping into water-filled pans: they are active feeding and breeding sites, not puddles.
- **Temperature monitoring.** Some ridges are cold enough that the resident animals avoid them. Those may offer a quieter route, but they may also chill a human quickly or conceal slick, brittle, or fractured stone.

**While you are there**

Move slowly and keep your movements predictable. Sudden light, noise, vibration, or a fall could send the wing-cloud up and disturb the animals below it. The larger creatures may not be hunting you specifically, but their slow, heavy movement and open, sifting mouths are hazards enough; give them a wide berth and never put yourself between them and a hollow, channel, or gathering place.

Do not touch, feed, collect, or step on the animals. Do not put bare equipment into seepage or scrape the dark films from the stone. The basin’s food web is built from tiny microbial growth, waste, shed skins, and the animals that consume them; a seemingly small disturbance could alter a feeding site or carry unfamiliar organisms somewhere they do not belong. Keep all food, waste, and chemical products sealed.

Expect the landscape to be hard to read. In the low, flat light, color will tell you little. Watch for movement, but don’t stare into the faint blue glimmers as if they were landmarks: they may be living bodies close to the ground, not lights in the distance. Use your instruments and your marked route rather than trying to navigate by sight. The sound may blur individual calls into one continuous pressure, so check in with your companion on a fixed schedule instead of relying on shouted conversation.

Rain is not a reliable source of water. It comes briefly, gathers in familiar hollows, and then drains away or evaporates. If rain begins, don’t assume it is harmless relief: animals will be drinking, bathing, feeding, and laying eggs in the new pools, and their activity may intensify quickly. Take shelter only in a surveyed location—not beneath an overhang occupied by hanging animals or dripping with condensation.

**Plan your exit before you arrive.** Set a time limit, a check-in procedure, and a clear abort signal for suit damage, disorientation, rising animal activity, or failed communications. Leave by the same surveyed route if it remains safe. On return, quarantine the suit and every object that entered the basin; check for attached organisms before anything is brought into a shared habitat or vehicle.

The most important preparation may be accepting that this is not a quiet wilderness. It is an ecosystem whose ordinary movement fills the ground and the air. A good visitor should aim to be small, sealed, slow, and temporary—and be willing to experience most of the basin from a distance.
