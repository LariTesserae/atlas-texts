## Prepare for a submerged world, not a dive

The first fact to plan around is that **there is no dry ground**. The ridges, settlement, and highest towers are all underwater; the bright surface overhead is distant and unreachable by an ordinary swim. Treat this as a visit to an underwater habitat with an uncertain environment—not as a scuba excursion.

### Before you go

- **Establish the depth and pressure profile at the arrival point.** Do not emerge from a portal, vehicle, or transfer chamber into open water until pressure, temperature, salinity, and currents have been measured. A normal diving suit is not enough for an unknown depth. Use a certified pressure suit or a pressure-controlled vehicle, with redundant life support and a reliable emergency return route.
- **Bring your own breathable atmosphere.** Carry independent oxygen, carbon-dioxide removal, power, and reserve supplies. Do not assume the water is breathable, that local air pockets exist, or that the settlement can support your equipment.
- **Make the first destination a sealed shelter.** Arrange permission and a safe route to a pressure-controlled room or visitor chamber before arrival. If none is available, remain in a sealed vehicle until a safe procedure has been agreed with the locals.
- **Check materials and seals for heat and chemistry.** Hot seams, mineral-rich water, and suspended ash may damage unfamiliar seals, filters, lenses, or exposed electronics. Use equipment rated for the measured conditions and bring spare filtration and sealing components.
- **Agree on contact and rescue signals.** Sound may travel unusually far through water and stone, while currents or terrain may muffle it locally. Have a visual or wired backup, a buddy system, and a fixed rendezvous point.

### What to bring

- A pressure-rated suit or vehicle with **independent, redundant life support**.
- A compact emergency beacon and a locator that does not depend on seeing the surface.
- Depth, temperature, current, and pressure instruments; a compass or inertial navigation backup; and a map of the agreed route.
- Low-intensity, adjustable lights. The water’s illumination shifts unpredictably, and bright beams may disturb residents or obscure your own dark adaptation.
- A tether or route markers for unfamiliar passages—but use only markers the settlement approves. The terrain and its pathways change over time.
- A small medical kit suitable for pressure-related injury, cold or heat exposure, and possible contamination. Keep your suit’s interior clean and do not open it casually.

### Moving through the landscape

Go slowly, and stay on routes confirmed as stable. The plateau is still forming: its rounded stone and pale ash are not dependable ledges, and new shelves may be softer or more fragile than they look. Root mats and plant-bound banks can appear solid while shifting under load. Old steps may end in water; a passage that was passable before may have filled with silt or become deeper.

Keep clear of the hot seams and the cloudy mineral plumes around them. Do not rest on unfamiliar ground, wedge equipment into cracks, or cut through vegetation to make a shortcut. Plants and rootlike cords help hold the slopes together; damaging them could alter a path or destabilize a small bank.

Visibility may be poor even where the water looks clear. Dark channels, dense groves, shifting shadows, and suspended sediment make distance difficult to judge. Navigate by instruments and agreed landmarks rather than by “up” or by the apparent brightness of the water. The silver surface is not a useful compass, and the light can make a valley seem brighter than the ridge above it.

Use propulsion sparingly. Strong jets, loud machinery, and unnecessary sonar could stir ash, damage soft growth, or carry sound through the stone in ways you cannot predict. The inhabitants shape their surroundings by tying, fitting, and waiting; a visitor should avoid treating the place as a space to be cleared or engineered.

### Visiting the settlement

Approach the residents as hosts, not as specimens or guides. Their buildings are woven into a living, changing terrain. Ask before entering chambers, touching bridges, collecting plants or minerals, or using lights and acoustic equipment. A walkway that looks abandoned may be under repair—or may be part of the structure holding a bank together.

Do not assume that the settlement’s inhabitants breathe the same medium you do, share your sense of personal space, or interpret sound and light as you do. Arrange communication and safety signals in advance, and make it clear how your suit works and how to assist you without compromising it.

Carry food and water sufficient for the visit. Do not eat local organisms, drink local water, or bring biological material back through your transfer point without explicit clearance. The translucent vent creatures, moss, and other growths may be sensitive to contamination even if they appear abundant.

### Pace and expectations

Plan for a **slow visit**. The landscape changes over seasons and lifetimes, not in dramatic bursts, but passages can still shift, fill, or become unsafe. Build extra time into every trip, keep a record of your route, and check conditions again on the return journey.

The place may feel quiet, but it is not empty: water moves through its ravines, stone carries sound, plants make new ground, and the settlement is continually adapting. Let your equipment be quiet, your movements deliberate, and your footprint small. Above all, do not mistake the distant brightness overhead for an exit. The safe way out is the one you arranged before entering.
