## Before you go

Treat the High Quiet as an uninhabited wilderness, not as a city with convenient shelter. Its buildings look purposeful, but nothing suggests that they are maintained, safe, or meant for visitors. There is no dependable water, visible power, transport, or sign of local help.

**Do not begin the descent until you have checked the atmosphere.** The air is described as thin; that could mean anything from difficult high-altitude breathing to an atmosphere a human cannot safely tolerate. Bring instruments to measure pressure and breathable gases, plus appropriate respiratory support. If the readings are outside your equipment’s safe range, use a sealed, pressure-capable suit—or do not leave your arrival point. A mask alone will not help if the problem is inadequate pressure or oxygen.

Bring everything you need for the entire stay, with reserves for a delayed return:

- **Water and food:** There is no visible liquid water. The white deposits are crystal, not snow, and should not be treated as drinkable or edible. Bring a generous reserve and a way to ration it.
- **Cold-weather protection:** Use insulating layers, wind protection, gloves, and footwear suited to abrasive powder and rough stone. Pack backup layers; the dim, steady light may make it hard to judge how long you have been exposed.
- **Dust protection:** Fine gray dust is likely to get into eyes, lungs, and equipment. Carry sealed eye protection, respiratory protection compatible with the atmosphere, and ways to keep supplies and suit seals clean.
- **Medical and emergency equipment:** Include a first-aid kit, communications, lighting, repair materials, and a way to shelter from exposure. Test equipment before arrival. Do not assume the city will provide power, signal, or usable materials.
- **A reliable return plan:** Keep your means of leaving independent of any route through the city. If your arrival point or return mechanism can be monitored, check it often. Do not assume that a visible landmark will remain in the same place relative to you.

Tell someone outside the location when you expect to return. Agree on a response to missed check-ins. The High Quiet offers few obvious ways to summon help.

## On arrival and descent

Pause at the rim. Check the atmosphere, temperature, communications, and your return plan before committing to the descent. The apparent closeness of the ranges is misleading: distances open up as you travel, and the descent leads through immense steps and cliffs. Use a conservative route and avoid overhangs, cliff edges, and slopes where dust or stone may move. Thin sheets of rock can detach intact before crumbling; do not stand beneath exposed faces just because they look solid.

Expect travel to take longer than the view suggests. Keep enough supplies to turn back, and set limits on how far you will go before you start—not after fatigue, cold, or disorientation have made the decision for you. Stay together. In a place where echoes and apparent distances are unreliable, a companion can notice changes in your condition even when the landscape is giving you poor feedback.

## Moving through the city

Do not navigate by distance, direction, or a single remembered route. A stair may lead somewhere lower than it appears to; an avenue may draw a gate close, then leave it behind you when you turn away. Shadows, echoes, and familiar-looking views are not dependable guides.

Instead:

- Learn **neighborhoods and features**—for example, a distinctive court, arch, or arrangement of towers—and note how they look from several viewpoints.
- Keep a written log, but treat it as a record of what you observed, not as a guaranteed map.
- Make a fresh plan for each leg of travel. The description suggests that districts may retain their own arrangement, while routes between them do not reliably repeat.
- Check your position frequently against your departure point and your intended return method. Do not let a successful-looking route persuade you to go farther than your supplies allow.
- Avoid entering doorways, climbing stairs, or crossing bridges unless you can assess the landing and exit. Some openings face sheer drops; some structures may no longer meet where they appeared to.

Ordinary navigation tools may still be useful, but do not rely on them alone. Keep time with a device you brought, while remembering that local day and night may be hard to identify. The sun’s position and the light level are unreliable cues.

## In the buildings and on the stone

The city’s emptiness is not proof that it is inert. Walls, bridges, and windows appear to change position over time, while cliffs and ridges slowly weather and migrate. Prefer broad, open, stable-looking ground to narrow ledges and suspended structures. Avoid making a shelter in a place that depends on an overhang, a single bridge, or a wall that could shift relative to its neighbors.

Leave the architecture and the small life alone. Pale creatures cling beneath bridges, and branching mineral patterns appear to respond to warmth, pressure, or living passage. Do not collect, feed, disturb, or touch them unless there is a compelling emergency reason. Avoid carving permanent marks or taking material from the city; it may be fragile, responsive, or simply impossible to understand. Use temporary, non-damaging markers only if necessary, and never trust that they will stay where you left them.

Be careful around the dust. It can hide uneven ground, collect in streets, or form patterns that might look meaningful. Do not assume a clear avenue will remain clear, or that dust-free ground is safer.

## Look after your mind as well as your body

The High Quiet seems likely to make a traveler doubt ordinary senses: distances mislead, echoes arrive at the wrong time, and familiar places may no longer line up with memory. The vast empty districts and persistent silence could make that disorientation more unsettling than any single physical hazard.

Use a buddy system. Check one another’s breathing, warmth, alertness, and confidence about where you are. Say your observations aloud and compare them, rather than silently trusting one person’s impression of a route or landmark. Schedule regular pauses to consult your notes and supplies. If your group cannot agree on what it sees, stop somewhere safe instead of pressing onward.

Most of all, resist the urge to interpret the city’s activity as an invitation. A door closing somewhere in the quiet is not evidence that someone is waiting for you. The safest visit is a short, well-supplied survey with a clear return plan—not an open-ended exploration of a place that can alter the meaning of “near,” “back,” and “the same way.”
