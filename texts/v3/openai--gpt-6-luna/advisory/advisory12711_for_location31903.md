Treat this as a **living, heat-active cave**, not an empty chamber with animals in it. The main dangers may be subtle: heat accumulating under the roof, unstable mineral crust, unseen animals beneath every surface, and movement or vibration that travels through the whole basin. An ordinary visitor should observe from a prepared position at the edge—not enter the hollow.

### Before you go

- **Check the air and the rock remotely.** Use a probe or remote instrument to measure temperature, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and any volcanic or mineral gases before sending anyone inside. “Warm” rock does not guarantee breathable air. Map heat seams, loose crust, and the safest route back out.
- **Go with a trained guide and a small team.** Agree on a quiet signal system, a turn-back time, and what to do if the animals begin moving. Keep an independent line to the exit; do not rely on the old chamber or its narrow doorway.
- **Use protective, low-contamination equipment.** Wear sealed, heat-tolerant footwear and clothing that can be cleaned or quarantined afterward. Bring eye protection and respiratory protection appropriate to the air and dust readings. Avoid loose straps, dangling gear, strong scents, and anything that sheds fibers or debris.
- **Carry your own water and essentials in sealed containers.** Do not pour water, food, sweat-soaked cloth, or waste onto the floor. The place has an ecology built around tiny residues; even a small offering—or spill—could become part of it.

### While there

- **Stay on a surveyed, stable viewing point.** The pale crust may look solid but could cover softer layers and crowded cracks. Never test it with a foot or hand. Keep well away from the floor, walls, and warm seams unless a local expert has confirmed a route is safe.
- **Minimize vibration and sudden movement.** Walk slowly, keep equipment from knocking against stone, and do not tap, clap, or call out. The animals seem to sense contact through the rock; a careless footfall may be the equivalent of shouting into a room full of listeners.
- **Use the least intrusive light possible.** The hollow is dark, but powerful lamps may disturb animals that rely on subtle cues. Start with dim, indirect, shielded light; increase it only if necessary, and never shine directly into a mass of eyes.
- **Do not touch, feed, collect, or try to move anything.** A shell, scale, or “empty” crevice may be part of a living passage or shelter. Do not test whether a creature is alive. Do not step over a cluster or put a hand against the basalt to feel the vibrations.
- **Watch the crowd, not just individual animals.** If bodies begin leaning toward a seam, the crust rises and settles, a wall veil starts descending, or movement changes direction, retreat calmly along the route you came in. Do not run, scatter, or try to cross the flow. Give it room.
- **Treat the old constructions as occupied.** Their human shape does not make them safe or abandoned. The dense nest behind the open doorway is a reason to stay out, not an invitation to investigate. Do not enter, block, or illuminate it closely.

The place may feel quiet, but it is not still. Stay only as long as conditions remain predictable, keep the exit in view, and leave before fatigue or fascination makes you careless. On departure, clean or isolate your gear and follow the guide’s instructions about decontamination. The safest visit is a brief one from the margin: enough to understand that the hollow is listening, not enough to make it answer.
