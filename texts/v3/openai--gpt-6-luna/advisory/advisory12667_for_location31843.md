Treat this as a **small, active geological process**, not as a safe little valley. The hollow is only a few hundred steps across, but the ground is changing, visibility is poor, and the structures appear to be part of whatever is making it change. Your best visit is a short, cautious one with a clear way back out.

### Before you enter

- **Check your health and limits.** The ridge is high, and the hollow is warm, wet, and misty. Be rested, hydrated, and prepared for altitude and damp cold or heat shifts. If you have asthma, heart or respiratory problems, or poor balance, reconsider.
- **Bring waterproof layers and reliable traction.** Use grippy, ankle-supporting boots; a helmet; eye protection; and gloves. The stone is slick, newly exposed, and sharply edged. Keep electronics and essential supplies in waterproof cases.
- **Carry ordinary navigation redundancies:** a compass, a physical map or route sketch, and a bright visual marker system. Sound is unreliable here, so don’t depend on echoes, calls, or audio beacons to find your way.
- **Plan an exit and a turnaround rule.** Tell someone outside the site when you expect to return. Agree on a missed-check-in plan. Don’t enter if cloud, wind, or conditions on the ridge make the return route uncertain.

### Moving through the hollow

Stay near the rim at first and look for a stable route before descending. The dry, wind-scoured edge may be unpleasant, but it is likely easier to navigate than the wet floor. Don’t assume yesterday’s safe path—or even a path you crossed earlier today—still exists. Channels can disappear, fresh ridges can rise, and water may be redirected.

Move slowly, test every foothold, and avoid walking on pale seams, fresh-looking ridges, or the edges of warm cracks. Don’t put your hands into fissures or beneath shelves. The water is warm, but its temperature and contents are unknown; treat it as potentially scalding or irritating, and **don’t drink it**. Keep a generous distance from the narrow towers, arch, and planted rods. They may be measuring or influencing the ground, and there is no way to know whether touching, leaning on, or obstructing them would be harmless.

The floor can change with little warning. If the rods pulse, if the shelves begin to shiver, or if you hear a deep roar building, **stop exploring and retreat toward your established exit**—without running over the slick stone. Don’t wait to watch the ridges rise. Once the movement and noise stop, give the area time to settle before deciding whether to leave; newly formed stone and channels may still be unstable.

### Protect yourself from the life and the place

The small animals seem accustomed to the structures, not necessarily to visitors. Avoid handling them, disturbing nests, blocking water channels, or reaching under shelves. The larger animal heard beneath the stone is a reason not to investigate openings, however tempting. Don’t touch the rust-colored fans: they respond to shadow, and their effects are unknown. A loose veil or clothing held close to the body is safer than waving fabric around them.

Wear a well-fitting mask or respirator suitable for fine mist and airborne particles, especially if you are sensitive to mold, spores, or unfamiliar biological material. Seal cuts and wash exposed skin after leaving. Follow your multiverse’s usual contamination precautions: don’t bring local organisms or water away, and don’t introduce foreign food, waste, or microbes. Avoid collecting samples unless you have appropriate authorization and containment.

### Sound, visibility, and communication

Keep visual contact with your companion rather than calling to one another. Sound may seem to come from the wrong direction, and droplets can ring like bells; this could make a nearby disturbance hard to interpret. Use agreed hand signals and simple, redundant light signals—but don’t shine bright lights into animals or stare into the blue-flashing pools. In the mist, keep within easy sight of your partner and the route marker. If you lose sight of either, stop; don’t search by following a sound.

### A sensible visit

Go with at least one companion, carry a first-aid kit, water, food, a headlamp with spare batteries, and a dry insulating layer. Keep the visit brief. Observe from a stable, marked route, take notes or photographs from a distance, and leave the hollow as you found it. Don’t camp on the floor or shelter under the shelves.

The place sounds beautiful, but not settled. Think of it as visiting a landscape that is still being assembled—one that may be responsive in ways you cannot yet read. The safest attitude is curiosity at a distance: watch the changes, don’t provoke them, and be ready to leave before the hollow decides to change again.
