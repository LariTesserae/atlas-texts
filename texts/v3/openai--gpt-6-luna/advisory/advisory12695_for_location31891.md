Treat this as a living cave, not a patch of ground with plants growing on it. The central hazard is not an animal attack; it is that the hollow may gradually absorb anything left in contact with it. Your safest visit is brief, quiet, and deliberately untidy in nothing but your own notes.

### Before entering

- **Do not assume the atmosphere is breathable.** Bring a sealed breathing system with its own oxygen supply, carbon-dioxide removal, and independent monitoring. Test pressure and gas composition at the threshold before committing. The cold, saturated air could make an ordinary respirator inadequate.
- **Dress for wet cold.** Wear insulated, waterproof clothing inside a sealed outer layer, with redundant hand and foot protection. Cold water and constant damp can rob heat without dramatic warning. Carry a reserve heat source, but keep it regulated and shielded: unnecessary warmth could disturb a system whose red threads appear to circulate something heat-like.
- **Make your gear difficult to incorporate.** Use smooth, nonporous, tethered equipment; avoid loose straps, fuzzy fabric, exposed foam, adhesives, and dangling cables. Inspect seams and joints before and after contact. Keep supplies inside sealed containers, and don’t set anything down “just for a moment.”
- **Plan for a living, slippery floor.** The leaf mat is not soil, and its ridges may conceal pools or soft hollows. Bring a compact load-spreading platform or stepping panels that can be retrieved intact. A tether to a partner or fixed point is sensible, but do not anchor it by cutting, pinning, or forcing anything into the vegetation.
- **Prepare for disorientation.** Carry independent timekeeping, a simple compass or inertial navigation aid, and a physical route plan. Electronic or magnetic instruments may behave unpredictably near the resonance; no single instrument should be trusted.
- **Establish a short visit window and an outside observer.** Agree on check-in times, a return deadline, and a retrieval plan that does not depend on you leaving footprints. Have the observer track your tether and elapsed time. Do not enter alone.

### While inside

Move slowly and keep to the exposed stone at the hollow’s edge where practical, while remembering that wet stone may be as slick as the plant mat. Test each step. Avoid leaning on the walls or roof: roots cross the whole enclosure, and a surface that looks like a wall may be active tissue.

Keep lights dim and steady. Do not use flashes, loud signals, or bright heat lamps. The pearl-colored bulbs respond to falling drops, and the vegetation reacts to minute changes; a dripping suit, leaking canister, or repeated footfall may be more disruptive than it seems. Check for condensation on your own equipment and contain it rather than letting it fall.

Do not touch, feed, collect, cut, or taste anything. The pale animals, translucent fronds, red threads, and pools are all part of an unfamiliar ecosystem. Even a harmless-looking sample could carry organisms in either direction—or prompt the hollow to grow around you. Keep tools sheathed and use cameras or remote probes for close observation.

Take care around the pools. They may be shallow, but their edges are changing, and blind swimmers or other life could be difficult to see. Avoid splashing: water is part of the hollow’s circulation, and its droplets may be meaningful signals. If you notice a creature passing through a wall, do not follow or obstruct it.

If the deep resonance begins, stop moving and secure yourself without attaching anything to the living tissue. It may be a natural cycle, but the revealed geometry suggests that distance and orientation could become unreliable. Mark your current position using your own instruments, keep the exit tether under tension, and wait for the event to fade unless staying put is itself unsafe. Do not attempt to cross newly apparent paths or “shortcuts.” What looks close may not be reachable in the ordinary sense.

### Leaving

Leave by the route you entered, carrying every panel, tether, and tool back out. Check that nothing has been softened, overgrown, or caught in the floor. If gear is partly incorporated, do not tear it free: withdraw yourself first and treat the lost item as contaminated or irretrievable.

Outside, quarantine your clothing, equipment, and samples—ideally without taking any samples at all. Inspect for plant material and small animals, and decontaminate according to a plan chosen before entry. Do not assume that an organism or residue harmless to you is harmless elsewhere.

The hollow does not seem malicious. It simply does not recognize the boundary between its own living structure and an object resting inside it. Prepare accordingly: stay warm, sealed, tethered, quiet, and easy to remove.
