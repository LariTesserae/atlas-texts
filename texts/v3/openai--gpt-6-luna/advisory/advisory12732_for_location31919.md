Treat this as a **working, unstable underground neighborhood**, not as a cave attraction. The machinery and walkways make it possible to visit, but they do not make the ground, water, air, or route dependable.

## Before you go

- **Arrange the visit with the residents or site operators.** Go with someone who knows the current walkways, pump cycles, safe rooms, and exit route. Ask what areas are closed and what warning signs mean. Do not assume yesterday’s map is still accurate: channels widen, ledges slump, and pipes may be moved.
- **Get a current hazard briefing.** In particular, ask about flooding, pump failure, changing water levels, hot surfaces, electrical equipment, and any recent collapse or seepage. Agree on a turnaround time and what to do if you are separated from your guide.
- **Check the air rather than guessing.** A place can feel warm and inhabited yet still have unsafe oxygen levels or pockets of carbon dioxide, methane, or other gases. Bring a recently checked, continuously reading gas monitor if the local operators cannot provide reliable monitoring. A particulate mask does **not** protect against bad air or low oxygen; don’t enter if the atmosphere has not been assessed.
- **Plan for a fast return to the surface.** Know exactly how you leave, carry a reliable communication method, and make sure someone outside knows your route and return time. A portal or multiverse transit point should be tested and kept accessible—not left on the far side of an uncertain passage.

## Wear and carry

- **A properly fitted helmet with a chin strap.** The ceiling is low and irregular, and small fragments are already falling.
- **Sturdy, grippy, waterproof footwear.** Expect slick grates, wet concrete, soft clay, and loose gravel. Use the raised walkways; do not wade into channels or step onto untested banks.
- **Gloves and eye protection.** Pipes and metalwork may be hot, oily, sharp, or coated in wet residue. Avoid using your hands to steady yourself on machinery or pipe bundles.
- **Light clothing that dries quickly**, with a light waterproof layer available. The air is warm and saturated; heavy rain gear can trap heat and sweat. Carry drinking water and electrolytes in sealed containers. Do not drink from the channels, however clear they look.
- **A dependable headlamp plus a spare light and batteries.** The amber lamps leave deep shadows. Use a steady beam and keep it aimed at the path; flashing or sweeping lights may disturb residents and animals.
- **A small first-aid kit in a waterproof pouch**, including supplies for cuts and blisters. Cover broken skin before entering. Ask about locally relevant disease and vaccination precautions rather than assuming the animals or water are harmless.
- **Hearing protection that still lets you hear instructions and alarms.** The pumps are loud, while echoes make it difficult to tell where sounds are coming from. Agree on hand signals with your guide.

## While you are there

Stay on the walkways and raised pads. The dark water can hide depth, moving animals, loose grates, or a soft edge ready to give way. Keep clear of the channel margins, ceiling cracks, new sediment, and any section that looks freshly wet or recently disturbed.

Do not touch valves, cables, pumps, rails, or pipes. The machinery is maintaining the district’s conditions; an apparently minor adjustment could change water levels, heat, or power. Don’t block grates or drains, and don’t lean over an outlet to inspect it. If a pump’s rhythm changes, water begins rising, a walkway shifts, or the air monitor alarms, follow the host’s evacuation instructions immediately. Do not stop to investigate.

Leave the animals alone. Their distribution is part of how the inhabitants read the hidden heat and water flow, and an animal that seems small or passive may still bite, cling, or carry unfamiliar microbes. Keep food, rubbish, and samples sealed. Don’t collect organisms, scrape growths, or leave anything behind.

Move slowly and stay close enough to your guide to see them clearly. Sounds carry strangely here: a clink may seem distant, while a large engine may seem right behind you. Use agreed signals rather than trying to navigate by sound. Keep your route and exit in mind; beyond the last walkway there is no useful “further in,” only low rock and uncertain darkness.

## Expect the atmosphere

The difficult part may not be the heat alone, but its persistence: wet air, warm stone, animal musk, hot iron, and machinery that never quite stops. There is no clear night, and the overlapping clicks and mechanical knocks can make the whole district seem to be moving. Take breaks before you feel overheated, watch for dizziness, nausea, headache, confusion, or unusual fatigue, and leave at the first sign of heat illness or breathing difficulty.

A short, escorted visit is the sensible first visit. The district is still coming into being; behave as though its paths and systems may change while you are there, because they may.
