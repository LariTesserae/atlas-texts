## Preparing for the Bell Pocket

Treat the Bell Pocket less like a ruin to explore and more like a small, sealed environment whose habits have lasted longer than anyone can remember. It is compact, but compact does not mean easy: the lanes are cramped, the floor is wet, and the chamber’s sounds can make empty passages seem occupied.

### Before you enter

- **Confirm the route back out.** The chamber is buried beneath a hillside, and its description gives no reliable entrance, exit, or ventilation route. Don’t go in unless someone outside knows where you are, how long you expect to be gone, and what to do if you miss a check-in. Carry a backup light and a way to mark your return path without damaging the city.
- **Check the air rather than trusting the atmosphere.** Cool, damp air can still be poorly ventilated. Bring an atmospheric monitor appropriate to your equipment and body, and leave if it signals danger or you feel dizzy, short of breath, confused, or unusually tired.
- **Dress for cold wet stone.** Wear sturdy, grippy footwear; warm layers that retain insulation when damp; and gloves that still let you handle a line or map. A helmet is sensible beneath the low ceiling and around narrow stairs. Keep electronics and spare batteries in waterproof cases.
- **Bring a reliable light, but don’t expect it to reveal everything.** The chamber has a faint, source-less dimness, not dependable illumination. Use a headlamp and a backup, but avoid flooding the place with harsh light if you can; it may make the close lanes harder to read and will erase the subtle differences that help you orient yourself.
- **Carry a simple, quiet navigation kit.** A hand-drawn plan, a compass if it behaves normally in your universe, and a non-damaging way to mark junctions are better than relying on echoes or a device that assumes ordinary architecture. Keep a low-tension safety line to your entry point if the route permits.

### While inside

Move slowly and keep one hand free. The lanes are narrow enough that you may have to turn sideways, and bridges, steps, and floor channels can be slick. Test unfamiliar footing before committing your weight. Don’t climb the stacked houses or lean on old-looking ledges just because they appear solid; centuries of settled damage are not the same as a safety guarantee.

Pay special attention to the **water level and the channels**. The pool is shallow-looking and apparently stable, but its depth, its connection to the hidden rush behind the western wall, and the strength of any covered channel are unknown. Avoid stepping into dark water or reaching into cracks. Don’t drink from the pool or gutters unless it has been tested for your biology and your equipment. Keep your hands away from the hollow bowl: its regular note is part of the place’s character, and striking it could disturb the sound, the stone, or something you don’t understand.

The sound will be a poor guide. A footstep can return from an empty lane; a whisper can come back sounding like a low voice; water behind a wall may seem closer or farther away than it is. **Don’t follow a sound just because it seems to answer you.** Use sight, touch, your map, and the route you have already confirmed. If you need to communicate, agree on simple signals before separating—and better yet, don’t separate.

Avoid forcing doors, opening plugged arches, moving masonry, or prying up paving stones. The city may be empty, but it is not inert: water travels through it, and the arrangement seems unusually stable. A small alteration could redirect a leak, dislodge a stone, or change the acoustics in a way that makes finding your way out harder.

There is little or no plant life, but the rare translucent creature beneath a loose stone should be treated as wildlife, not debris. Don’t handle it, trap it, or turn stones over looking for more. Leave the basins and channels undisturbed, too. They may have been built for inhabitants that are absent now, or for a purpose that is no longer obvious.

### How to behave

The Bell Pocket may feel like a city waiting for its people to return. Let that impression make you careful, not curious in a reckless way. Speak softly; don’t mistake an echo for a reply. Take photographs or notes from the paths, but leave nothing behind and take nothing away. If the familiar rhythm changes—if the pool rises, the drip stops, a passage sounds different, or the dimness shifts—stop exploring, check your bearings, and return by the route you know.

Plan a short visit, with a firm turn-around time. The chamber’s most unsettling quality is not that it is obviously dangerous; it is that it seems to keep every drop and every sound in its appointed place. A visitor should not assume that a place so still is harmless—or that their own disturbance will be easily undone.
