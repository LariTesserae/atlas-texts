## Before you go

Treat the uplands as a remote wilderness, not as an empty town you can comfortably explore. The settlements and roads may look durable, but they offer no dependable services, inhabitants, food, or rescue. Bring everything you need to survive and to leave.

The most important preparation is for **cold, damp exposure and unreliable navigation**. The plateau is broad, the weather local, and distances deceptive. A place that looks close may take far longer to reach than expected; a road or landmark may not lead where your map says it should.

### Pack for the plateau, not the picture

- **Dress in layers** for cold, wind, and persistent damp. Bring waterproof outerwear, warm insulating layers, spare dry socks and gloves, and a way to keep sleeping gear dry. Wet stone, fog, and cold ground can drain heat even when the weather seems mild.
- **Bring a proper tent and ground insulation.** Do not count on finding an intact roof, a working cistern, or a dry place in the settlements. A roofless house may still shelter you from wind, but it is not a substitute for dependable gear.
- **Carry all food and drinking water**, plus a reserve. There is no sign of animal life, and the plants should be treated as inedible unless independently identified. The channels, pools, and mineral-scented water are not automatically safe to drink. Filter and disinfect any water you collect.
- **Take sturdy, grippy footwear** and a repair kit. Expect wet rock, uneven paving, mossy steps, hidden cracks, and long stretches of hard ground. A walking pole may help, but do not use it to probe unknown openings or test the stability of old structures.
- **Bring reliable lights, spare batteries, first aid, and a way to signal your party.** The towns will not be lit for you, and dark doorways, covered channels, and staircases may conceal sudden drops.
- If the plateau is high in the ordinary physical sense, **allow time to acclimatize** and avoid strenuous travel at first. Its elevation is not stated, so plan conservatively.

A fire may be difficult to make and may create risks without offering much benefit. Prefer a safe, enclosed stove if you carry one, and never leave flame unattended in or beside an old building.

## Plan around uncertainty

Use a map, but regard it as a record of an earlier arrangement rather than a promise. Keep a written route log with times, bearings, terrain notes, and recognizable features. If available, compare several independent navigation methods; do not make any one device, map, or landmark your sole reference.

Set conservative daily destinations and turnaround times **before** setting out. Mark a route in more than one way, and make markers easy for your own party to recognize without leaving permanent damage. Check your position often. If the road seems to return to a place you never passed, or the landscape no longer matches your notes, stop and reassess rather than continuing in the hope that it will “correct itself.”

Travel with a companion if possible. Agree in advance that no one follows a distant tower, unfamiliar road, or apparent shortcut alone. Keep within sight or reliable contact, especially in fog, among buildings, or near ravines. If your group becomes separated, meet at a previously agreed, unmistakable point rather than improvising a search through unfamiliar streets.

Do not judge distance by appearance. A near ridge may be far away, and a tower on the horizon may be much less accessible than it looks. Measure progress by time and observed terrain as well as by what you can see.

## Treat oddities as navigation hazards

The unusual events described here are quiet, not necessarily harmless. If rain rises from the ground, shadows shift, a hill seems transparent, or a road shows you a stretch you have not yet walked:

1. **Stop moving.** Do not run toward or away from the effect.
2. **Keep your party together** and note your location, time, weather, and what each person observes.
3. **Use ordinary physical checks**—a compass, a marked line, a companion’s position—without assuming they will all agree.
4. **Wait for the event to pass** if the area is otherwise safe. Do not cross an apparent boundary or enter a feature solely because it looks newly accessible.
5. **Re-establish your route from more than one reference point** before proceeding.

Be especially cautious around ponds and still water, where reflections may not behave normally; around hills or escarpments that seem see-through; and on roads that appear to show impossible destinations. Do not trust an image in water, a view through stone, or a path seen only when looking backward as a route you can safely follow.

A sudden dull report or a vibration underfoot may mean distant settling. Move away from obvious unstable slopes and walls, and do not shelter directly beneath a cracked overhang. Gravel slides may be slow and nearly silent.

## Explore buildings cautiously

The towns are empty, but “empty” does not mean safe. Old-looking stone may be sound in one place and unstable in another; covered channels, blind doorways, cellars, and stairways below ground level are particular hazards.

- Inspect a building from outside before entering.
- Avoid roofs, unsupported upper floors, and walls with fresh-looking cracks or displaced blocks.
- Do not enter a dry chamber or underground passage without a light, a route back out, and a companion who remains outside. If you carry air-quality or gas detection equipment, use it; the chambers have not been demonstrated to be breathable or safe.
- Do not assume a doorway in a cliff leads to a room, or that a staircase ends where it appears to. Keep a handline or other reliable return reference if you explore enclosed spaces.
- Use established surfaces where possible. Mossy slabs and wet steps may be slick, and narrow canals may conceal sudden drops.

The stonework may seem almost preserved, but that appearance is not proof of stability. A missing roof, a long retaining wall, or a bridge over a grass-filled ravine can all invite risky assumptions.

## At camp

Choose a sheltered, well-drained site away from cliff edges, ravines, loose slopes, and structures that could shed stone. Keep food sealed even though there is no visible wildlife; it preserves supplies and avoids attracting anything you have not accounted for. Keep wet gear separate from dry sleeping gear. Check your surroundings again after changes in fog, rain, or frost.

Water may be intermittent, and channels may connect cisterns in ways that are hard to trace. Never depend on finding water at a particular settlement unless you have already confirmed it, and keep enough reserve to retreat to your last dependable source.

## The right attitude

Expect long stretches of quiet and little immediate feedback. There may be no birds, insects, or other animals to mark the passage of time or signal trouble. The place’s sounds are sparse: wind in grass, water in stonework, and, occasionally, a deep settling felt through the ground. Do not mistake that quiet for safety, and do not mistake the absence of visible activity for proof that nothing is changing.

Travel slowly. Keep your plans modest, your supplies sufficient, and your attention on the ground as well as the horizon. The uplands reward patience, but they do not make navigation easy. Your best safeguard is to avoid becoming committed to any one road, shelter, map, or expectation—and to turn back while you still have a clear way to do so.
