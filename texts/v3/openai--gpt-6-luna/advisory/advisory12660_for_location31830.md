Treat this as a place where the ground is not reliably finished deciding where the water goes. The basin is small, but don’t plan it as a quick crossing: routes can flood, drain, or shift underfoot, and a familiar passage may not be usable on your return.

**Before you enter**
- Ask a resident to guide you, and learn which ledges, drains, and bridges are safe *today*. The inhabitants read signs that a visitor is unlikely to notice. Don’t assume a route described yesterday is still sound.
- Agree on a meeting point above the waterline and a simple signal if you become separated. Keep your party small; narrow passages and changing channels make groups difficult to manage.
- Find out how the locals interpret “before the light changes.” Brightness, reflections, and the apparent layout may be unreliable cues for time or direction. Carry a dependable clock, but let local advice—not your schedule—set the crossing window.

**Wear and carry**
- Dress for cold, damp air even if the day seems bright: insulating layers beneath a waterproof outer layer, plus gloves and a warm hat. Wool or synthetic fabrics will serve better than clothing that stays heavy when wet.
- Use boots with excellent grip and ankle support. Wet stone, thin films of water, frost, and smooth mineral crusts are a dangerous combination. Bring spare dry socks and keep a warm change of clothes in a sealed bag.
- Protect your eyes from the hard reflected light with good sunglasses. The glare off wet stone and water may be intense even in cold weather.
- Pack light, with essentials secured against immersion: identification, communications equipment, a first-aid kit, a headlamp, and any medicines in waterproof containers. Avoid bulky luggage that could catch on a bridge or become a hazard if you fall.
- A walking pole may help, but ask before using one on narrow steps or old bridges; it could snag, damage a channel covering, or send you off balance.

**Moving through the basin**
Walk slowly and keep one hand free. Test each unfamiliar step before putting your weight on it. Don’t jump drains, follow water through openings, or climb onto roofs and ledges unless a local explicitly invites you. Water may appear shallow but run beneath stone, and a harmless-looking crack may connect to a strong current or a sudden drop.

Stay on the paths your guide chooses. Bridges and stairs are part of a working system, not ruins or attractions; residents may be actively repairing them. Don’t shift stones, block channels, or tie anything to reeds, growths, or animal passages. If water begins to rise, follow local instructions promptly and move to higher ground rather than waiting to see how far it will come.

The place is quiet partly because sound is softened by its construction and damp air. Speak softly, avoid whistles or loud alarms unless there is an emergency, and don’t mistake silence for distance: someone may be close by even when you can’t hear them.

**Water, animals, and the settlement**
Don’t drink from the channels or pools without asking how the water is made safe. Don’t eat unfamiliar plants, fish, or animals. The wildlife shares the settlement’s routes; check before sitting, opening drains, reaching into cracks, or leaving food outside. A small creature may be using a passage you regard as empty, and the larger animals may emerge through openings that appear far too small.

Respect the town as someone’s home, not an abandoned labyrinth. Ask before entering buildings, photographing residents, or touching objects in the wall niches. In particular, leave unusual stones, shells, clay, and anything else the water deposits where you find them unless a resident says otherwise.

**If the reflections behave strangely**
Treat the water’s images as observations, not directions. Reflections of unfamiliar buildings or skies may be remarkable, but don’t step into a pool, break a surface, or try to retrieve something because of what you see in it. If you want to look, do so from a stable, dry place and with a local present.

Plan to sleep somewhere raised and dry, with your equipment secured and your exit route checked before nightfall. The basin may be beautiful and still feel strangely submerged after dark; brightness on the cliffs is not proof that a ledge, stair, or channel is safe. Above all, travel at the basin’s pace. The people who live there don’t defeat its changes—they notice them, move what matters, and wait.
