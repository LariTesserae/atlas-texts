Treat this as a remote, high-altitude wilderness with excellent visibility, very poor shelter, and a soundscape that can make you feel alone even when animals are nearby. The plateau is passable, but it offers few easy places to rest, refill water, or get out of trouble.

**Before you go**

- **Check that the environment is actually breathable and tolerable.** Confirm atmospheric pressure, oxygen, temperature range, and any unusual chemical or biological hazards before leaving your arrival shelter. If the air is compatible with human life but thin, take altitude seriously: acclimatize, keep the first day easy, and plan a route back to lower ground. Headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, or confusion are reasons to stop and descend—not to push on.
- **Bring a partner if possible, and leave a route plan with someone who can summon help.** Set check-in times and clear rules for what happens if you miss one. There are no roads, markers, or obvious human refuges.
- **Study the terrain, not just the distance.** Map ridges, basins, water sources, cliffs, and possible descent routes. The plateau’s open appearance is deceptive: a basin may hide hollows, channels, and sudden drops, while mist can obscure a route that looked clear from afar. Carry a map and a navigation device, with a compass and paper backup if they work in this world.

**What to pack**

- **Warm, windproof, waterproof layers.** Bright sun does not mean warmth. Wind, cold shade, wet rock, and waterfall mist can chill you quickly. Bring gloves and a hat, plus eye and skin protection for the strong, exposed light.
- **Sturdy, grippy footwear.** Expect polished or wet stone, loose fragments, and slippery margins around pools and streams. Trekking poles can help on uneven ground, but they are not a substitute for keeping well back from edges.
- **A shelter that handles wind and damp.** Choose one that can be secured on exposed stone, and bring insulation from the ground. Natural shelter is limited to lee sides, overhangs, and clefts; some of those places may also funnel wind or amplify the waterfall roar.
- **More reliable drinking water than the landscape seems to promise.** Carry a reserve and a filter or purifier. Streams and pools may be contaminated by animals, and the water’s clarity says nothing about what is in it. Don’t count on a convenient refill: water is present, but scattered among cracks, seepages, and distant falls.
- **Food and emergency supplies for a longer stay than planned.** Progress may be slow if weather, visibility, or a difficult descent interrupts the route. Carry a first-aid kit, repair materials, light, and a means of signaling.
- **Hearing protection, used with judgment.** The roar is likely to be exhausting and can make conversation or calls for help useless. Earplugs or protective earbuds may make rest easier, but keep enough awareness to notice alarms, a companion’s signals, or nearby hazards. Agree on hand signals before setting out; visual communication will be essential.

**While crossing the plateau**

Keep a conservative distance from escarpments, waterfall edges, and wet ledges. Mist and spray can slick the stone, and the apparent size of a fall may be hard to judge until its sound reaches you. Don’t follow a stream toward a drop simply because it looks like the easiest route.

Avoid camping in old channels, shallow hollows, or below unstable ledges. Rain may fall on one ridge while leaving the next dry, so a channel that seems harmless may not stay that way. Camp out of the wind where you can, but not where seepage, falling water, or drainage can reach you.

Use landmarks repeatedly, and take bearings before entering broad basins where the ridges may look similar from every direction. In fog or low cloud, stop rather than improvising near cliffs. The distant views may be astonishingly crisp on a clear day, but that does not make the ground between you and the horizon simple.

Give wildlife room. The grazers and birds may appear far away, but the plateau’s openness leaves little cover for either you or them. Watch where you step around pools and seepage cracks, and don’t approach animals for a closer look. They may be accustomed to the landscape, not to people.

**What it will feel like**

The strange part may be how empty it seems. The waterfall roar will fill the space where ordinary travelers expect quiet, and it can make the whole plateau feel both immense and oddly sealed off. You may see life only as distant movement—a bird turning above a cliff, a pale grazer on a shelf—while the small signs of it remain underfoot in wet cracks and hollows.

Plan to travel slowly, pause often, and turn back before conditions force you to. This is not a place that needs to bar the way. Its lesson is gentler and more persistent: water, shelter, and companionship are all here, but rarely where you want them, and never enough to make care unnecessary.
