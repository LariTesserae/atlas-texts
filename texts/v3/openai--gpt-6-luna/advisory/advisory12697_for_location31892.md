## Prepare as for a flooded, uncharted cave—with the added assumption that the cave may not obey geometry.

The hollow sounds quiet and undisturbed, not hostile. That is not the same as safe. It is almost entirely underwater, has no dependable horizon or current, and gives several signs that distance, light, and the entrance may behave oddly. Plan a short, cautious visit whose first purpose is to learn whether a safe return is possible.

### Before entering

- **Do not try to squeeze through the visible crack.** It is only hand-wide, and the hollow’s apparent space may not be physically connected in an ordinary way. Use a tested passage or portal that can accommodate a person and equipment. Do not force the opening wider or assume that what looks like an entrance is a usable route.
- **Send instruments first.** Use a small, recoverable probe to test the route, water temperature and pressure, visibility, and whether a line or signal can cross back reliably. Give the probe time to observe: the hollow changes slowly, and an immediate reading may miss things.
- **Bring an outside team.** Someone should remain in a safe, known location, keep a log of departure and expected return, and know the abort plan. Agree on simple signals and a firm time limit. Do not make the first visit alone.
- **Treat all measurements as local and provisional.** Clocks, compasses, radio, sonar, lights, and distance estimates may fail or disagree. Record multiple independent cues, but do not let an instrument overrule a clear safety concern.

### Equipment

Assume you will be submerged from the moment you arrive.

- Wear a properly fitted **cold-water diving suit** and use a reliable breathing system with an independent reserve. Do not rely on the ceiling’s trapped air: the pockets are unstable, and even bubbles may not rise normally.
- Carry **two independent lights**, with batteries protected against water and cold. Their beams may bend or cast shadows in unexpected directions, so use them to see nearby hazards—not to judge where the exit is.
- Use a **slack, recoverable guide line** and an independent way to mark or log your route. Test the line before committing to it. It may snag in the dense growth, and the passage may not remain where you expect. Never treat a taut line or a pull on it as proof that the entrance is in that direction.
- Secure every loose item. The hollow is cramped, plant-choked, and full of sharp new mineral edges. Dangling hoses, straps, and clips could catch on the growth or disturb the silt.
- Carry redundant communication and tracking gear, but expect that radio and distance-based signals may be useless. Your safety plan must still work if you cannot contact the outside team.

### Inside the hollow

Move slowly, stay close to your partner, and keep the visit short. The black silt could quickly spoil visibility; avoid kicking it up or touching the pale scars, mineral lips, plants, or the smooth stones arranged like a doorway. Nothing here appears to invite interference, and there is no practical benefit to testing that assumption.

Do not follow the passage reflected in the hanging droplets, or treat a glimmer, shadow, sound, or apparent landmark as a route. The reflected passage draws nearer while the hollow seems to shrink around it; that is a warning not to use it for navigation. The three stones resemble a frame, but the space beyond them is solid rock. Do not try to open, cross, or “complete” it. The sand ridge may be a marker left by someone else; leave it as it is.

Keep a written or recorded account of **elapsed effort**, not just estimated distance. A nearby stone may take two strokes to reach on one attempt and twenty on another. Agree in advance on conditions that mean immediate return—loss of the guide line, equipment trouble, disorientation, unexpected pressure changes, or uncertainty about where the entrance is—and do not extend the visit to investigate a curiosity.

### Returning

Turn back early, while everyone is calm and equipment is sound. Confirm your return route using more than one cue; do not assume the crack will be precisely where you remember it. Keep the outside team informed if possible, and follow the agreed abort procedure rather than improvising if the route seems to have shifted.

Afterward, quarantine and inspect equipment, log what changed, and avoid bringing water, organisms, or sediment out unless sampling was explicitly planned and can be done safely. The hollow is not clearly dangerous, but it is a place where ordinary assumptions—about air, light, distance, and exits—are poor foundations for a rescue plan. Treat the first visit as reconnaissance, not exploration.
