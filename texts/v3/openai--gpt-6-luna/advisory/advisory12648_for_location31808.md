## What to expect

Treat this as a remote, exposed high plateau—not as ordinary mountain country. The broad, worn surfaces may look easy to cross, but they’re cut by deep ravines, unstable slopes, and sudden drops. Weather can change quickly, water is unreliable, and the wind and echoes can make distances and sounds hard to judge. There may be few people, no roads, and no dependable rescue.

Before setting out, check the world’s basic conditions if you can: elevation, air pressure and composition, gravity, temperature range, and any biological or chemical hazards. The description suggests familiar air and life, but it does not establish that they are safe for you.

## Plan around exposure, not distance

- **Acclimatize if the plateau is high.** Spend time at lower elevation first, ascend gradually, and do not push through headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, confusion, or breathlessness at rest. If symptoms worsen, descend.
- **Travel on broad, stable ridgelines and upland surfaces**, as local travelers do. Keep ravines in view, but don’t use their bottoms as shortcuts: they can be difficult to escape, and rain can send water through them suddenly.
- **Don’t mistake a calm morning for a safe forecast.** Watch cloud and wind, and set conservative turnaround times. If a front approaches, get off exposed rims and away from gullies, but don’t shelter directly beneath a cliff, overhang, or fresh rock scar. Look for a broad, stable, sheltered spot well clear of drainage channels.
- **Allow more time than a map suggests.** Loose stone, wind, poor visibility, and route-finding around ravines will slow you down. Avoid traveling in darkness or fog.

## Carry water and treat every source cautiously

There is no dependable surface water. Carry enough for the planned journey **plus a substantial delay**, and don’t count on finding a pool or catching rain. After a storm, streams and pools may appear briefly, but they can be muddy, contaminated, or gone by evening.

Bring a filter and a second means of treatment, such as chemical disinfectant or boiling. These address biological contamination, not necessarily dissolved minerals or chemicals; use a known source or test water where possible. Collect rainwater cleanly if the opportunity arises. Treat cisterns and channels as valuable local infrastructure: use them carefully, leave water for others, and don’t damage or contaminate them.

Pack all food. Animals are scarce, and there is no reason to expect reliable foraging. Don’t eat unfamiliar plants.

## Dress for wind, sun, rain, and cold together

The air may be warm, but wind can strip heat quickly, especially when clothing is wet. Bring:

- Windproof outer layers and warm insulating layers
- A waterproof shell and dry storage for spare clothing
- Sturdy boots with good grip, plus gaiters if you have them
- Sun protection: hat, sunglasses, and high-SPF sunscreen
- Gloves and a warm hat for exposed or overnight travel

At elevation, sunlight may be intense even when the air feels cool. Rain can turn bare rock slick, while gusts can make a pack or loose clothing catch the wind. Secure straps and keep important equipment tethered or stowed.

## Protect yourself from the terrain

Use a helmet where loose slopes or rockfall are unavoidable. Carry a first-aid kit, headlamp, repair supplies, and navigation backups: paper map and compass as well as electronic navigation, if available. Record your route and position rather than relying on memory—the plateau’s open expanses can look alike, and mist may erase landmarks.

Do not cross beneath a slope that is shedding stone, linger under unstable ledges, or approach a fresh white collapse scar. A rockfall may continue long after the first collapse, and its sound can travel misleadingly far. Keep a generous distance from cliff edges; rounded, old-looking stone is not necessarily solid at the rim.

## Prepare for the sound and isolation

Wind, water, and the cliffs can overwhelm voices and distort echoes. A shout may vanish, return from the wrong direction, or sound like something else. Agree on **visual signals, radio checks, and meeting points** with your party before separating. Keep a whistle and radio accessible, but don’t rely on hearing a reply. Avoid headphones, and don’t navigate toward a sound without verifying it.

If traveling alone, tell someone outside the country your route and check-in schedule, and carry a beacon or other suitable emergency communicator. Assume help may be slow or unavailable.

## Camp carefully

Choose a campsite on stable ground, sheltered from the strongest wind but well away from cliff faces, overhangs, ravines, and channels that could carry runoff. Check overhead and uphill for loose blocks. Anchor your shelter securely; gusts can be powerful and irregular. Use a stove rather than a large fire. If you make a small fire where permitted, keep it controlled and extinguish it fully.

Leave cairns, water channels, shelters, and other signs as you found them unless there is a clear reason to use them. Their makers may know this terrain better than you do.

**A sensible first-day rule:** travel light but self-sufficiently, stay on a conservative route, identify more than one escape or shelter option, and turn back while conditions are still manageable—not once the weather has made the decision for you.
