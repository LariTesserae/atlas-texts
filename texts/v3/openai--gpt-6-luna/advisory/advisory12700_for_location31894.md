## Before you go

Treat this as a **working settlement inside unstable, hot rock**, not as a cave tour. Go only with a local host or guide who knows which galleries are open, which routes have shifted, and where the current safe refuge and exit are. The layout is cramped and constantly changing; a map may be out of date by the time you arrive. Agree on a meeting point and a way to signal for help before separating from anyone.

If you can, ask your hosts about the air, the latest ground movement, the shift schedule, and their rules for visitors. The description does not establish whether the air is safe to breathe. Heat, steam, smoke, grinding dust, and industrial fumes may collect in low or poorly ventilated spaces. Do not assume that ordinary breathing—or a basic dust mask—is enough. Have the atmosphere checked with suitable instruments, and carry an appropriate respirator or escape breathing equipment if local conditions require it. If the air cannot be assessed, or your equipment is not suited to it, do not enter.

## What to bring

- **Sturdy, grippy boots** with toe protection. Floors may be slick with condensation, crossed by channels and grates, or strewn with grit and metal debris.  
- **A hard hat, eye protection, and work gloves.** Falling grit, low ceilings, sparks, sharp rock, and hot metal are ordinary hazards here. Gloves are for handling safe surfaces—not permission to touch machinery or glowing stone.  
- **Durable, breathable clothing** that covers your skin without trapping too much heat. Avoid loose sleeves, dangling straps, scarves, and anything that can catch on belts, brackets, or tools.  
- **A reliable light** that can be secured to you, plus a backup. The settlement is dim, crowded, and full of stairs and obstructions; keep your hands free. Use only lights permitted by your hosts, especially near furnaces or flammable industrial materials.  
- **Drinking water in a sealed container and some compact food**, subject to local rules. Do not drink from channels or tanks unless your hosts specifically say it has been treated for visitors. The water may carry minerals or contaminants, and it is scarce enough that wasting it would be both unsafe and discourteous.  
- **A way to communicate and navigate**, preferably with your guide’s help. Do not count on wireless signals, familiar street layouts, or a device that depends on an external network.  

Consider heat acclimation before arrival, and avoid entering already dehydrated, ill, or exhausted. The lower galleries may be considerably hotter than the upper ones, while the dampness can make it harder for sweat to cool you. Plan a short first visit, take rests where your host considers safe, and leave at the first signs of heat illness—dizziness, headache, nausea, confusion, unusual weakness, or faintness.

## Moving around

Walk slowly and watch the floor as much as the walls. Narrow stairs, overlapping walkways, pipes, overhead brackets, and changes in level leave little room to recover from a slip. Keep away from marked depressions, newly cut corridors, fresh fractures, and areas where grit is falling. A warning chime is important, but the absence of one does **not** mean the rock is stable. The settlement is still shifting.

Ask before entering any workshop or crossing a work area. Furnaces, presses, grinders, winches, belts, and electrical arcs can be dangerous even when they sound quiet or appear idle; machinery may start as another shift begins. Stay behind local barriers, keep clear of cables and suspended loads, and never touch a control, tool, pipe, handrail, or wall simply because it looks unused. Some surfaces may be hot, some electrically live, and some newly fractured.

If the ground trembles, follow your guide’s instructions rather than rushing for the nearest stair. Move away from fresh cracks, unsupported rock, and hanging equipment; protect your head and wait for the local all-clear. The people who live here will know which parts of the settlement are designed to shelter in place and which routes are likely to become blocked.

## Water, plants, and courtesy

Condensation can release suddenly, and pipes may sweat or leak without warning. Keep electronics and personal supplies protected, but do not block gutters, channels, or drains: they are part of the settlement’s water system. Ask before washing, filling a bottle, or taking anything from a cistern.

The pale growths in the cool seams and runoff channels may look insignificant, but the residents tend them deliberately. Don’t scrape, sample, step on, or “improve” them. Avoid introducing seeds, microbes, or other living material without permission; in a place with so little vegetation, even a small import could matter.

Keep your voice and movements measured around workers. The enclosed stone makes sounds carry in odd, muffled ways, and the machines are part of people’s shifts and livelihoods. Ask where you may stand, whether photography or recording is acceptable, and which passages are private or unsafe. Don’t treat residents’ warning chimes, gutters, or improvised supports as curiosities to handle.

## One last rule

Have a clear turnaround point. Leave if your guide loses contact, the air becomes difficult to tolerate, machinery behaves unexpectedly, water begins flowing across a route, or the rock starts shedding grit or moving. This settlement’s dangers are not necessarily dramatic: a new bend in a corridor, a silent inward push, or a cupful of water from a crack may be the first hint that the place has changed again.
