Treat this as an **industrial ocean**, not an ordinary sea. It may look calm and open, but the channels, gates, and pumps are working infrastructure—and the water may be moving according to a schedule you don’t know.

### Before you enter

- **Get local authorization and a briefing.** Ask where visitors may go, when pumps and gates cycle, which channels are active, and how to summon help. Find out the water’s depth, temperature, chemistry, and pressure profile. The description does not establish any of these, so don’t guess.
- **Arrive through a proper access point**—a station, lock, submersible dock, or other sealed facility. Never step through an underwater portal or descend from the surface without knowing the pressure and having equipment rated for it.
- **Use a rated submersible unless the site explicitly supports diving.** Ordinary recreational scuba is no basis for exploring an immense basin of unknown depth. If diving is permitted, go with a qualified local guide, a buddy, redundant breathing gas, and a conservative plan with a known ascent and decompression procedure.
- **Bring a locator and navigation plan.** GPS will not work underwater. Use the basin’s approved acoustic or visual beacons, carry an independent locator, and agree on a lost-contact procedure. In a place this large, “I’ll swim back the way I came” is not a plan.
- **Check for biological and chemical hazards.** Ask about water treatment, microbes, and quarantine rules. Keep your gear clean, and don’t release food, waste, or organisms into the basin.

### While exploring

Stay well away from **intakes, sluices, gates, conduits, and active service routes**. A quiet-looking channel can become a powerful current when a cycle begins. Pumps may start, gates may close, and suspended material may obscure your view. Keep a generous distance from machinery; don’t touch it, climb it, or use its structures as a handhold.

Treat the broad, empty floor as open water—not as a safe place to walk or swim. The next tower or marker may be kilometers away. Stay within your approved route, maintain contact with your guide, and leave before visibility or conditions change. Machinery noise carries as pressure and vibration, so don’t rely on hearing warnings or judging distance by sound.

Give the fish and larger animals room. Their routes may be shaped by the currents and equipment, and a group turning together could be responding to a change you haven’t noticed. Don’t feed, chase, or corner them. The cultivated plants are managed crops, not wild vegetation to collect or disturb.

### Pack and plan for

- A pressure-rated vehicle or approved dive system  
- Redundant life support, thermal protection, and a backup light  
- Navigation, a tether or equivalent recovery method, and an underwater locator  
- A clear turnaround time and emergency extraction plan  
- A way to communicate that does not depend on speech carrying through the water  

Expect the place to feel strange in a particular way: not ruined or wild, but so large that engineered channels begin to seem like rivers and the fabricated floor like geography. The scale may tempt you to keep going. Resist that temptation. In this basin, distance, current, and machinery—not the apparent emptiness—should set the limits of your visit.
