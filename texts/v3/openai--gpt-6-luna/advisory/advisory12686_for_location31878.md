Treat this as a **working water system that happens to be a city**, not as a city with some inconvenient puddles. The water is warm, the ground shifts between dry and flooded, and a route that was safe this morning may not be safe at noon. Your best preparation is less about endurance and more about staying oriented, dry enough, and out of the machinery’s way.

### Before you enter

- **Arrive with a local guide or host.** Ask for someone who knows the current condition of the district, not just someone carrying an old map. Routes change as gates operate, passages flood, and structures are moved. Official maps are useful for orientation, but locals’ recent knowledge is more valuable.
- **Learn the evacuation signals and procedures before setting out.** Find out which markings mean “water rising,” which doors lead to higher ground, where floodgates close, and where you must not wait. Ask what people do when the channels reverse or the walls begin to sing. Those are not colorful local quirks; they are warnings from the city’s plumbing and foundations.
- **Check the day’s weather and water schedule with residents.** There is no simple tide timetable to rely on. Rain elsewhere in the basin, pump changes, and gate operations can alter conditions locally.
- **Confirm your usual cross-world arrival precautions:** breathable atmosphere, pressure, temperature, and any local biological or chemical hazards. The description suggests ordinary sunlight and abundant water, but not that the air or water is safe for an offworld body.

### What to wear and carry

- Wear **light, quick-drying layers** that allow you to move freely. The air is warm and damp; heavy clothing will stay wet and become exhausting.
- Use **sturdy, closed-toe footwear with excellent grip**, ideally with drainage and a secure fit. Expect slick mineral surfaces, submerged steps, loose grates, and sudden changes in water depth. A walking pole can help test unfamiliar water, but don’t use it to probe machinery or places locals warn you away from.
- Bring a **compact flotation aid** and a short tether or safety line if you’ll be near deep channels or moving water. Don’t assume ankle-deep water stays ankle-deep.
- Protect your eyes from spray and grit with **clear, sealed or close-fitting eyewear**. Carry gloves for rough metal, wet stone, and handrails.
- Use a **waterproof, securely fastened bag** for identification, medicines, communication devices, and a light. A headlamp is useful in lower passages, but keep it aimed low; glare bouncing off wet stone can make it harder for other people to see.
- Pack drinking water and a method of keeping it clean. **Do not drink from channels, walls, or open cisterns**, however clear or warm the water looks.
- Consider hearing protection, but don’t block out the city entirely. The constant mechanical roar can fatigue you and make conversation difficult, while visual alarms and gestures may carry important information. Ask your host what signals you need to notice.

### How to move through it

Stay on **currently approved routes**, preferably elevated walkways and passages that a local has confirmed are open. Avoid low tunnels, flooded floors, and old doorways below the waterline unless you are with someone equipped and authorized to enter them. A dry passage is not necessarily a safe passage: it may be about to fill, or it may be part of a pressure route.

Expect travel to take longer than the distance suggests. Narrow streets, crowds, noise, slick surfaces, and detours around works all slow movement. Agree on a meeting point and a fallback meeting point in case you lose one another. Do not rely on shouting: sound ricochets through the buildings, machinery masks voices, and bells are reserved for emergencies. Use the local system of signs, lights, or agreed hand signals.

Be especially cautious around **plazas, spillways, maintenance yards, and floodgate lines**. A plaza may be busy and safe on an ordinary day, then clear rapidly when water is expected. If residents start moving with purpose, follow their lead promptly rather than stopping to watch. Never linger to photograph a gate closing or a channel changing direction.

### Water, animals, and health

The water is part of the city’s circulation, and its appearance won’t tell you what it carries. Keep cuts covered, wash your hands before eating, and avoid swallowing spray or splashed water. If you have respiratory sensitivities, ask about mist, runoff, and local air quality; carry appropriate protection rather than assuming every visible haze is harmless.

The animals are inhabitants, not attractions. Don’t feed them, touch them, corner them, or reach into drains and cavities. Some creatures move through stairwells and buildings when their usual channels run dry; others may appear when a drainage failure brings food into the streets. Give them room and take local warnings about particular doors, channels, or hours seriously.

### The main thing to remember

The city is noisy, wet, and continuously adjusting itself to the basin beneath it. Don’t interpret a change in pump rhythm, a backward-running channel, or a tremor in the walls as spectacle. Watch the residents: they know how the city announces trouble. Keep your belongings secured, stay with your guide, and be willing to turn back even when a route still looks passable. In this place, “it was safe a few minutes ago” is not a reliable safety plan.
