Treat the Bright Works as a functioning, unfamiliar system—not as a ruin, and not as a place whose geometry will reliably obey your expectations. It seems patient rather than immediately hostile, but it may not recognize that a human is lost, thirsty, or standing in the wrong place.

## Before you go

- **Confirm your way back.** Establish a return point that is independent of local roads, sightlines, or landmarks. Test the return method before venturing far, and agree on a firm turnaround time. The Works can make a destination seem to shift from ahead to behind without anything visibly moving.
- **Bring an independent companion plan.** Travel with at least one other person, set check-in times, and agree on what to do if separated. Don’t count on shouting: voices travel poorly, and sounds can seem to come from directly above or below you.
- **Pack for cold, glare, and exposure.** The light is bright but gives little warmth. Bring warm layers, eye protection, sun protection, and footwear that grips smooth, hard surfaces. The high shelf may also mean thin air or unfamiliar atmospheric conditions; test the air before committing to a long walk, and bring whatever breathing support your own physiology requires.
- **Carry your own essentials.** Bring water, food, first aid, a sheltered rest option, and a means to signal your party. Don’t assume the Works’ channels, cisterns, or machinery are there for visitors—or that their water is safe to drink.
- **Keep your kit simple and redundant.** A compass, GPS, laser rangefinder, maps, and reflective markers may be useful as records, but none should be trusted as the sole guide. Carry a plain written plan and a reliable way to keep track of your own time.

## How to move around

- **Walk by observation, not measurement.** Some courts reportedly lengthen when measured. Don’t pace out distances, repeatedly check a rangefinder, or turn a crossing into a survey. Choose a visible destination and walk toward it without stopping to prove how far away it is.
- **Don’t navigate by polished metal.** It may show the place as it was a few seconds ago, or a nearby place that does not yet exist. Still water is said to reflect reliably, but use it only as a curiosity—not as your map.
- **Pay attention to shadows.** In most places they point away from the sun; on certain terraces they point toward it, and those terraces are considered safe for resting. Treat that as a local warning or useful clue, not a universal guarantee. If you find a terrace with sunward shadows, note it as a possible rest point.
- **Don’t assume roads, barriers, or crossings behave conventionally.** A barrier may turn up on the other side of your route; roads may pass through one another without meeting. Keep your party in sight where possible, and use agreed hand signals. If you use a tether, keep it short and treat it as a backup, not proof that you are still on the same route.
- **Don’t leave permanent marks.** Junctions are marked by workers to remind them which way the junction was facing. Your own paint, chalk, or carved arrows could interfere with that practice—or simply become misleading. If you need a temporary marker, ask a worker first, or use something you can retrieve.
- **Stay clear of channels, grates, and machinery.** Water can run uphill and vanish into wall grates. Don’t block it, follow it into drains, or stand over an outlet to investigate. The machinery is quiet, not necessarily safe; keep away from vents, conveyors, service doors, and moving equipment.

## Around the living things

The Works has abundant life, but nothing in the description suggests that the animals are domesticated or accustomed to people.

- Give the roof-grazing herds plenty of space. Their apparent lack of hoofprints doesn’t make them harmless, and their size may be hard to judge among rails and pipes.
- Avoid nests and signal housings where birds gather. If a flock rises, don’t run through it; step back and let it pass.
- In the evening, use covered or low-output lights where practical. The tiny winged creatures gather in knots around lamps, so bright exposed lighting may surround you with moving darkness. A hood or eye protection could make that less unpleasant.
- Don’t try to catch the quick, gap-slipping animals. Dust spirals may be the only sign they are nearby, and reaching into narrow openings in an industrial landscape is a poor bargain.
- Leave the lichen and tough roof plants alone. They may be part of a much larger network, and they grow in places that could be structurally important or difficult to reach.

## What to do if something goes wrong

If a route stops making sense, **stop trying to force it into sense**. Don’t chase a footstep that seems to come from beneath you, follow a reflection, or keep measuring a court that appears to be lengthening. Regroup somewhere sheltered and recognizable. Use your prearranged signals, wait for your companion, and return to the last place your group agreed on—not necessarily the last place you remember seeing.

If you become separated, don’t wander in search of voices. Find a safe, open resting place, make yourself visible to your party, and conserve water and warmth. If a junction has changed orientation, treat that as information: don’t repeatedly cross it to test the effect.

## The right attitude

Be a quiet visitor. The Bright Works feels occupied by systems too large to notice you, but that is not the same as being empty. Don’t dismantle anything, improvise repairs, redirect water, or assume a strange feature is broken. If you encounter workers, ask before approaching machinery or entering enclosed structures; their customs may explain rules that no instrument can.

Expect the place to be beautiful, legible in detail, and unreliable in the large. The safest traveler will enjoy the sharp light and immense constructed landscape without insisting that every path, distance, reflection, or sound behave like one back home.
