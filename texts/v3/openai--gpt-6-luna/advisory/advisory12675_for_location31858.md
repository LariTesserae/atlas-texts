Treat this country as a **vast, elevated wetland growing on the back of a forest**—beautiful, quiet, and easy to mistake for solid ground. Its greatest dangers are likely not dramatic predators, but hidden edges, concealed water, exposure, disorientation, and the slow failure of places that look dependable.

## Before you go

- **Confirm the basics of the world first.** Check atmospheric pressure, oxygen level, temperature range, and airborne irritants before removing your breathing gear. The air sounds mild and breathable, but a human traveler should not assume that from appearance or scent. Bring a compact respirator or mask in case of spores, plant dust, or an unexpected change in air quality.
- **Arrive with a guide or a reliable route plan if possible.** The plateau has no dependable roads, and familiar landmarks may disappear behind mist or change as the living ground shifts. Leave your intended route and return time with someone outside the country, and carry a locator or emergency beacon that works across the boundary between worlds.
- **Plan for dampness rather than cold.** Persistent wet can chill you even in mild air. Wear quick-drying, insulating layers beneath a genuinely waterproof outer layer, with gloves and spare socks kept in sealed bags. Favor sturdy boots with good grip and ankle support; soft, waterlogged ground can roll an ankle as easily as rough stone.
- **Bring more light and timekeeping than you think you need.** There may be no clear sunrise, sunset, or direct sun by which to judge the hour. Carry a clock, a headlamp with spare power, and a low-intensity backup light. Use light sparingly around animals and other travelers.

## Moving across the plateau

- **Do not trust the green surface.** The plant-mat may be several feet deep, hollow beneath, or floating over water and cavities. Probe unfamiliar ground with a pole before stepping onto it. Avoid dark, unusually smooth patches, concealed pools, and any section that flexes, sags, or sends water bubbling up.
- **Keep well back from the margins.** The plateau’s edge may be hidden by vegetation and mist, and the apparent ground can continue farther than its support does. Do not approach a cliff to “get a better view.” Use a belay or fixed safety line only where a trained guide has established a safe anchor; roots and old trunks may look strong while being hollow or rotten.
- **Move slowly and leave a way back.** Mark your route with a map, bearings, and a tracked return line or locator rather than relying on memory. If you use physical markers, make them conspicuous to your party but removable and harmless to the ecosystem. Mist can erase distant landmarks, and paths may simply peter out into a pool, a hollow, or a drop.
- **Treat streams as routes to investigate, not guides to follow blindly.** Water may vanish beneath the mat and reappear elsewhere. A stream bank could be a thin roof over a void, and a seemingly shallow pool may have a soft, deep bottom. Never wade into unfamiliar water without probing it and securing a line.

## Water, food, and health

- **Do not drink untreated water.** Clear water can still carry unfamiliar microbes, dissolved plant compounds, or contaminants from the living mat. Use a purifier appropriate to the local biology, and carry a backup supply. Boiling is useful only if local hazards are known to be heat-sensitive.
- **Bring all food you need.** Do not eat plants, flowers, or animals unless a qualified local expert has identified them and confirmed they are safe. The pale flowers and silver dust are especially poor things to experiment with.
- **Protect your skin and lungs.** Cover cuts, wash hands before eating, and check yourself daily for bites, ticks, embedded plant material, and skin irritation. In a warm, saturated environment, small wounds and soaked clothing can become problems quickly. If you have asthma, severe allergies, or a tendency toward fungal or respiratory illness, consult a medical professional before traveling and carry the medication you rely on.
- **Do not assume quiet means harmless.** Animals here appear adapted to remain unnoticed. Give any movement beneath the ground or in the canopy room to pass; do not prod, chase, feed, or corner wildlife.

## Resting and shelter

Choose a **firm, raised place well away from cliffs, pools, streams, and obvious drainage routes**. Avoid hollow trunks, leaning pillars, and old trees as shelters: they may be fragile, and their interiors may contain animals or water. Keep sleeping gear dry and sealed overnight. Use a small, controlled light rather than a bright floodlamp, and keep food secured so that you do not invite curious visitors.

Because the country has little obvious day–night cycle, keep your own schedule deliberately: meals, rest periods, and check-ins at fixed clock times. Mist and muffled sound can make distance difficult to judge. Stay within sight or reliable communication range of your party, and agree in advance what everyone should do if separated—usually to stop, shelter, and signal rather than wander in search.

## Respect the place

The old stones, paths, and signs of passage may still matter to someone. Treat them as potentially significant, not abandoned debris. Do not carve, collect, break, transplant, or leave objects behind. Avoid trampling pale flowers or disturbing anything that might be a marker, dwelling, or local practice. The landscape is not inert wilderness: it is a living structure that has incorporated almost everything around it.

The safest mindset is **patient, light-footed, and ready to turn back**. This plateau does not seem hostile, but it gives little warning when a soft path becomes a hidden hollow, when a familiar landmark dissolves into mist, or when the ground gives way at the edge of the world.
