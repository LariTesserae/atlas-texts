The plateau has no horizon in the usual sense. It rises so high that the air is thin and warm, and spreads so far that its farthest ridges disappear into darkness before they can become distant. There is no visible sun. Light collects only in small, temporary ways: a dull glimmer along a wet seam in the rock, a pale flash when a storm moves behind a ridge, the brief blue sparks of mineral dust scuffing together in the wind.

The stone is old and settled into its shapes. Broad shelves lean into shallow basins; long cliffs have been softened, not broken, by ages of slow weather. The ground is mostly dark basalt, with bands of dull metal running through it like old grain. Here and there, the rock opens into deep fissures. Warm vapor breathes from them, and condensation beads on the rim. It gathers in hollows as dark, still water, then vanishes over the edge or sinks through cracks too narrow to see.

Rain comes irregularly. Sometimes it drifts for hours as a fine, warm mist; sometimes a single heavy squall crosses the plateau and leaves the stone shining. The water does not make rivers so much as routes: temporary runs down sloping shelves, pools in the lee of ridges, damp ribbons that last a day or two and are gone by morning. The air smells faintly of wet stone, iron, and something mineral and bitter.

Vegetation is scattered. Low, tough growth clings to protected pockets: blackish mats, pale thorned shrubs, and small fans of leaves that open when moisture reaches them. It never forms a continuous cover. The emptiness between plants is part of the landscape, as broad and definite as the ridges themselves.

Yet the plateau is full of animals. Most are heard only as tiny disturbances: a dry clicking from beneath a stone, the brush of many feet crossing grit, an occasional soft flutter from a cleft. During the damp hours, long-legged grazers emerge from hollows and move in loose, widely spaced herds, following the wet seams across the rock. Smaller scavengers trail them. Above, broad-winged hunters circle without calling. In the dark, their shadows are impossible to distinguish from the darkness below.

The strangest sound comes from underfoot. Far below the surface, pressure moves through the metal-rich rock in slow, repeating cycles. The plateau gives off a low mechanical thrum, too deep to be heard clearly except where the stone narrows into a ridge or a fissure. There the vibration becomes a muted clank, a distant turning, as if enormous engines were running somewhere beneath the world. Nothing has built them. No towers, roads, or machines stand on the surface. The process belongs to the place: heated mineral water enters the deep seams, pressure builds, and the stone shifts by a fraction. It is industry without a factory, a geology that has learned to work in regular stages.

Now and then, a seam discharges. Warm vapor rises; a thin electric shimmer passes over the rock; creatures withdraw into cracks and wait. Then the plateau quiets again, and the small lives return to their routes. These changes are neither sudden nor dramatic. They are simply the local weather of a system that has been running for longer than anything that walks across it.

In the darkness, distance is difficult to judge. A ridge may be a short climb away or a day’s journey; a low hum may come from beneath one’s feet or from the far side of the plateau. The place offers few landmarks, but it has its own measures: the direction of the warm air, the wetness of the stone, the pulse of the ground. To cross it is to move through a vast, quiet cycle—mist gathering, animals feeding, pressure turning in the deep rock, and the ancient surface holding its shape.
