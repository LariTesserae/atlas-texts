The upland begins above the weather. From the lower slopes, its ridges look like a pale seam pulled tight across the sky; up here, the seam opens into a broad, raw tableland of dark stone, snow, and shallow water. The elevation is severe, but the terrain has not yet settled into the familiar shapes of an old mountain country. Ridges stop abruptly. Basins are only partly scooped out. Long ribs of fresh rock show where the ground has lifted and cracked, and the scree at their feet has not had time to travel far.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in deep or generous forms. It beads on the black stone, seeps from hairline fractures, and gathers in flat tarns whose edges are still being decided by the next freeze. Snowmelt slides over the plateau in thin sheets, vanishing beneath crusts of ice and appearing again downslope as a row of steaming springs. The air is cold enough to keep the wetness sharp: droplets cling to the undersides of ledges, and fog collects in hollows, then lifts in ragged strips when the wind turns. Nothing here is lush. Mosses and low, dark plants occupy the warm seams around springs; a few tough grasses hold in gravel pockets. Between them lie long stretches of bare mineral ground.

The place has an industrial look without any industry. Its stone is iron-dark, veined with pale seams and scattered with nodules that shine like slag when wet. In certain hollows, the earth breathes in a steady cycle: pressure builds below, then a spring vents with a low hiss, sending vapor and water across the rocks. Farther off, broad plates of ice shift against the edges of tarns, groaning once and falling silent. These processes make a landscape of valves, channels, and cooling surfaces, but there are no pipes, towers, roads, or cut faces. Nothing has been assembled here. The mechanism is geological, slow in one measure and startlingly forceful in another.

The light is usually low. Cloud sits close to the high ground, turning midday into a gray interval rather than a clear event. When the cloud breaks, it reveals a surprising distance: neighboring ranges, blue-black and snow-striped, and between them a wide country of basins and ridges, large enough to suggest a region but spare enough to make each feature feel alone. At dusk, the wet stone holds what little light remains. The tarns reflect a dim, metallic sky.

The quiet is not emptiness. Wind brushes the open ground with a soft, continuous rasp. Water ticks into pools and runs under thin ice. Now and then, a distant crack travels through the rock, so low that it is felt more than heard. The animals make the real movement. Herds of pale, thick-coated grazers cross the shallow valleys in scattered bands, stopping to crop the sparse growth around warm ground. They are followed by long-legged hunters that move with patient economy, almost disappearing against the broken ridgelines. Birds gather at the springs and in the lee of rock walls, where insects rise from warmer mud. Their calls are brief and carried a long way; between them, the plateau returns to its hush.

Life is abundant but not crowded. The animals use the land in passing: crossing between feeding hollows, resting in sheltered cuts, gathering where water stays liquid. Their tracks are among the few marks that persist, and even these fill with meltwater or disappear beneath a fresh sift of snow. The ground is still young in its arrangements. A new seep may open during a thaw; a slab may tilt after a night of frost; a basin may deepen by inches as ice works at its floor. The land does not yet have many old paths or settled borders.

Nothing here is made for permanence. Water shifts, stone opens, animals pass through, and weather keeps revising the surface. The high country feels less like a finished wilderness than a vast piece of terrain in the act of becoming: cold, wet, spacious, and quietly in motion.
