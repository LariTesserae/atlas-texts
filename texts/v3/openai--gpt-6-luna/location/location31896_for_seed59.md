The town occupies a high, shallow bowl in the mountains, no more than a few streets’ width across. From the ridge it looks less built than overgrown: a dark green cushion caught between pale rock shoulders, with roofs and walkways appearing only where the vegetation parts. The bowl has held its shape for a long time. Its rim is worn smooth by weather, and the slopes descend in settled folds of stone, peat, and old root. Nothing here is pristine, but almost nothing is bare.

Water comes out of the ground everywhere. It seeps from cracks in the uphill wall, beads on the moss, and runs in narrow, quick channels under the houses. After rain, the whole basin seems to loosen: steps shine, banks slump a little, and the channels braid together through the streets before finding a spillway at the low end. There is no single river, only many small waters keeping one another company. The ground gives slightly underfoot, springy with soaked roots and deep layers of old plant matter. Stone is used where weight must be held; everywhere else, people build with what grows.

Vegetation is not a cover over the town but its main material. Sedge and rush fill the open spaces. Moss pads the walls and rounds the edges of the steps. Willow-like shrubs are trained into screens and windbreaks; their living stems thicken around the frames that guide them. Thick grasses roof sheds and storehouses, their blades dark with moisture. Between buildings, fern fronds overlap at shoulder height, and tough ground plants are worn into paths by repeated passage. The plants have been cut, woven, bent, and replanted for generations. The town is garden, marsh, and construction at once.

Its buildings stand close together on low stone feet, linked by plank walks and raised, root-bound causeways. The streets are narrow, curved, and always busy with runoff. Some pass beneath living arches; others climb short flights of stone steps to dry platforms where grain, baskets, and tools are kept. There are little courtyards, but even these have channels crossing them and plants growing through the paving. People use hand saws, wedges, wooden mallets, simple iron hooks, and long knives. A good deal of their craft consists of persuading things to hold: a roof to stay up under wet weight, a bank to keep its shape, a channel to go where it is wanted. Repairs are constant and communal. A new branch, a slipped stone, a blocked drain—each is noticed quickly, because everyone shares the same narrow ground.

The air is cool without being severe. In daylight it is ordinary daylight, though often softened by thin cloud and water vapor rising from the basin. Colors are clear at close range and subdued beyond: black wet stone, yellow-green stems, the silver undersides of leaves, brown water carrying bits of moss. At dawn, condensation gathers on every surface. It hangs from eaves and drips from the plants long after the rain has stopped. The smell is rich but not foul: crushed leaves, wet mineral earth, wood smoke, and the clean, faintly bitter scent of cold water.

The animals are almost too numerous for the place to seem quiet. Birds crowd the shrubs and rooftops, bursting up in flurries whenever someone opens a door or crosses a bridge. Small frogs sit in the channels and call over one another, their voices rising and falling in bursts. Insects stitch the air above the wet ground. There are fish in the deeper runnels and pools, where the water slows long enough to hold them; snails and larvae cling beneath stones. Small mammals move through the plant walls and under the walkways, taking the same routes often enough that people know which boards will tremble before they appear. Larger birds patrol the rim and descend to the town’s outer beds. Nothing about the animals is magical or monstrous. Their abundance is simply the fact around which every other fact has had to arrange itself.

So the place is loud. Water ticks and gurgles underfoot, then roars briefly when a channel takes a fresh surge. Birds call from every level—roof, hedge, ridge, and air. Wings beat against the close-set leaves. People shout across walkways, and the sound is swallowed, returned, or carried strangely by the wet walls of vegetation. At times a whole section of the town seems to start moving: a flock lifting, frogs answering, a rush of water passing beneath the planks, residents hurrying to clear a spillway before it backs up. There are quieter moments, but even then the silence is crowded with small motions.

The town survives by keeping its changes small. Its people do not try to drain the bowl or straighten its streams. They guide water through several channels rather than trusting one, leave broad strips of vegetation uncut to bind the banks, and build replacements just uphill from things the water may take. They clear paths constantly, but never so completely that the exposed ground can wash away. The basin is lively, even restless, yet its disturbances follow familiar routes. A storm can flood a lower walk; a wet spring can bring frogs into the courtyards; a burst of birds can empty a drying rack in seconds. Then the waters fall, the plants close over the damage, and the town resumes its dense, practiced life.

From the ridge, the whole settlement still seems small: a knot of roofs, green corridors, moving water, and animal noise held within a worn stone hollow. Down inside it, there is no empty space to speak of. Every ledge has a plant on it, every channel has a creature in it, and every path belongs, briefly, to whoever is crossing it.
