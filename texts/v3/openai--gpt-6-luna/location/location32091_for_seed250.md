The sea has not yet decided where its bottom belongs.

It fills a basin so broad that the horizon is a weather system: a low, blue-black wall of cloud, rain, and distant water, rising and falling in slow convulsions. The basin is high above the old world—if there is an old world—and the air is thin, cold, and bright. Here the ocean lies against the sky. Its surface is not level but heaved into long ridges, miles apart, whose backs catch the light like the shoulders of submerged mountains. Between them, valleys of water open and close without warning.

There are no shores in the ordinary sense. Now and then a raw spine of stone breaks the surface: pale, unfinished rock, still soft at its edges, still drawing itself upward from beneath the deep. These heights look less eroded than interrupted, as if the planet had begun making them and then turned its attention elsewhere. Their faces are glassy with cold water. In creases and hollows, a little sparse vegetation clings—fine dark fronds, nearly colorless, lying flat whenever the wind passes. It grows without visible roots, or with roots that vanish into the stone as if the stone were remembering how to be soil.

Most of the world is empty water. There are stretches where nothing breaks the surface for days of travel, though the water is never still. Great currents cross under one another, rising in clear columns and falling as rain. Sometimes the rain goes upward: drops lift from the sea and disappear into the high cloud, while beneath them the surface dimples as if something enormous were breathing from below. The clouds have depth enough to contain their own horizons. A storm may be visible for a week before it arrives, turning slowly in place, lightning threading through its interior in silent white veins.

Then the sound reaches you.

It comes through water and air together: a long grinding roar, like continents being dragged across one another, though there are no continents here, only the promise of them. Waves strike the new rock with a crash that travels for miles. Far out, unseen sheets of water collapse into trenches. The wind makes a constant low note through the empty reaches, and beneath it a finer noise persists: the tick and crack of cooling stone, the hiss of mineral growth, the faint shivering chatter of matter rearranging itself. Nothing calls. Nothing answers. There are no animals in the water, no birds above it, no small beating lives to interrupt the vast labor of the place.

The sea is also a machine, though no hand built it. Beneath its surface, invisible structures of force hold the currents in patterns too intricate to be called natural and too ungoverned to be called designed. Pressure travels in bright, geometric pulses, making the deep water briefly transparent. For a moment, one can see down through impossible distances: terraces forming in the dark, ridges suspended over other ridges, a seabed assembling itself out of mineral dust and blue light. Then the pulse passes, the water clouds, and the depths return to their great blankness.

There are no towers, vessels, lights, or marks of habitation. Nothing has been placed here. Yet the world occasionally behaves as if it has received an instruction. A wave will stop halfway through its fall, hang in the air as a curved wall, and release all at once in another direction. A mountain will surface without displacing any water, the sea simply parting around it. Once, far across the basin, an entire sheet of ocean lifted into the sky and remained there for several hours, trembling like a suspended lake. Under it, the exposed seabed shone with fresh facets. By morning the water had settled again, and the new ridge was already wearing a thin crown of ice.

The cold is not severe, but it is everywhere: in the metal taste of the spray, in the blue shadows beneath cloud, in the long pause before a stone warms under sunlight. The light itself is ordinary—clear when the weather opens, silvered by distance, pale at the edges of storms—but there is so much space for it to cross that it seems to arrive with the quality of an event. Sunrise may take a whole day to move across the basin. At night, the water keeps a little brightness in its depths, not glowing, exactly, but refusing complete darkness.

This is a place before its outlines have hardened. Its storms are not passing through; they are helping decide what the land will be. The sparse growth is not a beginning of abundance so much as a notation, a few marks written on the stone to show that the process has started. Everywhere, the unfinished world shifts beneath itself. New heights rise. Old ones sink. The sea fills the gaps, and then, in sudden columns and lifted curtains, abandons them.

From a high rock above the water, the scale is almost impossible to keep. The nearest wave may be the size of a country. The distant thunder may be the sound of an ocean floor splitting open. The sky does not feel above you so much as continuous with the sea, another depth in which weather is being made.

And the whole landscape, for all its noise and motion, remains nearly empty. No life looks back from it. Nothing claims the new shores.

The planet is still at work.
