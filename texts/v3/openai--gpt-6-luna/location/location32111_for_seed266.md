The high country begins above the clouds, though there are few clouds to speak of. From the lower slopes it appears as a pale rim in the sky: a continent-sized tableland lifted so far above the surrounding world that distance becomes difficult to judge. Its cliffs are not sheer so much as unfinished—vast faces scored by old collapses, long ramps of scree, shelves worn into one another over ages. The plateau itself is a country of ridges and basins, much of it open enough that a person can watch weather travel for days before it arrives.

There is no water lying about. No rivers, no lakes, no green seam in the distance. The ground is mineral through and through: chalk-colored stone, iron-dark ribs, broad crusts of salt, and fields of glassy grit that shift with the wind. In the basins, wind has sorted the surface into shallow ripples and fine, pale drifts. On the heights, the rock is bare, polished on one side and rough on the other, as if the whole plateau has been slowly combed by a patient hand. Its forms are old. Nothing here looks newly broken except where people have cut into it.

The warmth is steady rather than bright. Sunlight falls cleanly through the thin, dry air, and the day’s heat sinks into the dark rock without much shimmer. At night the stone releases it in a slow exhalation. A traveler can feel the temperature change most strongly in sheltered hollows, where warm air collects and hangs close to the ground. The horizon often appears closer than it is. The air is so clear that far-off escarpments seem etched against the sky, but there is a faint mineral haze near the ground, raised by every passing current.

Life is plentiful here, though nothing grows in the familiar sense. There are no plants, no mats of moss, no visible forests of stone. Instead, the plateau belongs to animals that make their living from heat, minerals, one another, and the minute organic dust gathered out of the upper atmosphere. Some are small and numerous: pale, many-legged grazers that scrape salt from the rock; clicking colonies of burrowers that retreat into cracks at the first change in temperature. Others travel in great, widely spaced herds, their hard backs catching the light like loose pieces of the landscape. At certain seasons the high air fills with drifting, sail-bodied creatures. They cross the plateau without flapping, riding currents too weak for a person to feel.

The largest animals are seldom seen all at once. Their tracks may run for miles along a ridge, each impression holding a little heap of windblown dust. They gather at the warmest exposed stone, where mineral seams draw smaller life, and then pass on, leaving the ground almost unchanged. The ecosystem is abundant in presence but not crowded. One may cross a day’s walk of open country between encounters, then find a basin alive with motion: burrow-mouths opening and closing, scales flickering in the light, a whole slope subtly rearranging itself as animals move beneath the crust.

The settlements are built where the plateau narrows into useful corridors—beside old quarry faces, at the mouths of passes, around the few stable geothermal vents. They do not sprawl continuously. A city may consist of several districts separated by long reaches of empty ground, linked by straight service roads and raised causeways. From far away the buildings look low and blunt, colored to match the stone. Up close they are layered constructions: foundations sunk into the rock, towers braced against the wind, pipes and enclosed conveyors threading between workshops. Nothing is decorative without also being functional, though the people have learned to make function beautiful. Cooling fins cast long shadows across pale walls. Factory stacks are broad, squat chimneys that release little visible smoke.

Industry here has learned to work without water. The factories crack stone, sort salts, refine metal, and use heat directly wherever possible. Great engines turn slowly in sheltered halls. Some are powered by the plateau’s deep warmth; others draw energy from the high winds, whose force is gentle at ground level but immense across a span of months. Conveyors move ore in measured streams. Cargo crawls along rail lines sunk into shallow cuts, making no sound until it passes close, when the ground gives a muted, sustained vibration.

This is not a silent place, but sound has to work hard to become audible. The open country absorbs voices. Wind makes a low, even brushing across the grit. In the settlements, a distant turbine turns with a soft recurring tremor; metal expands and ticks in the warmth; somewhere a warning horn sounds once, and its note travels so far it seems to belong to the horizon. The animals are more often heard through contact with the ground than by their voices: a shiver in a wall, a faint patter inside a service tunnel, the scrape of claws on the other side of a sheet of stone. Loudness feels wasteful here. Most things announce themselves only when they are near.

Weather is slow. A broad current can take weeks to shift the dust from one basin to another. The high clouds, when they form, are thin and widely spaced, with long gaps of clear sky between them. Storms arrive as changes in color first: a distant ridge becomes violet, then the light loses its sharpness, then the air begins to carry the taste of cold metal. Wind-carried grit polishes exposed surfaces grain by grain. Cliffs retreat by small collapses that may be centuries apart. Roads remain in use for generations, though each is gradually buried at its edges.

The plateau’s rules are easy to learn and difficult to forget. There is no dependable surface water, so every settlement measures its waste, even where its machines do not need water to run. Shade is more important than shelter from rain. A fissure can be a refuge from heat, a route for animals, or a warning that the crust is thin. The most fertile places, by local standards, are not soft or dark but chemically complex: old impact hollows, warm vents, the margins of industrial spoil, where mineral dust and animal remains have accumulated over very long periods.

From a height, the whole country looks nearly empty. Roads are pale scratches; the cities are small knots of geometry; animal migrations vanish into the scale of the land. But the emptiness is only a kind of spacing. Beneath every ridge are hidden routes and chambers. Across every basin, invisible currents carry fine material and the seeds of animal life, though nothing there is a seed. The factories work, the herds pass, the stone sheds its grains. Everything changes, but so slowly that the plateau seems less like a place in motion than a place remembering how it became.
