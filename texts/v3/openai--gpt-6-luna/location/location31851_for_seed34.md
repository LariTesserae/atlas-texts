The highland is so broad that its far rim is mostly a change in the color of the sky. It lies above the forests, though little forest remains here: only scattered mats of tough grass, low gray shrubs, and dark cushions of moss where water seeps from the stone. The ground rises and settles in long, weathered folds, a plateau broken by basins and shallow escarpments. Nothing looks newly made. Even the cliffs have the softened edges of things that have been exposed to rain for ages.

Water is never far below the surface. It gathers in shallow hollows, threads down cracks, and shines in the seams between paving stones. After rain, the whole country seems to exhale. Mist lifts from the basins in slow, pale sheets; roofs bead and run; the cut faces of the hills darken. The air is cool and close, sometimes carrying the clean mineral smell of wet slate, sometimes the faint, earthy smell of moss. There are no great rivers, but water is everywhere in small, persistent forms: a rill under a wall, a pool in a courtyard, a line of droplets falling from a ledge.

Across this country, settlement has accumulated rather than spread. From a distance, the cities resemble bands of darker rock laid along the folds of the plateau. Closer in, they resolve into tightly packed houses, terraces, covered passages, courts, stairs, and retaining walls. Buildings are low, thick-walled, and made from the stone beneath them, their roofs weighted against weather. One roof becomes another’s path; a stair disappears into a doorway and emerges higher up; courtyards are divided into narrow plots where a little rain-fed green can be coaxed from the soil. The oldest walls are difficult to distinguish from the ridge itself. Newer ones are simply less worn.

The density is not noisy. People move through the settlement in small numbers, mostly under cover, crossing a passage or pausing beneath an overhang while water drips nearby. There are no engines or bright signals, no machinery to interrupt the hush. The city’s works are made of hand, stone, and gravity: channels cut to carry runoff, cisterns sunk into courtyards, steps worn into slopes, walls placed to slow the water and keep the hillside from slipping. What has to move moves slowly. What can be left to flow is left to flow.

A person walking through the streets hears the soft scrape of soles on grit, a door settling in its frame, water tapping into a basin. The larger sounds belong to weather: distant rain crossing the upland, wind passing over rooflines, the muted rush of runoff somewhere beneath the paving. Even these sounds seem contained by the place. In the inner courts, walls and damp stone absorb them until only the nearest drops remain.

Life is sparse but not absent. Moss darkens the shaded joints; pale grasses root in roof cracks; small flowering plants appear briefly beside drains and then vanish into the cool months. Birds are occasional visitors, seen crossing open sky rather than nesting in the dense roofs. Now and then a small animal leaves prints in the mud along an outlying channel. Most of the living presence here is subtle: roots working through old mortar, lichen spreading over a parapet, seeds carried into a courtyard by wind and water.

The landscape is active without seeming restless. Rain alters the shine of every surface. Mist shifts between districts and hides one ridge while revealing another. Channels fill, spill, and empty; thin soil gathers against walls, and the walls hold it. The city changes at the pace of weather and wear, adding a passage here, losing a corner there, making use of whatever the hillside gives it. Nothing announces a beginning. Nothing has quite reached an end.

In clear light the highland is ordinary in its colors—gray stone, brown earth, pale sky, dull green—but it is immense in its repetitions. Terrace after terrace follows the contours, and beyond them the same damp ridges continue until distance erases the distinction between natural slope and inhabited ground. The place feels less like a city set upon a landscape than a landscape that, over a very long time, learned the shape of a city.
