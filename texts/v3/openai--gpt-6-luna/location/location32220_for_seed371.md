The place is a cleft in a high, newly broken ridge: a small landscape with an enormous vertical sense. Its boundaries are close enough to find in a few minutes, but the walls rise so steeply that the sky is only a narrow strip, often lost altogether in shadow. The rock is dark basalt, split into sharp planes and raw ledges. Nothing here has had much time to soften. Fresh chips lie beneath the cliffs, and thin cracks still shed grit when the wind shifts.

Greenery crowds every place the stone permits it. Ferns push from seams; moss thickens on ledges; wiry shrubs take hold in pockets of soil no wider than a hand. In the deepest part, the growth is lush but pallid, stretched toward the sky-slot. It beads with moisture even when no rain falls. Water arrives intermittently: a brief shower, a film of condensation, a sudden spill from some unseen hollow above. It gathers in dark basins and slips away through cracks before it can become a stream.

The air is cool and close. Light rarely reaches the floor. Near the opening, the plants shine wetly for a little while; farther in, their shapes merge into black layers, and the rock seems to absorb the distinction between surface and depth. There is no animal life to stir the growth. No wings flicker in the high air, no small bodies rustle in the ferns. The vegetation moves only when gusts funnel down the cleft or when the ground trembles.

It trembles often. The ridge is young, unsettled stone, and its narrow floor is restless with runoff and loose debris. A ledge cracks; stones rattle down; water hisses briefly over a warm seam and vanishes. Wind surges through the cut in uneven bursts, making the ferns flatten and rise again. The sound is loud enough to fill the place: a constant low rush from the upper slopes, broken by clatters of falling rock and the hard slap of occasional water.

One small industrial machine stands near the cleft’s entrance, bolted to a shelf of stone: a drainage pump with a squat engine, exposed pipes, and a belt that shudders as it runs. It looks less like the beginning of a settlement than a practical concession to the place’s instability. The pump coughs and rattles over the natural roar, drawing water from a sump and sending it away through a pipe that disappears into the rock. Around it, the ridge remains largely untouched. Moss has already begun to cover the bolts.

The machine gives the cleft a rhythm, though never a reliable one. Its engine labors, falters, catches; the wind drowns it, then drops away and leaves the clank distinct. Each pulse seems to travel through the stone. The plants quiver. A shower of grit comes down from above. Somewhere in the dark, water starts running again.

Nothing here is mysterious in the supernatural sense. It is simply a young, steep, damp piece of earth still finding its shape: too confined for easy passage, too green to look barren, too dark to feel welcoming. Its smallness is deceptive. The walls make every sound vast, every movement immediate, and every new crack feel like the beginning of the landscape changing once more.
