The town occupies a shallow bowl where the ground has only recently decided to stay above the water.

It is not built beside a marsh so much as threaded through one: a tight knot of lanes, sheds, cisterns, drying racks, and little squared courtyards squeezed onto a low island of dark silt. Beyond the last walls, the land goes level and shining. Rainwater gathers in every hollow; seepage beads from the banks; the channels between buildings are never quite empty. Even on a clear day, a skin of water lies over the paving stones, reflecting the gray sky in broken pieces.

The ground is young. It has risen in small, uncertain ridges where mud, gravel, and the pale rubble of older shores have been pressed together. Its edges are raw: short cuts in the bank, fresh slides, tiny cliffs that collapse without ceremony after heavy rain. Here and there, a stone juts from the soil with its sides still sharp, as if the land had just dropped it there. There are no old trees to hold the place in shape. Sparse reeds grow beyond the walls, and a few tough, salt-paled plants cling to the drier rooflines. Moss occupies the seams in the masonry, but not much else takes root.

The buildings are low and packed close. Their walls are made from what can be gathered without hauling it far: bundled reed, mud brick, split boards, flat stones, woven mats rubbed with clay. Roofs overlap above the narrow lanes, shedding water into a carefully tended web of gutters. Some gutters are hollowed logs; others are little channels shaped by hand into the walls. They lead toward communal settling pits, where rainwater stands brown at the edges and clear in the middle. People know which water is for washing, which is for cooking, and which must be left alone until the next hard rain flushes it clean.

The town has no grand center. It has a well, a storehouse raised on stone feet, a patch of dry ground used for trade, and a meeting shelter with a roof thick enough to keep the drumming rain from drowning conversation. All of these are hemmed in by dwellings and workspaces. Nothing is far away, yet nothing is spacious. A person carrying a basket must turn sideways for another to pass. Nets hang over doors; damp cloaks darken the walls; bundles of kindling are stacked beneath eaves and watched anxiously for leaks.

The people have learned to build for movement rather than permanence. Floorboards can be lifted. Door sills can be raised. A wall that begins to slump is braced with forked poles before it can pull its neighbors down. At the edge of town, stakes mark a boundary that shifts a little each season. Nobody mistakes it for a true shore. It is only where the water has agreed, for now, to stop.

The weather is cool and close. Cloud light fills the streets without casting much shadow. In the morning, moisture hangs in the woven walls and gathers on the undersides of roofs. By afternoon, mist may loosen from the shallow water beyond town and drift low through the reeds. When the sky clears, the place brightens plainly, almost cheerfully, but the stones remain slick and the mud holds the cold.

There is little birdsong. A few small waders pick their way along the outer pools, quick-legged and watchful. At night, tiny pale insects sometimes gather above the water channels, though no one agrees whether they are newly hatched or merely drawn from somewhere unseen. Fish are scarce in the nearest pools; they seem to favor the deeper channels beyond the town’s reach. People set woven traps anyway. The traps come back empty often enough that no one relies on them, but they are repaired every morning.

Sound here is close and softened: water clicking through gutters, the scrape of a paddle against stone, a reed roof ticking as it dries. Now and then a hammer taps, though metal is costly and most work is done with wedges, bone, stone, and fire-hardened wood. Conversations travel only a few doorways. The damp air seems to keep them from going farther. When the wind rises, it makes a faint, hollow note through the channels under the houses, like someone blowing across the mouth of a buried jar.

The water has its own small rules. It drains slowly from the north side of the bowl and faster from the south, though no one has found a visible cause. Certain puddles vanish within an hour of rain; others remain through the dry weeks, clear and dark as polished slate. Every few days, a narrow stream appears under the eastern wall, runs across the lane, and disappears into the ground beneath the storehouse. The builders have left a gap there rather than seal it. They say the water needs somewhere to go. They also say that if it is blocked, it will choose a worse place.

The town is always being adjusted. A plank moved here, a channel scraped clean there; a basket of gravel tipped into a rut; a house shifted a handspan back from the sinking bank. The whole settlement feels less like a city placed upon the land than a temporary agreement with it—crowded, damp, industrious, and alert to the next change beneath its feet. Outside the last ring of walls, the new ground gleams under the ordinary light, still taking shape.
