The drowned highland lies beneath a clear, warm sea.

Its peaks do not break the surface, but the water above them is shallow enough that daylight reaches the stone. From below, the sun is a trembling pale disk, softened by the moving skin of the sea. Light falls through it in broad, ordinary shafts: bright on the ridges, green in the hollows, and blue where the land drops away. Beyond the high ground, the shelf descends into deeper water, and the brightness thins gradually rather than ending at a hard edge.

The terrain is young. Black volcanic rock has split into fins and steep-sided knobs, its edges still sharp, its fractures crowded with life. Here and there, pale mineral seams show through the dark stone. Fresh slopes of pumice and ash gather in sheltered pockets; larger slabs have tilted against one another as if the ground has only just finished settling. The highland is not a single mountain but a broad region of ridges, basins, and drowned valleys, each visible from the next until the farther formations fade into blue.

Vegetation fills nearly every space that can hold it. Long, translucent blades stream from cracks in the rock. Thick fronds rise in submerged groves, branching upward and then spreading beneath the bright surface like canopies under a ceiling. In the basins, rounded mats of soft green growth cushion the stone, while narrow red and amber plants thread along the ridgelines. Their colors are vivid but never gaudy; filtered through water, they seem to hold their own light without shining. In places, roots have stitched loose rubble together, making living ledges over steep drops.

The growth is dense enough to make corridors. Passing through one, a traveler might see only layers of leaf and frond, all gently inclined in the same direction. Then the thicket opens without warning onto a broad stony saddle, and the full scale of the drowned highland appears: long crests, sunlit slopes, dark basins, and the suggestion of still more terrain beyond. Nothing built interrupts the view. There are no posts, walls, anchors, or marks of human passage. The rock and the growth have made every boundary themselves.

The sea is warm even where the ridges rise high. Heat seeps from occasional vents in the stone, gathering in quiet pools where the water wavers and plants grow in unusual abundance. These places are not violent. A faint shimmer rises from cracks, and small clouds of mineral dust drift upward before settling over the foliage. The rest of the water is remarkably still. Currents move the forest slowly, more like a long breath than a tide: fronds bow, pause, and return, and the motion may take most of a minute to complete.

Animal life is scarce. A small, pale creature may appear in the distance, cross an opening, and vanish among the vegetation before it can be followed. Now and then something stirs the underside of a frond, though nothing answers the disturbance with a call. The absence is not desolate so much as spacious. The plants seem to occupy the place’s attention; everything else is occasional. Even the silence has layers—the hush of water, the far-off murmur of deep channels, the soft brush of leaves rubbing together. There is no wind down here, and no thunder, only the faint, continuous sound of water passing over stone.

The strange thing about the highland is the way its depth refuses to feel simple. A ridge may appear close, sunlit and easy to reach, yet the valley beyond it drops away into a blue so deep that distance becomes difficult to judge. Some slopes seem to continue farther downward than the surrounding sea should allow. At other times, the surface light gathers over a basin and makes the whole formation look suspended, as if the stone were floating beneath the water rather than buried in it.

Nothing changes quickly. New rock breaks down grain by grain. Plants widen their hold over decades. Fine sediment settles on the sheltered sides of the ridges, and the sea carries only a little of it away. The place is still forming, but it does so patiently: a young landscape already grown lush, its sharp bones softened only by living things.
