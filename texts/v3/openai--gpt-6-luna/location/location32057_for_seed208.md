The country lies almost level, though not quite: a long, shallow basin cupped between low shelves of stone, open far enough that the horizon seems to have been worn down rather than reached. Everything is old. Ridges have softened into backs; cliffs have slumped into ledges; the stones underfoot are rounded, polished, and fitted together by ages of water. There are no young peaks, no sharp breaks. Even the cracks look tired.

Water is everywhere without becoming a lake. It seeps through the pale rock and gathers in broad, shallow sheets, blackening the ground. Thin streams cross the flats, divide around islands of stone, then vanish under them, only to surface again a little farther on. The air holds a cold wetness. In hollows, it condenses on the undersides of overhangs and falls in slow, steady drops. Rain passes often, but lightly; the land is already saturated enough that more water merely changes which channels are speaking.

There is no green. No grass takes hold in the seams, and no tree rises above the stones. The ground is mostly gray, pearl-white, and the deep brown of old iron, with occasional bands of glassy black where ancient heat once melted the surface. These colors show through a low, dim light that seems to have no clear direction. Clouds veil the sky, and the wet stone gives back only a dull gleam. Distance fades into silver haze.

The life here is animal life, abundant and difficult to see all at once. At the water’s edge, crowds of low-shelled creatures cling to the rock—some no larger than a thumb, others broad as shields, their shells ridged and worn like miniature landscapes. Pale, many-jointed things thread the flooded cracks. Long-bodied swimmers move in the channels, surfacing without a splash, their smooth backs stippled with tiny eyes. On the flats, squat beasts shuffle from pool to pool on thick, jointed limbs, scraping films of mineral and smaller organisms from the stone. Their tracks fill with water almost as soon as they pass.

Above them, the air is full of wings. Dark, narrow shapes rise in loose clouds from the shallows, wheel once over the basin, and settle somewhere beyond sight. Larger fliers coast low through the mist, making no effort to avoid the ground until the last moment. Their calls carry a strange metallic edge, as if the sound has passed through a hollow pipe. Nothing here sings in a single voice. The whole basin makes noise.

Water pours and ticks and booms under the rock. The channels roar softly at first, then swell into a continuous thunder where they drop through buried passages. Shell-creatures knock together in the pools. Wings beat the air. Calls answer calls across the flats, overlapping until the distance itself seems to vibrate. Near one of the deeper seams, the sound is so great that a person can feel it in the teeth. There are brief pauses, but never silence—only moments when one part of the landscape falls quiet enough for another to be heard.

Scattered across this emptiness are small signs of hands. A ring of stones surrounds a vent where warm water rises. Beyond it, a low shelter has been made from flat slabs, its roof weighted with smooth boulders. A narrow causeway runs toward a higher patch of ground, though much of it has sunk beneath the water. The structures are plain and weather-softened, made with tools that leave simple scars. No wall is high enough to keep out the flood, and none is meant to. Whoever built here knew the water would return.

A few poles stand at the edge of the shallows. Their tops are polished bare by wind, weather, or use; small shells hang from them on cords and chatter violently whenever the air moves. Nearby, a stone basin catches dripping water. It is worn nearly smooth, but someone has cut a channel into its lip to guide the overflow. The channel is old enough to have rounded at its edges. There may have been generations of builders, or only one people returning again and again to the same useful places.

The basin changes in slow, audible ways. Water levels rise through the night and drain by morning. Whole shelves of stone disappear beneath the dark sheet, then emerge shining. The animals follow those changes with practiced urgency, pouring into newly flooded channels or gathering around the pools that remain. When the water draws back, the exposed flats are alive with movement: shells opening, wings lifting, narrow bodies slipping into cracks. The land is not fertile in the familiar sense. It does not grow. It reveals, covers, and feeds.

Beyond the last visible ledge, the basin continues under fog. The stone there is no different from the stone nearby, and the calls are no fainter; they simply come from places that cannot be seen. The country feels less empty than unoccupied by anything large enough to mark it. Water, animal life, and time have made nearly all the decisions. The scattered shelters and carved channels are only small agreements with a world that was old before they were made, and will remain much the same after they have worn flat.
