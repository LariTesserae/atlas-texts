The lowland begins without a shoreline. It is a level of dark surfaces, stretching beyond sight in every direction, with only the occasional rise of a gantry, chimney, or broad-backed pump house to measure distance. Nothing here is quite ground. The basin is paved in overlapping plates of fired ceramic, poured slag, and blackened concrete, all laid so long ago that their joins have softened into shallow channels. There is no soil beneath them, or none that the water can reach. Even the hills at the far edge are made: old spoil mounds settled smooth beneath a skin of stone panels, the last unevenness left in a country almost worn flat.

Water lies everywhere, but rarely deep. It gathers in the seams, silvers the broad plates, and moves in thin black sheets toward the drains. Rain falls often, though the sky is difficult to see through the low ceiling of cloud and vapor. The air condenses on railings and pipes. Water beads on the undersides of bridges. It seeps from cracks in the higher structures and hangs there before dropping, one slow drip at a time, into a landscape already full of water.

The old machinery is still operating. Far apart, enormous pumping halls draw and discharge the basin’s water in cycles whose purpose is no longer evident. Their fans turn behind grilles furred with mineral deposits; pistons rise and fall with a steadiness more like a tide than a factory shift. Every few minutes, a series of gates opens somewhere out in the dark, and a new current runs through the shallow channels. The water does not always take the lowest route. Sometimes it pauses at a junction, shivers, then climbs a slight incline toward a hidden intake. The whole plain seems to breathe through its conduits.

Sound fills the place more completely than light. The pumps produce a continuous, immense roaring, layered with the hiss of rain, the slap of water against metal, and the low clang of plates shifting in their beds. Near the machinery, the noise is physical: it presses against the chest and makes speech useless. Farther away, it becomes a single unbroken note, so deep and wide that smaller sounds disappear inside it. Once in a while, a pipe or tower catches the wind and gives a long, wavering tone. Nothing answers.

There are no animals. No birds cross the haze; no insects gather around the few lamps. The sparse growth that does exist seems less like an ecosystem than a residue. Short, colorless mats cling to warm seams near the outlets. Pale crusts spread over sheltered steps. Here and there, a thin stalk emerges from a drain grate and bends under the rain, though it never flowers. These forms appear to draw something from the water or the metal, rather than from any buried earth. They are widely separated, and the dark between them is immense.

The light seldom rises above a dim gray, and most of the landscape remains black. A few old industrial lamps still burn behind cages, making small amber stains in the vapor. Their reflections stretch across the wet plates and break apart whenever the water shifts. In the distance, a row of red indicators may blink on a tower, vanish, and reappear much farther along the basin—as if the system is passing a signal from one end of the plain to the other.

Age has rounded nearly everything. Corners have sagged into curves; ladders descend into the water at odd angles; the edges of platforms are worn smooth by weather and by the slow grinding of sediment. Yet the place has not collapsed. It has settled into a final, durable arrangement. The structures that once might have been temporary—conveyor bridges, spillways, service roads, rows of cylindrical tanks—now have the stillness and authority of cliffs. Their surfaces are scarred, but the scars have become part of their design.

The basin is active without seeming alive. Water rises, drains, returns. Gates open in sequence. Pressure moves through the pipes and passes on. The machinery does not hurry, and it does not stop. Beneath the constant roar, the old constructed land continues to arrange its water, preserving a rhythm that may once have served a city, an industry, or some need no longer remembered.

At night—or what passes for night here—the lamps grow smaller in the haze. The rain keeps falling. Far across the flats, a pump engages with a tremendous shudder, and a dark sheet of water begins moving toward the horizon. Nothing else moves with it.
