The heights begin abruptly, as though the ground has been pulled upward and left unfinished.

A dark plain runs into a range of immense, sharp-backed ridges. They are not mountains in the softened sense: no rounded shoulders, no valleys patiently worn into them. Their faces are planes of fresh fracture, black basalt and dull iron-gray stone, with pale mineral seams exposed like the first lines in a drawing. In places the ridges rise into needle summits, so high that their upper edges vanish against the darkness. Below them, the land is broken into shelves, steep ramps, and long tilted slabs. Nothing has had time to settle into familiar shapes.

The ground is warm underfoot. Heat leaks from narrow cracks, not as flame but as a wavering distortion, bending the little light there is. Here and there, a seam shows a low red or amber glow far beneath the surface. The glow is too faint to brighten the landscape; it only reveals that the rock is still forming, still being pushed into place. There is no water, no mud, no snowmelt. Loose grit lies in shallow drifts where the slopes have shed it, but even these drifts seem temporary, interrupted by abrupt edges and angular heaps.

The darkness is nearly complete. The sky has no clear stars, only a faint, diffuse pallor above the highest peaks, as if light were trying to pass through stone. The land is readable by contrast: the occasional mineral glimmer, the dim red of a fissure, the pale faces of newly exposed rock. Far off, a ridge may appear and disappear—not because it moves across the horizon, but because the darkness makes distance uncertain.

Yet the landscape is not still. The whole region is in the act of becoming. A shelf tilts by a fraction; somewhere beyond sight, a new cleft opens. A line of stones slowly tips down a slope, not in a landslide but one by one, as if each has just remembered gravity. In the distance, a tall pinnacle leans, pauses, and breaks at its base. Its upper half settles onto a neighboring ridge with such slowness that the impact is almost inaudible. Dust rises, hangs briefly in the warm air, then sinks without wind to carry it away.

The sounds are rare and immense. Long silences are interrupted by a deep internal knock, a crack traveling through the bedrock, or the brief hiss of hot stone meeting colder stone. These sounds do not echo much. The cliffs swallow them. At times a low vibration passes through the ground and can be felt in the teeth before it is heard, then the region falls quiet again.

There is little that could be called life. No grasses or trees take root in the fissures; no animals cross the open shelves. Fine, branching mineral growths gather around a few warm cracks, dark and brittle, closer to crystal than plant. They catch the dim light along their edges. Their presence is sparse enough that the eye can mistake them for fractures in the rock. They neither sway nor spread visibly, but after a long interval a new cluster may appear somewhere the ground has opened.

Scattered across the ridges are small constructions, too few to make a settlement. Some are low, roofless enclosures of stacked stone, wedged into the lee of a wall. Others are narrow uprights set in pairs, their surfaces unmarked except for bands of pale mineral fused around them. They have no visible machinery, and nothing moves inside them. A few are already split by the shifting ground. Others stand on ledges that did not exist long ago, or cannot have existed for long. It is difficult to say whether they were built here, or whether the rising land simply gathered them out of its own layers.

The place offers no stable path. A route across one shelf may be interrupted by a fresh drop the next time it is seen; a ridge that seemed impassable may open along a clean, new fracture. The land does not erode so much as rearrange itself. Its shapes are raw, severe, and briefly held—mountains caught at the instant of their emergence, under a dark sky that gives no sign of how much time has passed.
