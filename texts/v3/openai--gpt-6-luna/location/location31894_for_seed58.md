The settlement occupies a pocket in the earth no larger than a few city blocks, though its stacked galleries make it feel deeper than it is. It has been cut into a fresh seam of black volcanic stone. The walls are still sharp-edged, their fractured faces glossy where heat draws moisture out of them. In places, the rock has not quite cooled: thin red-brown veins glow beneath the surface, and the air above them wavers.

Everything is close together. Walkways cross over one another on iron brackets; narrow stairs disappear between pipes, cisterns, and the backs of buildings. The buildings are mostly brick, slag-block, and riveted metal, pressed shoulder to shoulder around a central excavation. Some are workshops, some sleeping rooms, some merely enclosures built around machinery. Nothing rises very high before meeting the low stone ceiling. Smoke and steam drift along it in a slow, dim layer, caught by the ribs and flues that have been fixed there.

The light is not quite darkness. Caged lamps burn behind cloudy glass, supplemented by the muted orange of furnace doors and the occasional pale-blue flicker of an electrical arc. Moisture collects on every surface. It beads on handrails, shines in mortar joints, and runs down the rock in narrow threads. Most days there is no rain here, but water arrives in short, unpredictable events: condensation gathers until it breaks loose in a sudden shower from the ceiling; a pipe sweats through the night; a newly opened crack gives up a cupful, then goes dry for weeks. The residents have placed gutters beneath all of it.

Water is valuable, but never abundant enough to make a river. It travels through the settlement in channels cut into the floors, passing under grates and between workshop foundations before reaching a set of warm collecting tanks. These tanks are stained green and coppery at the edges. The water tastes of stone and iron. When it stands too long, a skin of mineral scale forms on top.

There is little vegetation. A few stubborn mats of pale growth cling to the coolest, wettest wall seams, and tiny, colorless fronds grow in the runoff channels where the lamps never reach. They are tended more like useful equipment than plants: protected from boots, scraped free of scale, and left to do what little they can. No animals live here. Nothing rustles or nests in the rafters. The pipes knock, belts turn, water ticks into metal, but the silence between these sounds is unusually complete.

Industry provides the settlement’s pulse. Small furnaces burn behind low doors. Presses shape sheets of soft metal; grinders throw brief, dazzling sprays of sparks. A cable winch hauls stone from a new cut in the far wall, where pale, unweathered surfaces show through the black crust. The machinery is compact but crowded, often sharing walls and power shafts. It runs in shifts, not continuously. When one workshop goes quiet, another begins, so the place never entirely stops—yet its sounds are muffled by the enclosing rock, subdued to a distant hum, an occasional clang, the slow breathing of bellows.

The ground is still changing. Fresh fractures open where the heat has swollen the stone. Fine grit sifts down after each small tremor. A corridor built only recently has already acquired a new bend where the wall pushed inward, and a row of lamps marks the edge of a depression that deepened overnight. These are not disasters so much as ordinary adjustments. The inhabitants build lightly where they can, leave joints unsealed, and hang warning chimes from the newest cracks. Sometimes a chime rings by itself. Sometimes the stone moves without sound.

The air smells of hot metal, wet ash, and mineral water. Heat pools in the lower galleries and lifts through the shafts, carrying steam and the faint oily tang of the machines. The coolest place is near the cisterns, where people gather to work, wash, or simply stand in the damp air. Even there, the stone is warm to the touch.

This is a small city built inside a place that has not finished becoming a place. Its streets are narrow, its walls raw, its water intermittent. Light, heat, and machinery make their own weather in the close dark, while the earth continues, quietly, to press new shapes into the settlement.
