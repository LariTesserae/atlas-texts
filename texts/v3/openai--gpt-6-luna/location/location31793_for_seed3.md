The city has been built on a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain.

It rises above a low country of cloud, not in a clean summit but in a series of enormous shelves and tilted slabs, as if the earth had begun to buckle and then paused to consider the shape. The highest districts are lost in a dim ceiling of vapor. Below them, pale cliffs show fresh stone: raw seams of slate, green-black basalt, chalky mineral crusts still too sharp to have been weathered into familiar forms. Here and there the rock has folded into ridges that stop abruptly, or opened into narrow ravines with no visible source. The paths between them are old in use and new in substance. A stair may descend for a thousand steps through the city and end at a bare wall of unbroken mountain.

The air is cold and damp enough to bead on iron. Rain comes rarely, and usually sideways, driven through the heights in brief silver veils. More often, moisture gathers out of sight: in the undersides of bridges, inside abandoned furnaces, along the cold faces of retaining walls. It drips from pipes that carry no water and beads on windows facing away from the clouds. The city’s gutters are mostly dry, but the stones around them are dark with a permanent wetness. In low courtyards, shallow pools collect without anyone seeing them fill. By morning they may be gone, leaving behind a fringe of moss or a scatter of tiny white shells.

The settlement is extensive but never quite dense. It occupies ledges, saddles, and artificial platforms across a broad span of mountain. From one district, the next is visible across a gulf of fog or open air, marked by smokestacks, lift towers, and the long horizontal lines of covered conveyors. Whole slopes remain bare between neighborhoods. There are gaps where no building stands—not because the ground is unusable, but because no one has yet agreed that it is ground. The city’s maps give these spaces names such as Eastward Shelf, New Rock, or the Unmeasured Quarter. A few maps simply leave them blank.

Construction is methodical, industrial, and unfinished. Foundries squat near the lowest shelves, their furnaces glowing dull orange behind thick, mica-clouded windows. Cableways haul stone uphill in creaking baskets. Rail lines run along the edges of the inhabited terraces, sometimes vanishing through tunnels that were not there the year before. Great cranes move slowly against the fog, their arms turning by small increments, as though the whole city were being assembled by a machine too large to see. Most buildings are made from local stone and riveted metal. Their foundations are deep, though no engineer can say how deep is enough. Supports are bolted into rock that occasionally shifts by the width of a finger—not in an earthquake, but over the course of months, with no tremor and no sound.

There is an order to the city, though it is an order made to accommodate uncertainty. Streets are numbered by elevation as well as direction. Public stairways have landings where surveyors wait for their instruments to agree. Bridges are built with joints that can be loosened and reset. The old quarter has been reinforced three times, yet its oldest houses stand at angles that seem to belong to a different slope. They have doors opening onto blank stone, windows looking down over roofs that are now above them. Residents use these rooms anyway. They hang curtains over the stone-facing doors and put flowerpots on the sills.

Vegetation is richest where the city leaks. Moss spreads along warm exhaust ducts. Ferns crowd the mouths of drainage tunnels. Gardens are laid out on narrow terraces, their beds edged with scrap rail and old boiler plate. They produce dark leaves, small tough roots, and pale fruit that ripens without much color. On the higher shelves, wind-pruned junipers grow in crevices, their roots threading through new rock. Hanging from some cliff faces are long veils of grass and flowering vine, nourished by invisible condensation. In the mist, these plants give the impression that the mountain is growing a green coat from the inside out.

The animals have made a city of their own among the human one. Black-winged birds gather on signal wires in such numbers that the wires sag. Small, blunt-nosed mammals nest in warm machinery housings and emerge at shift changes to forage among the rails. Pale moths cloud around lamps even in daylight, though the lamps are rarely bright. On quiet terraces, herds of narrow-legged goats move along ledges that appear too thin to hold them. They sometimes pass through fences without breaking them. Their bells can be heard several districts away, though the animals themselves may be standing just across the street.

There are other sounds: the measured thump of stampers, the hiss of steam, wheels grinding over joints in the rail, the deep whistle used to signal between shelves. None of it forms a constant roar. The city’s noise travels oddly through the damp air, arriving in separated pieces. A hammer strike may sound close enough to touch, followed by half an hour of silence and then the faint clatter of a workshop far below. Birds are louder than the machines in the upper districts. At dusk, when the factories reduce their output, thousands of wings beat through the fog, and from somewhere beneath the city comes a sound like water pouring into a cavern.

The light is dim even when the clouds lift. The sun appears as a blurred disc, weak and colorless; it casts shadows that do not always fall downhill. In some alleys, the daylight arrives late, sliding along the walls as though it has to find a route through them. Lamps burn behind blue glass. At night, the city is marked by scattered windows, furnace mouths, and the red beads of warning lights along the cableways. Beyond those points, the mountain disappears. The sky and the open air become indistinguishable.

The strangest effects are treated as ordinary hazards, like loose masonry or bad wiring. In the West Lift station, people sometimes wait for a car that has already passed them, arriving from the direction it was meant to travel. A bell rung in the upper foundry can be heard before it is struck. Some staircases are longer on the descent than on the ascent. Every few years, a survey crew discovers that a familiar ridge has moved several hundred meters—though the ridge is still visible from the place where it was first measured. The city records these discrepancies carefully. It does not attempt to correct them all.

Change here is slow enough to be mistaken for permanence. New rock presses up through old paving. Condensation darkens one wall, then another, over decades. The cranes turn. The cableways carry their loads. Moss advances a finger’s breadth each season over the cold iron ribs of the city. High above, the unfinished mountain continues to rise, so gradually that only the oldest residents claim to notice it. They say that the clouds used to pass beneath the highest bridge. They say that the mountain was once lower, or the sky was once closer.

At the edge of the last inhabited shelf, a rail spur runs into a tunnel bored through stone still warm from the pressure beneath it. The track ends a short distance inside. Beyond the buffers, the tunnel continues downward, but its mouth is sealed with new masonry and marked with the city’s oldest warning sign: **DO NOT MEASURE BEYOND THIS POINT.**

From the overlook above it, the city seems small against the raw slopes, a scattering of metal and lamps on a continent of fresh stone. Yet its whistles, bells, and wingbeats travel far across the mountain. The place is loud in fragments, green in patches, and damp in all the wrong places. It does not seem ruined or complete. It seems to be waiting for the ground to decide what it has made.
