The basin is so low that its edges seem less like a shore than a slight lifting of the ground. It is small enough to take in at once: a shallow, enclosed reach of mineral flats, bounded by a dark shelf of stone. The air is warm and close. Everything is wet, but nothing is moving quickly.

Water lies in nearly every hollow, gathered into shallow pools and narrow seams between the stone. It slicks the surfaces in a permanent glaze; beads collect beneath overhangs, and the lowest ledges wear thin curtains of water that scarcely fall. The pools are clear at their edges and cloudy toward the middle, where pale mineral sediment has settled into softly banded floors. Their surfaces are so still they hold the dim light without quite reflecting it.

There are no plants. No moss softens the stones, no roots prise apart their seams. The ground is entirely mineral: dark, close-packed plates pressed against rounded knobs of pale rock, with narrow channels between them. The forms are old and settled, smoothed by long contact with water, though not worn away. Here and there, small crusts rise in folded ridges, like cooled wax or the edges of old waves. They are stone, not growth. Nothing in the basin appears to be spreading.

The closeness of the terrain gives the place a crowded feeling. There is little open water and little bare ground; each pool, ridge, and ledge fits tightly beside the next. A person can cross the basin, but only by choosing a careful path from one dry-topped stone to another. The surface is ankle-deep in most places, deeper in a few dark bowls whose bottoms cannot be seen. The boundary shelf is low enough to step over, yet it encloses the basin all the same.

Dim light reaches it from above—perhaps through a narrow cleft, perhaps under a low roof of cloud. It spreads evenly rather than making strong shadows. The stone is dark where it is wet and faintly silver where a thin film catches the light. Colors remain restrained: iron gray, chalk white, dull brown, the occasional greenish stain that is only mineral discoloration. Even at midday, the basin looks as if it is waiting for evening.

A few human-made marks interrupt the natural pattern. A low wall of fitted stones runs along one edge, half sunk in silt. Beside it stands a small shelter, roofed with flat slabs and open on one side; inside are a shallow hearth, a cracked clay jar, and a set of simple hand-cut grooves leading water toward a catchment hollow. The work is practical and old. Nothing has been repaired in a long while, but nothing has collapsed, either. The wall still holds. The channels still guide the water. The place has absorbed these small constructions into its settled arrangement.

The basin is quiet. Water makes no distinct sound unless disturbed: only a faint ticking from drops gathering and releasing beneath the stone lip, spaced so widely that each one seems deliberate. There may be an animal here—a pale, small thing tucked into one of the deeper pools—but life is scarce and keeps to the cracks. No calls carry across the flats. No insects cloud the air. The silence is not empty so much as occupied by the close presence of stone and water.

Nothing here seems inclined to change. The pools do not drain; the ridges do not crumble; the water does not hurry toward an outlet. The basin holds its warmth, its shallow water, its dimness. Whatever first shaped it has long since passed, leaving a world in which nearly everything has found its place—and stayed there.
