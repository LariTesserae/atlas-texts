The plateau rises from the ocean floor so gradually that, for days of travel, the depth changes by no more than the height of a hill. Then the ground breaks upward.

Black ridges thrust through the water in long, sharp-backed lines, their edges still raw from the upheaval that made them. Between them lie pale fields of fractured stone, shallow basins, and shelves of blue-grey rock. The formations look newly made: little has rounded them, and their faces are scored with clean fractures. Far below, the plateau’s flanks drop away into a dimness that seems less like distance than an opening in the world.

It is all submerged, but not all dark. The water is exceptionally clear. Ordinary daylight filters down from a distant, often clouded surface and lies across the high ground in wide, diluted bands. The upper ridges are bright enough to show every crack; the lower slopes fade through cold blue into black. Above, the ocean has its own weather: immense slow swells, storms whose shadows travel for hundreds of miles, and pale curtains of rain trembling down from the surface. Down here, those motions arrive as pressure and sound.

The cold is constant. Stone, water, and the metal of the scattered installations all seem to have been kept at the same temperature for ages. There are no plants, no kelp forests, no soft growth on the rocks. The mineral surfaces remain bare except where animal life has fastened itself in clusters: shells like porcelain knots, thin translucent mats, and small dark animals packed into the lee of ridges. They do not make a garden of the plateau. They make a map of its currents.

For the ground is not empty of life. In the immense water above it, shoals pass like moving weather. Silver-bodied animals stream in sheets so broad that they dim the light over whole valleys. Larger shapes travel singly beneath them, slow and pale, turning with a patience that suggests the plateau has been here for a very long time. Near the rock, small quick creatures emerge from cracks in bursts, then vanish again. They gather at the same ledges and channels generation after generation. Their abundance is startling when encountered—and then the water opens around them, miles of blue with nothing in it.

The sound never stops. Water presses over the ridge crests with a roar felt in the bones, not heard so much as endured. Narrow passes whistle and boom. Shoals crackle with innumerable clicks; the larger animals call in long, low notes that travel through the stone and arrive from impossible directions. On some shelves, old industrial machines add their own frequencies: a hammering pulse, a turbine’s steady thunder, the metallic complaint of cables drawn tight beneath the current. The combined noise is so large that silence has no place to settle.

The machines stand far apart. A pressure station occupies one ridge, its square towers and thick pipes anchored into fresh rock. Elsewhere there is a cluster of lights behind a window, a gantry straddling a chasm, a line of rust-dark pylons descending into the blue. They seem less like a settlement than a few marks left by a project whose scale was once difficult to imagine. Some still work. Their lamps burn steadily; their engines turn at the same rate whether or not anything is listening. Nothing here appears to be in a hurry.

The water moves, but the place scarcely changes. Currents loop around the same pinnacles and pour through the same cuts. The shoals return to familiar routes. Storms roll overhead and pass; the ridges do not soften, the machines do not spread, and the vast basins remain as they were. Even the newest fractures look as though they have been waiting a long time to become old.

At a distance the plateau has no clear edge. Its high country runs beneath the sea in every direction, with isolated summits lifting toward the light and deep valleys dividing them. A traveler can spend weeks crossing a single shelf and still see neither a shore nor another living presence. Then, without warning, the water fills with bodies and noise: a moving multitude in the empty blue, passing over the sharp stone as if following a road no one built.
