The basin is small enough that, from its rim, you can see nearly all of it: a shallow bowl of dark stone, perhaps the size of a village green, sunk just below the surrounding flats. It is never quite dry. Water lies in its cracks and hollows, sheets itself over the rock after rain, and gathers in a few deep, glassy pools. The air is cool and close. Even when no rain falls, the stone sweats.

Nothing grows here. No grass softens the edges, no roots split the paving. The surfaces are mineral: slate-grey, iron-brown, and black where they stay wet. They have been rounded by a very long time of water moving slowly over them. Channels cross and recross the basin, shallow as scratches, worn smooth at their bottoms. Along the lowest wall, the stone has been polished to a faint shine, as if the whole place were a bowl that someone has been washing for centuries.

Life is everywhere, but it does not rise on stems. The pools are crowded with small animals: pale, many-jointed things that cling beneath the waterline; dark shells that creep over one another like slow pieces of broken machinery; fine translucent threads that quiver from holes in the rock. In the deeper water, flat-bodied swimmers turn without disturbing the surface. Their backs show for an instant, slick and dull as wet slate, then vanish among the others. Little clicking mouths worry the mineral crust from the channels. When the water drains, the animals do not leave; they fold into seams, burrow beneath the basin’s skin, and wait for it to return.

Here and there, old human work remains. A squat iron housing leans against the upper wall, its door gone and its interior furred with orange corrosion. A pipe enters the rock and ends without explanation. Beside one pool, three short posts of black metal rise from the water, each capped with a ceramic knob. They may once have supported a pump or a gauge. Now a bead of water forms on each knob at the same interval, though no one has found a source for it. The buildings are too few to make a settlement, and too deliberate to be ruins of nature. They seem like tools left behind by someone who understood the basin’s habits and no longer needed to explain them.

The light never becomes strong. A low ceiling of cloud, or perhaps only the basin’s depth and the color of its stone, keeps everything in a softened half-dark. Reflections are close to the things they reflect. The surface of a pool may show a sliver of the iron housing, then hold it after the housing has moved out of view.

The place is quiet in the way a room is quiet when people are listening. Water ticks from the wall. A thin trickle passes under the stone with a sound like breath drawn through teeth. Beneath that, the packed animals make an occasional dry patter, too fine and numerous to separate into individual movements. Once in a while, something under the basin gives a low mechanical thrum. The metal posts answer by trembling, and the creatures in the pools turn together toward the sound.

The basin is not changing quickly. Its channels are old; its iron is nearly gone; its inhabitants have settled into the wet hollows and narrow ledges as if the rock had been made to fit them. Still, the water rises and falls. After rain, the little bowl fills to the marks on the wall, and the animals spread into every flooded seam. Later, as the level recedes, they gather again in the pools, darkening them until the water seems almost solid.

At the very bottom is a circular depression, no wider than a cartwheel. It is always full. Its water is clear enough to show the smooth stone beneath, but the bottom appears farther away than it should. Nothing swims there. Nothing grows there. From the rim, you can see the rest of the basin reflected in it—crowded, dim, and gently moving—and, for a moment, the reflection seems to contain more animals than the basin itself.
