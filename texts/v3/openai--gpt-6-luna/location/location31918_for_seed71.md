The uplands begin so gradually that, for a long time, they seem not to rise at all. A person could walk for days over cold, nearly level stone before noticing that the clouds have dropped below the horizon behind them. Then the land opens: a high country broad enough to make its own weather, its far edge lost in the pale blue of distance. There are no sharp peaks. The old mountains have worn down into long backs and shallow basins, their surfaces smoothed by ages of ice, wind, and rain until even the cliffs seem tired of falling.

Moisture comes by intervals. A veil of rain crosses one district and leaves the next untouched. Water beads on dark rock, gathers in hollows, and vanishes into cracks too fine to see. In the colder months, frost forms inside those cracks and pries them open by a fraction; in warmer ones, the stone closes again, or appears to. Sometimes the fog gathers in a valley and rises uphill, flowing against the slope in slow, deliberate sheets. The air tastes faintly of mineral water and green things.

Vegetation is plentiful, but never crowded. Moss fills the seams between slabs. Pale grasses make soft mats over the flatter ground, bending in broad, unhurried sweeps. There are groves of low, dark trees in the sheltered basins, their trunks thick and crooked, their crowns sparse enough to let the grey daylight through. In the wettest places, waist-high plants grow among standing stones; their leaves are glossy and cold to the touch. None of them flower. There is no hum of insects, no bird-call, no footfall in the undergrowth. The plants move only with weather, with water, or with the slow pressure of their own growth.

Here and there, the land is built upon. Not densely, and not all at once. A road enters the uplands as a shallow groove worn into the rock, crosses an empty plain, and becomes a stairway descending into a settlement. The towns are made of the same stone as the country: blocks fitted by hand, walls laid low against the wind, roofs held down with slabs. Narrow canals and covered channels pass through them, carrying occasional trickles of water from one cistern to another. There are squares paved with immense, uneven flags, and long rows of small houses whose doorways open onto silent streets.

The construction is simple but extensive. A retaining wall may run for a hundred miles, dipping and rising over the ancient folds of the plateau. Roads meet at junctions where no town stands. Bridges span shallow ravines that have since filled with grass. Some staircases climb to doorways in blank cliff faces; others descend below the level of the surrounding land and end in cold, dry chambers. The settlements are empty, yet not ruined in the usual way. Their stones are neither freshly tended nor collapsed. Moss has grown into the joints, but never enough to prise them apart. A roof may be missing for generations without the walls beneath it weathering further. The whole country has settled into a state that looks abandoned and permanent at once.

Distance is unreliable. From a high terrace, a nearby ridge may appear blue and remote, while a pale tower on the horizon seems close enough to touch. Walking toward it can shorten or lengthen the journey without any change in the terrain. Sometimes a road returns to a town it never passed through. Maps work well for a few days, then begin to show slight discrepancies: a stream crosses a contour line; a building is drawn on both sides of a valley; a hill is marked where the mapmaker remembers none. The maps are not wrong so much as they belong to an earlier arrangement of the country.

The strangest changes are quiet. Rain falls upward from the stones for a few minutes, collecting beneath low clouds. A line of houses appears reversed in a pond that has no reflection of the sky. Shadows lie beside their objects instead of beneath them, thin and sharp as ink, then return to their proper places. In one district, the horizon can be seen through a hill; in another, a person looking back along the road sees the path they have not yet walked. Nothing announces these events. Grass keeps bending in the wind. Water keeps slipping through the channels. The stone remains cold.

Light is ordinary, though the sky is often immense: a flat, wintry brightness spread over the uplands, with the sun softened by thin cloud. At dusk, the settlements do not glow. Their windows are dark, and the streets take on the same muted colors as the rock around them. Still, the landscape is not inert. Fog migrates from basin to basin. Frost advances and retreats. Plants extend into cracks, and the slow workings of water revise the courses of streams. Far away, a whole escarpment may shed a skin of gravel, the fall taking hours and making almost no sound.

The quiet has depth to it. Wind passes across the grass with a dry brushing noise. Water ticks in roof gutters, or drips steadily into cisterns whose bottoms cannot be seen. Once in a long while, stone settles somewhere beyond sight—not a crash, but a dull report felt through the soles, as though the uplands had shifted in their sleep.

There is no animal life here to disturb the silence, and nothing hunts or flees. The ecology has become a conversation between stone, water, frost, and plant: a slow exchange conducted over distances too great to observe all at once. The towns stand within it like another kind of growth, orderly but sparse, their streets and walls laid down upon the plateau and left to weather at the same patient pace.

From above, the high country might look almost level, a grey-green expanse scored by old roads and pale waterways. Only by crossing it does its vastness become clear. Its emptiness is not a lack of detail but a space in which details have room to persist: one wet stair, one leaning wall, one grove in a sheltered hollow, one stretch of road that seems to be waiting for a destination. And beyond each of them, the plateau continues—ancient, cold, and still quietly changing.
