The basin is not deep so much as completely given over to water. It lies low beneath a dim, warm sea, its far edges lost in silt-colored darkness. There is no clean horizon below the surface: only a gradual thickening of shadow, broken here and there by the pallor of exposed stone. The water has the heavy stillness of a place sheltered for a long time, though it is never truly still. Currents pass through in slow, broad breaths, stirring suspended grit and combing the fine growth from ledges.

Across the basin stands a settlement.

It rises only a little from the seafloor, but spreads outward in close-set clusters: arched chambers, narrow passages, broad hollows like small plazas, and walls worn smooth where generations of water have passed over them. The buildings are not made of anything cut to a uniform shape. They are built up in layers of shell, mineral crust, and pale stone, joined so gradually that it is difficult to tell where a wall ends and the reef begins. Some roofs have collapsed inward, making cool pockets where fish shelter. Others are still intact, ribbed with growth and furred along their edges. The whole place looks less abandoned than settled into its own final form.

The streets are channels. They wind between walls close enough to brush with an outstretched hand, then open suddenly into courtyards littered with shell fragments and rounded stones. Higher passages connect one structure to another in low, uneven arches. A few are clogged with sand; a few carry a steady, gentle current that draws pale particles through like smoke. There are no lamps, no moving mechanisms, no marks of tools. In the darkest rooms, the shapes of doorways are barely distinguishable from the shadows behind them.

Vegetation is scarce. Here and there, dark fronds cling to a roofline, or a thin stand of water-grass leans from a fissure in the paving. These patches are small and guarded by stone, their leaves folded close against the current. Most of the living color comes from animals: the blue-black glimmer of small fish tucked into cracks; pale crabs picking at the silt; broad, slow bodies passing overhead and blotting out what little light reaches the basin. In the deeper courtyards, clusters of shellfish open and close in patient unison. Long, translucent animals hang beneath arches like strips of drifting glass. Their faint points of light appear only after the eye has adjusted to the dark, and vanish again when they turn away.

The abundance is quiet. A school can fill an avenue without making more than a soft, collective shiver in the water. Claws tick lightly against stone. Somewhere beyond sight, a larger creature exhales, and the sound travels as a low pressure through the walls rather than a call. Now and then, a loose shell rolls across a floor and comes to rest against a threshold. Most of the basin’s noise is the small, continuous hush of water moving through familiar spaces.

Nothing here seems newly made. Erosion has rounded every edge, but has not erased the settlement’s arrangement. The walls have taken on the contours of the current; hollows have deepened where water repeatedly turns; sand has collected in sheltered corners and been lifted from exposed ones. The place is active without appearing restless. Animals shift their territories. Growth advances by fractions. The current follows its old routes until a storm or distant tide persuades it otherwise.

From above, the settlement might have resembled a town crowded around a low harbor. From within, it feels more like a reef remembering one. The passages still suggest movement between places, the courtyards still hold open space, and the chambers still offer shelter—but none of them have to serve their first purpose now. The sea has filled every room, and life has arranged itself around what remains.

At the edges, the architecture thins into broken ridges, then into a plain of silt and scattered stone. Beyond that, the dark continues. The basin has no clear border; it simply becomes less built, less crowded, until the last arch is only another shape on the seafloor, occupied by a fish that does not distinguish it from a cave.
