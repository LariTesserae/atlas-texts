The landscape is hidden inside a seam in the world’s machinery: a narrow cavity behind a tilted plate of black metal, no wider than a room, but rising so sharply that its upper ledges vanish into dimness. It has the vertigo of a mountain range compressed into a crack. A fall from one shelf to the next would be only a few strides; still, the shelves descend in a succession of cliffs, chimneys, and abrupt stone faces, each one seeming to overlook an impossible depth.

Nothing here is wet. The rock is pale, dry, and faintly crystalline, with a skin of mineral dust that gathers in the hollows like old snow. There are no pools, no damp seams, no suggestion that water ever passed through. Instead, the place is packed with growth: dark green fronds folded tight against the stone, silver-green mats filling every fissure, stiff fans of leaves crowding the narrow shelves. Their surfaces are waxy and finely ribbed. They take their nourishment from the air’s thin chemical traces and from the steady electric charge that runs through the rock. Their roots grip mineral grains and metal alike.

The growth is so dense that bare ground is rare. Where a path might run, it has been pressed into a dark, narrow groove beneath overlapping leaves. There are no flowers, fruits, or animals. Nothing hunts or feeds. The fronds do not turn toward the light, and no insect stirs among them. They occupy the cavity with the calm insistence of a stain that has been spreading for ages and has reached, at last, every available surface.

The light comes from far above—or from what looks like above: a hairline opening in the casing, too small to reveal the space beyond. It casts a thin, cold glow across the upper ledges and leaves the lower shelves in blue-grey shadow. Here and there, pale mineral growth catches that light and holds it, not brightly, but as a dull gleam, like a coal buried under ash. The temperature stays cool throughout. The air has a dry, metallic taste.

A few industrial objects interrupt the growth. A corroded ladder is bolted to one wall and disappears behind a curtain of leaves. A square housing, half sunk in rock, bears the worn outline of a warning stencil. Near the floor of the cavity, a small engine still runs behind a grille. Its purpose is no longer obvious. It could be driving air through the seam, or merely maintaining a pressure that nothing else would notice. The plants have grown around its casing without covering the grille.

The engine makes the place loud. Its low, continuous hammering fills the cavity and passes through the stone into the body: a blunt vibration in the teeth, a tremor under the feet. Above it, metal ticks at long, irregular intervals. The leaves themselves make almost no sound. Even where they crowd the vents, they do not flutter. The machinery seems to roar against a silence that cannot answer it.

Nothing here is in the process of changing. The mineral dust rests undisturbed. The leaves keep their fixed angles; the engine holds the same note. No new shoot emerges, no old frond falls. The place has found a strange balance between living growth and industrial ruin, and has remained there so long that age is visible only in the smooth polish of the stone and the softened edges of the metal. It feels less like a wilderness caught inside a machine than a machine that, over time, has acquired a wilderness—and now preserves it, without quite knowing why.
