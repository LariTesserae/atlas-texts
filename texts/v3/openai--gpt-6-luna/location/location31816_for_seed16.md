The city is hidden inside the salt.

From the plain above, there is almost nothing to see: a level glare of pale mineral crust, lightly scored by wind. No stream cuts it, no pool gathers in its shallow folds. The opening is a seam in the salt, narrow enough to miss unless the light is low and the ground is singing. It leads down only a little way. This is not a vast underworld, but a pocket hollowed beneath the plain, a room-sized world enlarged by its own architecture.

Inside, the ceiling hangs low and uneven. Salt and dark basalt have been worn smooth by centuries of hot, dry air. The place is dim, lit by a few thin cracks overhead and by the muted amber glow of mineral seams in the walls. Nothing shines brightly. Every surface seems to have settled into its proper shape long ago.

The settlement fills the hollow without making it crowded. Small square houses, stairways, terraces, retaining walls, and narrow lanes are fitted into the stone in orderly layers. Their builders used what the pocket gave them: slabs of basalt, salt blocks hardened with claylike dust, and thin hammered sheets of soft metal. The workmanship is careful but simple. Doorways are low. Roofs are flat and weighted against the restless pressure of the ground. There are channels and gutters everywhere, though none carry water. They guide hot air, mineral dust, and the slow fall of salt crystals from one level to another.

Between the buildings grows a dense, low vegetation. It is lush by the standards of this dry world: mats of dark green-black growth along warm walls, succulent-looking fans in the cracks, pale tendrils woven through the stonework. Some leaves are stiff as thin leather; others tremble like fine wire when the air moves. They do not need rain. Their roots take nourishment from mineral vapors seeping through the rock, and their surfaces feed on the faint light that reaches the hollow. They drink nothing, and leave no droplets behind. Where they grow thickest, the stone is fretted into shallow, intricate patterns, as if the plants were slowly writing on it.

There is no animal life. No wings stir in the rafters, no feet patter through the lanes. The plants move only when heat or wind moves them. Yet the pocket is loud.

Air enters through the narrow seam above and passes through a maze of chimneys, roof slots, and old ventilating shafts. It whistles, moans, and sometimes gives off a sudden, blunt roar, as though a great bellows had been opened below the city. The dry fronds answer with a papery rattling. Salt grains patter down the walls. Deep in the rock, joints click as the temperature shifts. At intervals the entire hollow gives a low, resonant boom—not an impact, but the sound of hot stone expanding against stone. The noise rises and falls in a regular cycle. In the quietest part of that cycle, the plants can be heard crackling as they grow.

Everything here is in motion, but nothing seems hurried. The ground exhales heat. Dust migrates along the lanes in narrow streams. New crust forms around the mouths of the vents, then breaks away in thin flakes. The oldest walls have rounded corners; the oldest steps have worn shallow hollows. Erosion has not destroyed the city so much as taught it how to endure: every ledge catches dust, every arch flexes with the heat, every passage leaves room for the air to pass.

The place has rules of its own. Water does not collect, even in the deepest hollows. Loose objects creep toward the vents as the air shifts. Plants grow best where the stone is warm, but too close to the fissures they become brittle and pale. The light never becomes strong, even at midday; the roof admits only a few diluted bars of it, and the mineral haze softens those further. The city’s lanes are quiet of people, yet its buildings still serve their old purposes, sorting heat and air, sheltering the vegetation, and keeping the pocket from collapsing into the plain.

Above it, the salt wastes remain open and empty. Below them, in a space scarcely larger than a house, the dry city keeps breathing.
