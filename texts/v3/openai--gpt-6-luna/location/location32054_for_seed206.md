The place is hidden beneath a mat of marsh plants, no larger than a room and easy to mistake for a shadow in the reeds. Above it, the wetland lies flat and unremarkable. Below, roots have woven themselves into a low, warm ceiling. Their pale undersides bead with water. The drops gather, tremble, and fall into a shallow basin where the ground has been held in its shape for a very long time.

Nothing here is bare. The floor is made of compressed stems, slick leaves, and root-felt, springy underfoot and faintly warm, like the inside of a living body. Yet the place does not feel crowded. Its growth leaves broad, quiet gaps: a still pool between two root-pillars; a clear patch of dark substrate beneath a hanging frond; a narrow corridor of water leading nowhere visible. The plants occupy everything without pressing together. Their forms are settled, weather-smoothed, as familiar with one another as stones in an old streambed.

Water is always present, but rarely in motion enough to be called a current. It films the leaves, gathers in cups, seeps through the root walls, and glows dully in the basin. The air is humid and close. A thin vapor hangs just above the water, not rising so much as remaining there, caught beneath the canopy. The temperature is mild-warm throughout, with no distinct sunny side or cold corner.

The plants do more than grow. Thick hollow stalks draw water upward with slow, powerful contractions. Seed heads turn on their own joints, clicking as they meter out grains. Long runners pass behind the walls of roots, drawing nutrients from one patch to another. Here and there, a bulbed stem hammers steadily against a stone, its leaves fluttering with each stroke. The whole pocket has the sound of a small industrial works: a low rushing drone, layered with thuds, rattles, hisses, and the rapid tick of tiny mechanisms. There is no single machine and no maker. The noise comes from the plants doing what they have always done.

Small animals are everywhere, though few show themselves at once. Clear-winged insects hover over the pool. Pale larvae thread through the root-felt. A blunt-snouted amphibian waits motionless in a cup of water, its sides moving in time with the stalks. Something with many feet travels inside the ceiling, making a dry, brisk patter that crosses the chamber and stops. The abundance is mostly a matter of traces: nibbled leaf edges, shed skins, tiny wakes, faint warmth under the floor.

The pocket remains remarkably steady. Water enters and leaves at the same unhurried rate; the plants pump, feed, and shed without any visible beginning or end. Even the loudness is constant, a broad mechanical murmur that never quite resolves into rhythm. No path is built through the place, no tool or artifact lies among the roots. Its order is not imposed. It has simply endured, until the living machinery and the wet ground seem to be one old, quiet system—hidden beneath the open marsh, making its noise to itself.
