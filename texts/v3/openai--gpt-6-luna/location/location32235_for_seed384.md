The basin is shallow only by the measure of the drowned world around it. Its floor lies in a long, settled hollow, a few hundred metres across, enclosed by dark slopes of stone. Nothing here rises very far above anything else. Streets, roofs, chimneys, and the blunt backs of buried machines crowd together beneath the water, making the whole place feel less like a city than a compact district pressed beneath glass.

The water is cold and almost black. Near the few lamps that still burn, it turns the colour of old pewter; beyond them, distance vanishes quickly. Silt lies everywhere, not in dunes but in soft, accumulated thicknesses: tucked into doorways, banked along the lee sides of walls, veiling tracks and paving stones. The stone beneath it has been worn smooth over a long time. Corners have rounded away. Brick has slumped into seams; iron has swollen beneath its skin of rust. The place is not collapsing so much as continuing, very slowly, to settle into its own shape.

Buildings stand close enough that their upper floors nearly touch. Some are narrow blocks with windows sealed by plates of cloudy glass; others are squat factories joined by pipes and covered walkways. The pipes have become a second street system, passing overhead in bundled runs, bending around towers, disappearing into walls and emerging again a few metres away. Cables droop between them in dark, silt-fringed loops. At the lowest level, streets are difficult to distinguish from channels. A railing might lead down a stairwell, or across the roof of a buried tram, or nowhere at all.

There is an order to the construction, though it takes time to see it. The oldest buildings occupy the basin’s centre, where the ground is most level. Later ones have been fitted around them, supported on piles, bolted to their sides, or wedged into whatever spaces remained. A little farther uphill, the factories give way to compact blocks of housing and workshops, all their entrances raised above the street on short platforms. The lowest doors have been sealed. The highest are still attended to: their hinges move freely, their pressure latches are polished, and on some, a small enamel sign remains legible.

A number of the machines are still working. Their motions are economical and hard to notice at first: a flywheel turning behind a grille; a piston making one slow stroke every few minutes; a chain advancing by a link and pausing. The machinery draws on pressure differentials in the basin, on currents that pass through buried culverts, on warmth leaking from the deep rock. It does not run brightly. Thin lines of amber light mark a few control panels, and greenish lamps glow in the mouths of inspection tunnels. These lights are too weak to illuminate the city. They merely show that some part of it is keeping count.

The water itself has currents, but they do not behave like tides. They travel in narrow, repeated paths through the streets and pipes, circling particular structures and slipping away underground. A loose thread or scrap of paper may hang motionless for hours, then drift steadily uphill, against what seems to be the basin’s slope. Fine particles occasionally stream through the lamps in straight horizontal bands. The inhabitants—if there are any left—built around these habits. Doors open inward. Screens face the channels. On certain buildings, metal vanes turn almost imperceptibly, though no current can be felt against the skin.

There is very little vegetation. A few pale films cling to shaded stone, and in the mouth of a warm outlet, small fronds tremble like frost growing underwater. Thin, translucent growths spread along the undersides of ledges, too delicate to alter the hard outlines of the place. Fish are rare. Now and then, a single silver body appears beyond the light, moving with such steadiness that it resembles a reflection sliding loose from the wall. Smaller animals inhabit the vents and cracks: tiny blind things, perhaps, though none are often seen whole. Most openings remain empty. The city seems to have many niches and few occupants.

Sound is nearly absent. The machinery makes no roar, only a pressure in the water: a low pulse felt through the teeth or the soles of the boots. A cable can tap a wall once in an hour. Somewhere deep below, a gate moves, and the vibration passes through the district without becoming a noise. Even footsteps are swallowed. The only clear sounds occur close by—a soft click at a latch, the brush of fabric against a rail, a bubble escaping from a joint and rising toward a surface too distant to see.

For all its density, the basin feels small. Its edges are always near, and every route soon returns to a known wall, a familiar pipe, a staircase descending into opaque water. The darkness does not open into grandeur. It folds the place inward, compressing its factories, streets, and courtyards into a single close arrangement. There is no visible horizon, only successive layers of construction fading into the same black.

Nothing here appears newly ruined. There are no raw breaks, no violent heaps of wreckage. The water has had time to soften the event that put the district beneath it. Whatever happened, the place has outlasted the moment of disaster and become an environment in its own right: cold, crowded, patient. Its machines maintain their small cycles. Silt continues to gather. The few living things keep to the warmed seams. Lamps glow where they have always glowed, under water that never quite rests.
