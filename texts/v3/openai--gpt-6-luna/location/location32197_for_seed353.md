The plateau is called the Low Heaven, though it stands high above the surrounding country. From its rim, the land seems to have been rubbed almost flat by ages of heat. Ridges survive only as long, blunt wrinkles under a dark sky. Nothing glitters. The stone is buried beneath a continuous skin of vegetation, and the vegetation is buried beneath the works made from it.

There is no water here—not a lake, not a spring, not even the suggestion of a riverbed. The air is dry enough to make a breath feel like paper. Plants draw their substance from the hot mineral ground: salts, gases, and the slow seep of warmth through the plateau’s crust. Their roots are not delicate threads but pipes and cables, driven deep into the rock. Some have grown through old machinery; some machinery was built around them. After so long, it is hard to say which came first.

The surface is entirely made. It lies in layers: black paving pressed from ancient stems; ridges of bundled fiber, baked hard as brick; terraces stitched together with metal struts that have gone green and dull. Here and there, an industrial tower leans above the canopy, its ribs wrapped in thick creepers. Its wheels still turn, though no one can say what they produce. The stalks feed into hoppers. The hoppers feed into furnaces. The furnaces exhale a faint, hot breath through vents furred with leaves.

There is no sunlight. Above the plateau hangs a roof of overlapping fronds, pipes, cables, and smoke-darkened screens. It lets through only the occasional pinprick of a far-off glow, too weak to make a shadow. Most of the light comes from the plants themselves: not a luminous garden, but small signs of activity—amber seams under bark, a red pulse in a cut stem, pale points of heat along a buried root. In the open, these are barely visible. The darkness has weight. It gathers beneath every bridge and in every hollow between the packed growth.

The plants fill every available space. They form walls, ceilings, roads, and the soft-looking banks beside the roads, though their surfaces can be harder than iron. Broad mats of leaves cover whole districts. Their edges constantly curl, split, and knit together again. Tall seed-columns push up through the roofs of factories, only to be cut down by automatic blades that feed them back into the works. Nothing grows freely, but nothing is fully controlled either. Growth is the labor the place cannot stop doing.

Its age shows less in ruins than in the absence of unfinished things. The oldest terraces have settled into the plateau so completely that their corners are gone. The exposed faces of the ground are smooth with long abrasion, as if wind and time had been sanding the whole place down for millions of years. Yet the surface is turbulent. It buckles in slow waves. At intervals, whole blocks of the green-brown crust rise, shift a few feet, and sink again. Vent stacks cough. Conveyor belts shudder into motion. Roots break through their channels and are routed, crushed, or incorporated into a new wall. By the next visit, a familiar passage may have become a sealed corridor, its door padded over by fresh growth.

There are no animals. Nothing runs or flies through this landscape. The only movements are those of plant and machine: leaf-surfaces turning toward a heat-source, gears cycling without visible purpose, long root-bundles inching through the ground. Even the winds seem manufactured. They travel through ducts cut into the vegetation, arrive as warm pressure at the back of the neck, and vanish without stirring the canopy. The place makes little noise. A visitor hears the soft grind of some immense mechanism behind the floor, the dry rasp of fibers tightening, and, now and then, a single metallic knock from far inside the built landscape. The silence between these sounds is enormous.

Across the plateau, the works gather into a low, crowded horizon. There are no clear borders between factory, grove, and terrain. A tower is also a trunk; a pipeline is also a root; a roof is a field of leaves trained to bear weight. The whole region seems to be an old machine that has become a forest, or an old forest that has been persuaded to keep operating. It does not appear abandoned. It appears to have outlasted the reason it was made, and to be continuing through habit.

Where the plateau falls away, the growth follows the slope in thick, dark curtains. Far below, the surrounding land is lost in haze—not mist, for there is no moisture in it, but heat wavering above mineral flats. The Low Heaven remains sealed within its own warmth and its own making: a high, sunless place, almost motionless at a glance, forever remaking itself in the dark.
