The plateau is high enough that weather arrives already tired. It drifts over the rim in pale sheets, loosens into a brief rain, and is gone again, leaving beads of water caught in every seam. The air stays cool. Beyond the plateau’s broken edge, the lowlands are usually hidden—not by cloud so much as by distance, which here has a way of becoming thick and opaque.

The land is old almost past recognition as land. Its ridges have been rubbed down to long, shallow backs; its ravines are broad and quiet, their bottoms furred with dark green. Stone shows only in a few exposed shelves, worn smooth as the bones of something enormous. Most of the plateau is covered by a continuous living skin: mats of small leaves, root-felt, and tough mosses stitched over the ground so completely that there is no loose soil to see. Rain passes through this skin and gathers in hidden pockets. For a few hours after a shower, the slopes shine with water; then the shine withdraws, leaving dampness below and a faint metallic smell above.

The plants are not merely growing on the city. They are its material, its weatherproofing, and, in places, its load-bearing structure. Old streets run as pale channels through the green, paved with compressed stem-fiber and stone. Buildings stand in scattered groups, their walls made from layered trunks, mineral plates, and the dark, close-grained wood of trees that have grown around the original foundations. Some are tall and narrow, others low and sprawling, all joined by bridges that bow under curtains of vine. At a distance, the settlement looks sparse. There are miles of open, plant-covered ground between its districts, with only a few towers or factory stacks marking the horizon. But underfoot and overhead, living matter is everywhere.

The city’s oldest industrial works are still operating. Their machines are housed in deep buildings half-swallowed by growth: presses that turn stalks into structural boards, mills that grind leaf-fiber into pale powder, pumps that draw condensation from the plateau’s buried hollows. Power lines run in bundles between towers, some ordinary and black, some thickened into the living stems that have grown around them. In certain districts the machines are quiet for days, then start up together with a clank and a shudder, as if responding to a signal too slow for human hearing. Their noise travels across the open ground: a low engine-throb, the knocking of huge gears, the intermittent shriek of metal under load. The plants answer with their own sounds—dry clicking from the upper branches, a soft tearing as tendrils tighten, the distant hiss of leaves turning their undersides to the wind.

There is little animal life. Now and then a small, pale thing darts through a drainage slot or disappears into the root-mass before its shape can be held in the eye. Birds are scarce, and their calls are rarer still. The most persistent movement belongs to plants: seed-dust lifting in sudden spirals, broad leaves folding and unfolding when no wind is felt, roots slowly raising the edges of old paving. It can seem that the whole plateau is breathing, though nothing in the green moves at the same pace.

The rules of distance are unreliable. A factory seen across a valley may take half a day to reach, or only a few minutes if approached along one of the old service roads. Bridges sometimes meet their opposite towers at a slight angle, though the towers themselves have not shifted. Echoes arrive before the sounds that make them. On clear mornings, a person’s shadow may fall toward the sun for several minutes, then return to normal without any change in the light. Nobody treats these as emergencies. The city has been built and rebuilt through too many such irregularities; there are painted marks on walls where doorways have occasionally appeared, and warning bells at the ends of paths that are known to forget where they lead.

For all its strangeness, the place does not feel new or unstable. It feels settled into its errors. The old distortions are as much a part of the plateau as the worn ridges and the damp under the green. The factories keep time imperfectly; the streets wander by a few degrees; the vegetation closes around whatever is left unattended. In the long open reaches between neighborhoods, the sound of the city thins to a distant industrial roar, while the living carpet spreads unbroken over the ancient ground. Then a rain crosses the heights, brief and cold, and every leaf turns bright at once.
