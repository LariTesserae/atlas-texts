The country lies under the earth, though it has the breadth of one.

Its ceiling is so high that it vanishes into a white glare. In places the roof is translucent: bands of ice and newly formed mineral glass admit light from the frozen world above, spreading it until there is no visible sun, only a bright, cold day without direction. Elsewhere, fissures in the roof pour down narrow shafts of brilliance. They catch the drifting dust and make the air look full of slow, pale rain.

The ground is not quite ground. It is a thin crust of dark stone, fresh-broken in long, sharp ridges, and over it lies the vegetation: a continuous skin, pale green at its roots and almost silver at the tips. It quilts the flats, climbs the walls, and hangs from ledges in curtains that descend hundreds of feet before fraying into threads. In the deepest hollows it builds itself into towers, light enough to tremble, dense enough to bear its own weight. The plants are not especially leafy. They are ribs, membranes, hollow stems, branching fans—architecture made from living material. Walkways of braided root span crevasses. Broad leaves roof shallow basins. Here and there, a stalk as thick as a house has split open, exposing a honeycomb of moist chambers.

For all that growth, the place feels empty. Between the green structures are immense distances of bare stone and open air. A person standing on a ridge might see a forest, then a plain, then another forest so remote that its pale towers look like cliffs in the haze. Nothing fills the space between. Even the vegetation does not seem crowded; it has spread with extravagant patience, leaving whole regions untouched, as though it were waiting for something to arrive.

Water comes irregularly. It gathers as condensation along the cold roof, beads there for days, then releases in a brief and sudden weather. Drips become streams; streams leap from the ceiling in silver ropes and vanish into the root-mats. Some basins hold black water that reflects the light too sharply. Most are dry again by the next long spell, though the plants keep a dark dampness beneath their skins. The air smells faintly of wet stone and crushed stems.

The weather is made by the cavern itself. Far-off changes in pressure send gusts rolling across the plain, bending the hanging gardens in great, synchronized sweeps. The vegetation can appear to move like a single animal: first a shiver in the distant growth, then a wave crossing the land, lifting fronds and shaking loose sheets of cold water. Sometimes the gusts collide. Loose leaves spiral upward; root-bridges pull taut and snap; entire towers fold, then begin growing again from their broken bases. New cracks open in the young stone with a sound like a distant door being forced. The landscape is always making small revisions to itself.

There are few animals. Pale mites inhabit the wet undersides of the leaves. Now and then something winged passes through a shaft of light and disappears into the green before its shape can be understood. Larger creatures leave little sign beyond a trail of flattened fronds, and those trails grow over quickly. The plants do not seem to depend on them. Their life is gathered from the diffuse brightness, the mineral water, and the warmest currents that rise from below.

Human presence is more tenuous still. A few low shelters stand on the firmest ridges, made from cut stalks and slabs of stone, their doors hung with woven membranes to keep out the drafts. The people who built them left no road, only occasional notches cut into the rock where a safe crossing once lay. Their small lamps are unnecessary by day. At night—if the cavern has a night—the light merely pales, becoming a soft, even blue. The inhabitants mark time instead by the wet seasons, by the flowering of the ceiling-vines, and by the great storms of moving air that return without warning.

Sound travels strangely here. The open distances swallow speech, but a single snapped stem can be heard for miles. Most of the time there is only the faint hiss of leaves rubbing together, water ticking down through hollow stalks, and the subdued breathing of the wind. Then the roof sheds a sheet of frost, or a faraway stand of plants collapses, and the silence fills with a low rolling thunder that seems to come from the stone itself.

Nothing in the cavern is old enough to feel settled. Its ridges are sharp, its pools newly cut, its gardens still testing the limits of their reach. Yet the brightness and the living mass make it seem complete—an entire country flourishing in the hollow of the world, with more room than it knows what to do with.
