The sea lies high.

It covers a continental shelf lifted far above the dark ocean around it: a broad, submerged tableland whose rims descend into blue distances, while its interior stays shallow enough for daylight to reach the living floor. The water is cold and clear, though never empty. From above, the light comes down with ordinary steadiness, softened by depth into a pale green-gold that seems to fill the whole immense basin. There is no visible surface here, only a remote brightness overhead, wavering faintly where the water presses against the air.

Plants occupy nearly everything. They grow from the stone in forests of long, jointed fronds, across ledges in close mats, and upward in immense suspended curtains whose ends vanish into the high water. The largest growths are spaced far apart. Between them lie open tracts of bare rock, wide as inland seas, edged by low, dark meadows. This emptiness is part of the vegetation’s design: each forest has room to draw and turn the currents that feed it.

The plants are not merely leafy. Their bodies do work. Thick bladders take in water and release it through narrow throats; ribbed stalks flex against the current with the regularity of pistons. Along some trunks, hard seed-pods grind minerals into fine sediment, which drifts down in slow, precise fans. Nothing has been built, and nothing here is made. Yet the living systems have the scale and function of industry: immense, unhurried organs processing water, light, and stone. From far away, a stand of them resembles a factory whose machinery has grown directly out of the seabed.

Their operation makes the place loud. A steady bass roar travels through the shelf, the combined sound of millions of leaves straining in a current that cannot be seen. Near the growths, this becomes a layered din: hollow knocking from seed cases, deep resonances in the stalks, and sharp, repeated clicks as small animals graze along the fronds. In the open tracts, the noise thins but never ceases. The whole plateau seems to hum—not with a single note, but with a vast, fixed chord.

There is abundant animal life, though it rarely gathers in crowds. Glass-bodied grazers move through the meadows in loose files. Broad, finned things hang beneath the canopies, keeping station in the plant-made flow. Small pale animals cling to the rock, where they are easy to overlook until the stone appears to ripple with them. Predators pass at long intervals, large enough to darken a forest edge, then disappear into the bright water. Their presence is felt more often than seen.

The landscape is strange without being unsettled. Its stone shelves are worn smooth in old channels; the plants have rooted into cracks that have been open for ages. There are no fresh scars, no young forests spreading into old ones, no advancing seasons. Currents hold their routes. Growth balances decay so exactly that fallen fronds remain in place, woven into the seafloor and still serving as habitat. Even the animals seem to follow paths that have existed longer than memory.

The water does not storm. It does not calm. The light does not strengthen or fade. Here, change has not stopped so much as arrived at its final arrangement. A seed-pod opens, but there is always another already open; a leaf breaks, but its place is already occupied by its successor. Every movement is real, every sound continuous, and yet the great pattern does not alter.

Across the immense height of this submerged world, forests stand like distant provinces. Between them, the open stone stretches on under its pale water, silent only by comparison. The shelf rises toward an unseen rim; beyond that rim is the abyss, and beyond the abyss perhaps the lower sea. Here, high above it, the living machinery keeps turning in a perfect, cold equilibrium.
