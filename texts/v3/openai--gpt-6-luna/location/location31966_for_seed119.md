The place is called the **Lower Choir**: a walled basin no larger than a hamlet, sunk just below the surrounding ground. Nothing here is untouched. The floor is a fitted skin of old brick, dark stone, and broad, shallow tiles, all sloping by fractions toward channels cut with hand tools. There is no soil. Where green things grow, they cling to mortar seams or sit in little clay cups left for them centuries ago.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a lake. It beads on the walls, threads through the joints, and gathers in the channels with a steady, purposeful chatter. After rain, the basin seems to exhale. The air cools and clouds; drops loosen from the ceilings of low archways and patter into the drains. Each channel has been worn smooth by long use, its corners rounded, its lips dark with mineral stains. The whole place looks less ruined than settled into a shape it has chosen.

The buildings are low: arched passages, cistern mouths, ledges and culverts, all close to the ground and close together. Their makers used simple things—fired clay, timber, stone, wedges, rope—and repaired them by hand. There are no grand towers, only roofs bowed by damp and walls thick enough to hold the cold. The basin’s boundary is unmistakable: a ring of retaining walls, old and slightly leaning, with a few narrow gates. Beyond them, one can hear rain or wind, but not see much of either.

The light arrives weakly. It filters through slotted vents and overcast openings, then breaks on wet tile into dull pewter glimmers. Even at midday, the far corners seem to be waiting for evening. Shadows gather under every lip and bridge. The place is small, but its many ducts and hollows make it feel deeper than it is; sound goes into the walls and comes back altered.

And the walls are full of life.

Bats hang beneath the arches in loose black clusters, shifting and clicking in their sleep. Swifts wheel through the upper vents, vanishing and reappearing through openings too narrow to seem useful. In the water, pale eels thread between the tiles. Small fish flash in the overflow bowls; frogs crowd the warmest drain mouths; snails stipple the wet ledges. Insects hatch from the channels in brief, trembling clouds. There are more animals than the quiet, bare surfaces seem able to support, but each has its place: a notch, a pool, a slit in the brick, a ledge above the flood line.

Their noise makes the Lower Choir loud. The bats chatter in the roof spaces; the birds rasp and whistle; frogs knock out blunt, hollow calls that seem to come from several rooms at once. Water adds its continuous clatter. At certain times—usually just before the rain stops—the sounds align into a wavering roar, as if the basin were breathing through a thousand small mouths.

The living things have made rules of the construction. Fish travel only through channels that run toward the lower gate, though the water there is shallowest. Bats avoid one arch entirely, where the stone stays dry even in the wettest weather. Frogs gather in a circular basin whose bottom is always cold; no eggs hatch there, yet every spring it fills with them. No one knows whether the builders intended these arrangements. The animals behave as if the place was made for them, and perhaps, over enough generations, it was.

There is little growth. A few strands of sedge rise from wall cups; moss makes a muted green fringe along the darkest seams. One pale creeper has crossed an entire lintel, though it has no visible roots. The vegetation never takes over. It seems to understand that there is room for only so much softness here.

The Lower Choir changes constantly, but never quickly. Water rises, drains, returns. Flocks arrive and leave. Old bricks loosen, then settle into their neighbors. A tile may turn over in the night, revealing a clear pool beneath it. The basin does not feel abandoned, nor newly built, nor truly wild. It is an old machine with no maker left to tend it, still turning by rain, animal traffic, and the patient pressure of things wearing one another down.
