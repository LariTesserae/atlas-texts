The ground is low and nearly level, but it has not finished becoming ground. It lies in broad, warm flats interrupted by fresh ruptures: black seams, raw shelves of clay, ridges pushed up like the edges of a torn skin. Water gathers only briefly. A shower passes, or a sheet of condensation forms beneath the canopy, and the whole place shines; then the water vanishes into the roots, leaving dampness in hollows and a mineral smell where the earth has split open.

There is almost no earth visible for long. Vegetation covers it, fills it, and builds upon itself until the distinction between plant and landscape becomes difficult to keep. The lowest layer is a dense mat of small, tight leaves, slick and springy underfoot. Above that rises a tangled construction of trunks, stems, fronds, and braided roots. Some are as thick as walls. Others are fine as cord, crossing one another in curtains that tremble even when the air is still. The plants do not merely grow over the place; they provide its substance. A fallen mass is not debris but a new floor. A hollow in a trunk becomes a passage. A clump of pale, jointed stalks forms a high arcade, then bends over and closes it behind itself.

The settlement is made this way: not planted in the landscape but grown through it. Its avenues are broad channels where the vegetation has repeatedly parted, leaving dark, resonant ground between green banks. Narrow lanes wind through living walls. There are open courts beneath roofs of overlapping leaves, stairways of fused roots, balconies that are simply branches thickened into ledges. Some structures look startlingly deliberate—rectangular chambers, long covered walks, rows of pillars—until their surfaces flex, bud, or slowly tilt toward the light. The town has no fixed boundary. At its outskirts, streets fray into thicket; at its center, the thicket has arranged itself into something like a civic plan, though the plan changes from one day to the next.

The growth is young. New surfaces are bright, wet, and sharply edged; split stems show interiors the color of cream or raw fruit. Small mounds rise where root masses thrust upward, and the cracked ground between them is still too clean, too fresh to have acquired much dust. A path that was level in the morning may have lifted by evening. A roof can lengthen over a square while its supporting trunks withdraw into the soil. There is no sense of ruins, exactly. What has ended is taken up immediately by what is beginning.

The air is warm and close beneath the canopy. In the open lanes, ordinary daylight reaches the ground, pale and steady, but inside the denser growth it is divided into leaf-colored bands. Moisture beads on every surface. It gathers in the folded mouths of blossoms, in the cups of bark, along the undersides of huge leaves. Now and then, drops fall from high above without any rain having passed. At other times a brief shower arrives from one direction, strikes the roofs, and seems to stop at the edges of a single courtyard. Beyond that boundary, the leaves remain dry.

The sound is immense. The place makes it itself: stems knocking together, leaves rubbing in layers, root cords tightening with a low wooden groan. Somewhere in the deeper growth, whole walls of stalks shudder and snap upright, producing a rolling crack like a distant collapse. Water ticks and patters through the canopy; seedpods split with sharp reports; the shifting ground gives off a continuous bass vibration that can be felt through the feet. No animal calls interrupt this. There is no wingbeat, bark, insect rasp, or breathing in the undergrowth. The noise belongs to growth and weather, and it is so constant that quiet becomes a kind of pressure—the brief, uncanny instant when every leaf holds still at once.

The rules of space are unreliable. A lane may be longer on the return than it was on the way in, though no turn has been missed. An arch can frame a view of the same court from two different heights. Sometimes a person-sized opening appears between trunks and, from a few steps away, proves too narrow to enter; sometimes a wall that looked solid admits passage when approached without looking directly at it. The shadows are not always obedient to their leaves. They may climb the trunks ahead of the sunlight, or pool in the roots of a structure that has no roof. These disturbances do not feel like tricks. The landscape has simply not settled on a single arrangement of distance, direction, or cause.

Everything is active, but not purposeful in any familiar way. The plant-city rearranges itself by surges: a sudden lifting of the floor, a swift uncoiling of green through a passage, a rain of dry leaves from a crown that has not yet grown. Whole blocks seem to migrate by inches, their roots dragging beneath the surface. New growth presses into old corridors; old growth opens to receive it. There are moments when the town appears to be breathing—not as one body, but as thousands of overlapping rhythms, swelling and releasing until the streets themselves seem to inhale.

Beyond the organized growth, the same life spreads across the low country without becoming forest in the usual sense. It forms terraces, mats, towers, curtains, and broad green roofs, all close-packed and joined beneath the ground. From a rise in the terrain, the settlement might look like a single vast organism with a few exposed seams of earth. From within, it feels like a city assembled by weather: crowded, loud, warm, and perpetually unfinished. Its roads are temporary clearings. Its buildings are living decisions. Its horizon is a wavering line of foliage, interrupted by fresh earth where the world has just opened—and already, at the edges of every break, new stems are pushing through.
