### The Unfinished Crown

The whole landscape fits inside a hollow no wider than a cupped hand, hidden in the crown of a mountain that does not appear on any horizon.

Its entrance is a slit between two walls of fired clay. Beyond it, the slit opens onto a country of impossible height: terraces climbing into a ceiling of cloud, stairways narrowing toward a pale summit, bridges crossing abysses that are only a finger’s breadth across. The eye understands the scale in two incompatible ways. The place is small enough to cover with a palm; the air is thin enough to make every distant ledge seem above the world.

Nothing here is quite natural. There is no exposed bedrock, no loose earth beneath the plants, no untouched face of stone. The ground is tile, rammed clay, woven reed, and overlapping plates of some soft, chalky material, all made by hand—or by something that knew how hands worked. Walls buckle into ridges. Ridges become roofs. Roofs continue upward as cliffs. Every surface bears the thumb-smooth hollows and blunt tool marks of construction, as if the landscape were still being shaped and had not yet decided what it was meant to be.

It is hot, and water is everywhere. It stands in shallow basins on the terraces, slips through joints, and sweats from the walls in clear beads. Warm vapor hangs low, thickening until the uppermost ledges vanish. Water runs uphill along certain channels, pauses at corners, then spills over the edge in a fine silver sheet that falls for a long time before striking anything. The drops sometimes arrive from above before they have been poured. When the air shifts, condensation gathers on leaves, bricks, and the undersides of bridges at once.

The vegetation crowds every available seam. Broad, shining leaves overlap so densely that the paths appear to have been threaded through a green fabric. Tendrils grip the claywork and enter its cracks. Small flowers open directly against the walls, pale and damp, their petals cupping droplets that tremble but do not fall. There are no meadows, only patches of growth pressed into built surfaces: a fringe on a stair, a thicket on a parapet, roots braided through a drain. The plants seem lush, but not old. They have not softened the place. No wall has crumbled beneath them; no ledge has rounded with age. The greenery is new, vigorous, and already crowded, while the structures beneath it remain sharply unfinished.

The sound is much larger than the chamber. Water rattles in its channels. Leaves slap and shiver though there is no wind. Somewhere behind the walls, a repeated tapping travels upward, stops, then begins again beneath the feet. A low rush fills the gaps between these noises, like a storm heard from inside a sealed vessel. Occasionally the entire landscape gives a single hollow knock. The sound seems to come from the mountain outside, but the mountain seems to be listening from within.

There is little animal life. A few thin-winged insects hang in the humid air, keeping still even when the leaves move around them. Now and then something small and pale slips between the terraces, too quick to identify. Its passage makes no sound, but the plants lean aside along its route. The rare inhabitants do not appear frightened. They behave as if the place has already made room for them.

The highest terrace is always visible and never reachable. It might be a shrine, a roof, a distant plateau—or the underside of the entrance seen from a direction that should not exist. Stairs lead toward it, but each flight arrives at the bottom of itself. A bridge may cross the narrow gulf between two ledges and end on the same ledge it left, though the traveler who crosses it returns with wet feet and a view of a new valley behind them. Shadows fall upward along the walls. Reflections show the terraces with their plants grown tall and dry, though the water in the real basins is warm enough to steam.

The place has no weather from beyond itself. Its cycles are internal. The air thickens; the channels fill; the leaves close. Then the walls become warm, the water retreats into their seams, and new clay-colored surfaces push out beneath the old ones—not breaking or eroding, but appearing, clean-edged and soft. A ridge may rise overnight where a pool had been. A room may unfold inside a solid wall, its floor already wet, its corners already threaded with roots.

Nothing here seems ruined. Nothing seems finished. The landscape is packed so completely that even its emptiness has been built: narrow, deliberate gaps between leaves; black doorways under staircases; little pockets of air preserved within the walls. It is a mountain reduced to a secret, a weather system folded into a cavity, a construction site whose builders have left but whose work continues—hot, dripping, green, and climbing toward a summit that may be only another surface of the thing enclosing it.
