The place is called **the Unfinished Height**, though it is not especially tall. It occupies one small shelf of mountain, above the tree line but not far above the last crooked shrubs. Beyond its rim, cloud moves through the valleys like slow water. The shelf itself is no larger than a hamlet’s pasture. A person can cross it in an hour, if they take the stairs.

The stone looks newly made. It is pale, almost blue in the shade, and bears few marks of weather: no rounded boulders, no deep gullies, no old scree. Ridges come to abrupt ends. A wall of rock may rise cleanly from a mat of moss, then stop at a flat, unfinished face, as if the mountain has paused in the middle of forming itself. The ground is firm underfoot, but not settled. Here and there, narrow seams hold a dark, damp shine. Water gathers in them, beads along the rock, and slips away without ever cutting a channel.

The town has been laid out with care. Low houses stand in rows around a spring court; a broad stair climbs to an upper square; covered passages link a storehouse, a meeting hall, and a cluster of workshops. Their materials are simple: split stone, timber, woven brush, clay roof tiles. Everything has been lifted and fitted by hand. The buildings are sturdy but spare, with heavy lintels and unfinished corners. Some have no doors yet. Some end in walls that face open sky.

There is space between them—too much space for a town. Streets run past empty plots where the rock is still bare. A few narrow bridges connect separate shelves, their railings tied from cord and young branches. The town feels organized, even municipal, but it has not filled in. It is a settlement waiting for its own ground to catch up.

Greenery occupies the gaps. Ferns unfurl along the foundations; soft grass grows in the joints of paving stones. Small trees, bent by the high wind, have taken root beside cisterns and stair landings. Moss covers the north-facing walls in thick, bright layers. It is lush without being wild: vegetation spreading into whatever room the builders have left for it. There are no gardens in the usual sense, only patches of edible leaves and berry shrubs planted close to warm stone.

The air is cool and wet. Clouds often comb over the height, leaving the roofs dark and the wooden rails beaded with water. Even when no rain falls, the undersides of arches sweat. The spring in the court is shallow and clear; water rises there from beneath the paving, as if the mountain has not yet decided how much of itself to reveal. It is gathered in a stone basin, then carried through open gutters to small troughs. The channels are too new to be stained.

There are few people, and fewer animals. A watchkeeper may be seen on the upper stair; someone tends the spring; someone else beats dust from a rug outside a house. Their voices carry a long way. Birds visit briefly, usually pale ones that vanish into the cloud, and leave no nests behind. Most of the time, the town seems empty—not abandoned, exactly, but unoccupied in the way a room is empty before its furniture arrives.

It is not silent. Wind moves through the unglazed windows and the hollow passages beneath the squares, producing a low, sustained booming. Water ticks and patters in the gutters. Loose roof tiles click against one another. Every footstep on the stair returns from the surrounding stone, larger and more resonant than it should be. At times, a gust catches the bridge cords and makes them hum like a single plucked string. The sounds are loud because there is so little else to absorb them.

The height changes slowly. A crack may appear in a paving slab over the course of a season, but no one has seen one widen. The spring sometimes rises by the depth of a finger. A ledge that was bare in one year may, in the next, carry a new, smooth lip of stone—too regular to be a fallen block, too gradual to have been built. The inhabitants mark these changes with small stakes and chalk lines. They do not try to hurry them. Their rule is that no house should be built on a surface that has not held moss for a full year.

So the town is both settlement and survey. Its builders live among stairs, cisterns, planted moss, and careful markers, listening for the next small sound from the rock. They have made a place for themselves on a mountain that is still arriving.
