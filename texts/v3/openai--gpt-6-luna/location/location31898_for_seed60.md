The place is low enough that the sky seems to rest on it. Not close—there is ordinary daylight, a pale, serviceable brightness—but the horizon is hidden behind a continuous thickness of green. The ground is underfoot only in occasional glimpses: dark silt, smooth as kneaded clay, and shallow water caught between roots. Most of the land is made of plants.

They grow over one another in settled layers. Broad leaves roof the paths; corded stems make walls; mats of tiny, glossy fronds soften every hard edge. There are trunks, though some are hollow and some are braided from many smaller trunks, and here and there a woody pillar rises from the mud with the solemn, worn look of old masonry. Nothing is young in the usual sense. New growth appears at the edges of old growth, then thickens into it. A fallen branch becomes a ridge, the ridge becomes a bed of moss, and the moss takes root in the branch until the distinction is no longer useful.

Water is everywhere without quite being a river. It gathers in leaf-cups and root-bowls, trickles down the backs of stems, seeps from the stone-colored bark of certain plants. After rain, the whole place gives off a damp warmth, as if it has been holding its breath. Even in dry weather, beads of water cling to the undersides of leaves. The pools are shallow and dark, reflecting fragments of brightness rather than the sky. Their surfaces are crowded with floating leaves so small they resemble scales.

The paths are narrow, but not because anyone has made them that way. They are the channels left where the roots have not yet closed together. A person can pass sideways through some of them, pushing aside curtains of vine. In wider clearings, the vegetation rises into loose chambers: roofed hollows with pillars of living wood and hanging roots that brush the shoulders. The rooms are not empty. They are full of leaves, warm air, and a steady patter of droplets falling from one surface to another.

Scattered among these living structures are the remains of an industry. A squat iron shed sits almost swallowed by reedlike stalks. The round mouths of old pipes protrude from banks of root, discharging a thin, clear thread of water into a basin of green. A narrow-gauge track runs for a few dozen paces before vanishing beneath a soft hump of growth. There are rusted wheels, riveted plates, a cracked gauge with its needle fixed at an unreadable mark. In one place, a machine still turns.

It is not a large machine. Its flywheel moves slowly behind a screen of leaves, each rotation followed by a wooden clack and a long vibration through the ground. A belt, black with age, disappears into a wall of stems. Whatever it once drove is gone, or has become part of the wall. The machine runs at the pace of something that has forgotten urgency. Its metal is warm to the touch. Vines pass through the housing without breaking it, and fine roots have grown around the bolts in close, pale spirals.

The sound of the place is surprisingly strong. Water ticks and patters everywhere, but underneath it is a broad, continuous rustling, too dense to be called wind. Leaves rub together in layers. Stems creak under slow pressure. The old wheel knocks at its regular interval, while pipes murmur and the wet ground makes small, sucking sounds underfoot. Every so often a pocket of vegetation releases a burst of seed, a dry hiss that sweeps through the chamber and stops as suddenly as it began. In the thickest passages, these sounds gather until they seem to come from inside the listener’s head.

Animals are scarce. There may be a small pale thing in the water, but it is difficult to tell whether it swims or is carried by the current. Insects occur only in a few sheltered pockets, where they cling to the underside of broad leaves and make thin, intermittent clicks. The plants have little need of them, or have made other arrangements. Some flowers open without attracting anything. Some close around a patch of warm air and remain that way for days. The place is not lifeless; it is simply occupied by something other than animals.

Its strangeness is quiet and physical. Certain stems grow toward the sound of water, though they bend away from its actual source. A hanging root may take several days to lower itself into a puddle, then remain suspended a finger’s breadth above the surface, drawing moisture without touching it. In one chamber, all the leaves point toward an empty iron frame bolted to the ground. The frame casts no shadow, even at midday, though everything around it does. Nobody seems to have built a shrine there. The plants have merely treated the frame as a direction.

The landscape changes slowly enough that its old forms feel permanent. A pool fills with fallen leaves over a season; a path narrows over years; a roof of vegetation thickens until the daylight beneath it turns greenish and soft. The industrial remains sink, rust, and disappear by increments. Their edges are furred with moss, their interiors threaded with roots. The plants do not conquer the machinery so much as continue through it. Iron gives them a place to brace themselves; pipes provide channels; warm housings shelter their finer growth.

Beyond the last visible structures, there is no clear boundary between wild growth and the former works. The same dark water lies under both. The same stems rise through cracks and drainpipes. If the factory once stood in a clearing, the clearing has closed, leaving only a low pocket where the machinery makes its patient sound.

At ground level, the air is warm, wet, and close. Higher up, the leaves part in places to show ordinary sunlight passing through. It falls on the canopy without reaching the floor. The whole small country seems to live in that difference: brightness above, dampness below; the memory of machines inside a living thickness; water moving very slowly through a world that has already decided what shape it wants to be.
