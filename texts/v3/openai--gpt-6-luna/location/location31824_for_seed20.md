The place is called the Bell Basin, though there is no bell and no one remembers who named it.

It is a small, high lake held in the crown of a newly risen mountain. The surrounding ridges are black and raw, their edges still sharp enough to look cut. Pale mineral seams stripe the stone. Here and there, the rock has cooled into folded shelves and blunt towers, as if the mountain tried to build itself a city and stopped after a few rooms. The basin is not broad, but it feels bottomless: dark water fills the gaps between the ridges and climbs over their lower faces until the whole summit seems submerged.

The water is warm. It gives off no steam, but its surface trembles constantly, crowded with little impacts and abrupt swells. Currents cross one another without meeting. A stream may pour off a ledge, curve sideways in midair, and strike the lake above its own source. At the margins, water hangs in sloping sheets against bare stone. Sometimes it recedes from a wall and leaves a clean, wet outline of a place that was never there.

The sound is enormous. It comes from everywhere at once: a surf-roar with a hard metallic edge, layered with the crack of water striking rock and a low vibration felt in the teeth. Words can be shouted a hand’s breadth away and still vanish. Yet the basin is not large enough to explain the noise. Sound seems to collect beneath the water and swell there, pushing up through the stone. Now and then the whole summit gives a single booming note. The lake’s surface flattens for an instant, and every loose pebble on the shore hops in place.

Light reaches the basin weakly, through a milky ceiling of water and cloud—or perhaps through neither. The sky above is often visible, dim and close, though its clouds appear to drift below the surface as well as above it. Shadows do not always fall in the direction of the light. A rock may have a shadow that moves ahead of it. Reflections lag behind the things they reflect, then hurry to catch up.

There is little vegetation. A few wiry red growths cling to cracks in the stone, slick as eelgrass but rooted above the waterline. Small, translucent fronds drift just under the surface; they turn toward voices, though voices are difficult to hear. There are rare animals: pale, eyeless fish in the deeper cuts, and narrow-winged creatures that skim the water without touching it. They appear only during the loudest surges. No one has seen what they eat.

Scattered across the ridges are a handful of low structures, built from fitted slabs of dark stone. They are too small to be dwellings and too regular to be natural. Their entrances face the lake. Inside, each room is dry, though the water outside may cover its roof. The walls are bare except for thin grooves that resemble writing. The grooves change after storms, but never into a script anyone can read.

The basin is still forming. New shelves appear overnight; old ones break away and reappear elsewhere, their edges bright and freshly fractured. The mountain rises by small, visible increments, most noticeably at the rim, where the rock lifts like the lip of a bowl. Meanwhile, the lake seems to deepen without growing larger. A person can wade across one stretch in the morning and find a trench there by afternoon.

The simplest rule is that the water does not obey level. The second is that it remembers shapes. A thrown stone may sink, return to the hand that threw it, and then fall upward through the surface. A footprint left in wet ash can fill with water while the rest of the ground remains dry. Travelers who stay too long begin to hear their own movements coming back to them from elsewhere in the basin: a step from the opposite shore, a breath from under the lake.

Still, the place has boundaries. One ridge, a few rooms of stone, a narrow ring of violent warm water. Beyond it, the mountain falls away into cloud. The roar remains audible from below, but no one looking up can see the basin. From the summit, with water shifting over the black rock and the whole world sounding like it is being torn open, it feels less like a lake than a small, unfinished piece of another world—one that has surfaced briefly and has not yet decided what shape to keep.
