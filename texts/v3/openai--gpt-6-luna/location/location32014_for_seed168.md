The plateau has the unfinished look of a world that has only just decided to be solid.

It lies high above the surrounding country, a broad shelf of pale mineral crust interrupted by long seams of dark, newly exposed stone. The ground is not yet worn into valleys. Ridges end bluntly, as if the forces shaping them have paused midway through their work; shallow basins hold no water, only loose powder the color of bone and iron. Here and there, the crust has buckled into plates with clean, sharp edges. Nothing seems ancient enough to be ruined.

The air is warm and very dry. Distance is easy to read: a low range of copper-colored hills, a farther rise of black rock, the pale sky beyond. Between these landmarks stretches so much open ground that each stands alone. The place feels empty without feeling barren.

Vegetation gathers in scattered expanses. It is lush by the standards of this waterless world, though the plants grow far apart, leaving generous intervals of bare stone between them. Their stems are thick and translucent, green shading into amber at the joints. Some spread in flat, overlapping fans across the mineral soil; others stand upright in loose groves, branching into fine filaments that tremble faintly in the light. They do not flower. There are no seeds in the familiar sense, no fruits, no signs of anything waiting to eat or be eaten. The plants seem to draw nourishment from the ground’s chemical gradients and from compounds carried in the air. Their leaves hold a muted sheen, like the surface of a machine kept clean.

There are no animals. No tracks cross the powder. No wings pass overhead, no insect-hum gathers in the growth. Even the plants make almost no sound. When the wind moves through the taller stands, it produces a low, soft rasp, as if a brush were drawn across hundreds of taut mineral threads. The quiet returns immediately afterward.

The landscape has an industrial character, but nothing here appears built. Across the plateau, natural formations repeat with the intimidating regularity of production: ridges of identical height, circular pits with precisely stepped walls, long parallel bands of dark crystal running beneath the crust. In a few places, the stone has opened into broad chambers where enormous mineral vanes turn almost imperceptibly around central pillars. They are not machines assembled from parts. The vanes have grown that way, their surfaces ribbed and scored, their motion driven by a slow exchange of heat and charge within the rock. The whole plateau seems to be processing itself.

Thin columns of warm vaporless shimmer rise from cracks in the ground. They distort the view but carry no moisture. Beneath them, pale filaments extend from the plants into the stone, following the same straight seams as the crystals. The arrangement is suggestive of a network, though no single point appears to control it. Energy passes through the land as pressure, warmth, and faint electrical tension. The occasional vane turns; somewhere deep below, a plate settles a fraction of an inch. These changes are so slow that the landscape seems fixed.

Light falls normally, without theatrical color or strange shadow. At midday the mineral flats brighten almost to white, while the plants retain their dark, bottle-green hue. Toward evening, the plateau cools only slightly. The far hills become less distinct, and the crystals along the ridges catch the last light in narrow lines. Nothing calls the dark in. Nothing stirs to greet it.

The place has no ruins, roads, towers, or visible inhabitants. Its machinery is geological, its greenery without wildlife, its emptiness spacious rather than lifeless. It is a high, warm, waterless province at the beginning of its own making: not wild in the sense of tangled abundance, but fertile in a more austere way, with everything spaced out, newly formed, and quietly at work.
