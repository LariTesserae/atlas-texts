### The Upper Seam

The city occupies a crack in the crown of a mountain.

From outside, there is little to mark it: a pale scar among newer, darker stone, just below the summit ridge. The rock around it is sharp and unweathered, as if the mountain has only recently finished opening. Wind skims the ridge, but barely enters the seam. Inside, the air is still, cold, and faintly metallic. Water gathers everywhere the stone changes direction.

The city is built along the walls of a single high chamber. Its structures climb rather than spread: narrow decks fastened to ledges, stairways that switch back across the rock, towers no wider than chimneys, and bridges fine as wire. Some buildings are plainly assembled from dark ceramic and silvered alloy. Others seem to have been grown out of the stone by a process that has left them smooth, ribbed, and faintly luminous at their joints. Nothing is ornamental without also being useful. Even the handrails carry warmth away from the rock and down toward the city’s lower machinery.

There is no street level. The floor of the chamber is a shadowed basin, broken by angular shelves of fresh mineral and shallow pools. The oldest routes cross high above it. New construction glitters farther up, close to the slit where daylight enters: clear panels, folded struts, and structures whose supports seem to disappear into the wall before reaching it. The architecture gives the impression of a much larger settlement seen through a narrow opening, though every district is close enough that its lights can be counted.

The light is ordinary daylight, whitened by height and narrowed by the seam. It moves slowly across the chamber, catching the wet faces of the rock and turning each drop into a brief, precise spark. There are no lamps on the main walkways. When the sun leaves them, their surfaces simply become gray. Deeper in the city, pale indicator lines come alive on bridges and doors, subdued enough not to compete with the sky.

Water is the place’s most constant motion. It beads from the ceiling, traces the rock in thin black threads, and collects in channels cut through ledges. At intervals, the channels empty themselves without a splash: the water lifts in narrow columns and is drawn into sealed conduits, moving uphill to tanks suspended beneath the upper decks. It returns colder, filtered through the city’s machinery, and begins its descent again. On very cold days, ice forms in delicate fans around the drains. By midday it has vanished, not melted so much as dismantled—its structure taken apart by a faint vibration in the stone.

The city is sparse. Whole tiers are unoccupied, their doors sealed and their walkways kept clean. The people who live here are rarely seen together. One figure may cross a bridge far overhead; another may appear behind a translucent wall, moving from one pool of light to the next. Their presence is suggested more often by the adjustments they leave behind: a warmed handrail, a pressure door easing open, a cable shifting its angle by a fraction. The settlement feels maintained, not abandoned, but its maintenance is largely invisible.

Nothing grows in the seams between buildings. There is no moss, no grass, no rooted life at all. The mineral surfaces support a faint, nearly imperceptible film that alters the color of wet stone from charcoal to green-black, though it may be chemistry rather than biology. Once in a long while, something pale and many-legged crosses a pool’s edge and disappears into a drainage slot. No one follows it.

The machines are active in ways that are difficult to watch. A bridge will lengthen by a finger’s width while no one is on it. A tower subtly changes its lean as the mountain settles. Near the upper opening, a ring of metal tilts toward the light, though it has no visible joint; the shadows around it bend a little later than they should. The city’s deeper systems make no distinct noise. Their work is felt as a pressure in the soles, a brief loosening in the ears, the quiet correction of a hanging cable after the rock shivers.

Most sounds are small and separate: water ticking into a basin, the thin scrape of a door, a distant tone when two elevated decks align. Sometimes wind reaches the opening and produces a low, hollow note, like air passing over the lip of a glass. It lasts only a few seconds. Then the city returns to its held quiet, with the wet stone shining in the daylight and the mountain continuing, almost imperceptibly, to make room for it.
