The place is hidden behind the innermost wall of a mountain station, in a maintenance space too small to appear on any plan. It is called a catchment, though it catches nothing from the mountain. The mountain’s rock is nowhere exposed. Floor, walls, ledges, and ceiling are all made: a close-fitting shell of dark ceramic, poured in shallow folds and polished by centuries of water. Its seams are narrow as hair. Condensation beads along them, gathers weight, and drops.

The room is warm enough that every surface seems to be breathing. Water shines in the dimness: a thin runnel crossing the floor, little pooled basins under overhangs, silver threads weeping from the walls. The ceiling is low and irregular, though no part of it was ever broken or worn down. Its hollows were shaped to hold moisture; its ridges steer it. Here and there, translucent panels glow faintly from within, the color of old tea. Their light shows only what is near: a step, a slick ledge, a crowded tangle of pipes disappearing into dark.

There is no view outward, but the air tastes high and thin, filtered through a long climb of machinery. The chamber sits above the cloudline. Warm vapor is drawn up from far below, cooled through a buried exchange, and returned here in a steady, patient rain. The system makes no attempt to imitate wilderness. It simply keeps the water moving.

Almost everything in the room is alive. Small pale crabs crowd the cracks, their shells soft-looking and faintly opalescent. They climb the damp walls and feed on films too fine to see. Long-legged insects hang beneath the ceiling in clusters, ticking their wings against the ceramic. In the runnel, blind fish flicker between submerged slots. At the upper end of the chamber, where a warm vent breathes, a knot of thumb-sized amphibians presses together in the mist. They are quiet until the water shifts. Then the whole place answers them.

The vegetation is sparse: a few black-green fronds rooted in fitted cups, a velvet of moss-like growth along the wettest seams. Each plant seems carefully rationed, each leaf held out in the dark as if listening. The animals are not rationed. They occupy every level of the space—under the floor grates, in the wall joints, on the hanging chains, in the pools and warm air. Their small movements overlap until the chamber feels densely inhabited, though no creature is large enough to cast a proper shadow.

The sound is enormous for such a small place. Water hammers the shallow basins, spills through slit channels, and beats steadily inside the walls. A pump somewhere beyond the shell gives off a deep, almost inaudible thrum beneath the sharper noise. Insects rasp. Crabs scrape their shells over ceramic. The vent whistles in a pitch that makes the teeth ache. There is no silence here, only moments when one sound yields to another, like a wave passing over a reef.

Nothing appears to be failing. The surfaces are worn smooth where they should be, but not eroded: the wear is anticipated, measured, part of the design. The channels alter their flow by fractions. A ledge warms when the amphibians gather on it. The ceiling opens tiny pores when the air grows too damp. The whole catchment adjusts without visible haste, maintaining a balance that must have held for a very long time.

To enter it is to step into a world made entirely of shelter: a pocket of manufactured rain, high above the weather, humming and crowded in the dark. The door closes softly behind you. The water does not notice.
