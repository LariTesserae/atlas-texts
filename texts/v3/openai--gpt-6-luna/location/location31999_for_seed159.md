The place lies beneath the earth, but it is not a cave in the old, settled sense. It is a young hollow: a long region of crust lifting and cracking as if the ground has only just begun to make room for it. The roof hangs low in some districts and climbs out of sight in others, broken by seams through which ordinary daylight sifts. It falls in broad, pale shafts, softened by steam and drifting grit, so the immense space is lit like an overcast plain. Nothing grows in that light. It only reveals.

The floor is still deciding what shape to take. Ridges swell from it in blunt, unfinished folds; fresh faces of dark stone shine where they have split open. Pits fill, empty, and fill again as warm water seeps from the walls. Here and there, thin runnels cross the rock, briefly joining into shallow pools before vanishing into new cracks. The air is damp enough to bead on the skin, especially beneath the cooler roof, but there is no steady river and no great underground lake. Moisture comes in fits: a misting from the stone, a sudden downpour from an overhead seam, the soft hiss of a whole wall sweating at once.

The stone is warm. Not furnace-hot, but warm as a living animal’s flank. In places the surface pulses upward by a finger’s breadth over the course of a day. Cracks widen. New shelves push out, then crumble at their edges. The ground has little patience for anything built on it.

And everything else seems to share that impatience.

Fauna crowd the hollow so thickly that the empty spaces between them feel temporary. Pale, many-jointed crawlers move over one another in layered traffic, their hard backs ticking against the rock. Long-bodied animals slide through the shallows, lifting their blunt heads to taste the air. Some cling in clusters beneath ledges; others tumble in loose, churning masses down the slopes and break apart at the bottom. Near the light shafts, thin-winged creatures rise and fall in clouds. Their wings are almost transparent, and when they turn together the whole cavern flashes, not with glitter but with the brief, flat brightness of a great school of fish.

There are larger shapes among them. Broad, plated animals wedge themselves into fresh fissures and lever them wider. Blind herds push from one basin to the next, making the ground shiver under their weight. A few seem to have no clear front or back: soft rings of gripping mouths that roll slowly across the stone, leaving behind clean, wet surfaces. The smaller animals feed on the shed skins, on eggs, on one another, and on the thin mineral films that form wherever water pauses. The whole ecology is bluntly practical. Nothing blooms. Nothing stands still long enough to put down roots.

The sounds accumulate until they become part of the terrain. There is a constant dry chatter of shells and claws, a low, uneven rushing from the moving herds, and above it the flap and beat of countless wings. Water taps from the roof into puddles, then is swallowed by the noise. Now and then the stone itself gives a deep, hollow crack. Everything goes quiet for a breath. Then the animals resume, louder than before, as if the sound were something they had to keep in motion.

The few marks of people are scattered and provisional. A walkway of flat stone slabs crosses one flooded hollow; it dips where the floor has sunk and ends at a wall that was not there last season. Hand-cut steps lead to an observation shelf. On it stands a crude frame of poles and knotted cord, hung with little clappers that ring when the air shifts. No one has tried to make a town. There are small stone shelters near a dry ridge, built low and weighted against the ground, and a few smoky lamps protected behind screens. The inhabitants mark safe passages with scratches and cairns, knowing that neither will remain safe nor recognizable for long.

A person crossing the hollow does not feel underground so much as enclosed inside a process. The air streams in one direction, then reverses. A shallow basin drains uphill as a tremor lifts one side of it. A herd passes through a place where a wall was yesterday, and behind it the rock is already sweating. Light moves over the fractures without changing much in brightness; it is the landscape beneath the light that keeps changing.

By night—or what passes for night here—the daylight seams dim only slightly. The crowds of animals continue to move, and their many small sounds do not fade. Warmth gathers in the hollows. Condensation thickens, and the roof lets go of water in slow, scattered drops. In the morning, some of the pools have shifted, some of the ridges have slumped, and the tracks of the great herds have been filled with new stone dust.

This is not a place that has been worn into its shape. It is being made, noisily and all at once, by pressure, water, and the immense, busy insistence of life.
