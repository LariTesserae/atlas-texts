The basin is small enough to cross before the light changes, but it contains more edges than distance: shelves within shelves, narrow courts, stairways descending into shallow water, bridges passing under other bridges. It sits high in the mountains, cupped between pale cliffs whose faces have been rounded by centuries of seepage. Nothing here looks newly made by the land. The cliffs have settled into their shapes—arched recesses, blunt fins, deep grooves that end in hanging beads of water. Yet the water is never still for long.

It threads over the rock in clear, shallow sheets, gathers in the joints between paving stones, vanishes through a crack, and appears again several yards away. At times it seems to move sideways, though the slope says otherwise. The people of the basin do not argue about which way it is going. They build for all directions.

The stone is chalk-gray, blue in shadow, and white wherever the light catches its wet surface. It is veined with dark mineral seams and tiny flecks that glitter like ground glass. The basin is cold even at midday. Its air lies close to the skin, damp enough to bead on wool and eyelashes, and the light is startlingly bright: a hard, clean brightness reflected from the water, the cliffs, and the crusts of frost that linger in sheltered places. Shadows have sharp edges. In them, everything seems submerged.

The settlement is packed into a crescent of terraces at the basin’s narrowest end. Its buildings are low and tightly joined, made from fitted stone, timber, woven reed, and slabs of dark horn-like material gathered from the upper slopes. Roofs overlap; passages pass beneath houses; doorways open onto ledges barely wider than a person. There is no grand wall. The cliffs and the water make one. From above, the town looks like a knot tied into the rock.

The people use little iron. Their tools are stone-edged, bone-handled, or made from soft, dark metal that can be hammered cold. They wedge, lash, carve, and weight. Every structure shows the hand that made it, but almost nothing is straight. A wall bends around a spring. A stair narrows to let a stream pass. A bridge is rebuilt in pieces whenever the water changes its mind. These alterations are not signs of decay; they are the town’s ordinary maintenance, as familiar as sweeping a floor.

For all its close construction, the place is strangely quiet. Water runs everywhere, but most of it travels under stone or through shallow channels lined with felt-like growth. Falls break into mist before they can roar. The strongest sounds are small ones: a drip landing in a bowl, a reed scraping against a roof, the soft clicks of animals moving behind the walls. Voices carry a short way and then seem to be absorbed by the wet air. When a storm comes, it is possible to watch the whole basin shudder—water climbing steps, roofs streaming, ledges briefly turning to channels—without hearing much more than a faint, continuous hush.

There is little green. Tufts of wiry grass cling to cracks above the waterline. Dark cushions of moss grow where the sun never reaches. A few low shrubs, silver-leaved and crooked, have rooted in the gravel at the foot of the cliffs. They are not enough to soften the stone. The abundant life belongs mostly to animals.

Small pale fish slip through gutters and flooded courtyards, even where there seems to be no connection to the larger pools. Long-legged birds roost in the eaves and pick insects from the walls. Thick-furred, blunt-snouted creatures nest under the lowest houses; they emerge at dusk to browse on moss, then vanish again through drains too narrow for them to have entered. There are bright, jointed things in the wet cracks, transparent except for their moving black eyes. People share their passages with these animals without much ceremony. A bowl set outside a door may contain water in the morning, a sleeping bird by noon, and nothing by evening.

The basin changes constantly, but not as a new place changes. Its turbulence is old and practiced. A whole courtyard may flood in the space of an hour, then empty through openings that were invisible before. Stone steps shift by a finger’s width. A channel that has run beneath the town for generations may begin to pour over a roof instead. The inhabitants read these changes in small signs: the angle of a drifting feather, the sudden stillness of insects, a cold draft rising from a drain. They move their goods, lift their sleeping platforms, and wait.

Sometimes the water leaves behind objects that do not belong to anyone: smooth white stones with warm centers, tiny shells far above the sea, strips of blue clay folded like fabric. The town keeps these things in niches along the upper walkways. No one says where they come from. On certain bright mornings, reflections appear in the pools that show the terraces crowded with houses not yet built, or show the basin under a sky with unfamiliar stars. The images last only as long as the water remains unbroken.

By night the settlement becomes a collection of dim openings, though the basin itself stays bright beneath the ice-glow of the cliffs. The small animals stir in the walls. Water shifts in its hidden channels. The people sleep lightly, ready for the familiar rearrangement of the world before dawn. At first light, the same old stone is there, worn smooth and shining; the same narrow terraces cling to it. Only the routes between them may have changed.
