The place occupies a shallow hollow on a high, old ridge—small enough that, from its center, you can see the rim all around. The rim is not a wall so much as a gathering of dark shoulders: rounded stone, softened by ages of rain, with a few crooked pines leaning out over the drop. Beyond it, the land falls away into cloud. On clear mornings the hollow seems to float above the lower world, a green pocket held in the open air.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in a hurry. It shines between the grasses, wells up beneath flat stones, and beads along the roots of low shrubs. A narrow runnel crosses the hollow, vanishes under a mat of moss, then reappears a few paces away with a louder voice. After rain, the ground gives slightly underfoot. Even in dry weather, the air smells of wet earth and crushed leaves.

The plants grow thickly where the water settles and leave generous bare spaces where the stone rises. Long, bright sedges ring the pools; broad-leaved herbs crowd the sheltered edges. Moss covers the shaded rocks in velvety patches, while the sun-facing faces remain pale and smooth. Here and there stand small trees, widely spaced, their trunks bent by the ridge wind. They do not form a forest. Each has room around it, and each seems to have been growing in precisely that spot for a very long time.

Though the hollow is warm and lush, the light is clear and ordinary. Sun falls cleanly through the moving cloud, brightening the water without making it glitter too fiercely. Mist comes and goes at ankle height. Sometimes it gathers in the runnel and travels against the current, slowly, as if deciding where the lower ground lies.

The sound is much larger than the place. Water talks constantly: ticking from the stones, whispering in the sedges, booming softly where it passes beneath the turf. Wind catches the hollow and makes the grass hiss in broad waves, though the trees barely move. A few small birds call from the rim, then fall silent. There are insects in the warm hours, but not many; their thin buzzing seems to come from the ground itself. When everything pauses, the stones can be heard contracting in the sun with faint, sharp clicks.

Animals visit rather than inhabit it. There are narrow tracks through the grass, paw prints in the soft banks, and occasional tufts of fur caught on a thorn. The creatures that make them remain mostly unseen. The pools contain little life that is immediately recognizable: dark larvae cling beneath the water’s skin, and pale threads drift among the reeds. They gather in the same corners year after year, though no one has seen them arrive.

Nothing here is built. There are no cut stones, paths, or cleared places—only a few natural stepping-rocks set at the most convenient crossings, though they do not form a route so much as a suggestion. The hollow’s order is old and unhurried. Water has worn channels around certain rocks but not through them; roots have grown over the channels and held them in place. Every feature seems to have met every other feature long ago and settled the matter.

The strangest rule is that the water remembers its shapes. A footprint in a muddy edge fills at once, but the tiny pool it leaves remains after the print itself has softened away. A fallen leaf may float for days at the same bend, turning slowly without traveling downstream. In the runnel, two currents sometimes pass side by side without mingling for several feet, one clear and one faintly clouded, before the stones bring them together again.

These oddities are modest. They do not disturb the hollow’s calm; they belong to it as plainly as the moss and the wind. The place changes, but only by degrees: a bank slumps over several wet seasons, a tree leans farther toward the rim, a pool deepens by the thickness of a fallen leaf. Most days, the hollow appears unchanged. Yet the water is always making some small adjustment, and the green things are always taking it up.
