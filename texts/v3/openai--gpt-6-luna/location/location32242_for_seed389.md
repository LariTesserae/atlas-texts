The city stands so high that its upper districts seem built into the weather. Below them, the land falls away in successive shelves of black stone, pale mineral crust, and sheer faces still too new to have acquired the softened shapes of age. The horizon is not a line so much as a series of rising walls: a distant range thrust up behind the nearer one, then another beyond it, each ridge sharply cut against the brightness. In places the mountains end abruptly, as if the world has only just lifted them and has not yet decided what to do next.

The stone is warm even in shadow. Heat rises through cracks in the plateau and gathers beneath the city’s foundations, where pipes and broad metal ducts carry it from one district to another. The air is thin, dry, and faintly metallic. It smells of hot mineral dust, machine oil, and the clean, sharp scent that comes just before rain. The sun is ordinary in its light but relentless in its warmth, whitening exposed rock and making distant structures tremble at their edges.

There is little water, though the place is never entirely dry. Moisture appears in brief, local ways: a skin of condensation beneath bridges at dawn, a trickle down a wall where warm air meets a cold seam of stone, rain caught for a few hours in shallow basins. Water collects in the hollows of the new terrain, dark and still, reflecting the undersides of towers. Some pools disappear before they can gather silt. Others remain for years in deep fractures, their surfaces filmed with mineral colors. Nothing grows in them. Nothing green takes root in the cracks.

The city follows the ridges rather than covering them. Its districts are packed tightly along the available shelves: stacked workshops, furnace halls, narrow residential blocks, lifting platforms, reservoirs, substations, and walkways spanning gaps too wide for streets. Construction is dense, but not old. Buildings have the blunt, provisional look of things erected before the ground beneath them has finished moving. Their walls are plates of ceramic, dark alloy, and pale stone cut directly from nearby slopes. Some structures seem to brace the mountain; others disappear into it, their windows and service doors set flush with the cliff.

At the city’s center, industrial towers rise above the roofs in clustered ranks. Their vents breathe slowly. A low red glow marks the mouths of furnaces, visible through grates and glass, but the factories do not roar. Their machinery turns at a measured pace, each cycle spread out and deliberate. A belt may carry one load across an entire hall while no worker is in sight. Cranes stand poised over quarries where blocks are being separated from the plateau one careful cut at a time. The sound of the city is mostly contained: a faint vibration through the ground, the occasional clank from a cooling pipe, a distant motor winding up and then holding its note.

Between the built districts, raw stone is left exposed. Not preserved as wilderness, exactly; it is simply too early for the city to have covered everything. Newly opened cuts show layers that have not yet been worn into recognizable patterns. Fresh faces of rock shine with a dark, glassy sheen. Hairline fractures widen almost imperceptibly over the span of a season. Now and then, a seam exhales warm vapor. The city’s survey markers move from week to week—not because the mountains shift dramatically, but because the measures themselves keep becoming out of date.

Erosion has barely begun. Rain does not carve valleys so much as leave temporary stains. Dust gathers in corners, then is drawn away by the next faint gust. Sharp edges remain sharp for generations. Where water does find a path, it makes narrow, shining channels that stop at the first ledge and vanish into stone. The cliffs are full of unfinished forms: overhangs that have not collapsed, pillars not yet separated from the wall, ledges that may become roads or ruins or neither.

There is almost no life. No grass in the pavement, no trees on the slopes, no visible bloom of anything. The few animals that cross the upper terraces are rare and difficult to place: small, pale shapes with broad feet, appearing briefly at the edge of a rain pool and then slipping into cracks too narrow to enter. They leave little trace. Sometimes, high above the rooftops, a dark shape circles once around a vent stack, then disappears into the glare. The city records these sightings without agreement about what they are.

At certain hours, the altitude makes the distances seem wrong. A tower on the next ridge looks close enough to touch, though reaching it requires a long descent through switchbacks and lifts. The air between the ridges holds a faint shimmer. When rain comes, it arrives in short, isolated sheets, silvering one district while the next remains sunlit. Water ticks on metal roofs, runs briefly along gutters, and drops from the city’s underside into open air.

The place does not feel abandoned, but it feels patient. Its engines operate. Its lamps come on. People must live in the close-built blocks and cross the suspended walkways, though they are rarely visible from the open slopes. The city keeps taking material from the mountain and making shelter from it, while the mountain continues to rise beneath the city at a speed too slow to hear. For now, the two fit together: dense construction on enormous heights, dampness in a hot and largely lifeless world, and a landscape whose most striking feature is that it has not finished becoming a landscape yet.
