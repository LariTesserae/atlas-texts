The high country is called the Glassback, though little of it is glass. It is a broad, sunlit tableland lifted so far above the lowlands that its far rim seems to float in the haze. The air is thin and hot; shadows are hard-edged at noon, and the bare stone holds warmth well into the night. From one ridge, a person can see miles of broken terraces and folded basins—but nowhere is there much empty ground. Every shelf is crowded with a different arrangement of rock, water, animal, or ruin.

The plateau is old, and its shapes have settled into themselves. Dark basalt makes long steps across the slopes. Pale mineral seams run through it like dried rivers. In the basins, wind has gathered grit into smooth, shallow fans, while the higher edges remain sharp enough to catch a boot. Erosion is slow here except where water briefly takes hold. Rain arrives in sudden, slanting sheets, runs in narrow silver lines over the stone, then disappears into cracks before it can make a stream. At dawn, moisture beads on the undersides of ledges and on the cold metal of abandoned machinery. By midmorning it is gone.

Vegetation is sparse and low: tough grey cushions wedged into joints in the rock, a few thorny shrubs around seep-holes, and copper-colored grasses that grow in tight patches where salts and water meet. These small green places are crowded with life. Wingless insects sift through the grass. Broad-backed lizards hold the sun on their flanks. Pale, long-legged grazers pick their way across the ledges in compact herds, while black carrion birds circle above them without flapping. In the deeper basins, something heavy moves beneath the scree, leaving fresh furrows that are already filling with dust.

The abundance is easy to miss because the Glassback is so quiet. Most animals have learned not to waste sound in the exposed air. There is no continuous roar of surf or forest, only the faint ticking of insects, the dry scrape of claws on stone, and the occasional abrupt rush of wings. Far away, a metal wheel turns once, then again. When the wind rises, it carries a low, almost vocal hum through the narrow cuts between ridges. The sound seems to come from underground.

Here and there, the plateau bears the remains of an industrial age: a line of iron pylons marching across an empty span, a squat pumping station built against a cliff, a cableway whose hanging buckets have rusted in place. A few installations still work. Condensers draw the night’s dampness from the air and drip it into covered cisterns. Their pumps labor slowly, powered by heat from deep vents or by engines that cough awake for an hour at a time. The buildings are scattered, but the marks of their work are everywhere—drill holes in the stone, pale spoil heaps, old rails half buried in grit.

The machines do not rule the landscape. They have become part of its rhythm. Small animals nest inside the warm housings; lizards gather on the pipes; birds perch on the cables to watch the grazers below. At times, pressure in the geothermal cracks releases a breath of steam from the ground, and the condensers begin to click as the air cools around them. Then the sparse water gathers briefly in little basins, and the whole plateau seems to draw its inhabitants toward those shining points. For a few hours the shelves are alive with motion: feeding, drinking, hunting, jostling for space. By evening, the pools have sunk into the stone, and the animals have melted back into its many folds.

From a distance the Glassback looks empty, a country of heat and dark rock beneath an ordinary, brilliant sky. Up close, it is packed almost to the limit: ledges layered over ledges, colonies fitted into cracks, old routes crossing newer ones, each scarce wet place claimed and remembered. Nothing here is lush, and almost nothing is truly still. The plateau simply keeps its activity close to the ground, quiet under the sun.
