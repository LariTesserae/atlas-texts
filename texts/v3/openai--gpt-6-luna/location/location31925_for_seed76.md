The high country has no horizon at first. It rises through pale distance after pale distance, a continent of old uplands whose ridges have been worn smooth by ages of wind. Beyond them, more uplands lift into the thin, bright sky. The land is so broad that weather takes its time crossing it. A bank of dust may be visible for days before it reaches you.

There is no water: no river-cut valleys, no glimmering tarns, not even the suggestion of a damp place beneath the stones. The ground is mineral-dry, powdered in hollows with grey-white grit and crusted on the exposed slopes with salts that catch the light. Yet the emptiness is not barren. Across the flats grow immense, low fields of living color—olive, blue-green, and deep plum—close-packed mats that swell over the rock like fur. In sheltered clefts, taller stalks rise in loose groves, their narrow leaves thin as blades of glass. Their surfaces are waxy and cool to the touch. They draw what they need from air, light, and stone; nothing here needs a stream.

From a distance the growth seems continuous, a lushness spread almost to the world’s edges. On foot, its pattern resolves into islands. A green slope ends abruptly at bare scree; a whole valley may be empty except for a few soft cushions around the base of a ridge. These gaps are wide and undisturbed. The plants have had time to settle into the land, to thicken where the rock holds warmth and minerals, to retreat from the wind-scoured crowns. Their borders are neither neat nor advancing. They look less like a wilderness in the act of conquering the ground than one that has long since found its balance.

The terrain has been shaped by slow, persistent forces. Ridges are rounded at the top and fluted down their lee sides. Stone shelves descend in broad steps, each edge softened by abrasion. Here and there, isolated pillars remain where harder rock resisted the weather; at their feet lie fans of fragments, fine as meal near the center and scattered into the vegetation at the edges. Nothing looks newly broken. Even the sharpest stones have a dull, polished calm.

The light is startlingly clear. It falls hard from a pale sun, bright enough to make every grain of grit distinct, while the air stays cool and thin. Shadows are crisp, but they have little warmth in them. At midday the slopes shine silver-green; by late afternoon the plants darken toward ink, and the salt crusts turn faintly rose-colored. The sky is enormous, almost empty. Clouds are rare and high, more like faint stains than weather, and they cast shadows that crawl across the uplands with glacial slowness.

The wind is the chief movement. It combs the plant mats until their surfaces bend in wide, traveling ripples. It lifts dry dust from the ridges and sets it drifting in low veils, then lets it settle without a sound. In the open, there is little to hear beyond a soft brushing, the occasional click of grit striking stone, and the far-off sigh of air passing through the taller stalks. The quiet is not absolute; it is simply too large to be filled.

Animals are scarce. A long-legged grazer might appear on a distant slope, moving with such patience that it can be mistaken for a stone changing position. Smaller creatures shelter beneath the mats, leaving only faint seams through the growth. Once in a while, a pale glider rides an updraft between ridges, turning its narrow body to the light and vanishing against the sky. Their traces fade quickly. There are no nests clustered together, no trails worn deep, no calls answering across the plain.

Still, the place is active in its own measured way. The plant fields change color as the sun moves; dust gathers, shifts, and exposes old bands of rock. Warm air rises from dark slopes and draws cooler currents down from the heights. Along the great ridgelines, the wind changes direction with the long turning of the seasons, and whole regions of vegetation flex as though breathing. The changes are slow enough to seem permanent while you watch them, then unmistakable if you return.

Nothing has been built here. No road crosses the flats, no marker stands above the growth. The land does not seem unfinished for want of either. It has its own scale of presence: ridges that take days to cross, weather that belongs to a whole province, living fields that spread and thin across distances too wide for any one creature to possess. The bright, cool silence holds all of it without haste.
