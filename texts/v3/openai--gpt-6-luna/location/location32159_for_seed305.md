The plateau rises so high that weather has to gather its breath before crossing it.

It is a young place: lifted, split, and still sharp at the edges. Black volcanic shelves shoulder through pale stone; ridgelines look newly broken from the earth, their faces raw and angular, with only the first softening of ice and wind. Between them lie basins big enough to hold their own weather. Clouds snag on the upper crags and come apart into bright veils. Rain is uncommon, but moisture is everywhere in brief, precise forms: beads of condensation under leaves, a slickness on stone where fog has passed, thin streams appearing at noon and freezing again by evening. In the deepest hollows, water persists beneath translucent ice, dark as a held breath.

The light is astonishing. At this altitude the sun seems close and unfiltered, bleaching the snowfields and turning every wet surface to a shard of brightness. Shadows are blue and sharply edged. Even on the coldest days the plateau gleams—mineral seams glittering in the cliffs, frost shining on tangled growth, the backs of animals flashing silver as they turn together.

There is almost no empty ground. Vegetation crowds the cracks and shelves in dense, low masses: cushion plants, wiry shrubs, broad dark fronds that lie flat against the wind. In sheltered folds, growth rises higher, stiff stalks and branching thickets packed so closely that their interiors are dim and humid. Some plants are furred with pale filaments that catch passing mist. Others bear copper-colored leaves that tremble without seeming to move, each edge alive with tiny beads of water. The green is deep, not gentle. It has the tough, saturated look of life that has learned to grow fast in every pocket where the rock permits it.

And the rock permits very little. Roots wedge into new fractures, pry them wider, and bind the broken pieces in place. Meltwater etches narrow channels down the slopes. Fresh scree slithers now and then, a small collapse in a landscape whose larger changes take centuries. The plateau is restless by geological measure, but to anything living on it, it is almost still.

The animals fill that stillness completely.

They occupy the ledges, the passes, the shallow lakes under ice, the tunnels between root mats. There are thick-bodied grazers with brushlike coats, moving in immense, close-packed herds that flow around stone outcrops without breaking formation. Long-legged scavengers pace the ridges in strings, their cries carrying across whole basins. Small pale creatures pulse through the vegetation in such numbers that a hillside appears to shiver. In the sky, broad-winged gliders ride the updrafts shoulder to shoulder; when they bank, their shadows sweep the ground like a passing cloud. Beneath the ice, something larger turns slowly in the dark, and the water above it dimples in a line.

Their abundance does not make the place feel fertile in a mild or welcoming way. It makes it feel occupied. Every shelf has a resident. Every patch of vegetation is grazed, burrowed, nested in, or guarded. The animals press against one another for warmth and space, forming living knots in the lee of boulders. Their breath clouds the air. Their tracks overlap until the snow resembles a written surface no eye can read.

The sound is equally crowded. Wind pours over the heights with a steady, metal-edged roar. Herds grind across stone; claws click on frozen shelves; wings beat the air in heavy flurries. The calls of the larger animals travel far, but are rarely alone. Underneath everything is a low, persistent vibration from the plateau itself: ice shifting in hidden chambers, water forcing through stone, mineral springs boiling softly beneath caps of frost. At certain ridges, vents exhale in measured pulses. Their breath condenses into white banners, while the ground answers with a deep rhythmic thudding, as if some enormous engine were turning very slowly below.

Nothing here is built. No walls, roads, towers, or abandoned instruments interrupt the land. Yet the plateau has the aspect of a vast works. Its processes repeat with daunting precision. Wind-driven fields of stiff grasses flex and return. Warm seepage is routed through branching channels of porous stone. Mineral-rich water gathers in natural basins, passes through layers of root and lichen, then emerges clear and cold somewhere lower down. In sheltered craters, dense growth converts the brief summer light into heat, gas, and a sharp metallic smell. The landscape manufactures and transports; it filters, grinds, vents, stores, and releases. Its machinery is not separate from its ecology. The animals migrate along its currents; plants grow over its conduits; ice supplies pressure to the hidden mechanisms.

The cycles are slow enough to be mistaken for permanence. Herds return to the same windbreaks over generations. A basin fills by imperceptible increments. A ridge loses one stone at a time, then suddenly wears a different profile. The brightest season lasts only a little longer than a blink, but the growth set in motion by it continues through the long cold. Nothing hurries. Even the great migrations have the stateliness of geology.

From a distant height the plateau looks almost clean: a white and green expanse beneath a hard blue sky, with dark ridges drawn across it like fractures in glass. Nearer, it is all texture and pressure—fur brushing stems, ice beneath leaves, water hidden in stone, the ground pulsing with slow force. It is not quiet, not empty, and not finished becoming itself. It simply takes its time.
