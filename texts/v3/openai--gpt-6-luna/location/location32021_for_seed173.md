The basin is small enough to know by its boundaries: a sunken industrial court, perhaps a hundred paces across, enclosed by walls that once held back water and now hold it in. There is no natural ground. The floor is poured concrete, patched in places with ceramic plates and iron grates; the walls are brick, riveted steel, and old glazed tile. Every surface was made for a purpose, though most of those purposes have ended.

Time has taken the sharpness from it. Corners are rounded down to broad curves. Mortar seams have opened into shallow grooves; bolts wear smooth under the steady passage of water. A fallen gantry lies across the floor like a low bridge, its beams softened with pale crust and silt. Nothing here looks newly broken. Even the wreckage has settled into a final arrangement.

The water is cold and close, a dark blue-grey that thins only near the upper gaps in the walls. There, a weak wash of light trickles down from the open water beyond the basin—not sunlight so much as the lingering suggestion of it. Below, the machinery gives off a few dull amber lamps. Their light catches drifting particles, the occasional silver flash of a fish, and the wet shine of metal. Beyond the lamps, the basin ends quickly in shadow.

Machines still work. A pump turns somewhere behind the western wall, slowly enough that its beat feels geological: a low, even *thrum*, followed by a tremor through the floor. A chain in the high rigging knocks every few seconds. From a narrow vent comes a rushing hiss that swells and ebbs with the pump’s cycle. Underwater, these sounds travel everywhere. They fill the basin so completely that silence seems impossible.

The current moves in broad, dependable circuits. Water draws down through a grated trench, passes through the old pump chamber, then spills back from a row of high outlets. The circulation keeps the basin from becoming stagnant, and has made a home of every ledge and cavity. Fish gather in the outlet streams, where small creatures are brought within reach. Eels thread through the conduits. Crabs pick at the worn joints of the floor. Schools of pale, thumb-long fish turn together around the legs of the gantry, their bodies flashing whenever they cross the lamps.

Life is abundant but not lush. There are few plants: thin fans of algae along the tile seams, soft green-black tufts at the mouths of pipes, a little weed wavering in the lee of the fallen gantry. The animals have more room than the vegetation. They occupy the machinery as if it were reef: a shaft is a burrow, a valve housing a hollow, a cable run a route through the dark. Larger fish shelter beneath the overturned plates, stirring silt with their tails.

The basin changes by small, regular degrees. The pump deepens its note as pressure builds; the current strengthens; suspended grit lifts from the floor and travels in slow veils around the room. Then the machinery eases, the grit settles, and the fish return to their stations. Nothing in the cycle is mysterious. The place is old, but it is not strange. Its laws are the familiar laws of water, weight, rust, hunger, and a machine that has not yet stopped.
