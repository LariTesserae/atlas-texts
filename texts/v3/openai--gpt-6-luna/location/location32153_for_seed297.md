The plain lies almost level beneath a warm, ordinary sky. It is wide enough for distance to matter: low ridges withdraw toward the horizon, and beyond them more low ridges, all with the blunt, unfinished look of land that has only just learned how to be land. The ground is dark, sharp-edged mineral—basalt-black in some places, bronze and greenish where light catches its grain. It has not yet softened under weather. Fractures end abruptly. Slabs meet at clean, improbable angles, as if the whole region rose from beneath the world in one careful movement and was set down before it could crumble.

There is no water. No riverbed, no salt lake, no dampness hidden in the shade. The air has none of the cool, shifting smell of wet ground. Even the low places are dry. A pale powder rests in the seams between the rock, though it never blows. It resembles salt, but when examined closely it is made of tiny overlapping scales, each one holding a thin image of the sky.

The vegetation is abundant, crowding the plain in thick, overlapping growth. It does not resemble a forest so much as a green mineral weather. Tall fronds rise from the cracks, jointed and translucent, colored deep jade at their bases and almost clear at their tips. Broad, low fans spread between them in layers, their surfaces ribbed with fine silver lines. Some forms stand upright like reeds; others make soft mounds, piled petal over petal. Their roots are visible everywhere, not buried but gripping the stone in intricate nets. The roots shine faintly, as if they are conducting something too slow to be called light.

Nothing here needs water. The plants draw from the rock, from warmth, and from the pale radiance that settles over the plain. Their tissues hold no liquid. Cut or broken, they do not bleed or wilt; they divide into hard, flexible filaments and remain open to the air. A fallen frond can lie among the living growth for what seems like years, still faintly green, its veins continuing to rearrange the sunlight.

The growth is dense, close to the ground and close to itself. Fronds brush one another without making much sound. The silence is not empty: it has the pressure of a great number of small presences withholding their voices. Here and there, a click passes through the stems, too delicate to locate. It may be the plants exchanging some signal, or the mineral beneath them settling—though nothing ever seems to settle.

Across the plain, the land occasionally fails to agree with its own shape. A narrow cleft appears to run toward the horizon, but looking along it reveals the same patch of plants that stands behind you. A stone may cast two shadows, both perfectly still and pointing in different directions. In certain places the distance folds gently: a ridge seen far away is also just beyond the next cluster of fronds, close enough to touch. These irregularities are not dangerous in any obvious way. They do not flicker or spread. They are simply part of the terrain, held in place as securely as the rock.

Sometimes a visitor steps around a low black boulder and comes out on the other side beneath a different arrangement of stars, though it is still daytime. The warmth remains the same. The plants do not react. If the visitor turns back, the boulder is where it was, and the original sky has returned. There are no marks left behind by these passages. The place refuses to preserve evidence of having been otherwise.

The vegetation occupies nearly every space that might be called soil, yet large clearings open between the thick growth. They are not bare in the usual sense. Their surfaces are smooth mineral plates, faintly iridescent, crossed by sharp seams that make patterns too regular to be accidental and too irregular to be designed. No path connects them. No structure stands anywhere on the plain. The apparent order belongs to the ground itself.

A few animals live here, though one can spend a long time without seeing any. At dusk—if dusk can be called a change in a sky that barely changes—small, glassy shapes sometimes move through the roots. They are low and many-legged, or perhaps they are single bodies briefly dividing into several pieces. They leave no tracks. Once, a larger presence may be glimpsed between the fronds, tall and narrow, its outline wavering as if seen through heat. Then the stems close behind it without a sound. The plants do not part for it; they are simply no longer in the way.

The light is clear and natural. Shadows fall cleanly, colors remain vivid, and the warmth lies evenly over the plain. There is no glaring sun to account for it. The sky is a mild, untroubled blue, crossed by a few thin clouds that never seem closer or farther away. The clouds keep their shapes for as long as they are watched. When no one is looking, they may change; the landscape offers no way to know.

The region feels new, but not unfinished. It has the crispness of a thing recently formed and the absolute composure of something that has been finished forever. No wind scours the ridges, no rain rounds the stone, no seasons move the vegetation through any visible cycle. The fronds remain lifted. The mineral scales remain in their seams. The air holds its warmth. Even the strange distances stay stable, as though reality here has settled into a deep, motionless equilibrium—not quite the equilibrium of nature, but of a machine whose workings are too large and too quiet to be heard.

From a height of only a few meters, one can look over the plain and see its several worlds at once: the dark rock, the dense green growth, the pale openings, the low ridges, the doubled shadows. They do not combine into a single view. They lie over one another without conflict, like transparent layers that have never been separated.

And yet the place is not hostile. It asks nothing. It does not welcome or refuse. Under the warm light, the growth continues to shine, the stone keeps its sharp edges, and the silent plain extends beyond the horizon—lush, dry, newly made, and so perfectly still that its stillness begins to feel like a law.
