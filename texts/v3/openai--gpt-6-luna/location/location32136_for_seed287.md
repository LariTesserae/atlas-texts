The place is called the Lower Conservatory, though no one can say what it was meant to conserve.

It lies beneath a low shelf of black stone, in a chamber no larger than a village square. The ceiling hangs close overhead, ribbed with old mineral deposits and the roots of things growing above. In places it has been braced by iron arches, now swollen orange with rust; in others the stone has sagged into smooth, dark folds. The floor is almost level, but not quite. It dips toward a shallow basin at the center, where a few inches of warm water gather and slowly evaporate.

The air is hot enough to feel thick. Moisture comes and goes: a fine rain ticking from the ceiling after some unseen shift in the rock, then hours of dry warmth, then beads of condensation trembling on every cold edge. The air smells of wet stone, green sap, and the faint metallic tang of machinery that has been running for a very long time.

Plants occupy nearly every surface. They crowd the cracks between the floor’s stone plates, hang from the ironwork in long ropes, and climb the walls in dense mats of waxy leaves. Some resemble ferns, though their fronds are broad and stiff as hammered sheet metal. Others grow as pale, fleshy columns that split into translucent fans near the tips. Their colors are subdued: bottle green, bruised purple, chalky white. There is no sun here, only a dim blue-green glow that seems to gather in the wet leaves and in the mineral seams of the ceiling. No single plant appears to produce it. The light is strongest where the roots overlap.

Nothing moves quickly. A leaf may take days to uncurl. Water slides down a stem by the width of a finger over an entire afternoon. The plants do not flower so much as slowly open seams along their sides, letting out a powder that clings to damp stone and settles into cracks. New growth rises where the old growth has rotted away. The Conservatory has been making itself over and over, almost without change, for longer than the iron has been iron-colored.

There are no animals. No wings stir the leaves; no small bodies live in the warm pools. Even the water seems uninhabited. The plants reproduce without visitors, their dust thickening in hollows, their roots finding one another beneath the floor. In places, a root has followed the course of a buried pipe, entered through a split in the metal, and grown inside it until the pipe’s mouth is plugged with green. Elsewhere, roots have taken the shape of old bolts and brackets, leaving behind a perfect impression when the iron finally flakes away.

The machinery is scattered rather than organized: a heavy wheel fixed into one wall, a length of ribbed pipe crossing the chamber, a squat tank half-submerged in the central basin. Most of it is fused with mineral crust or swallowed by roots. Still, at intervals, the wheel turns a few degrees. The tank shudders. A valve coughs, and hot water runs briefly through the pipes overhead before the whole chamber settles again.

The sound is much larger than the room. A low mechanical bellow hangs in the stone, interrupted by clanks, soft hammering knocks, and the sudden hiss of steam. Each drip from the ceiling becomes a clear, amplified note. The basin answers with a hollow ringing when a drop strikes it, as if water were falling into a vast metal vessel far below. The loudness has no urgency. It is simply the room’s way of existing: a deep, uneven pulse beneath the slower sounds of dripping and growth.

Nothing here seems broken in the ordinary sense. The machinery is worn, the stone is weathered, the plants have occupied every available seam, and all three have reached an arrangement that requires no caretaker. If the pump stops, the room may slowly dry. If the ceiling opens, the plants may spread upward. For now, moisture returns, the wheel turns, and the green cover thickens by imperceptible degrees.

At the far edge of the chamber, behind a curtain of leaves, a narrow stair rises into darkness. Its steps are worn concave, though no footprints remain. Warm air travels up it in a steady breath. Sometimes the plants nearest the stair lean toward that draft. Sometimes they lean away. The change is slow enough that only a person staying for a very long time could be sure it had happened at all.
