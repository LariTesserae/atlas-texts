The plateau is high enough that the sky feels close, though it gives no light. Above it lies a blackness without stars, and below it a country of dark stone that runs so far the eye cannot hold its edges. The air is warm and dry. It carries no smell of soil, rain, or decay—only the faint metallic tang of heated rock.

The land has been worn down for an immense time. Ridges have softened into long, blunt backs; cliffs have retreated into terraces; whole mountains seem to have been rubbed nearly level. Yet the plateau is not flat. Its surface breaks into shallow basins, pale seams, and immense channels where ancient flows of dust and mineral grit once scoured the stone. Nothing runs in them now. There is no water to catch, pool, or remember the shape of a river. The channels are dry to their deepest cuts.

Most of the ground is a dark, fine-grained mineral, matte as ash but hard as fired ceramic. Here and there, broad plates of glassy stone lie exposed, fractured into polygons that fit so closely they seem manufactured. The cracks between them are filled with a softer gray powder. Walking leaves prints that last for years, perhaps centuries: there is wind, but it is too weak and regular to erase much. A few steep faces still shed grains. The rest of the world appears to have reached an agreement with gravity and kept it.

The city occupies a long basin near the plateau’s crown. It is not large by the measure of a living world, but it has the layered density of a place built and rebuilt for a very long time. Towers, stacks, galleries, and enclosed bridges gather around a central spine of dark masonry. Beyond them, smaller industrial compounds stand at intervals across the plain, linked by straight causeways and buried conduits. The city is not crowded so much as concentrated: a mass of construction with wide, empty stretches between it and the next mass.

Its oldest buildings are almost indistinguishable from the rock. Their walls have been cut from the plateau itself, in broad blocks joined without visible mortar. Wind has rounded their corners until doorways look like worn hollows. Later structures are thinner and more precise—ribbed metal frames, ceramic pipework, bolted housings, tall exhaust stacks with their mouths stopped by caps of stone. Some are industrial plants, judging by the feed lines and loading bays. Their machines are still there, though most have not moved for a very long time. Great wheels sit fixed in their housings. Cables sag between pylons without swaying. A few indicator panels give off a dull, reddish gleam, too faint to illuminate anything beyond their own scratched faces.

Light is scarce enough to behave like a material. The city does not shine; it absorbs. A person emerging from a sealed interior sees only the nearest shapes at first, then the larger masses, then—after a long adjustment—the faint suggestion of distance. The black surfaces of the buildings take on slightly different shades, and their edges appear only where one darkness gives way to another. The rare pale stone catches what little radiance exists and holds it without reflecting it. At certain angles, the long causeways seem to disappear, leaving their support arches suspended over nothing.

There are no lamps along the roads. Instead, the inhabitants have marked thresholds and junctions with shallow grooves, raised ridges, and differently textured slabs. The city is navigated by foot, by touch, and by intimate knowledge of its planes. In the industrial districts, pipes have warm outer skins; a hand laid against them can tell which channels still carry heat. The machinery gives little sound. Now and then a deep, almost sub-audible vibration passes through the ground, steady as a distant engine idling beneath the plateau. It does not grow nearer or recede. No one looks up when it happens.

The structures are mostly empty, but not abandoned in the usual sense. Doors remain sealed because they have always been sealed; corridors terminate at blank walls whose purpose is recorded nowhere. Some enclosures are maintained, their joints packed and their vents cleared. Others have been left open to the city’s slow settling. Dust lies undisturbed across control desks. Small metal tags are fixed beside certain doors, each carrying a line of shallow cuts. The marks are not writing, or not writing that anyone now reads.

Sound travels oddly here. A footstep may be absorbed at once by the floor, or return much later from a wall too distant to see. There is no rustle of leaves, no insect chorus, no patter of water in pipes. Most of the time the place is silent except for the low, even warmth of its machinery. When a gate is moved, the noise does not ring out; it grinds through the stone in a short, intimate shudder. Voices are kept low. A shout can feel less like a sound than an interruption in the air.

Life is rare and difficult to recognize. In the warmer foundations, small mineral-colored creatures sometimes occupy the narrow gaps between masonry blocks. They are flat, jointed, and nearly motionless, with pale sensory filaments folded against their bodies. They do not flee quickly. They seem to register a passing person as a change in pressure, then settle again. In the open country, occasional tracks cross the powder: a sequence of paired dents, each identical in depth and spacing, as if whatever made them moved without tiring. No one has seen the maker nearby. The tracks persist for years, though on some mornings they end abruptly in unbroken stone.

Nothing grows. There is no green, no lichen, no film on the rocks, no delicate fringe in the shaded joints. The sparse inhabitants of the city draw what they need from mineral stores and from the old industrial systems. Their culture, if culture is the word, has arranged itself around maintenance and interval: check the pressure, clear the shaft, count the pulses, renew the seal. There are few gardens, few personal objects, few signs of waste. What is not useful is placed carefully aside rather than discarded.

The strangest quality of the plateau is not any single impossible thing, but its refusal to proceed. Dust settles and stays. Machines hum at the same pitch. The warm stone remains warm, neither cooling at night nor heating in the faint day that may or may not exist. The city is ancient without seeming ruined, and active without seeming alive. Even the rare creatures appear less like inhabitants than like notes the landscape has chosen to preserve.

From the outer causeway, the basin and its buildings recede into darkness. Beyond them, the plateau continues in low ridges and shallow cuts, then rises again into distant shelves of stone. There is no bright horizon, only a gradual loss of difference between land and sky. The world has been worn almost to its final form. Whatever forces shaped it have dwindled to small, patient adjustments: a grain slipping from a ledge, a pipe tightening as it cools by an amount too slight to measure, a footstep passing through the city and leaving its print behind.
