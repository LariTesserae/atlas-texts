The city stands high enough that the clouds pass through its lower streets.

From a distance it seems less built than measured: a long arrangement of dark walls, chimneys, bridges, and immense open squares, set across a plateau of black stone. There are no trees to soften its outline, no green at all. The buildings rise in separated clusters, leaving broad distances between them—empty courtyards large enough to hold weather, streets where the wind can travel for a mile without meeting a person. Even at the center, the city feels sparsely occupied. Its infrastructure is extensive; its life is not.

The stone is warm underfoot, often slick. Water seeps from joints in the pavement and gathers in shallow, iron-colored sheets. It beads on railings and hangs from the undersides of bridges. Some of it falls from the sky, though the clouds are hard to distinguish from the vapor coming off the city itself: a dim, low ceiling drifting between towers. The rain is warm and faintly mineral, leaving pale rings around drains and evaporating from sunless walls in thin, wavering veils. Where the ground is cracked, water can be heard far below, moving through the plateau.

The city’s older districts were built to conduct that water. Their stepped roofs funnel it into cisterns; their gutters descend through the walls and vanish into pipes wide enough to walk through. The pipes feed the industrial quarter, where heat wells up from deep seams in the rock. There, squat engines turn slowly in cavernous sheds. Pumps lift water, turbines grind against the heat, and long banks of condensers breathe out white mist. Some machines are still attended by small lights and gauges, but most operate without anyone visible to watch them. Their work is steady rather than urgent. A flywheel turns. A valve knocks once, then stays quiet for hours.

Heat gathers in the streets even when the sky is dark. It comes from below, from the industrial wells, and from the airless warmth trapped beneath the cloud layer. Thin air makes every breath feel brief. The city’s highest roofs stand above the vapor, but they receive little true sunlight: a dull glow filters through the haze and seems to come from everywhere at once. At times, the clouds brighten from within, showing the silhouettes of towers and crane-arms for a few minutes before the whole settlement fades back to charcoal and wet silver.

The architecture is old enough to have become part of the terrain. Corners have rounded under wind and grit. Water has cut narrow channels into steps and worn shallow bowls in the plazas. On the lower slopes, the city’s foundations merge into natural shelves of basalt, and the walls are streaked with minerals that have leached out over centuries. Nothing appears freshly ruined. Even the collapsed structures have settled into stable shapes: a roof folded inward, an avenue blocked by a smooth mound of masonry, a row of arches standing open to the weather.

There is little sound. Wind sifts through empty windows. Water ticks into drains. Somewhere behind the industrial quarter, a chain moves over a wheel with a dry, unhurried clatter. Sounds travel a long way across the open spaces, then stop abruptly at walls or in the vapors. The largest engines are almost inaudible from a distance; their vibration is felt through the soles before it becomes a sound.

The life here is sparse and difficult to notice. No plants take root in the city’s mineral soil. In warm culverts, pale threads of microbial film gather where water runs continuously, shining briefly when a maintenance lamp passes. Once in a while, a small blind animal surfaces in a cistern—soft-bodied, nearly transparent, with whiskers fine as wire. It disappears at the disturbance, leaving only a widening ring. Larger creatures are said to travel the cloud banks above the plateau, but the roofs and chimneys are usually empty.

The city’s strangest rule is that its water rarely goes where gravity says it should. In some districts, runoff climbs a wall before draining upward into a roof channel. In others, a fine sheet of water hangs across a doorway without falling, trembling whenever the deep pumps change rhythm. The oldest maps account for these deviations with careful arrows and measurements. Newer ones simply mark the places as wet.

The system is active, but never busy. Clouds arrive, condense, and pass through. The cisterns fill by increments. Pumps raise their loads. Mineral crusts grow along the seams of bridges and are slowly worn away again. In the wide plazas, rain spreads into a mirror over the dark stone, reflecting a city with more towers than the one above it. Then the water drains, leaving the pavement shining, and the city waits in its vast, warm quiet.
