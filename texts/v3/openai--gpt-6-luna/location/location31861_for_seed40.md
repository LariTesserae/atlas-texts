The plateau is called the Bright Works, though no one remembers what it was built to make.

It occupies a high shelf above the clouds. From a distance it looks like a pale, level country, but the levelness is an illusion produced by scale: the Works are a crowded arrangement of terraces, ridges, basins, ramps, and long roofs, all rising and falling by small, abrupt degrees. There is no soil beneath them. Every surface is made—cast ceramic, poured stone, ribbed metal, translucent panels dulled by weather. The materials meet in crisp seams, and the seams are so numerous that the ground seems assembled from pieces too large to lift.

The construction is young. Edges have not yet softened. Drainage channels end in sharp mouths; stairways descend to landings that have not settled into the earth, because there is no earth to settle into. Some structures look newly raised from the plateau, with their pale undersides still exposed. Others have begun to tilt by a few inches, just enough to make doors and walkways disagree about where level is. Across the open stretches, whole districts lean gently toward one another, like pages closing.

The light is strong and cold. It enters the air cleanly, with little warmth in it, and makes even distant objects look close and sharply outlined. At noon, the roofs shine so brightly that the seams between them disappear. In the afternoon, when cloud-shadow passes over the plateau, the surfaces retain a faint inner glow, as if they were remembering the sun. Below the Works, cloud banks move slowly through the gullies. Sometimes they rise high enough to wet the lower terraces. Then condensation beads along railings, gathers at the undersides of bridges, and falls in intermittent silver threads. Rain is infrequent. Water is collected obsessively: in covered cisterns, in shallow basins, in narrow channels that cross the plateau and vanish beneath buildings.

The channels are not always sloped downhill. Water follows them anyway. It may travel uphill for several hundred paces, slow to a trembling stop at an intersection, then drain suddenly into a grate on a wall. No one treats this as a failure of engineering. The people who maintain the Works say the water is taking the shortest route. They do not say shortest through what.

The place is dense with construction, but it does not feel enclosed. Beyond each cluster of towers or machinery is another open sweep of roof, another wide court, another ridge of fabricated terrain, continuing until brightness and distance flatten it. Roads run in parallel across the high country, then pass through one another without crossing. A traveler can walk for hours beside the same low barrier and eventually find it on the opposite side of the path. Distances are patient here. A destination grows nearer gradually, except for the rare moments when it is suddenly behind you.

The industrial equipment is everywhere and almost quiet. Engines turn behind thick walls. Conveyors creep beneath walkways. Tall vent-stacks breathe out slow, transparent plumes that shine in the light and scarcely stir the air. The loudest sound is often a small one: water clicking into a basin, a cable tightening, the ticking of a cooling plate. Even these sounds seem to be absorbed before they can travel. Voices carry only a short way. A shout across a court may arrive as a whisper at the far end, while a footstep from somewhere above sounds as if it were made directly under the listener’s heel.

Vegetation is sparse, but life is not. Pale mats of lichen grow in protected joints, arranged in branching patterns that resemble circuit diagrams. A few tough, fingerlike plants rise from roof-drains where dust and mineral salts have accumulated. They have no apparent need of soil. Their roots grip the undersides of ledges and spread through the manufactured substrate, sometimes emerging several floors away from the plant itself.

Animals are abundant. Small black birds nest inside signal housings and gather in the open courts in such numbers that they briefly dim the pavement. Long-legged grazers move across the roofs in quiet herds, picking at the lichen and the mineral films left by condensation. They are too large to have entered through any visible gate, and their hooves make no marks on the shining surfaces. At dawn, many of them face the same direction, toward the empty northern horizon. By midday they have dispersed among the structures, where their outlines are difficult to distinguish from rails, pipes, and shadows.

There are also quick, low creatures that slip through gaps narrower than their bodies. They leave no tracks, but wherever they pass, dust settles into neat spirals. On cool evenings, the air fills with tiny winged things that gather around lamps. They do not fly toward the light so much as occupy the space where it ought to be, forming moving knots of darkness against the white glow.

The Works have rules, though they are not written down. Shadows usually point away from the sun, but on certain terraces they point toward it, and those terraces are considered safe places to rest. A person who walks straight across a court may reach its far side; a person who measures the crossing may find the court lengthening with every measurement. Reflections are reliable only in still water. In polished metal, they show the same place a few seconds earlier—or sometimes a nearby place that has not yet been built. Workers leave small colored marks at junctions, not to show the way, but to remind the junction which way it was facing.

Nothing changes quickly. The machinery cycles with immense slowness. New seams appear over decades; old ramps rise by fractions of a handspan. The herds return to the same roofs, though the roofs have subtly shifted. Moisture gathers, evaporates, and gathers again. The plateau does not seem abandoned, exactly. It feels occupied by systems too large to notice individual visitors, and by animals that have learned to live among those systems without needing to understand them.

At night the Bright Works remain visible from below, a broad, cold glow above the clouds. The lights do not flicker. The buildings do not go dark. Somewhere inside the plateau, hidden machinery continues its careful, nearly silent work, while the bright surfaces hold their places in the dark—sharp-edged, newly made, and not quite attached to the world beneath them.
