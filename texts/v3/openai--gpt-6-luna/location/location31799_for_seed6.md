### The Upper Reservoir

The place occupies the crown of a machine.

It is not a mountain, though it stands high above the weather. It is a compact district of poured ceramic, bolted steel, and pale composite stone, raised on the roof of an industrial complex whose lower floors have vanished beneath water. The district is scarcely larger than a village square. Its boundary is a ring of retaining walls, curved inward against the pressure of the reservoir. Beyond them: water, bright and depthless at first, then darkening as it falls away. The horizon is not visible. From the upper walkways, the water seems to continue through the sky.

Everything here was made to hold something else. The paving is molded into shallow channels. The walls have seams where sections were joined. Even the rounded ledges, softened by constant wetness, are manufactured surfaces. No soil shows anywhere. The plants grow from trays, seams, suspended racks, and deep green mats fixed to the structures with fine mesh. Their roots have been guided through conduits and around pipes. Their stems crowd the passages until movement is possible only along the narrow paths left for it.

The growth is lush, but orderly in the way a flood is orderly. Broad leaves overlap the railings. Vines drape over pumps and run along the undersides of bridges. Dense fans of green emerge from wall slots, their ends wavering slowly in the water’s faint circulation. There are no blossoms that anyone has noticed; if they form, they do so within the foliage, unseen. There are no animals. Nothing flits through the leaves, nothing grazes, nothing nests. The greenery has the whole district to itself, though it does not seem to know this. It grows according to its timers and nutrient lines, pressing steadily into every available space.

Water fills the place but does not quite conquer it. The lower platforms are submerged. The upper walkways lie beneath a clear, warm depth, where the light reaches almost undisturbed and the surfaces retain the softened brightness of an ordinary day. There is no visible sun—only a broad, steady illumination from above, as if daylight has been filtered through an immense sheet of glass. Beneath it, colors remain plain and legible: green leaves, white rails, orange corrosion-control paint, dark apertures in the machinery.

A person entering from below would rise through a sequence of flooded chambers, each smaller and more densely planted than the last. Water would slip through grilles and around the body without current enough to tug. At the top, the district opens into a single crowded space: terraces, cistern mouths, handrails, planters, pressure gauges, and the pale ribs of an old filtration tower all enclosed within the retaining ring. There is always just enough room to turn around.

The structure is young in the way a fresh cut is young. Edges are still sharp beneath the thin slickness of algae. Corners have not yet rounded. The water has started to leave mineral pale traces beneath valves and along joints, but it has not had time to wear anything down. Small deposits bloom around the bases of the plants. A few newly formed cracks run through the artificial stone, narrow as pencil marks, and have been sealed with darker material. The district is beginning to change, but not visibly from day to day.

Its machinery is present everywhere, though most of it has learned to be quiet. Pumps thrum behind walls. A relay ticks once, pauses, and ticks again. Farther down, some great engine turns at a speed too slow to be felt as motion; its vibration arrives through the handrails as a soft, sustained note. The loudest sound is the tiny, repeated spill of water into a catch basin. Even that seems less like a sound than a mark placed carefully in the silence.

The systems work without haste. Water is lifted, filtered, and returned through a closed circuit. Nutrients pass through narrow pipes to the growing beds. Lamps brighten and dim by fractions, too gradually to notice unless one watches the shadows against a white wall. The district’s pressure changes with the reservoir, but only slightly. The whole place gives the impression of pausing between two instructions.

There are rules here, though they are written into construction rather than posted. No unsealed material may be introduced. No opening in the outer wall may remain unlatched. The plants may extend into any space not needed for access or repair, and the machinery will route around them if it can. Water is permitted everywhere; air is not required. Human passage is possible, but the place has not been arranged for human comfort. Its narrow bridges, close growth, and constant dampness suggest that people were expected to visit only briefly—or that the original meaning of “visitor” has been forgotten.

From the highest platform, one can look over the enclosing wall. Below, the reservoir descends through layers of suspended green and pale engineered structures, a drowned landscape of towers, pipes, and other districts dissolving into the blue. Above, the water is so clear that its surface is difficult to believe in. Light falls through it evenly, and no weather crosses it. Nothing moves at a distance.

Only here, in this small crowded crown of a machine, does anything seem to be growing.
