The place is called the Cup, though it is less a hollow than a shallow crease in the summit of a mountain. It is small enough to cross in a few hundred steps. Beyond it, the land drops away in white distances: ridges, cloud-shadow, and a blue so thin it seems to have been stretched over the world. The Cup sits high above the weather most of the time. When clouds rise to it, they pass through as cold mist and leave the stone beaded with water.

Its floor is crowded with mineral forms. Dark rock ribs push up between pale slabs; narrow stone fins stand close together, their edges softened by ages of wind. A person could thread between them, but nowhere could several people stand abreast. The surfaces are old and settled-looking, worn into shallow channels and rounded hollows. Here and there, the rock has split into fresh, sharp flakes. Erosion is not dramatic here. It works by patience: water finding a seam, frost widening it by a grain, wind carrying away the powder.

There is no vegetation—not even a stain of green in the cracks—and no animal sign. Nothing nests among the rocks. Nothing crawls over them. The only motion is the weather and the stone itself, moving by increments too small to watch directly.

Moisture arrives irregularly. A gray veil of condensation gathers along the upper fins, then draws into clear threads, running down their faces and collecting in the hollows. For a few hours, the Cup smells faintly of wet flint. The water is shallow and short-lived. Cold returns quickly: it skins the pools with ice, then the bright light breaks them open again. Meltwater disappears into hairline fissures, or slips over the rim and falls out of sight. In the deepest cracks, clear ice persists through the dry intervals, clouded with trapped bubbles.

The light is startling. It comes hard from above, almost without warmth, and fills every gap that the rocks leave. Their shadows are dark and precise. Pale crystals in the stone flash when the mist thins; the ice gives back little splinters of brightness. At times the whole hollow seems lit from within, though nothing there is luminous. The brightness makes the place feel exposed, even under cloud.

A few marks of human presence have endured. Small stacks of flat stone occupy ledges too narrow to be useful for shelter. They are not tall enough to be monuments, and some have slumped into heaps, but the oldest seem to have been made with care. A shallow groove, worked into one slab with a crude tool, runs toward the center of the Cup and stops at a round depression. When condensation gathers, water follows the groove and waits there. No roof, wall, or firepit remains—only these scattered arrangements, made from what was already at hand.

The sound is sparse. Wind passes over the rim with a dry hiss, then vanishes in the crowded stonework. Water ticks from ledge to ledge. Ice gives a muted click as it tightens in a crack. Occasionally a slab settles with a low, brief knock, as if a door has closed far below. The Cup does not echo much; its many fins break sound into silence.

The place has a small, strict rhythm. Mist beads. Water runs. Ice forms. Light returns. The seams widen, but almost imperceptibly; the stone remains where it has been for longer than any mark upon it. Nothing grows, and nothing visits. Yet the Cup is not still. It is being slowly sorted by cold and gravity—grain from slab, water from air, old edge from new fracture—under a sky so bright it makes every change seem deliberate.
