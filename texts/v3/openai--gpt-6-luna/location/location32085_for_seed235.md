The city stands above the clouds, though not on a peak. It occupies a broad shelf of mountain so high that the valleys below have become pale, distant weather: green folds, shadow, then a floor of cloud. Beyond the shelf, the land continues in broken tiers, each one holding its own small horizon. From a rooftop, you can see miles of terraces and ridges, but never the whole country. The farthest cliffs seem to lean away from sight.

Heat lies over everything. It shines from the stone and gathers under the thick leaves, where the air is wet enough to drink. Water runs everywhere—not in great rivers, but in countless sheets and seams. It leaks from retaining walls, beads on the undersides of bridges, pours down staircases that were built as staircases and have become cascades. The city’s foundations are dark with it. Moss and broad-leaved plants fill the joints between blocks, rooted so deeply that no one can tell whether the masonry made room for them or grew around them.

The buildings are close together without forming a single wall. Houses, courtyards, covered ways, old cisterns, and steep gardens crowd the terraces in layers. Their stone is smooth at the corners from long handling and rough where water has worried at it. There are no wheels, no engines, no glass instruments; no device makes the city work. Its order is architectural and bodily: channels laid along the streets, shallow pools at every crossing, steps set to the slope, doors opening onto other people’s roofs. Some passages are so narrow that leaves brush both shoulders. Others open abruptly onto immense balconies where the land falls away.

Vegetation fills every available depth. Ferns unfold from drains. Vines climb the inner sides of towers, vanishing over their roofs and reappearing in courtyards below. Trees grow in wells too small for their roots, their trunks bent around lintels and balconies as if they have always known the shape of the place. The greenery is luxuriant, but not wild in the ordinary sense. It does not take over. It occupies the spaces allotted to it, though the allotment seems to have been made by a patient mind that might be plant, stone, or neither.

There are no birds in the high foliage, no insects whining in the damp, no animals drinking at the pools. The city is empty of animal life, yet never quiet. Water makes a deafening, continuous roar. It falls from ledges, rushes through hidden channels, strikes the hollow undersides of bridges, and trembles in roof gutters. Wind adds a lower sound, drawn through streets and arches until each opening becomes a note. The notes overlap into a vast, steady chord. At first it seems to change as you move; then you realize the sound is the same everywhere, close and immense, as if the entire mountain were ringing inside itself.

The strangest things here are also the most dependable. Water flows uphill in certain channels, but only in those channels. A person standing on one terrace may look across at another terrace directly opposite, while the distance between them seems to contain an entire valley. Shadows fall toward the sun. A staircase of forty steps descends to a door that opens onto the roof above it. These are not accidents, nor do they happen at random. The city has rules. They are simply not the rules that govern the lands visible below.

The place does not seem to be changing. Water has run through the same grooves for ages, and the grooves are worn but not wearing away. Vines hang heavy and still, even when the wind is loud enough to shake the roofs. Pools brim without overflowing. Mist gathers beneath the bridges and remains there, glowing in the hot light like a second, lower sky. Nothing decays quickly. Nothing finishes. The city is settled into its impossible shape, as balanced and established as a mountain.

From the highest garden, the whole landscape can be read as a sequence of green roofs, wet stone, terraces, falls, and distant ledges, receding through haze. The roar persists. The light is ordinary and bright. Below, the clouds drift past the cliffs. Above, the city holds its water, its heat, and its silence of living creatures without loosening its grip on any of them.
