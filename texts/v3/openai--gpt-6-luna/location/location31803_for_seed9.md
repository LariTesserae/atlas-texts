The place occupies the crown of a small, unfinished mountain—though nothing beneath your feet is earth.

It is a raised garden, no larger than a hamlet, held inside a ring of pale retaining walls. Beyond them, the air falls away into bright haze. The walls are made of poured mineral composite, still faintly warm from the works that shaped them. Their surfaces are smooth in some places, ridged in others, as if the mountain were being printed in slow, imperfect layers. Here and there, a stair ends against a blank face. A drain begins in the middle of a step and has not yet found its way down.

The ground is a close-laid skin of tiles, grates, troughs, and shallow basins. No soil shows. Instead, dark growing mats lie over the constructed surface, stitched through with capillary tubes. Water beads in the seams, gathers in tiny pools, and slips under the tiles with a soft, persistent whisper. The place is warm and wet enough that metal sweats. Mist gathers beneath the lips of ledges, then loosens and drifts a few feet before dissolving in ordinary daylight.

Vegetation has taken the plan personally. Ferns fill the channels; broad, waxy leaves overlap the railings; vines climb the pipes and disappear into vents. There are little thickets in the corners where the irrigation runs richest, dense enough to hide the machinery behind them. Moss softens every surface that stays damp. Some of it is bright green, some almost black, and some grows in neat, velvety rectangles where the maintenance lamps once stood. The plants are lush but not wild: their roots travel inside the mats, their water comes by schedule, and the largest stems are guided through rings set into the walls. Still, they lean toward openings in the structure that were not made for them.

The machinery is everywhere, but subdued. A pump turns somewhere below the central terrace, its hum felt more than heard. Valves open and close in a distant chamber. Thin pipes twitch as pressure passes through them. At intervals, the whole garden gives a small settling click, like a house adjusting its weight. The noise is easily covered by water: drips from the high rails, trickles through the grates, the rainlike patter of condensate falling from a canopy that is not quite a roof.

The landscape is still coming into being. Freshly cast surfaces pale where water runs over them, and fine mineral grains collect at the foot of the newest walls. In a few seams, the material has swollen into blunt, unplanned bulges. The builders have left these alone. They are waiting to see whether the swelling will stop, or whether the mountain is learning how to make its own shape.

There are few animals. A small, dark-winged insect sometimes rests on the warm pipes; no one knows how it enters. At dusk, something may stir beneath a leaf, but the leaves are checked each morning and nothing is found. The garden seems full of life until you listen for it. Then you hear only water, pumps, and the patient creak of new construction settling into place.

The rules here are simple but not entirely reliable: water rises when the lower cisterns are full; the lights stay close to daylight; nothing is allowed to root through the floor. Yet roots have begun to thread the grout between tiles, and water has found a route beneath the central stair that is not on any diagram. The garden is too small to be a wilderness, too complete to be a ruin, and too young to be finished. It hangs above the world as a cultivated, dripping fragment of a mountain—green at the edges, warm at its core, and quietly making more of itself.
