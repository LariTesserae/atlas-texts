The ground here is not ground, but a floor laid in the shape of a valley.

It falls away in broad, shallow terraces, each one assembled from gray plates bolted to a framework below. Their edges are still sharp. Some plates have shifted just enough to make long black seams; from those seams water beads, gathers, and runs in thin silver lines toward the lowest channels. There is no soil beneath them, no buried stone—only girders, pipes, and the dark, measured depth of the works. The whole landscape has the provisional look of something recently made, though its machinery has been running long enough to acquire habits.

The space extends farther than the eye can comfortably hold. Low roofs and hanging walkways recede through a dim haze, interrupted by square towers, pressure doors, and the occasional immense drum turning behind a mesh screen. Beyond them, the built terrain rises again in a series of stepped embankments, as if a city had been dismantled and its foundations rearranged into hills. Most surfaces are matte and pale with cold; where water passes, they shine. Condensation hangs under the ceiling in fine droplets, falling now and then with the slow, distinct plink of rain in a cavern.

The air is cold, damp, and metallic. It smells of wet concrete, hot insulation, and something faintly mineral that has no mineral source: a smell manufactured by the pipes themselves. Ventilation fans push it in great slow breaths. When they pause between cycles, the whole place seems to listen. Then the fans start again, and the sound returns: a broad industrial roar, layered with the clatter of chains, the squeal of a trolley moving somewhere out of sight, pumps knocking against their casings, and water hurrying through enclosed channels. The noise is too large to belong to any one machine. It seems built into the landscape.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in a natural shape. It occupies narrow runnels, shallow catchments, grated sumps, and long transparent pipes that cross the open space at several heights. A few basins have overflowed, spreading films across the plates. These shallow floods reflect the overhead work-lamps as broken, trembling bars. Some drains pull water downward; others push it up through nozzles in brief, cold mists that bead on cables and handrails. The system is not perfectly controlled. It has moods: a channel may run dry for hours, then fill suddenly; a seam may begin to weep after a pressure change; an entire terrace may acquire a skin of water overnight and lose it by morning.

Vegetation has found only a few places to persist. It grows where the machinery fails to keep things clean: a dark green fuzz in the corners of a sluice, threadlike pale fronds clinging to the underside of a walkway, small mats of something mosslike around the warm collars of pipes. These patches are widely separated, more like stains than a covering. Their color is nearly black in the dimness. At intervals, tiny beads form along their edges and drop into the drains. No one can tell whether the plants are feeding on the water, the metal, or the electrical fields passing through the walls. They do not spread quickly, but they are difficult to remove.

Animals are rarer. Now and then, a pale, jointed shape slips through the grating and vanishes into a conduit. Small things leave delicate tracks in the condensation on lower ledges. The occasional wingbeat is heard but never seen. Most of the space appears empty until one notices that the water has been diverted around a particular corner, or that a shutter has been left open where the rules say it should be shut. Life here is sparse, but not absent; it has learned to move between the machine’s cycles.

The place has rules, though they are not written anywhere. Water travels toward the lowest numbered drains, unless the pumps reverse. Lights brighten when pressure rises. Walkways tremble a few seconds before a heavy transfer begins. The great doors open only when the air on both sides is cold enough, which means they sometimes remain closed for days, sweating steadily around their seals. One can cross the terraces safely while the fans are drawing inward. When they blow outward, the loose mist becomes a blinding sheet and the noise makes it impossible to hear an approaching vehicle.

Somewhere beneath the broadest level, a process is still underway. New sections are being extruded or poured, though no workers are visible. Fresh ribs of pale composite protrude from a wall, wet and gleaming, each one ending in a sharp lip. A crane hangs motionless above them, its hook swaying slightly in the vibration. Nearby, stacks of unfinished panels wait under tarps that breathe in the moving air. Nothing here is entirely settled. The landscape is being used and made at the same time.

Its strangeness is quiet rather than spectacular. Distances are difficult to judge: a tower may be a hundred meters away or half a kilometer, softened by mist and repeated lines of rail. A low hum sometimes gathers beneath the machinery, changing pitch as one walks across the floor. It may be another motor, or the sound of the whole structure flexing against its unseen supports. Water in the long pipes occasionally travels uphill for a short distance before turning down again. The lamps do not flicker, but their reflections do.

There are places where the construction opens into wide, nearly empty flats. Here the sound rolls unbroken from wall to wall, and the dim light makes the wet floor seem much larger than it is. A few isolated pylons stand in the open, supporting nothing visible. Between them, shallow water moves in fine ripples, though the air is still. Far off, a bank of machines starts in sequence—one after another, each answering the last—and the vibration crosses the plates underfoot.

The landscape never becomes silent. Even when the pumps stop, condensation continues to fall, cables tighten and cool, and water works its way through the seams. Even its emptiness is active: an immense, cold interior being continually drained, pressurized, ventilated, patched, and remade.
