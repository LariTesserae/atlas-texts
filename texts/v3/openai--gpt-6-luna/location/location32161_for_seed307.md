The landscape is hidden inside a bead of water caught in a fresh crack near the summit of a black volcanic needle. The needle rises above the cloudline, where the air is thin and the light ordinary: a pale, steady daylight that enters the crack through one bright pinhole. From outside, the whole place would fit beneath a fingernail. Inside, it has the depth of a mountain range.

Its floor is a shelf of newly cooled basalt, all sharp edges and glassy ribs. There has been little time for anything to soften them. Hot water seeps through the stone and gathers in shallow basins, then spills over their rims in threads. It does not fall straight. Near the walls it crawls upward in trembling films; where it breaks loose, droplets hang below ledges like clear beads, reflecting a second, inverted landscape. The air is close and wet. Heat gathers under the stone roof, and the drops are warm enough to blur the light.

There are no plants, no moss, no soft green covering the rock. The color comes instead from minerals: iron-red seams, sulphur-yellow stains, bruised purples where the wet stone darkens. Life is everywhere, but it has no need of leaves.

The animals crowd into every seam and pool. Pinhead-sized, many-legged grazers comb the slick walls. Pale, jointed swimmers fold themselves through water too shallow to cover them. Larger things—still small enough to pass through a human hair’s width—wait beneath the basalt flakes, their bodies folded into shapes that seem to have too many undersides. They feed, shed, divide, and vanish in the tight spaces between stones. One may be seen as a bead with six legs; blink, and it is a long translucent ribbon slipping through a crack that was not there a moment ago. Nothing calls. Nothing sings. Their abundance is nearly silent.

The place has its own weather. A warm seep swells; the basins fill. The ceiling cools by a fraction, and a fine rain falls upward from the pools, collecting beneath the roof before dripping down again. Each cycle rearranges the boundaries. A ledge that was a cliff becomes the bottom of a pool. The same narrow fissure may open onto a distant ravine, or onto its own interior, seen from the other side. Sometimes the pinhole of daylight appears in several places at once, though there is only one opening in the rock.

The animals behave as if these changes are ordinary. When the water rises, they gather on the highest sharp ridges. When it recedes, they pour into the newly exposed basins, packed so close that their movements seem like a single shimmer passing over stone. They do not flee the heat. They seem to require it.

There is no path into this country, no trace of a hand upon it. It is too small to enter and too vast to survey. A droplet trembles at the mouth of the crack, holding the black summit, the thin air, and this crowded hidden world in its curved surface. Then it slips inward. For a while, the bright pinhole goes dark. The tiny animals continue moving.
