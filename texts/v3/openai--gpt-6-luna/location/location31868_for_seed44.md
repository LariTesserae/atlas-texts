The district is called the Sedgeworks, though nobody has made sedge there for generations.

It occupies a shallow bowl of ground between an old river bend and a low embankment. From the road above, it looks almost level: roofs, water, and foliage pressed into one dark green plane. There is no skyline. The highest structures are the brick chimneys of the processing halls, blunt and blackened, their tops scarcely above the surrounding canopy. The whole place is small enough to cross in a few minutes, if the walkways are clear—and they rarely are.

Water lies everywhere, but seldom in broad, open expanses. It wells under paving stones, runs in narrow channels along the walls, and beads on the undersides of leaves. The air is warm and close. Even after a dry spell, the brickwork sweats. Rain leaves no clean shine behind; it sinks into moss, soil, leaf litter, and the woven beds of roots that have slowly lifted the streets into uneven shelves. Here and there, a clear pool gathers beneath a grating, reflecting the pale sky with the stillness of a stopped clock.

The vegetation is not reclaiming the Sedgeworks. It is the material from which the district is made.

Thick, clipped hedges separate yards and narrow lanes. Reed bundles, darkened with age, fill gaps in brick walls. Ivy and climbing fig have been trained over the old workers’ houses, their stems crossing windows and then disappearing beneath the eaves. In the factory courts, broad-leaved plants grow in long troughs set between the rails. Their roots anchor the soil, strain the water, and keep the ground from shifting too quickly. Small trees rise from enclosed courtyards, their crowns crowded against one another until each yard becomes a dim green room.

The buildings are old and settled, but not ruined. Their foundations are brick, stone, and timber; their additions are a patchwork of sheet metal, glazed panels, and living screens. Some roofs are tiled, some are planted, and some have sagged beneath years of wet leaves. A few still bear numbers painted over the doors. The paint has faded but remains legible beneath the green. The district has an orderly feeling under its overgrowth: straight drainage lines, numbered gates, a grid of narrow service roads, all softened rather than erased by time.

The Sedgeworks still produces things. A low industrial hall stands at its center, windowed high along its walls. Inside, slow presses squeeze plant fibre into board, insulation, and packing material. A chain of belt-driven rollers turns with a patient, heavy rhythm. Steam moves through pipes fitted with newer gauges, and electric lamps glow in the rooms where the daylight cannot reach. The machinery is neither grand nor fast. It groans, knocks, pauses, and starts again. Its work seems less like manufacture than a continuation of the district’s own habits: drawing water through, separating fibre from pulp, returning the surplus to the beds outside.

The sound is louder than the smallness of the place suggests. Pumps murmur below the walkways; belts slap; water drops from gutters onto metal roofs with a persistent, hollow tapping. The presses give out a low thud at regular intervals, and somewhere behind the green walls a fan rattles without changing speed. Leaves scrape each other overhead. When the wind comes through the embankment gap, it sets the entire canopy murmuring at once, a sound like distant rain. Then the machinery is briefly swallowed, though never silenced.

There are few animals. A handful of sparrows nest in the chimney braces. Small snails stipple the damp stone near the canals. In the deepest channels, pale fish move beneath mats of duckweed, so slowly that they can be mistaken for shadows. Larger creatures have little reason to enter. The district is dense, warm, and rich with plant life, but much of its water is controlled, channelled, filtered. The green abundance has a cultivated quality: tangled at its edges, disciplined at its roots.

Nothing changes quickly here. Water levels rise and fall by inches. The walls darken over decades. Trees lean, are propped, and continue leaning. When a path becomes impassable, a new plankway is laid over it; when a roof gives way, a plant bed is often built across the opening rather than removed. The Sedgeworks does not resist decay so much as distribute it. A broken brick becomes a shelf for moss. A disused pipe carries roots. The district keeps its shape by allowing each part to be slowly remade as another.

In the late morning, the light reaches the lower lanes in fragments. It catches the wet rims of leaves, the pale sides of reed bundles, and the clouded windows of the factory hall. Beyond the embankment, the larger city may be making more noise, but it feels far away. Here the day is measured by the steady turning of the presses, by the water passing through the grates, and by the gradual thickening of shade beneath the trees.
