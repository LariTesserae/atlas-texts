The basin lies almost level, though not flat: a broad low country of shallow shelves, worn ridges, and long depressions that hold water only when the air gives it up. The stone is dark and fine-grained, with dull metallic seams running through it. Here and there, pale mineral crust has gathered around cracks like dried foam. Nothing rises high enough to make a mountain of itself. The horizon stays open, softened by warm haze.

There are no plants. No green, no tangled growth, no softening of the ground. The few living things are difficult to notice: small, blunt-bodied creatures tucked into the stone’s cooler fissures, leaving little sign beyond pinprick trails in the mineral dust. Most of the surface appears entirely empty. A person could walk for hours without seeing anything move.

Water comes in fits. It beads on the undersides of overhangs, gathers in shallow pans, and drains away through narrow seams in the rock. Sometimes a light rain passes over the basin, more a brief darkening than a weather event. Afterward, the warm air lifts a thin veil of vapor from the ground. Condensation collects again on the stone by evening, as if the landscape were quietly returning what it had borrowed.

The place is loud, but not with voices or wind. A deep, steady industrial racket comes from below: throbs, metallic knocking, the long shudder of something turning under great strain. It seems to travel through the ground rather than through the air. In some stretches it is a low engine-note; elsewhere it breaks into a clatter that makes the smallest puddles tremble. The sound hardly changes from one day to the next. There are no buildings, no towers, no roads leading to a source. The exposed machinery is sparse: a few buried housings of dark metal, flush with the stone, their edges rounded by time. Most of the system is hidden.

Across the basin, narrow vents breathe warm air in measured intervals. Their rims are polished smooth, not by recent activity but by an enormous duration of repetition. Water marks descend from them in mineral bands. Nearby, the ground bears shallow furrows that might have been made by old channels or by something moving beneath the crust; they are too worn to tell. Nothing here looks newly made. Even the metal has settled into the landscape, dulled and pitted until it resembles another kind of stone.

The overall impression is of a process that has outlasted its purpose—or perhaps never had one that could be understood at this scale. The mechanisms do not build, harvest, or transform anything visible. They continue to work because continuing is what they do. Their noise fills the open spaces, but the basin itself remains almost perfectly still: warm, damp in scattered pockets, bare, and accustomed to the sound.
