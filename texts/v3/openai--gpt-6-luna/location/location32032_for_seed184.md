The district occupies a shallow hollow in the bedrock, entirely beneath the waterline and sealed in by the old foundations of the city above. It is not large: a handful of streets, a square, and the broad central hall of a factory, all close enough that a diver could cross the whole place before their lamp had dimmed. But nothing is empty. Stone, brick, iron, root, and growth fit tightly together, as if the district has spent centuries learning how to use every available surface.

The streets are canals now, their cobbles softened under silt and moss. Doorways open directly into water. Rusted railings and pipework cross overhead in a second street-level, tangled with ropes of dark green vegetation. Fernlike fronds crowd the windows; soft, bladder-shaped plants swell from the chimney mouths. In the factory hall, broad leaves have taken hold on the walls and spread across the old machinery, their stems threaded through the gears. The plants are lush but not vigorous. They move only when the water moves, which is rarely.

Everything has settled into its place. The masonry is worn smooth at the corners, not crumbling so much as rounded by a very long, patient passage of water. The iron has flaked into reddish scales. Silt has gathered in the lee of every sill and step. There are no fresh breaks, no debris caught in a recent current. Even the hanging cables seem less like wreckage than roots that have grown in careful loops.

Warmth comes up through the floor from somewhere below. It makes the water comfortable and gives the district a faint haze, so that the far end of a street blurs into green-black shadow. Light filters down from the drowned surface in thin, weak bands. In the factory, old glass housings still contain a few cloudy mineral lamps; they glow with the low amber of coals seen through ash. Their reflections tremble on the ceiling, though the water around them is nearly still.

The machinery has not stopped entirely. Somewhere behind the factory hall, a wheel turns once, pauses, then turns again. It drives a line of small pistons, which stir water through pipes too narrow to see. The rhythm is so slow that a visitor might mistake it for a vibration in the stone. Once each day—or perhaps once each month—the district’s drains exhale a row of bubbles from beneath the square. They rise through the weeds and disappear into the dark above without a sound.

There are few animals. A pale shrimp may appear in the shadow of a valve and vanish into a crack. Small silver fish sometimes gather inside the old ticket booth, where the roof is thick with plankton and the glass still holds its shape. Nothing larger seems to live here. The plants, the quiet machines, and the old construction have divided the space among themselves.

The strangest rule is that loose things do not drift very far. A dropped bolt will settle where it falls. A strip of cloth will lift and sag, lift and sag, but remain within the same narrow circle of water. The canals seem to have their own patient gravity, drawing everything gently toward the central hall. There, on the factory floor, a shallow pool lies beneath an open iron framework. Its surface is perfectly level, though it is surrounded by water.

No one knows whether the district is still working, or whether it has merely learned how to continue without anyone noticing. The pipes warm the stones. The wheel turns. The plants keep the dark crowded with leaves. In the little underwater city, maintenance and decay have become nearly the same process.
