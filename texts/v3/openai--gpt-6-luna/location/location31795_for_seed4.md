The place is a town hidden in the fold of a root.

From outside, there is almost nothing to find: a low hump of black earth, a spill of leaves, one pale stone slick with rain. The hollow lies beneath them, where a young tree has lifted a slab of ground and left a pocket between its roots. Water gathers there, but never quite floods it. It threads down the root-hairs, beads on the walls, and collects in shallow basins of clay. The air is cold enough to keep every surface damp.

Inside, the hollow opens into streets.

They are narrow channels pressed between living roots, their floors paved with bark chips, seed husks, and small flat stones. Here and there, walls have been braced with twigs tied by grass fibers. A few dwellings cling to the higher sides of the cavity: little dark chambers of folded leaf, stitched together with roots so fine they look like hair. Walkways cross the wet ground on reed stems. They bend under passing weight, then slowly rise again.

The construction has a town’s habits without a town’s size. Paths lead somewhere. Narrow drains guide the water around sleeping places and into a central pool. A ladder of thorn-bits climbs to a ledge where scraps of pale moss have been laid out to dry, though nothing here ever seems to become fully dry. Little markers—splinters set upright, shells stacked in pairs—divide one neighborhood from another. The buildings are crude, but their arrangement is not. Whoever made them understood the need to keep a roof above the waterline, and to leave room for the roots, which continue to thicken through walls and floors as if the settlement were being slowly rewritten from within.

There is scarcely any light. The entrance is a dim seam between earth and root, and even at noon it admits only a diluted grayness. Farther in, darkness is complete. A few patches of lichen hold a weak, greenish gleam, visible only after the eyes have adjusted. They illuminate no more than a cup’s rim or the wet curve of a stone. Most of the town is known by touch: the cool wall on one side, the soft give of moss beneath the feet, the sudden open air of a chamber where the root-ceiling lifts.

For all its stillness, the hollow is crowded with life. Pale water-mites graze on the film along the pools. Long-legged larvae hang motionless beneath leaf roofs until a ripple passes, and then they vanish into cracks. Snails travel the walls in slow, deliberate lines, leaving silver tracks that cross and recross the old ones. Blind, translucent shrimp flicker through the shallow water. Moths sit folded in the narrowest crevices, their wings patterned like dead leaves; when disturbed, they do not fly so much as tumble softly from one ledge to another. Small frogs wait at the pool’s edge with their throats pulsing, almost silent.

There are too many eyes, feelers, shells, and moving backs to count. Yet the place does not sound busy. Its sounds are close and small: a drop falling into water, a faint rasp in the leaf walls, the tick of a shell against stone. Sometimes the whole town seems to breathe as damp air shifts through the root-channels. The animals do not call much. They signal through ripples, vibrations, and brief touches of lightless movement. When something larger passes above the roof of soil, every creature goes still. Then, after a while, the streets resume their slow traffic.

The ground is new. Clay edges remain sharp where water has recently cut them. Root tips are bright at their ends, raw and pale, and the walls crumble in little fresh avalanches when a shell or foot dislodges them. Nothing has had time to become ancient. Even the darker, older surfaces are only a thin stain over something still forming. The settlement must continually shift around the growth of the roots and the arrival of new water. A passage that was open yesterday may be sealed by a swollen root today. A basin may deepen overnight as a thread of water worries at its rim.

The rules here are simple, but absolute. Water runs downhill, though “downhill” may mean only the width of a seed. Living walls cannot be cut without bleeding sap, and the sap draws crowds. The moss dries only on the highest ledge. No one crosses the central pool without disturbing it, and no one disturbs it without being noticed. The dark belongs to the small creatures, but the few who know the town’s routes can pass through it by keeping one hand against the root and listening for the difference between dripping water and moving bodies.

Outside, rain continues over a much larger world. Inside, the hidden town holds its dim arrangements together: a roof of root, a pavement of bark, a network of drains, chambers, nests, and narrow roads. Everything is close enough to touch. Everything is wet. And in the darkness, the whole pocket moves, almost too quietly to hear.
