The city occupies a hollow that has not quite finished becoming a hollow.

Its roof is low in places and impossibly high in others, an unfinished ceiling of pale mineral layers, cracked open into ribs and shelves. The floor has no river courses, no pools, no mud. It is a broad, dry plain of gray grit and soft, newly exposed stone, crossed by faint seams where the rock is still separating from itself. Here and there, warm seams show beneath the surface—not glowing, exactly, but giving off enough heat to make the air tremble.

Everything else is plant.

The growth begins in sparse colonies: dark mats pressed against stone, narrow stalks climbing the walls, branching fans spread like folded paper over the floor. Between them are long intervals of bare mineral. But the plants are not merely occupying the cavern. They have become its working material. Their dense, pale fibers form bridges, retaining walls, conduits, roofs, and the outer skins of buildings. Some districts are grown around old stone piers; others hang from the ceiling in tiers, their rootwork drawn into cables as thick as streets. The architecture has the slightly unfinished look of something still being assembled by both hands and weather.

The largest structures belong to the factories. They stand in scattered groups across the plain, squat and many-limbed, with metal housings wrapped in living insulation. Slow presses compact the fibers into beams. Spools turn, winding root-cord into enormous coils. In the quietest hours, the only sign that a plant-processing hall is operating may be a slow shiver traveling through its walls, or the pale drift of powdered husk escaping a vent. There are no chimneys of smoke. Waste heat is passed into the cavern’s mineral seams, where new growth gathers around it.

Light comes from above, though there is no open sky. Long shafts pierce the roof and descend through many layers of old construction. Somewhere higher up, unseen mirrors and lamps gather a steady, ordinary brightness and send it down. The light arrives softened by dust and plant film; it makes no grand sunrise, only a gradual distinction between the bright side of a pillar and the dim side. The plants turn toward it by fractions. In the more distant districts, where the shafts do not reach, electric panels imitate the same unremarkable day.

There is no water in the ground, and little in the air. The local growth draws what it needs from mineral vapor, warmth, and the faint chemical exchanges at the boundaries between stone and root. Its leaves are thick and matte, more like scales than membranes. At night—if the machinery permits such a thing—they close along their veins, conserving what little volatile matter the cavern holds. The people who live here do not speak of rain. They speak of a wall “sweating” when warm gases pass through it, or of a crop “taking breath” when its leaves loosen in a lit chamber.

The settlement extends across a region rather than filling it. From one roofline, the next district may be only a dark suggestion beyond an open stretch of gray ground. Transit rails cross these gaps on low trestles, carrying bundled stalks, machine parts, and small passenger cars that travel so slowly they seem almost stationary. Between built areas, the plant cover thins to isolated groves. Their trunks are hollow, their crowns combed flat by ancient currents of air. A person can walk for an hour without seeing another, while never quite leaving the reach of the city’s lamps.

Life is abundant, but not clamorous. Small pale animals nest inside the plant walls and travel along the overhead fibers, making almost no sound. They feed on softened husks and mineral nodules, and gather in the warm channels beneath the factories. Larger creatures are rarer, though their tracks sometimes appear in the grit: a line of paired impressions crossing a district overnight, too heavy for the quick, long-legged scavengers that people are accustomed to. The animals know the routes of the machines. They wait under freight bridges until the cars pass, then spill across the rails in a silent rush.

The ground is still forming. A seam that was a shallow groove last season may now be a narrow trench. New stone rises in blunt knuckles through the grit, while loose flakes slide from the ceiling and come to rest without a crash. The city repairs what it can with living material: roots are led into cracks, fibers pressed over fresh breaks, colonies trained to brace a pillar before the mineral beneath it has settled. No district is old enough to feel permanent. Even the factories have the look of temporary works, though their gears have been turning for generations.

Mostly, the place is quiet. Machinery hums at a distance; dry fronds click together where the air moves through them. Footsteps carry farther than seems reasonable over the bare ground, then vanish when they reach a patch of plant cover. In some chambers there is a faint, repeated tapping from within the walls—not a warning, and not quite a sound anyone can locate. It may be the roots measuring a change in temperature. It may be small animals moving through the structural fibers. The workers leave it uninvestigated.

The city is growing outward, but not quickly. Its illuminated avenues reach into the hollow in straight, confident lines; the living supports beneath them keep adding fine new branches, subtly altering the geometry. Buildings lean toward the light shafts. Bridges thicken where traffic is heaviest. At the settlement’s edge, plants extend beyond the last cultivated row into bare stone, making the first thin sketches of future streets. Here, the world is not yet a finished landscape. It is an underground region being negotiated—between mineral and root, machine and animal, open ground and construction—and every day the terms shift a little.
