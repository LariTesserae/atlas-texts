The plateau is high enough to catch the weather before it has decided what to become. Clouds pass below its outer ridges, thin as wool drawn over a frame. Above them, the light is startlingly bright: it lies flat across the land, whitening the stone and making every wet surface flash. The air stays cool even at noon. Moisture arrives in brief, unannounced ways—a slant of rain, a spill of fog, beads of condensation gathering along the undersides of leaves and metal roofs—then withdraws, leaving the ground darkened in patches.

The land itself looks newly made. Ridges break off sharply, exposing pale mineral faces and seams of black glass. In the cuts and gullies, loose stone has not yet settled into rounded slopes. Some cliffs bear clean, vertical fractures, as if the plateau were lifted only recently and is still holding the shape of the lift. Narrow streams appear after rain, shining briefly in the channels, then vanish into cracks or into mats of vegetation. Here and there, small basins keep water for a few days at a time. Their margins are edged in brilliant green.

The plants crowd close wherever they can find purchase. Thick cushions of moss soften the rock; tall, wax-leaved stalks rise in compressed stands, shoulder to shoulder, their pale undersides bright in the windless light. Ferns and wiry shrubs fill the hollows. Creepers lace the lower walls of ravines and climb the supports of old machinery, while tiny star-shaped flowers emerge from seams in the stone. From a distance, the plateau seems almost evenly green. Up close, it is a dense assembly of leaves, stems, damp soil, mineral crusts, and overlapping shadows.

There are few animals. Occasionally a small, dark shape slips between the stems or crosses a bare strip of stone, but the place gives no sense of being watched by a busy population. The plants seem to have inherited the whole surface. Insects are scarce; birds, rarer still. Nothing calls across the open ground.

The silence is not emptiness. It is a quality of the air, as if sound were caught just after it began. Rain can be seen stippling the leaves without being heard. A loose panel on a tower rocks on its hinge, but the motion makes no report. Even close to the industrial installations, one hears almost nothing—not a motor’s whine, not the clank of a belt. Instead, the machinery makes itself known through small changes in the world around it: a pipe warming the moss beneath it, a faint tremor underfoot, a tremble in a pool whose surface otherwise holds the sky perfectly still.

The installations are scattered across the plateau rather than gathered into a town. A squat processing hall stands alone on a ridge, its windows bright with reflected daylight. Farther on, several narrow towers mark the line of a buried conduit. There are cable gantries, valve houses, short runs of track that end at the edge of a fresh ravine. Their construction is heavy and practical—riveted plates, ceramic insulators, thick insulated pipes—but the plants have found every joint. Some structures appear abandoned; others are plainly maintained, though no workers are visible. A sequence of indicator lamps glows on a cabinet in the open air, steady and colorless in the sun.

The machinery seems to work by an unfamiliar patience. Pumps draw water only when the stone is wet enough. Conveyors advance by a few inches, stop, and wait. At certain times of day, a pressure gate opens somewhere underground, and every puddle on the plateau dimples at once. There is no dramatic transformation, only these quiet adjustments, so gradual that the land might be mistaken for stillness if one did not return.

The strange thing is how little seems to belong exclusively to either nature or industry. A metal gutter is furred with living green, and the water running through it is as clear as spring water. A line of pipes enters a cliff, but the cliff’s sharp seams continue beyond them in the same direction, making it hard to tell whether the machines followed a natural design or imposed one. On one slope, plants grow in parallel bands that match the spacing of buried cables. On another, a ventilation stack has filled with birds’ nests, though no birds are ever seen.

The plateau opens toward a broad valley, not vast enough to lose its details, but large enough that its far ridges take on a blue cast. Green terraces descend in uneven steps. Between them are raw cuts of stone, solitary buildings, and gleaming threads of pipe. The whole region is bright, cool, and densely occupied, yet nearly motionless. Moisture gathers, drains, and returns. Leaves thicken over the years. Fresh slopes crumble by a little at a time. The machines continue their nearly inaudible work beneath the vegetation.

Nothing here hurries. Even the newly lifted land seems to be waiting for its edges to soften.
