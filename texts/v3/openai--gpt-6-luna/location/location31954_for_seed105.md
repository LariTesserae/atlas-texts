The drowned highland begins where the water darkens.

It is not a trench, but a broad plateau sunk beneath a cold inland sea: ridges and shelves rising from a deeper basin, their old slopes softened by centuries of current. The highest ground is still far below the surface. Above it, the water keeps going—black overhead, black in the valleys, with no clear boundary between distance and depth. Now and then a pallid smear appears high above, perhaps daylight filtered through storm water, and vanishes before it can brighten anything.

The plateau is densely green in places, though nothing here is warm enough for a garden. Long, narrow fronds stream from cracks in the stone. Dark moss pads the ledges; pale rootlike fans spread over the sediment, gripping it in fine threads. In sheltered hollows, tall stalks rise in thickets, each ending in folded leaves that open and close slowly with the passing current. Their colors are subdued—black-green, slate, the occasional dim blue-white at a growing tip. They do not need the sun as ordinary land plants do. They draw what they need from mineral seepage and the faint chemical gradients along the rock. The brightest of them barely show in the dark.

There are no animals. No eyeshine among the leaves, no grazing, no calls, no sudden flight. Nothing hunts or hides. The plants bend, split, and grow over seasons so long that their movements seem almost geological. Fallen fronds gather in the lee of ridges and decay into soft black sediment. Here and there, new growth has covered old growth so completely that the slope seems upholstered in vegetation.

The water is never quiet. It presses through narrow cuts in the plateau with a deep, continuous roar, then slackens across the open shelves into a vibration felt more than heard. In the coldest channels, ice forms far below the surface: clear plates that grind against one another, making a sound like distant stone doors dragging shut. The ridges answer with their own low groans as the pressure shifts. These noises carry without direction. A listener cannot tell whether the thunder is approaching or has been there all along.

The rock is old and settled. Water has rounded the edges of basalt terraces and polished the sides of shallow ravines. Mineral chimneys, blunted and furred with growth, rise from fissures where warm seepage meets the cold. Sediment collects in long pale drifts behind them. Nothing in the landscape looks freshly broken, though the place is always changing: currents comb the plants one way, then another; seepage builds brittle shelves; the slow fall of leaf-matter raises the basin floor by imperceptible degrees.

Scattered along the higher shelves are small constructions of hand-cut stone. They have no doors now, if they ever did—only narrow openings, roof slabs, and short walls fitted together without mortar. Some are half-buried beneath roots. Others stand alone on exposed ridges, their stones furred with the same dark growth as the surrounding rock. A few lengths of braided cord remain caught around pegs, stiff and pale with mineral deposits. There are no tools in use, no lights, no signs of their makers. The structures seem less like settlements than marks left by someone measuring a world they could not keep.

The plateau’s strangeness lies in its scale and its patience. Each feature is near enough to make out—a shelf, a channel, a stand of fronds, a stone shelter—but beyond it lies another, and beyond that, another dim rise disappearing into water. The place offers no horizon. It offers a succession of ledges, each with its own currents and quiet growth, all joined beneath the same immense pressure.

At intervals, the plants release clouds of tiny pale spores. They drift upward in slow veils, gathering beneath overhangs where the current folds back on itself. The spores do not glow strongly enough to light their surroundings. They only make the darkness visible for a moment, like dust suspended in a room. Then the water takes them apart and carries them away.

Here, silence would be impossible. There is always the heavy movement of water, the mutter of stone, the rasp of ice, the soft drag of fronds across one another. Yet the loudness belongs to no living voice. It is the sound of a landscape turning over, very slowly, in the dark.
