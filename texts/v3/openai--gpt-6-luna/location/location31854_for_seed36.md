The place lies beneath the ground, but not in darkness.

A pale roof of unformed stone spans the basin in broad, shallow arches. It is thin in places, thick in others, with seams of translucent mineral running through it like milk in glass. Light comes down through these seams: warm, unwavering bars of gold, wide enough to fill whole groves. The rock has not yet settled into cliffs or been worn into caves. Its surfaces are blunt and unfinished, pressed into hollows and shelves as if the world had paused while still being made. Nothing looks broken. Nothing looks old.

Heat gathers under the roof. It rests on the skin, on the leaves, on the soft mineral floor. Here and there the stone sweats. Condensation beads along its underside and hangs in clear strings above the plants, though scarcely any of it falls. Dampness collects in shallow basins among the rock, darkening the earth and making small, still mirrors. Elsewhere the ground is dry and pale, dusted with fine grit that has not yet been carried anywhere.

Vegetation fills nearly everything.

It does not merely grow across the landscape; it is the landscape’s substance. Thick mats of low, waxy leaves cover the floor, overlapping until the ground shows only in occasional seams. Pillars of bundled stems rise around the larger hollows. Broad fronds stretch between them, making green ceilings beneath the stone ceiling. In the light shafts, leaf surfaces are so densely layered that the air seems to have depth and texture, as if one could push a hand into it and part it. Some plants are almost transparent at their edges, veined with amber. Others are dark and fleshy, swollen with stored moisture. There are hanging gardens, but no clear boundary between garden and wall: the growth clings to raw rock, bridges gaps, fills cracks, and gathers in folds wherever a fold exists.

The whole basin extends farther than its entrance suggests. A low, open country of green shelves and shallow depressions runs beneath the roof, narrowing into distant chambers that remain bright enough to see. The spaces between the larger groves are not empty. They are full of smaller growth: mats of fine, pale filaments; tiny cup-shaped leaves; low rosettes laid flat against warm stone. Seen from a height, these patches form a pattern of luminous greens and ochres, interrupted by the dull cream of the ground and the occasional dark pool. Seen from within, the growth is close around the body. Leaves brush the shoulders. Stems stand within arm’s reach on every side.

There are few animals. A small, nearly colorless insect may be found caught in the throat of a cup-plant, perfectly still. In one of the wetter hollows, something has left a delicate line in the film of mud, though the line is old and its maker unseen. The plants hold no visible signs of being eaten or trampled. Their surfaces are unscarred. Whatever lives here lives lightly, or rarely.

Sound is sparse. The basin carries a faint hush, not quite silence: the soft pressure of air in a closed place, the minute ticking of a bead of water settling against stone, the faint papery contact of one leaf against another. Nothing calls. Nothing rustles in passage. The light itself seems to make the loudest impression, though of course it makes no sound at all.

Scattered among the growth are a few signs of building. A low ring of stones encloses a patch of bare ground. A narrow, roofless shelter leans against a mineral outcrop, its walls made from pale slabs that might have been lifted from the floor. Farther on, a small arch stands alone over a path that the plants have nearly filled. These structures are simple and few, more like marks left in the landscape than works imposed upon it. No device, wheel, or tool breaks the quiet. The place feels older than its builders, yet it is difficult to imagine anything here changing enough to require repair.

The basin holds its shape without weathering. Water stays where it has gathered. Dust lies where it fell. Leaves keep their positions, overlapping in an intricate stillness. The light does not wander across them. Even the softest tendrils seem arrested at the instant before they reach farther. The landscape has the fullness of a living world and the calm of something not yet released into time: a hot, bright country under stone, lavishly green, damp in its hollows, and waiting—not for discovery, exactly, but for the first true event.
