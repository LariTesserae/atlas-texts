The place has no surface in any useful sense. It is a world-sized hollow beneath a young crust, a succession of chambers so vast that their ceilings vanish into darkness and their floors become horizons. The air is warm and close, dry enough to sting the throat. There is no standing water, no dampness underfoot, no smell of rain. The nearest thing to a river is a black seam of glass winding through the stone, laid down by ancient heat and now perfectly still.

The terrain is newly broken. Ridges have the sharp, unsoftened look of snapped pottery; splintered shelves jut from walls that plunge out of sight. Basalt lies in broad dark plates, interrupted by pale mineral fans and salt-white crusts. Here and there, the stone has blistered into bubbles, as if something beneath it once tried to breathe. The hollows between these formations are filled with fine grey powder. It settles without drifting. Nothing has weathered it away.

Scale is difficult to read. A ridge that looks close may take a day to reach; a scatter of lights on the far wall may be a continent of creatures, each glow no larger than a thumb. The chambers connect through cracks and arches big enough to contain their own weather. Warm air moves through them in immense, slow circuits, too steady to be called a wind. Dust rises in long veils, hangs for hours, then returns to the same ledges.

The darkness is not complete, but it is nearly so. Light comes in small, isolated quantities: a dull red mineral seam; the brief blue pinprick of a creature turning its underside toward the void; a faint pearly shimmer on the backs of things packed beneath a shelf. These do not illuminate the landscape so much as make darkness visible around them. Most of the time, the eye has nothing to hold. Shapes emerge only by degrees: a slope, a wall, the clustered bodies along it—and then the sense that the wall is much farther away than it first seemed.

Life is abundant, though nothing grows. There are no leaves, stalks, or spreading green mats. The animals draw what they need from the stone and the warm gases in its cracks, or from one another. Some cling in dense colonies, hard-shelled and almost motionless, rasping mineral dust from the walls. Some move in slow files along the cavern floor, low and many-legged, their overlapping plates clicking softly together. In the deeper fissures are pale, blind hunters that sense passing bodies through the pressure of the air. Their heads are narrow and folded, their mouths hidden until they feed.

The inhabitants are crowded, but not busy. They gather on warm faces of rock and around the same mineral vents generation after generation. A ledge may be thick with them, each creature pressed to the next, yet the whole mass seems asleep. When one shifts, its neighbors shift too, and the movement passes across the colony like a quiet thought. Nothing chases anything at speed. Nothing calls out for long.

Sound is scarce and strangely local. A scrape can travel a great distance through the stone, then stop as if it has struck a boundary. In some chambers the floor carries faint, regular ticks: shells touching as the animals breathe. Elsewhere, the only sound is a low pressure-hum that may be the air moving through the vast network, or the deep crust holding itself together. Voices, if there are voices, are clicks made close to the ground and meant for a few bodies at a time.

There are occasional signs of making. Small stacks of fractured stone mark safe passages across powder pits. Narrow tools, chipped from dark glass, lie near the mouths of inhabited cracks. In one sheltered hollow, a row of pale plates has been arranged into a low wall—part barrier, part landmark. The builders have not altered the landscape in any large way. Their works are swallowed by it, scattered and practical, visible only when one is near enough to touch them.

The world changes very little. Its heat persists; its air follows the same slow paths. New fractures appear rarely, and when they do, the sound is too deep to reach the living places. Dust returns to familiar shelves. Colonies occupy the same warm seams. Even the creatures’ migrations are more like adjustments of position than journeys. The land is young in its sharpness, but old in its stillness: newly split stone held in an equilibrium so complete that the inhabitants seem less to live upon it than to have been fitted into its crevices from the beginning.
