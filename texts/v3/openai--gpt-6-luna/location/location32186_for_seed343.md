The high country begins with a wall of cliffs, but the cliffs are so old they have lost the look of a wall. They lean, crumble, and descend in long, pale shelves, cut by gullies that wander as if they have forgotten where they were going. Above them lies the plateau: an immense, open table of stone raised into warm air. Its far edges recede into blue haze. From certain ridges you can see other plateaus, each one separated by a depth of shadow and each wearing the same patient scars of age.

Nothing here is newly broken. The stone is rounded at the corners, worn into shallow basins and ribs. Wind has polished the exposed faces to a dull sheen; water has worried narrow channels through them over spans of time too large to imagine. In the hollows, dust gathers in soft drifts, held in place by roots. The land feels less like a mountain being made than one that has already finished changing and is slowly forgetting its shape.

There is little standing water. Rain comes irregularly, sometimes as a brief sweep of fine drops that darkens the rock without seeming to fall from any particular cloud. At dawn, moisture beads along the plants and in the cracks. Mist collects in the deeper cuts, then lifts as the sun warms the plateau. Here and there, water persists in a stone bowl: a green-rimmed pool, a seep that darkens one face of a cliff, a thread of runoff that vanishes beneath a mat of roots. Most of the water is held rather than seen.

The plants do not make a forest. They make the ground.

Low, tough growths spread across the flatter stone in wide sheets: interlocking mats, cushion plants, wiry grasses, broad fleshy leaves lying close to the earth. Their roots stitch dust and gravel into a skin. In the gullies, that skin thickens into banks and hanging folds. A few enormous trees stand far apart, their trunks crooked by wind, their crowns flattened and sparse; between them, the land remains open enough to see for miles. From a distance, the plateau’s green appears almost even. Up close it resolves into thousands of forms: waxy rosettes, seed stalks, thorned shrubs, pale lichens, dark mosses in the shaded seams. The plants are everywhere, but they leave room between themselves. Life covers the place without crowding it.

That room is occupied by animals.

They pass across the high flats in loose, shifting bodies: herds of long-legged grazers, small enough at a distance to resemble moving patches of weather; larger animals moving alone along the rim of a ravine; dark flights of birds rising from the ground and settling again several slopes away. Their tracks are everywhere, pressed into the soft plant cover or polished into the dust, but no single gathering lasts long. The plateau seems empty until it begins to move.

The sound is the first evidence. Insects rasp from the mats and fissures in a continuous, astonishing chorus. Birds answer from the cliffs, their calls carrying across the bare reaches with nothing to soften them. Herds make a low rolling thunder as they travel; hooves click on the exposed shelves, and stones clatter into gullies behind them. At certain times of day, the air seems filled by all of it at once: a dense, many-layered noise that makes conversation difficult even when the nearest animal is only a speck on the horizon. Then, as the herds pass beyond the ridge, the roar thins to insect song and wind.

The warmth is dry on the open stone and close in the sheltered cuts. Sunlight is ordinary and strong, with the clear color of a high place, but the air retains a faint dampness wherever the plants hold it. The weather moves visibly here. Clouds cast broad shadows that cross the plateau like slow curtains. Wind travels unimpeded over the flats, bends the grasses in one direction, and goes on toward the distant escarpments.

Human traces are scattered and modest. A low wall crosses a narrow saddle, built from stones gathered nearby and worn down until it is almost another ridge. In a sheltered hollow, a few small huts stand on platforms of timber and packed earth. Their roofs are patched with woven grass and broad leaves; their doors face away from the prevailing wind. Old hearth circles, worn steps, and occasional markers carved into the stone suggest people have crossed this country for generations, following the pools, the herds, and the weather. There is no great road, only paths made and remade by feet, hooves, and the growth that slowly covers them.

Nothing in the landscape announces a mystery. The animals behave like animals; the plants follow the water; the stone keeps its old, slow answers to wind and rain. But the scale makes ordinary things feel immense. A seep becomes a landmark. A grove of three trees is a refuge. The departure of a herd can change the sound of an entire valley.

At evening, the light rests briefly on the plateau’s far rim. The open ground darkens, the plants give back the day’s stored warmth, and the pools hold small pieces of sky. Beyond them, the ancient shelves descend into shadow. The insects keep calling. Somewhere across the distance, the herds continue their passage, loud enough to be present long after they can no longer be seen.
