The plateau rises above the surrounding country in a single long shoulder of pale stone. From its rim, the land drops through cliffs and terraces to a distant sea, usually hidden beneath bands of warm cloud. The height is enough to make its own weather: rain gathers on the uplands, runs in shining threads down the rock, and collects in hollows before finding the old channels to the edge. Even in dry spells, the stone sweats. Water beads in the seams of walls and drips from the undersides of bridges.

There are no trees, grasses, or soft green margins. The plateau’s surface is mostly limestone, dark basalt, and the grey crust of mineral deposits left by countless rains. Wind and water have had a very long time to work here. They have rounded the ridges, hollowed the cliffs, and worn broad basins into the rock. Nothing looks newly broken. Even the ravines seem settled into their shapes, their edges softened by ages of runoff.

But the place is not empty. It is crowded with animals.

At dawn, great flights of seabirds climb the escarpment from the ocean, wheeling up through the fog in layered streams. They return from feeding grounds far below with fish in their crops and salt crusted on their feathers. The plateau is their nesting ground: every ledge, chimney, buttress, and abandoned roof is occupied. Smaller birds dart between them. Pale burrowing creatures work the loose stone beneath the nests; narrow-bodied scavengers patrol the gutters. In the flooded pits and old quarry basins, blind fish and thick-shelled crawlers stir in the dark water. The city’s drains are full of quick, whiskered shapes. Wherever a little water remains, something lives in it.

The birds are not visitors to the city. The city was built around their habits, as much as around its springs and channels. Its oldest districts occupy the low basin at the plateau’s center, where broad ramps and stepped streets follow the natural fall of the land. Buildings are made of local stone, stained almost black beneath the nesting ledges. Newer structures rise on iron frames above them: workshops, reservoirs, mills, and long processing halls with roofs of corrugated metal. Pipes run openly along the streets, carrying water from the higher catchments. The city’s engines pump, grind, and clatter, though no one seems in a hurry to make them quieter.

Much of the industry depends on the constant passage of life. Fish, shell, feathers, and mineral-rich deposits are sorted and carried through the lower yards. The work is old, practical, and arranged around the animals’ seasons. Broad covered walkways protect people from the worst of the nesting falls. At certain times of year, whole avenues are closed so the birds can cross them on foot, waddling between roosting grounds with a solemnity that makes the iron gates seem temporary. No one chases them away for long. They return in numbers that turn removal into a kind of weather.

The sound is therefore enormous, but not sharp. There is the deep, continuous wash of wind over the rim; the rush of water in the channels; the thudding and hiss of machinery; and above it all, the calls of the birds, overlapping until the whole plateau seems to murmur through a hundred thousand throats. Sometimes a single flock lifts at once, and the air fills with wings. The sound rolls across the basin, strikes the stone walls, and comes back altered.

Rain can last for days without becoming a storm. It lays a reflective skin over the city, darkens every ledge, and fills the air with the warm smell of wet stone, salt, and animal musk. Mist moves slowly through the upper streets, parting around towers and settling again in courtyards. At night, the lamps make long gold paths in standing water. The birds keep calling from the roofs.

The plateau changes very little at human scale. A retaining wall takes decades to slump; a new channel takes generations to carve itself into the basin floor. The city expands by adding to what is already there: another span over a runoff course, another tier of housing under an old terrace, another mill fitted into a disused quarry. The animals shift their nesting grounds by inches and then, over many years, by whole neighborhoods. People learn the new boundaries and build accordingly.

From far away, the settlement looks less like a city than a second geology: a dense band of pale masonry and dark machinery wrapped around the basin, with moving wings above it and waterfalls slipping down the rim beyond. Up close, the distinction between natural and built seems less certain. Stone ledges serve as roofs; roofs become nesting shelves; old drains become streams; abandoned factories become cliffs for the birds. The plateau has been inhabited so long that even its busiest works appear worn into place.

And under the rain, under the engines, under the dense restless life, the rock holds its patient shape.
