The plateau has only recently risen.

It lifts above the cloud belt in a single immense upheaval, a country of raw ridges and unfinished basins still settling into its own shape. The stone is dark, glassy in places, split by bright seams where the crust has torn open. Cliffs end in knife-edges. Scree slopes look freshly poured. Here and there, a mountainside folds down into a valley in broad, soft pleats, as if the rock has not yet decided whether it is solid.

The air is warm at this height. Moisture arrives in fits: a veil of rain sweeping over one ridge, a sudden condensation beading on leaves and stone, then hours of dry brightness under a dim sky. The light never quite becomes sunlight. It gathers behind layers of cloud, pale and diffuse, and drains away again. On clear days, a weak glow shines up from the plateau’s many mineral cracks, so that the undersides of clouds are faintly luminous.

Vegetation grows densely wherever anything can hold it. Ferns as tall as trees crowd the ravines. Thick mats of soft, copper-green grass cover the flatter ground, trembling though the wind is still. Vines descend from ledges and vanish into fog. Some plants grow in rings around warm vents, their leaves overlapping like scales; others cling to bare rock, opening and closing in response to distant thunder. The growth is lush, but not settled. New shoots emerge overnight. Whole thickets lean, turn, or flower within the span of a storm.

Water is scattered rather than gathered. It beads on the stone, runs briefly down steep channels, then disappears into fissures. Shallow pools form in hollows and reflect a sky that may not be overhead. Their surfaces sometimes show clouds moving against the wind, or a second plateau hanging below this one. When rain comes, it falls in distinct moving curtains, leaving the land beyond them dry. In the largest valleys, mist pools around the roots of immense plants and rises in slow columns, as if the ground were breathing.

The plateau is full of animals. Flocks of long-winged grazers move through the grass in close, silent banks, changing direction all at once without any visible signal. Pale burrowers thread beneath the surface, raising thin lines of disturbed soil that travel for miles. In the trees and high ferns, bright-eyed creatures watch from every layer: some feathered, some translucent, some difficult to focus on. Their calls are rare. The place is not silent, exactly, but its living noise is muffled by distance, damp air, and the strange way sound bends here. A bellow may arrive as a vibration through the soles of the feet, while a nearby wingbeat makes no sound at all.

There is an industrial quality to the land, though nothing has been built upon it. Deep under the plateau, immense forces cycle with the regularity of machinery. Warm currents rise through the rock; pressure gathers, releases, gathers again. Cracks open and close in sequences. Mineral fluids move through the earth like something being pumped from a hidden reservoir. The ridgelines shudder at intervals, and the valleys answer with slow, synchronized collapses. The whole country seems to be operating—working through some vast process whose purpose is not apparent.

This process makes the geography unreliable. A ravine can become a ridge over the course of a day. Water may pour uphill for a few moments before remembering gravity. In certain places, the horizon repeats: a distant mountain appears twice, once where it belongs and once just above it, slightly out of alignment. Shadows sometimes lag behind the things casting them. These disturbances are not constant enough to become ordinary, but common enough that the plateau’s animals seem adapted to them. They pause when the ground begins to disagree with itself, then continue once the disagreement has passed.

Weather forms quickly and locally. A storm can blossom over a single grove, spin without moving, and empty itself into one hollow while the next remains dry. Lightning glows within the cloud rather than striking down. Wind races along the ridges, then stops at the edge of a basin as if caught against a wall. Across the vast interior, such weather systems flicker and overlap, making the plateau feel less like a place beneath the sky than a second atmosphere with its own unsettled laws.

Nothing here bears the mark of habitation. There are no roads, walls, cut stones, or smoke trails. Even the cleanest planes of rock are fractures, not surfaces made level by tools. Yet the landscape has the intensity of a place under construction. It is young, crowded, warm with growth and pressure, forever assembling itself from raw material. Its quiet is the quiet of enormous mechanisms running far below hearing, while above them the plants, animals, weather, and stone keep changing parts.
