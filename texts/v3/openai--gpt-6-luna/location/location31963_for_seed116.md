The place is hidden inside the crown of a drowned machine.

From outside, the machine is a black column rising from a submerged industrial plain—boilers, pipes, gantries, and sealed towers furred with the slow growth of the sea. Its summit stands at a height that would be a mountain’s height on land, though water closes over it on every side. Somewhere near that summit, behind a valve door no wider than a person, there is a chamber no larger than a modest room.

Inside, the chamber climbs.

Its floor is a small, round grate. Above it, the walls rise farther than they seem they should, narrowing toward a pale circle of glass at the top. Everything is made: floor, wall, ledge, root-bed, even the lumpy terraces that look like old stone. They are cast from different generations of metal and ceramic, patched and overlaid until the original shapes are difficult to recognize. Time has softened every edge. Rivets have become shallow dimples; seams have filled with mineral glaze. The oldest surfaces have the smooth, heavy patience of things that have been underwater since before anyone remembers building them.

The water is warm. It moves in a slow upward spiral, carrying fine silver flecks from the lower grate toward the glass. There is no air in the chamber, only water, and no true sky beyond the small window—just the ordinary, diluted daylight of the ocean, blue-green and steady. A pale shaft descends through it, catching on suspended particles and turning the narrow interior into a column of dim weather.

Vegetation has made a forest of the machinery. Soft, broad leaves spread from brackets and bolt-holes; thin red fronds stream from cable conduits; pale root-mats hang from the ceiling in long curtains. They do not grow in soil. They feed from the mineral bloom on the walls and from the slow current that climbs through the chamber. Here and there, a plant has taken the shape of what it grew around: a fan of leaves following the arc of a pipe, a cushion of green settled into the cup of a broken lamp. Near the top, the growth is thickest, where the light is strongest. Below, the vegetation thins to scattered ribbons and tufts, leaving long stretches of smooth, bare metal between.

The animals are small but numerous. Glassy shrimp pick along the wall in bright, coordinated lines. Dark fish shelter inside the hollow ribs of the chamber, emerging in loose flurries when the current changes. A few larger, slow-bodied creatures hang near the root-curtains, opening and closing their frilled mouths to comb the water. Nothing seems to hunt in haste. Predation happens in brief, precise disturbances: a sudden flick of tail, a scatter of shrimp, then the chamber’s old quiet returns.

It is not quiet, though. The machine makes a deep, continuous sound, too low to be a hum and too steady to be thunder. Every few minutes a piston shifts somewhere far below. The whole chamber answers with a metallic groan, and the water tightens into a fast spiral. Leaves flatten. Fish disappear into their hiding places. The glass at the top gives a small, bright tick. Then the pressure passes, and the plants resume their slow, deliberate reaching.

There are no seasons here, but there are cycles. The current strengthens and weakens. Mineral deposits bloom and are scraped clean by feeding animals. Old leaves loosen, travel upward, and gather at the glass, where they darken into a thin green rim. Once in a long while, the machine’s buried pumps turn over, and a rush of warmer water rises through the grate. The whole chamber seems to breathe.

The place has settled into its final form, but it has not stopped living. It is a little vertical world held in the topmost chamber of a drowned structure: high above the unseen bottom, deep beneath the sea, contained within a single manufactured space. Its cliffs are walls. Its caves are conduits. Its forest is bolted to the architecture. And its weather is the old machine, moving water through the dark.
