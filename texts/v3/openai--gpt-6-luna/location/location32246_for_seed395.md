The plain has almost no horizon where it is not interrupted: not by hills, but by the close, interwoven growth of things. It lies low and nearly level beneath a warm, ordinary sky. The ground is young. Dark mineral crust, pale clay, and soft ash show through in broad patches, not yet worn into familiar shapes by rivers or long weather. Here and there the land rises into a low swell, its edges still blunt, as if the plain has only recently finished settling into itself.

There is no open water. No river threads the country; no pond catches the light. The soil is dry to the touch, and the air carries a faint mineral taste. Yet vegetation occupies nearly every seam in the ground. It grows in dense mats and clustered thickets: stiff, silver-green blades; broad, leathery leaves; low shrubs with pale trunks; and tall, hollow-stemmed plants whose crowns meet overhead. Their roots run deep into the new earth, finding hidden dampness and salts. Their leaves hold what little moisture they can, waxed and folded against the heat. From a distance the plain appears green. Up close, it is a crowded world of hard leaves, dry stems, and dark soil sheltered beneath them.

Settlements are threaded through the growth so tightly that it can be difficult to tell where one ends and the other begins. Low buildings, made of packed earth, plant fiber, and the tough trunks of the local growth, stand shoulder to shoulder along narrow passages. Walls swell around roots rather than cutting them; roofs are layered with clipped vegetation, turning the city into a continuation of the plain’s canopy. Courtyards are small and shaded. Paths are worn into the mineral ground by long use, their edges softened by creeping groundcover. There are no towers, engines, or bright devices. The built world is made by hands, repeated labor, and living material: practical, dense, and old in habit, even in a landscape that feels newly formed.

The city does not rise high. It spreads outward, following the level land, and its outer streets fray gradually into garden thickets and then into uninterrupted vegetation. In places the buildings seem to have been laid into the earth before the plants arrived; elsewhere the plants have grown through them, making doorways narrow and walls porous. People can move under the foliage for long stretches without stepping into the sun. A traveler sees only fragments: a pale wall between leaves, a passage dark with shade, a rooftop trembling slightly as a heavy seedpod opens.

There is constant activity, but little noise. Leaves flex and settle. Dry pods click together in the mild wind. Small animals pass beneath the roots—quick-bodied grazers, burrowing creatures, birds that hop rather than fly through the close canopy. Predators move too, though they are rarely seen for long. The life of the plain is abundant and vigilant, full of brief rustles and sudden stillness. Even the settlement seems to absorb sound. Its packed walls and crowded plants leave no long avenue for a voice to travel down.

Weather moves over the country in broad, slow changes. Warm winds cross the vegetation without lifting dust; at times they carry a dim shimmer of heat above the ground. The sky may cloud, but the clouds seldom give anything back. The plants respond to changes in light and pressure by folding their leaves, lifting their crowns, or releasing fine drifts of pollen. At dawn, when the temperature is mildest, the whole plain seems to breathe: leaf surfaces open, small animals emerge, and the lanes of the city fill with quiet movement.

Nothing here is finished. New crust still cracks in shallow seams. Plants colonize it almost as soon as it cools, their roots binding loose mineral fragments into the beginnings of soil. Buildings are repaired with whatever has grown nearby; old walls soften into earth and feed new growth. The land is neither wilderness nor fully tamed. It is a vast, crowded beginning: a warm, nearly waterless world learning, inch by inch, how to hold life and the shapes life makes.
