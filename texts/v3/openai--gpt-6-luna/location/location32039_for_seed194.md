The place is hidden inside a low, ancient fold of the ground: not a cave so much as a hollow in the grain of the world. Its entrance is a narrow seam beneath a plain of pale mineral dust. Nothing marks it from above. The plain has been worn almost level by ages of abrasion, and the seam has been worried open so slowly that its edges are smooth as bone.

Within, the hollow widens into streets.

They are not streets laid over soil. The whole city is made of plant: a vast, dry growth whose stems have thickened into pillars, whose roots have lifted into bridges, whose layers of bark have folded into walls and roofs. The oldest trunks are gray and dense, no longer soft enough to bend. Younger growth threads through their cracks in fine, papery sheets. It fills every seam, though it does not fill the space. Between the structures lie broad, vacant rooms—courtyards, galleries, long avenues with nothing moving through them. The city feels built for many, but holds almost nothing.

There is no open water here. No pool, drip, or dampness on the walls. The plants keep what they need bound inside their fibers, sealed away from the air; their leaves are narrow, dark, and leathery, and their roots drink from stone by a process too slow to be seen. The air is cool and dry, with the faint mineral scent of ground husks. Dust lies everywhere, but never in deep drifts. It is sifted and sifted again by motions too small to notice.

There is no light. Not dimness, not twilight: light simply does not reach the interior. The inhabitants who made the city—if inhabitants they were—left no lamps burning, and nothing here glows. To move through it is to learn by touch, by memory, and by sound. A hand on the wall finds a change from bark to woven stem; a foot finds the shallow groove worn down the center of a passage. Rooms are known by the way they return a voice.

And the place is loud.

A shift somewhere in the immense rootwork travels through the city as a dry, booming groan. Fine stems rasp against one another in the ceilings. Long hollow trunks answer with a rolling clatter that can last for minutes, though the cause may have been only a grain of mineral settling far away. In narrow passages, even a breath would come back enlarged and altered. In the great empty chambers the sound gathers, deepens, and seems to belong to the architecture itself. There is no wind, but the city is always making a little noise: the minute tightening of fibers, the slow collapse of an old joint, the whisper of dust sliding across a roof.

Its construction is orderly, almost urban. Raised walkways connect blocks of thick, interlocked growth. Doorways repeat at measured intervals; stairways descend to low chambers and ascend to bridges that span nothing but shadow. Some surfaces have been shaped with crude tools, their cuts still visible beneath layers of new bark. There are shallow channels along the floors, not for water but for guiding roots, or perhaps for keeping them out. The builders worked by hand and patiently, persuading living material into useful forms rather than mastering it. Where their work ended, the plants continued it in their own fashion.

No animal life disturbs the city. No wings beat in its darkness, no small bodies scurry through the walls. There are no nests, droppings, or gnawed edges. The plants have no need to invite anything else in. They grow by increments so slight that a whole lifetime might pass without a visible change. Still, nothing here is entirely finished. A bridge slowly thickens around its supports; a doorway narrows by the width of a hair. The city is not decaying so much as being absorbed into a final, settled shape.

The oldest passages have been erased by time. Their arches have become ribs within the roots; their floors have risen beneath layered growth. The city’s first plan, if it had one, is buried under the patient work of the plants and the finer work of erosion. What remains is a second city, made of the outlines of the first: lanes, plazas, chambers, all softened and hollowed until they seem less like buildings than places the growth has learned to leave open.

It is small, though its arrangement suggests a much larger world. A person could cross it in an hour, perhaps, but without light the distance would stretch. Every turn would be familiar and uncertain at once. The city’s greatest feature is not its height or extent, but its silence between sounds: long intervals in which nothing changes, followed by a single dry creak that passes from wall to wall and leaves the darkness feeling newly occupied.

Above it, the mineral plain continues to flatten. Beneath it, the roots hold fast. And in the hidden rooms between them, the plant-city waits—almost empty, wholly grown, making its slow, resonant noises in a world with no daylight and no water to spare.
