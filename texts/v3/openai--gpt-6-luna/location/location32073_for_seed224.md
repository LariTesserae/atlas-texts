The place is hidden inside the summit: a hollow in the high, cold spine of a mountain, sealed behind stone so completely that no wind has ever crossed it. It is small enough to be overlooked, but not small enough to feel that way. From its pale floor, the far ridge recedes for hours. From the ridge, the floor seems close enough to touch.

There is no water here—not even ice. The ground is a dry basin of grey salt and black mineral dust, stippled with crystals that catch what little light there is. Low shelves of stone step down toward a shallow depression at the center. Nothing has flowed over them. Their edges are worn smooth by some slower process: pressure, perhaps, or the patient passage of a boundary that leaves no mark except polish. Above, the chamber’s ceiling is lost in dimness. It resembles a night sky only because the eye insists on finding one.

Cold lies evenly over everything. The air is thin, still, and faintly metallic. A person entering would see their breath only as a brief distortion, a soft blur that appears below the mouth and then slips sideways, against gravity, into the rock. The temperature never quite changes. It is not a place where weather happens; it is a place that has settled into one long condition.

Scattered across the basin are structures too sparse to make a town. A narrow black frame stands on three legs. Farther away, a ring of pale material rises from the ground, broken at one point, its interior darker than the shadows around it. Near the highest shelf, a slender rod passes through a flat stone without touching it. These are not ruins exactly. Their surfaces are intact, but their purposes have outlasted any explanation. They do not glow. They make no obvious noise. Occasionally, a line of dim light travels along one of them and vanishes before reaching an end.

The place’s few rules are reliable until they are noticed. Gravity pulls toward the basin, except at the ring, where loose dust rests on its inner wall as if the wall were level ground. A shadow may fall a little ahead of the object that casts it. Distances remain consistent when walked, but not when measured: a ridge reached in ten minutes may be recorded as hundreds of meters, then as the width of a hand. The instruments agree with one another, but not with the landscape. After a while, it seems impolite to ask which of them is wrong.

Nothing grows. Nothing moves of its own accord. There are no tracks, nests, or signs of feeding. Yet the mineral surface is not wholly inert. Crystals very slowly turn their sharpest faces toward the scattered frames. Dust gathers in thin, orderly seams along invisible lines, then thins again over spans too long to watch. The chamber has no ecology in the living sense; it has correspondences. Pressure answers distance. Light answers the old structures. The basin keeps a kind of balance without needing anything to consume or produce.

Sound is scarce. A boot scuffs salt; the noise stops abruptly, as if clipped from the air. A dropped fragment may strike stone without a click, while its shadow makes a small, distinct tap. In the deepest silence there is sometimes a low tone, nearly below hearing, coming from no particular direction. It does not swell or fade. It is simply present, like a measurement being held.

The hollow has existed long enough to become settled, but not ordinary. Its oddities do not escalate. The ring remains broken. The distant shelf remains distant. The dimness neither deepens nor lifts. High above the world’s weather, with no water and no life to hurry it, the hidden basin persists—small, cold, and carefully out of agreement with the rest of reality.
