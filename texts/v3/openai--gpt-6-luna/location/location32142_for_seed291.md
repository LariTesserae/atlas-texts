The city lies almost level with the water, spread across a broad grey basin where the land still seems undecided about becoming land.

From a distance, it looks settled: roofs, chimneys, cranes, and long blocks of buildings occupying the low ground from one horizon to the other. Up close, nothing quite meets. Streets run out into fresh mud. A retaining wall will stop halfway through a block, its cut end showing damp layers of gravel, black peat, and pale clay. New ground swells against old foundations; yesterday’s curb is already sunk to its painted line. The basin is not sinking or rising so much as being assembled—slowly, then all at once.

Water is everywhere in small, withheld quantities. It beads on iron railings and gathers under the grates. Rain passes through now and then, thin and cold, leaving the brickwork dark. Narrow runnels appear in the streets and vanish by morning into porous seams in the earth. Between buildings, condensation hangs in the air like a second, finer kind of weather. There are ponds, but few large enough to earn the name: shallow sheets caught in excavations, reflecting a pale sky and the undersides of bridges.

The place is densely built. Housing, workshops, pumping stations, freight lines, and municipal sheds press close together, connected by pipes and covered passages. Pipes cross at shoulder height, disappear into buildings, and come out again on different streets. Some carry heat; others carry slurry, seed, or air from the lower works. The city has grown in layers without clearing away its earlier attempts. A stairwell may descend beneath a shop floor to an older shop, its windows now below street level. The oldest buildings stand on pilings, not because the ground is wet, but because the ground is new.

Beyond the central wards, the construction thins only slightly. Warehouses stand in long rows among belts of reeds and dark, waist-high grasses. Rail yards end at the edge of raw flats, where cranes unload stone into mounds that are already furred with green. There are districts that appear complete from above and unfinished from the street: orderly roofs over rooms with no interior walls, avenues laid down over ground that has not yet set. The region is open in scale, with long views through gaps between towers to the grey rim of the basin, but every near space is occupied. A person is rarely out of sight of a wall, a stem, a cable, or some slow-moving piece of machinery.

Vegetation thrives in the cold. It does not form gardens so much as take possession of every available surface. Moss covers the warm sides of boiler houses. Vines climb the factory frames and braid through cable trays. Broad-leafed plants grow in the lee of loading bays, their leaves dark and waxy, furred underneath with silver. Reeds push up through paving stones, bending but not breaking when trucks pass. In the vacant lots, young trees grow in tight thickets, their trunks pale and straight as unfinished columns. The city trims some of this growth, but never all of it; the cuttings are collected, pulped, and used as packing material in the same factories that make the tools for cutting it.

Animals are common, though many are glimpsed only briefly. Small, long-bodied creatures slip along the heated pipes. Pale birds nest in ventilation housings and roost on cranes that still move. In the marshy margins, something plashes beneath the reeds, leaving rings that spread too far for its size. At night, the lamps draw clouds of moth-like insects that settle on window glass in orderly bands, all facing the same direction. No one agrees on what most of the creatures are. The older residents give them practical names anyway: pipe-mice, mud hens, lift eels.

The city is industrial, but its machinery rarely roars. Engines run under insulation; conveyors roll behind sealed walls. Pumps thrum through the floors so faintly that people mistake them for their own blood. The louder sounds are small: a drip into a metal pan, the click of a signal changing, a cable tightening somewhere overhead. Even the railway seems subdued. Freight cars pass behind embankments of fresh earth, their wheels making a soft, continuous hush. At intervals the whole basin falls quiet enough to hear water travelling inside the walls.

That quiet is misleading. The place is in constant motion. Survey stakes shift overnight. A road crew arrives to find that the road has lifted by a handspan and no longer meets the bridge. New plots are marked before the ground beneath them has cooled. In one quarter, foundations are being poured while the neighboring block is already being taken apart for materials. Maps are issued weekly and treated as approximate. The city’s departments do not try to prevent change so much as give it useful directions.

The strangest rule of the basin is that new ground tends to arrive already furnished with traces of use. When an excavation exposes a fresh bank of clay, it may contain a rusted bolt, a root, a piece of glazed tile, or a complete stretch of old paving—none of them belonging to any known construction period. The objects are not considered mysterious by the crews. They are sorted, tested, and reused. Sometimes an empty lot settles overnight into a shallow rectangular depression, as if a building has been removed from it before it was built. By the next week, grass grows there.

In daylight the city has ordinary colors: soot-black metal, wet brick, green leaves, the chalky grey of unweathered stone. The sky is often visible. It does not press down or glow strangely; it simply looks cold and far away above all the crowded works. Yet there is no real boundary between city and landscape. The factories cultivate the ground they stand on, the plants occupy the factories, and the ground keeps making room for both. At the outer edge, where the last rail lines disappear into the flats, cranes continue to work against the open horizon, placing stone into a country that has not finished arriving.
