The first thing the dark gives back is distance.

The plateau rises above the weather below it: a lifted country of black ridges, broad shelves, and basins so wide that their far edges disappear without seeming to get nearer. The rock is young. Its faces have not yet softened into hills; they break in abrupt steps, raw fins, and fields of sharp scree. Here and there, the stone is still split by long, straight seams, as though the land has only just been pulled apart. Meltwater has found the cracks, but not enough of it to make rivers everywhere. Most of the plateau is dry underfoot, then suddenly slick: a thin glaze of condensation on a ridge, a shallow pool hidden in a hollow, a cold trickle threading down a wall.

Above, there is very little sky. Darkness sits close to the ground, though the air is open and immense. Clouds move through it in great dark sheets, sometimes low enough to catch on the heights. When they do, they comb the ridges with wet mist. The mist beads on stone and plant-skin, gathers at the ends of leaves, then falls in a brief, steady patter. Rain is occasional and fine. The water seems to arrive mostly by being taken out of the air.

The vegetation is everywhere, but not crowded. It has claimed the plateau without filling it.

Across the open reaches stand immense plants spaced far apart, their dark trunks rising like pillars from the rock. Some are jointed and hollow, with narrow crowns that shiver in the wind; others spread flat, many-tiered fans, each blade long enough to roof a hamlet. Between them lie distances of bare stone—miles of black ground beneath the shadow of one plant, then another. From a ridge, the whole place looks almost empty. Then the wind shifts, and the surfaces begin to move: low mats of moss flexing in the cracks, root-fibres tightening over a cliff edge, pale fronds folding and reopening like slow breaths.

The plants do not seem to grow toward the darkness. They grow toward moisture. Their roots follow the cold seams in the rock, reaching toward the places where fog lingers longest. In sheltered clefts, their growth becomes a woven mass: stalk over stalk, tough leaves layered into walls, roots bridging the gaps between stones. These thickets are green only by touch. In the dark they are deep blue-black, or grey as old bone, sometimes pricked with the faintest dull glow along their veins. The glow is not enough to light a path. It is enough to show that the leaf beside you is alive.

At the edge of a basin, a forest has grown into a kind of architecture. Its tallest stems lean together overhead, making a vaulted passage that runs for several miles. The floor is springy with old plant matter, though little has rotted away. Cold slows decay. Fronds that fell seasons ago remain intact, stacked like folded sheets. Small streams wind under them, audible before they are visible. Once in a while the whole roof groans as the wind presses on it. A shower of beads shakes loose and strikes the leaves below with a sound like handfuls of thrown gravel.

The air is full of voices. Wind pours through the plateau’s narrow gaps with a deep, continuous roar, then breaks into whistles where the rock narrows to a blade. In the plant-vaults the noise becomes a low thunder. Against it, animals call from all directions: sharp, hollow cries from unseen ledges; chatter from the roots; long, wavering notes that travel across open basins and return from the far cliffs. The animals are plentiful, but they are rarely gathered close. You may hear a whole valley alive and see only one moving shape.

Some creatures cross the open ground in long, deliberate lines. They have broad feet for the loose stone and coats that drink in the dark; their backs carry little gardens of pale lichen and seedpods. Smaller animals live in the high plant canopies, springing from stem to stem or gliding between the crowns on membranes thin as leaves. In the pools, black things stir under the skin of the water. Their ripples catch the faint light for an instant, then vanish. There are hunters, too: quiet shapes that wait beside the places where the roots draw water, patient enough to remain still through a whole spell of rain.

Nothing here hurries except the weather. Plants take years to bend into a new wind. Roots may spend a century finding a crack and then travel through it with startling speed. Animals follow the fog, the seedfall, and the hidden watercourses, their migrations marked not by paths but by changes in sound. A basin that seemed deserted can, over the course of one night, fill with calls from its rim to its center. By morning, the calls have moved on, leaving only the wind.

Human signs are sparse: a low stone shelter tucked beneath an overhang, a few narrow platforms lashed between living stems, a trail of pale chips where someone has tested the rock for a crossing. The builders use what the land offers—cut reeds, root cord, flat stones—and leave little that lasts. Their fires are small and hooded. Smoke is precious here; in the still moments it climbs straight up, then disappears into cloud. A few hanging bells made from hollow stalks mark the edges of their paths. They sound even when no one is near, stirred by wind too slight to feel.

There is no single boundary between wilderness and settlement, or between plant and structure. The old shelters are being wrapped in roots. A bridge may be a living stem coaxed across a fissure. A dry wall may be nothing but stones held in place by a mat of fibres. People make their marks where the plateau already allows a pause: at a spring beneath a rock lip, at a stand of plants that gives shelter from the western wind, beside a pool that returns after every fog.

The darkness never quite lifts, but it has textures. Over the open basins it is wide and featureless. Under the plant crowns it is close, crowded with invisible motion. Along the cliffs it thins enough to reveal the pale edges of new stone. When clouds pass low overhead, the entire landscape seems to draw nearer: the immense stems emerge, one by one; the ridgelines appear; water shines in every crack. Then the mist goes, the distance opens again, and the plateau becomes an almost empty country of rock, scattered living towers, and sound travelling farther than sight.
