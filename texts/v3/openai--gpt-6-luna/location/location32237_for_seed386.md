The plateau begins above the clouds.

From below, its rim is a dark, continuous wall, cut by clefts where rivers spill out of sight. Higher up, the land opens into a broad country of ridges, basins, and long sloping shelves. Distance is difficult to judge here. A waterfall that seems a short walk away may be a white thread dropping for miles; a dark line on the horizon may be the far side of a valley broad enough to hold its own weather.

The air is hot, even at this height. It holds the dampness of a greenhouse and the thinness of a mountain pass. Sunlight falls cleanly through breaks in the cloud, bright on black stone and hard leaves. Then vapor gathers from the ground, and the whole basin goes soft-edged: ridges blur, trees appear as scattered islands in a white sea, and water can be heard running somewhere nearby without being seen. Rain comes often, though rarely as a grand storm. It beads on leaves, lifts from warm pools, seeps from the rock, and keeps the soil dark.

The ground is old and settled. It has been worn into its shapes for a long time: rounded shoulders of volcanic stone, shallow ravines, shelves lined with mineral deposits, and basins where silt and fallen leaves gather in a dense black layer. The rock is cut through with seams of pale green and rust-red. In places, heat from below has glazed it with thin, glassy films. Hot springs issue quietly from cracks, feeding channels that wander across the plateau and vanish under mats of moss. Their water leaves pale rims around pools, then stains them orange where iron collects.

Nothing here is built. Yet the landscape can seem to be working.

Water moves through hidden channels under pressure, rising in bursts through narrow vents and sinking again through porous stone. Steam sighs from fissures. Mineral crusts grow along the edges of streams in delicate, hard flanges. In a few basins, the ground hums faintly underfoot, a deep vibration that comes and goes with the flow below. It is not a machine’s sound, exactly, but the pressure and regularity of one: the long, tireless labor of heat, water, and stone turning through a cycle too large to hurry. A quiet industrial country, assembled by geology.

Vegetation is abundant without becoming an impenetrable wall. Broad-leaved trees crowd the wetter gullies, their trunks dark with moss and draped in wiry epiphytes. Between them grow high grasses, flowering shrubs, and tough, waxy plants rooted directly in cracks. Open ground remains on the ridges, where wind and shallow soils keep the growth low. There are fern-choked hollows, bright patches of small flowers beside hot runnels, and trees growing in improbable places on ledges above the clouds. Their roots spread over bare stone, finding every seam where water has paused.

Animals are plentiful, though the place is mostly quiet. Small, quick shapes move through the brush; insects flicker above the warm pools, and birds call from hidden canopy. Larger animals leave signs rather than appearances: a trampled strip of grass, claw marks on damp clay, a sudden hush among the smaller calls. Near the water, pale amphibians cling to stone that stays warm after sunset. In the open basins, long-legged grazers cross in loose groups, taking advantage of new growth along the streams. Predators are present too, but they keep to the broken ground and the hours when mist makes distance unreliable.

There is little wind at the bottom of the basins, where vapor lies close to the ground. Up on the ridges it can arrive all at once, flattening the grasses and carrying the smell of wet mineral dust. The sound of the plateau comes in intervals: water ticking into pools, a distant plunge of falls, leaves rubbing together, then long silences in which even the insects seem to be listening. Now and then a vent releases a low, even roar. It fades before it can become alarming.

The rules of this place are simple but not gentle. Water is everywhere, but not always safe to drink: some streams are sweet and cold, others are hot enough to burn or sharp with dissolved minerals. Bare rock can be slick with clear films. A sunny path may cross a cloud bank without warning. The ground gives off heat in some hollows and drains it in others, so a few paces can change what grows and what can live there. Nothing seems newly made. Even the steam, the rushing water, and the bright mineral colors belong to an old balance, repeated over years beyond counting.

From the rim, the whole plateau appears serene: green, shining, and almost still. Within it, the hidden circulation never stops. Water rises, cools, gathers, falls, and returns through the stone. Plants draw it upward. Animals follow the growth it sustains. Heat works beneath everything, patient as a buried engine, while clouds drift across the high country and make the immense landscape vanish a little at a time.
