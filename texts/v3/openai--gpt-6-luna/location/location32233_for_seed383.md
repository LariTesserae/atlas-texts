The city lies beneath a plain that gives no hint of it. There is no grand entrance, only a long cleft in the limestone, narrow enough to pass unnoticed until the ground gives way and the daylight goes on without you. Below, the cleft widens into a country of chambers.

The first impression is brightness. It comes from above in pale shafts, slipping through cracks and old sinkholes, and from the walls themselves: broad faces of white mineral, polished over ages by water, returning every scrap of light in a thin, cold gleam. Some passages shine more strongly after rain. Their ceilings bead with moisture; drops gather, fall, and vanish into cracks too small to see. Here and there, a pool has survived the journey downward, shallow and still as a piece of cloudy glass.

The stone is old past any useful measure. Its surfaces have been rounded, hollowed, and smoothed until the cave seems less excavated than remembered by water. Yet much of it has been shaped by hands. Low buildings cluster on shelves above the deepest channels: rooms cut into the walls, stairways worn into ramps, bridges of fitted slabs crossing fissures. The construction is plain and stubborn—stone wedges, wooden pegs preserved in the dry air, occasional hammered bands of dull metal. No tower rises far. The builders knew the ceiling could change its mind.

What they made is spread across a great underground basin, with long open distances between districts. A house, then a court, then a whole reach of bare stone before another neighborhood appears. Streets follow the dry contours. Along the wetter paths, drains and sluices have been cut into the floor, though the water often ignores them. The city is organized, but not densely; it has the spaciousness of a place built for people who could never quite trust the ground beneath their feet.

The ground is always deciding where the water should go. A trickle may be absent for years, then arrive overnight as a clear, cold sheet running down a stairway and through a courtyard. Walls split without warning. A ceiling sheds a fall of pale gravel. Somewhere beyond the inhabited chambers, whole shelves of rock tilt, settle, and grind into their new positions. The movement is frequent enough that nothing is called a disaster unless it lasts past morning. Still, the place is not loud. Stone absorbs its own violence. A collapse may send a faint shiver through the soles, while the sound arrives as a soft breath—or not at all.

There is no green. No root hangs from the ceilings, no weed claims the cracks. Life here is animal life, and it is abundant in the way water is abundant: gathered in runs, hollows, and sudden concentrations. Pale crabs crowd certain cisterns, picking mineral film from the stone. Small, blind things thread the drains. Long-legged cave birds roost in the high shafts, where the light is strongest; their wings make almost no sound. Larger animals pass through the outer chambers in herds that leave smooth tracks in the dust and vanish again into the dark reaches. The city’s residents have learned to leave gates open for them. They are not domesticated so much as accommodated.

In the brightest halls, the animals gather beneath the shafts. Their coats and shells catch the light in patches: chalk-white backs, translucent feelers, dark eyes like wet beads. They feed on whatever the cave makes available—mineral crust, drifting spores too small to notice, the remains of other creatures. Nothing grows here in the familiar sense, yet something is being made and consumed all the time. The food chains are spare, efficient, and difficult to see.

People move quietly through the streets. Their tools are simple, their repairs constant. They scrape silt from channels, reset fallen blocks, hang pale cloth to catch condensation. At dusk—though there is no true dusk—they cover the brighter shafts with woven screens, not to keep light out but to soften its direction. The animals settle more easily under a diffuse glow.

The air is cool and damp against the skin. It carries a mineral smell, with a faint trace of smoke near the inhabited rooms. Across the open chambers, a person can watch a far wall brighten as light shifts overhead, or see a river of dust descend from a newly opened crack. The city does not feel abandoned, nor fully occupied. It feels like a careful agreement between stone, water, animals, and the scattered lives that have found shelter there.

Every morning, the map is a little wrong. A passage has narrowed. A pool has emptied. Some distant court has acquired a new arch of fallen rock, clean and white against the old walls. The city takes these changes in without ceremony. Its streets are worn smooth, its foundations ancient, its future already rearranging itself.
