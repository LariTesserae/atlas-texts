The basin lies beneath the last reach of daylight, under so much water that the surface is no longer a direction so much as a rumor. Cold presses evenly over everything. It has no sharp edge here: the old seabed has been planed down by ages of current, leaving a broad, sunken country of low ridges, shallow troughs, and shelves that slope almost imperceptibly into darker water. From one rise, the next is visible only as a change in blackness. The whole region seems level until a current draws a veil of silt aside and reveals how far the stone falls away.

The ground is ancient and crowded. Gravel lies packed into every hollow, each grain worn smooth and dulled by the same patient grinding. Between it are heavy nodules, pale mineral crusts, broken shelves of dark rock, and rounded stones embedded so tightly that the floor looks paved—though nothing here was laid by hand. In places, the seabed is furred with fine sediment. A passing surge lifts it in sheets, and the landscape disappears beneath its own dust. When it settles, every surface has shifted by a finger’s breadth. Grooves fill; ridges soften; old channels are buried and new ones begin.

There is little vegetation. A few short, tough growths cling in cracks where the current cannot quite peel them away: dim fronds, threadlike colonies, low mats the color of ash or old bone. They bow flat when the water moves and slowly lift again. Small pale animals hide among the stones, rarely seen except as a flicker or a closing shell. Larger life is uncommon. The basin is not empty, but its inhabitants keep to crevices and shadows, and the immense floor makes each of them seem incidental.

The water is in motion everywhere. It does not break into waves, but moves in heavy, crossing layers: a cold flow sliding one way above another, a deeper surge pushing back against it. The currents comb the sediment into long drifts, then tear them apart. At the edges of the low ridges they tumble and coil, drawing whole veils of grit upward before letting them sink again. Now and then the water accelerates without any visible cause, and the entire plain shivers—not violently enough to seem like a disaster, but with the tireless force of something that has never been still.

Yet the basin is quiet. The water swallows distance and softens impact. There is no surf, no wind, no ringing of stone. Only a low, remote pressure-sound, like a door held shut far below, and the faint hiss of silt moving over rock. Close at hand, a pebble may click against another. The sound seems startling, then vanishes into the dark.

Darkness gathers thickest in the troughs, but there is no true nightfall and no clear dawn. The few living lights—small glimmers from animals hidden beneath ledges—do not illuminate the ground around them. They mark presence, not shape. The eye learns the basin by interruption: a ridge that blocks what little glow there is; a pale crust emerging from a black slope; the sudden absence of drifting grit where a rock stands in the current.

Nothing here has been built. There are no walls, paths, or tools, and nothing suggests that the basin was ever meant to be inhabited. Its forms are all the result of pressure, abrasion, deposition, and the long repetition of water moving over stone. The place is old enough to appear finished, but it is never finished. The currents are constantly undoing the last arrangement of matter, and the next one begins before the old one has settled.

At a distance, the floor looks calm and nearly featureless. Up close, it is packed with detail: grains wedged into seams, crusts layered over older crusts, tiny hollows crowded with silt, life sheltering inside the smallest available darkness. The basin gives the impression of having no room left in it—no empty ground, no unoccupied crevice—while extending beyond sight in every direction. It is both exhausted and restless: a world worn almost flat, still turning itself over in silence.
