Behind a sealed wall, below the level of the street, there is a room full of warm water.

It is not a cavern. Every surface has been made: the floor of fitted clay tiles, the walls of brick under pale lime plaster, the low ceiling ribbed with hand-shaped beams and sealed against the seep. Even the scattered grit is broken tile, softened at the corners but still sharp enough to catch a fingertip. Nothing here is quite old. The mortar is fresh in places; the cut edges of the channels are clean and abrupt. Water has begun to work on the room, but it has not yet had time to make it gentle.

The chamber is small enough to take in at once, though its corners keep slipping from view behind pillars and narrow partitions. A person could cross it in a dozen strokes. Along one wall, a run of shallow niches holds nothing. Along another, a tiled gutter carries a steady current from a square opening to a plugged drain. The opening is no larger than a hatch, but the water comes through with the force of a distant tide, stirring the room in slow, persistent circuits.

Light enters through a slit high overhead. It has passed through water and several layers of glass, and arrives here as a bright, wavering sheet. The tiles throw it back in fragments: white flare, greenish shine, the brief blue of a shadow under a ledge. There are no deep darks except inside the drains. The room is warm, and the water holds the warmth close. Moisture beads on the plaster above the waterline, gathering, growing heavy, and dropping back with a regular tick.

The sound is much larger than the place. The inlet hisses against its stone lip. Water rattles through a set of small clay pipes, each pipe tuned by its own imperfect shape. The ceiling carries every impact and returns it as a hollow clamor. A loose wooden gate knocks when the current shifts; somewhere behind the wall, a float or lever taps in answer. The noises overlap without becoming a voice. In the pauses, the chamber is still loud with the movement of water.

A few dark-green threads of growth cling to the brightest grout lines. They bend with the current, then lift again, sparse as brushmarks. There are no fish, no insects, no small lives moving through the water. The only motion is made by the room itself: the circulation, the dripping, the gate working against its socket, the slow release of air from seams in the plaster.

Nothing strange is required to explain it. This is a hidden cistern or a drowned utility chamber, built by people who knew how to shape clay, lay brick, and persuade water to travel. Its rules are plain: the light comes from above; the current enters through the hatch; the channels divide and return it; the walls hold for now. Yet the room is changing. A little mortar powders away at the outlet. A new hairline crack has appeared beside the gate. The water is testing its workmanship, patiently and all at once.
