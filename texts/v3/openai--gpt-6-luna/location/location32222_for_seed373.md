The place is a high, enclosed hollow no wider than a village, cupped in the crown of an old mountain. Its rim is not a sharp ridge but a worn, uneven lip of dark stone, softened by ages of grit and wind. Beyond it, the world falls away into pale distance. Inside, the ground is a patchwork of slate shelves, powdery mineral flats, and shallow hollows where dust has gathered in drifts like snow. There is almost no water. No stream cuts the basin; no pool reflects the sky. Even the word *damp* seems misplaced here.

Yet the hollow is green.

Its growth rises in close islands from cracks in the stone: thick mats of silver-green fronds, knee-high fans with translucent edges, and low shrubs whose leaves are waxed against the dry air. Some plants anchor themselves with fine copper-colored threads that delve into the mineral seams. Others grow in hollow columns, taking what they need from the wind: salts, trace gases, static charge, the faint warmth of passing bodies. Their roots hold little soil. They hold the place together by gripping stone.

The air is cool and bright. Light reaches the basin cleanly, with little haze to soften it. Shadows are sharply edged beneath the plants and beneath the few structures scattered through the hollow. At this altitude, the sky feels near enough to scrape. Gusts rush over the rim, tumble down the basin walls, and collide in the center. They do not settle into a steady prevailing wind. They circle, reverse, and rise again, lifting mineral dust in swift, low veils.

The sound is the hollow’s most overwhelming feature.

It begins in the stone: a continuous, deep roar as the wind presses through narrow clefts under the basin floor. Over that runs the clatter and shivering rasp of the vegetation, each stand of fronds rattling at a different pitch. The animals make the sound unbearable. They live in the open, with long spaces between them, but each one seems to have found a way to fill the whole basin. Small, swift things spring among the growth, their calls bright as struck metal. Larger creatures crouch on the ledges and answer with booming pulses that can be felt in the ribs before they are heard. At night—or what passes for night here—the chorus changes its arrangement, never becoming quiet. The hollow seems to ring from within.

The animals are abundant but not crowded. They travel along habitual routes between the green islands, keeping wide distances from one another. Some are lean, many-legged grazers with dark glassy eyes; some are gliding shapes that ride the turbulent air without wings anyone can see. Now and then, a long-backed creature crosses the bare mineral flats, pausing to press its face to the ground. Its feeding leaves no obvious mark. Nothing stays still for long, but nothing appears to be in a hurry to leave.

Scattered among the plants are structures of an older and stranger kind. A narrow tower stands on a shelf of slate, open-sided and almost delicate, made of pale alloy that has not tarnished. Its upper rings turn slowly even when the wind drops. Farther in, half concealed by fronds, a cluster of low black pylons leans at slightly different angles. They are too smooth to be natural, yet old dust has settled into their seams. Once in a while the air around them seems to fold: a gust arrives from the wrong direction, a call echoes before the animal gives it, or a loose stone lifts, hangs for a breath, then falls.

These works do not dominate the landscape. They are too widely spaced, too quiet in their own manner. No roads join them. There are only faint, worn tracks through the growth, and even those vanish wherever the ground turns to bare stone. The structures seem less like buildings than instruments left set up for a measurement that has gone on for a very long time.

The hollow is old in its shape. Its ledges have been rounded by an erosion that has nearly finished its work, and the mineral flats lie settled in their shallow basins. But its weather is never finished. Dust pours over the rim in sudden curtains. The fronds bend flat, spring upright, and bend again. A gust can cross the hollow in a single violent sweep, changing direction halfway through and scattering the animals into separate pockets of silence—brief, local silences, each immediately filled by the roar of the stone and wind.

Nothing here is watery, and nothing is truly still. Life has made a garden out of what the mountain gives: cold, light, mineral, air, and the force of motion. In the middle of that immense brightness, the hollow remains a small place, bounded by its rim, crowded with green only in patches, and vast with noise wherever the eye sees empty ground.
