At this elevation, the air is already inside the vegetation.

The place begins as a high, dim plain, though “plain” is only what the land looks like from a long way off. Up close, there is no bare ground. The surface is a continuous press of living tissue: close-grown mats, folded blades, sheets of moss thick as felt, and dark, jointed stems packed so tightly that they support one another. Water shines in the seams, but there are few seams. It beads on leaves, wells from the ground, and hangs in the air as a cold, fine mist. The whole landscape seems to be quietly weeping.

Nothing here has had time to wear down. The rock is young—pale, fractured in clean planes, and only just thrusting through the green. Ridges rise without the softened shoulders of old mountains; their edges are sharp, their slopes scarcely scored by runoff. In places, new ground is still lifting beneath the vegetation, making broad, slow swells that travel for days before they can be seen to move. Roots catch the rising stone as it comes, not so much breaking it as taking hold of it while it is being made.

The land is enormous. Weather forms within it and stays there. Clouds snag in the upper growth, condense, and rain back into the leaves before they can fall far. Farther out, where the plain climbs toward a rim of cloud, the mist gathers into rolling fronts and comes inward like a tide. There is no clear horizon—only successive distances fading to grey-green, then to a dimness that might be cloud or more vegetation.

The light is weak and never quite direct. It enters through layers of leaf and membrane, broken into a greenish twilight. Pale stems glow faintly where water runs inside them. At night, the glow does not brighten the place so much as reveal its depth: a low, immense radiance under the canopy, like light seen through deep water. Shadows are soft. Even the rocks seem to have been wrapped in something.

The sound is the first thing that makes the scale difficult to understand. The vegetation rasps, clicks, flexes, and sings continuously. Millions of wet leaves turn against one another in the wind. Great stalks bow with a low, drawn-out groan; root systems knock against the young stone below. Water courses through hidden channels with the force of a distant storm. Above all of it is a steady, overwhelming rush—not one sound, but the combined voice of a whole living surface. A person could stand in the middle of it and feel the noise through the soles of their feet.

There are no animals to interrupt the pattern. No calls, wings, tracks, or sudden movements. The plants themselves provide the motion: fronds folding when the mist thickens; seedlike spores lifting in warm updrafts; slick, dark runners extending into newly opened cracks. The growth is so dense that nothing moves through it. Instead, the landscape moves around and through itself.

Its most extraordinary workings are nearly invisible. The roots carry signals faster than sap should allow, passing pressure, temperature, and the faintest tremor across distances too great for any single plant to sense. When a ridge begins to lift, growth thickens there before the stone breaks the surface. When clouds gather, entire valleys alter the angle of their leaves, not in obedience to a command but in answer to a prediction distributed through the living network. The system does not build machines. It makes the world behave, in certain respects, as though it has learned to calculate.

This is not a garden, and nothing has arranged it. It is an immense, unfinished ecology filling every available space before the space has fully arrived. The stone is still becoming land. The plants are already there to meet it. Each hour, somewhere beyond sight, a new ridge rises under the mat, water gathers on its first exposed face, and green closes over the wet rock. The noise never stops. The dimness never quite lifts. The whole high country is growing into its own existence.
