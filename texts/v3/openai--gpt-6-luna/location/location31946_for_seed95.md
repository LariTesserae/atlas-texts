The high country does not rise into peaks so much as remain after them. It is a broad, warm plateau, lifted above the lowlands and worn almost level by an age of wind. From a distance it seems empty: a pale expanse running out to the haze, broken by shallow basins, long ribs of exposed stone, and low mesas with their edges rounded to softness. Only when you cross it does the ground reveal how crowded it is.

The stone is the color of old bone, honey, and ash. In some places it lies in sheets, cracked into plates as wide as houses; elsewhere it is honeycombed with shallow pockets and narrow passages. Rain comes rarely and briefly. It gathers in the hollows, darkening the rock, then vanishes into cracks or under the warmth of the day. In the mornings, moisture beads along the shaded faces of ridges. By noon the beads are gone, but the stone keeps a faint coolness in its deeper seams.

There is little green. Low, tough plants occupy the rare places where grit has collected: small cushions of silver leaves, wiry stems, and thorny shrubs that lean close to the ground. They grow far apart, leaving wide expanses of bare rock between them. But the bare rock is not bare. A skin of lichen lies over it in muted colors—pale green, rust, blue-black—so thin it seems painted on. In the fissures, beneath ledges, and inside the hollows, life is packed tightly together.

The animals are everywhere, though the place gives little sound to announce them. Tiny pale crabs cluster in dry basins until a rain arrives; then they move together toward the water, flowing over the stone like a change in its texture. Flat-backed reptiles warm themselves in cracks, so still that their bodies pass for fragments of rock. Long-legged, nearly translucent birds stand among them, their narrow faces turned toward the wind. They do not call. They feed on the small movements below them, and on the soft, abundant insects that rise from the stone’s pores at dusk. In the deeper hollows, burrowing animals make whole chambers beneath a crust no thicker than a hand’s breadth. One step may disturb nothing; the next may send a ripple through a hundred hidden lives.

The plateau’s abundance depends on patience. Fog enters from the western escarpment on certain mornings and settles low, not as a weather front but as a slow white filling of the basins. It leaves water on the rock and a mineral taste in the air. The small creatures drink from the beaded surfaces; the larger ones gather where the fog has pooled. Afterward, the plateau dries again, and the animals return to their places. Nothing here hurries. Even the movements that seem sudden are the last visible part of something that has been preparing for hours.

Some of the hollows have rules of their own. Water gathers in one basin and not in the next, though their floors appear equally low. A line of dark stones may remain damp through a season of heat, while the ground around them whitens and flakes. At certain hours, the whole basin seems to hold its breath: the birds lower their heads, the crabs draw into the cracks, and a fine tremor passes through the lichen. Nothing emerges from it. The stillness simply loosens, and the life resumes.

There are traces of people, but no great works. A few low walls cross the plateau, built from slabs already lying loose on the ground. Some are barely higher than the lichen; others enclose small, roofless chambers that keep the morning shade. Their stones have settled into the land so completely that animals use them as they use every other crevice. Pale nests fill the corners. A reptile lies in the sun on a fallen lintel. In places, shallow channels run from one hollow to another, though it is impossible to tell whether they were made by hands or by water working along the same lines for many ages. Nothing has been repaired. Nothing appears abandoned, either. The structures have simply become another kind of stone.

The light is ordinary and clear. It falls without spectacle, making the plateau’s distances easy to misjudge. A mesa that seems nearby may take half a day to reach; a shallow valley may widen and widen as you cross it. The far escarpments are hazed with warmth, their faces worn into soft horizontal bands. From their tops, the country opens in every direction: a sequence of pale flats, low ridges, and basins holding the faintest stain of moisture.

Sound travels strangely in that openness. Wind can pass over the plateau without a whistle, as if the rounded land gives it nothing to catch. Most of the time there is only the hush of heat and an occasional dry click from the stone. At night, when the surface cools, the animals shift beneath it. You hear no chorus, only sporadic ticking, scratching, and the soft fall of grit into a crack. The silence is not empty. It is crowded with small, contained sounds.

The country feels ancient not because it is ruined, but because it has nearly finished changing. Its ridges are low; its slopes have lost their sharpness. The rains no longer cut deep channels, and the wind has no loose mountains of sand to drive. Even the animals seem fitted to a settled world: close to the ground, economical in movement, almost indistinguishable from the surfaces that shelter them. The plateau has not become lifeless. It has become so old that life and stone no longer seem like separate things.
