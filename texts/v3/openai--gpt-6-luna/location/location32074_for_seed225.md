The chamber is not deep so much as tucked away: a hollow beneath a low, stony rise, no larger than a grove and enclosed on every side. Its roof bows down in pale folds, then opens at one point to a narrow seam in the earth. Daylight slips through there—not as a beam, exactly, but as a broad, milky brightness that the walls seem to hold and pass along. The stone is chalk-white in places, honey-colored in others, polished by ages of slow water. Even the farthest corners are visible. Shadows gather only beneath ledges and in the shallow mouths of cracks.

The floor is mostly bare. Shallow hollows have been worn into it, some dry and powdery, some lined with a skin of water so thin it reflects the ceiling without quite becoming a pool. After rain above, the chamber sweats. Drops appear along the roof, hang for hours, and fall with great deliberation into the hollows. A few narrow channels connect them, their edges softened and rounded by long use. There is no river here, only the memory of one: a faint slope in the stone, a scatter of smooth pebbles, a place where water once had the patience to go.

Warmth gathers near the floor. The air tastes faintly of mineral and damp clay, but is not close or heavy. It shifts so little that a strand of root can hang from the ceiling for years without stirring. The roots are sparse, pale threads that descend through the seam overhead and stop where the stone gives them enough moisture. Here and there, a green film clings to a wet edge. It is the chamber’s only real vegetation, and even that seems less to grow than to persist.

For all its emptiness, the place is full of animals. Most are small enough to be mistaken for flecks of grit until they move: pale beetles that walk the dry ridges, fine-legged crickets that live in the cracks, translucent little swimmers turning in the deepest pools. A few broad-winged moths rest on the ceiling, their wings the same color as the stone. They stir only when the light changes. The animals are abundant in their own scale, but they do not crowd the room. Between each moving thing lie long stretches of still floor, open water, bare wall. Nothing hunts noisily. Nothing needs to hurry.

Some of the swimmers rise to the surface just before a drop falls. They do not seem to feed on it; they turn beneath it, as if sensing the slight alteration in pressure. The moths open their wings when the chamber brightens and close them again when clouds pass over the seam. Whether they see the light or feel its warmth is difficult to tell. The room has a few such rules, small and consistent. Water gathers where the stone is darkest. The little creatures avoid the one dry hollow that is brightest. A thin mineral crust there rings, very faintly, when touched by a falling drop.

Sound scarcely travels. A drop may strike water with a clear, delicate note, and the note will remain in the chamber longer than seems reasonable; then it is gone, leaving the quiet intact. There is an occasional dry ticking from the walls as the stone warms, or the soft rasp of beetles crossing a patch of grit. Above the roof seam, weather passes without announcing itself. The chamber registers it only afterward, in new beads of moisture, a shift in brightness, or the gradual arrival of a wandering root.

Nothing here looks newly made. The walls are rounded rather than broken, the hollows settled into their shapes, the pale surfaces marked by fine lines where water has repeated its passage. Yet the place is not dead or finished. It changes by amounts too slight to witness: a pool narrows, a crack fills with calcite, a root reaches a little farther down. In this small bright hollow beneath the ground, time works quietly, and the living things have learned to make a world out of what it leaves behind.
