The place is called the Silt Garden, though there is no garden wall and almost no soil.

It occupies a shallow bowl no wider than a village: a low, warm hollow between old ridges, so worn down by time that the ridges seem less like hills than the memory of hills. Their dark stone has been smoothed to a skin. Water beads from it continually—not falling so much as arriving, gathering in clear seams and running in narrow threads toward the hollow’s center. The air is bright and damp. Even at noon, when the sun lies broad and white over everything, the stones shine as if the night’s rain has only just stopped.

The center is a sheet of water, ankle-deep except where the ground has slowly given way. There, it darkens without seeming to deepen: a green-black window in which clouds, roots, and tiny moving specks are reflected with equal clarity. A few flat stones rise through it. Their tops are warm and dry; their sides are furred with moss the color of old copper. Between them grow reedlike plants with thick, translucent leaves. They stand far apart. The garden is lush, but not crowded. Every patch of life has room around it, as though the hollow were carefully arranged—or as though everything that could not find room had long ago left.

The oldest things here are not the rocks but the channels in them. Water has been following the same paths for ages, wearing smooth little basins into the stone, polishing some edges and leaving others untouched. A shallow groove circles the bowl. It is too regular to be natural and too worn to be recent. In several places it passes beneath the water, then reappears a few paces away, without a break in its course. The water follows it even when the ground seems to slope the other way.

Scattered around the edge are the remains of a few structures: narrow pillars, a low arch, half a platform sunk into moss. They are made of a pale, seamless material that has not cracked, though roots have passed through it and water has covered it for centuries. Nothing is built high enough to be seen from far away. Each fragment seems less like a ruin than a tool laid down gently and forgotten.

Some of the tools are still working.

A ring of tiny marks around the old platform brightens when the sun reaches it. No two marks glow at once. When one fades, a patch of water nearby lifts into the air—not in a splash, but as a smooth, trembling bead—and hangs there, reflecting the hollow upside down. After a moment it settles back, making no sound. At times the platform’s shadow points toward the sun. At other times it points toward the center of the bowl, where there is no object to cast it. The place’s old machinery does not seem to command nature so much as persuade it into brief exceptions.

Life is everywhere, though much of it is easy to mistake for detail in the landscape. Small pale animals cling beneath the leaves, their many feet moving in an orderly ripple. Long, ribbon-bodied creatures slide through the shallows without disturbing the reflection. In the reeds, broad-backed insects sit as still as stones until another creature passes; then the air fills with a sudden unfolding of wings, and the whole patch of reeds appears to flower and vanish at once.

There are larger animals too. One, a gentle grazer with a wide, soft head and four jointed legs, stands on a stone shelf above the water. Its hide is mottled with moss and tiny white shells. It lowers its mouth to the surface and drinks without bending. Beneath it, the water fills with silver shapes, dozens, then hundreds, all circling its reflection. A slender predator waits on the far bank, nearly invisible among the wet roots. It does not stalk. It simply remains where it is, and the smaller animals change their paths around it.

For all this abundance, the Silt Garden is quiet. There are no loud calls, no wind in the reeds. Sound seems to be absorbed by the damp air, or by the soft mats of life covering the ground. A visitor hears the small things only when standing very still: the tick of a water bead forming on stone; the brush of a leaf against another leaf; a low, intermittent vibration from somewhere beneath the platform, like a great machine dreaming below the earth.

The animals communicate in movements the eye nearly misses. A ripple travels through a flock of floating seeds. A line of color passes under the skin of the grazer. The insect wings open and close in answer to changes in light. Nothing here is silent exactly. It is simply that nearly all the conversation is too quiet for a human ear.

Warmth gathers in the hollow and stays. The water keeps the air cool at ankle height, while the stones hold the day’s heat and give it back after sunset. Mist forms in the low places, but never rises far. At dawn, it rests over the pools in thin, bright layers; by midday it has condensed on every surface, and the place shines. The weather has a small, dependable rhythm: light, condensation, a brief soft rain from the warm upper air, then light again. The rain falls only within the bowl. Beyond the ridges, the surrounding ground can be dry.

Nothing in the Silt Garden grows quickly. A leaf may take a season to unfold, though it will remain for decades. The animals breed in great numbers, but the population does not spill over the ridges. Some boundary is present—perhaps a mineral change in the soil, perhaps an old field woven into the air by the scattered structures. Creatures approaching it turn aside, even when frightened. Water reaches the edge and curls back into the channels.

The hollow feels complete, not sealed. It has no visible entrance except the narrow path between two worn ridges, and no one who follows that path comes upon a dramatic threshold. The ground simply becomes softer; the air becomes warmer; then the water begins to gleam beneath the plants. A little farther on, the ridges are behind you, and the whole world has contracted to this bright, damp bowl.

At evening, the old platform gives off a faint blue light. The hovering beads appear again, one by one, suspended above the water like drops that have forgotten how to fall. The animals settle around them. The predator drinks. The grazer stands with its eyes closed. Under the stones, the ancient mechanism makes its deep, nearly inaudible vibration.

The beads drift slowly toward the center of the hollow. For a few minutes, each holds a different image: the bowl as it is now; the bowl under a higher sea; the bowl before the ridges wore down; the same stones beneath a sky with unfamiliar stars. Then the images go dark. The water receives the beads without a ripple, and the Silt Garden returns to being only a small, old, living place—bright even in the dusk, full of creatures, and quiet enough to hear the age of the stone.
