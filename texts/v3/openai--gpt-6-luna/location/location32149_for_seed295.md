The highland is built all the way to its edges.

Not a city, exactly: a long, folded country of terraces and covered ways, staircases, retaining walls, cistern courts, and steep roofs pressed one above another along a warm mountain basin. There is no bare hillside between them. Every slope has been cut into steps and held in place by old, dark stone. Every hollow has a floor. Water runs in narrow channels worn smooth by centuries, vanishes beneath archways, then appears again in shaded pools. Even the earth is kept in beds behind low walls, where ferns, broad leaves, and woody vines grow thickly from the damp soil.

From a distance the place looks like a mountain wearing a single immense garment: roof over roof, wall over wall, green threaded through the seams. Closer in, it resolves into lanes too narrow for sunlight, courtyards open to the weather, and stairways that climb until they disappear under another level of roofs. The construction is old enough to have softened. Corners have rounded; steps have sunk into shallow bowls; walls lean slightly against the pressure of roots and rain. Nothing seems newly made, and nothing seems close to falling. It has settled into its shape.

The stone stays cool in shadow, but the air is warm and damp. Moisture gathers wherever the day’s heat meets the deep interior chill: on lintels, in hollows between paving stones, along the undersides of bridges. Rain comes intermittently, pattering over the roofs and pouring from carved spouts into tanks. Between rains, the channels are mostly dry. A trickle may persist beneath the walkways, audible but unseen, while aboveground the place feels almost dry enough for dust—until a wall sweats or a mist slips down from the upper terraces.

It is dark here even at midday. The mountain’s folds, the overhanging roofs, and the close-grown vegetation divide the light into brief pale fragments. A courtyard may be bright as a bowl; one turn later, the passage is black. People who know the place could cross it by the touch of the walls: cool stone on the left, a low step, then the rough root of a fig tree breaking through a niche. But there are no lamps burning in the passages, no machinery, no gleaming instruments. The darkness is ordinary darkness, made by shade and depth.

The living things are not kept to gardens. They have filled the construction.

Small birds nest in every ledge and drain-mouth. They pour from the eaves in gusts, return in flocks, and quarrel continuously over the dampest courtyards. Swallows thread the covered lanes so closely that their wings brush the stone. Lizards hold the warm faces of the upper walls. In the cisterns, frogs gather in such numbers that their calls become a dense, rising racket whenever rain refills the pools. Insects cloud the warmer chambers and flicker in the half-light. At dusk, bats stream from roof hollows, quickening the air above the terraces. Larger animals pass too—goats picking their way along the parapets, pigs nosing through the planted beds, dogs asleep across the stairs—yet the small lives are so many that the whole place seems to tremble with them.

The sound is immense. Calls overlap calls; claws skitter on tile; wings beat under bridges; frogs pulse from the cistern courts; water knocks and gurgles through hidden channels. The narrow architecture gathers every noise and sends it along, so that one courtyard seems to contain the whole highland. There are quiet moments, but they are not silent: a brief thinning of the birds, a pause between gusts of wings, the low rush of water continuing beneath the stones.

Nothing here behaves strangely. The animals breed, feed, nest, and migrate according to the season. The plants take root where moisture collects. Rain follows the clouds over the ridge. The extraordinary thing is simply the completeness of the making: this country was shaped, and shaped again, until nearly every surface could be walked, climbed, crossed, planted, or sheltered beneath. The builders are gone, or have become indistinguishable from the ordinary life of the place. Their work remains in the terraces, and the creatures have inherited it.

The highland changes slowly. Roots widen cracks. Silt gathers in the channels. A roof gives way somewhere each year, and a vine takes its place, holding the stones together for a while longer. The terraces do not advance or collapse; they breathe through small adjustments. Rain darkens them, warmth dries them, animals move across them, and the old architecture receives everything without surprise. Beyond the last roofs, the mountain continues upward, but even there the paths and walls climb farther than expected, following the ridge into darkness.
