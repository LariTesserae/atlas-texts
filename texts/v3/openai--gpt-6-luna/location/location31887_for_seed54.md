The city is built on a high shelf of stone that has not yet decided what shape to be.

It lies above the cloudline only in the maps. In practice, cloud pours through its streets, thick and cold, snagging on bridges and towers until the whole settlement seems to be taking slow, shallow breaths. Water beads on every surface. It gathers in seams, runs down walls, drips from cable spans, and stands in broad black sheets over the unfinished ground. Beneath the city, the rock sweats continuously. No one agrees whether this is rain, groundwater, or the plateau making itself.

There are no plants. Nothing roots in the thin gravel or clings to the walls. The exposed stone is gray, blue-black, and new enough in places to shine like cooled metal. Whole slopes end in raw faces where the mountain has split open within living memory. Elsewhere, narrow shelves of rock are still forming: mineral fans grow outward from cracks, slowly laying down pale ridges across the wet ground. The terrain is not ruined so much as incomplete. Channels stop abruptly. Basins have no rims. Some streets have been built across places where the rock will eventually rise.

The city accommodates this without trying to tame it. Its buildings stand on adjustable feet, tall-legged and closely packed, with their weight distributed through deep anchors that shift by fractions of a millimeter as the shelf moves. Walkways pass between them in several layers. Lower spans are often underwater; upper ones disappear into cloud. Transit cables cross the open basins, carrying silent cars through the dark. Some structures are clearly old and heavily repaired, though “old” here means they have survived several generations of geology. Others are still being printed into place: faceted frames extruded from the cliff face, dense clusters of modules that thicken a little each year.

The architecture has been designed to shed water but never quite succeeds. Rain travels in deliberate channels along the undersides of bridges, through transparent pipes, into cisterns suspended above the streets. From there it goes into the city’s heat exchangers, its pressure engines, its drinking systems—or back out through vents as cold vapor. Pipes and gutters overlap until the walls resemble the exposed insides of an enormous instrument. The technology is advanced enough that some of its workings are hard to distinguish from local weather. A bank of dark panels may be a power system, a sensor array, or a machine that persuades the fog to move elsewhere. At intervals the air thickens, and the city’s structures answer with a faint, coordinated hum.

But the city is not the loudest thing here.

The animals occupy every ledge, gap, roof cavity, spillway, and unsealed room. They collect in such numbers that, at a distance, the slopes seem to be moving. Most are pale or dark, depending on where they live: smooth, water-slick bodies in the lower channels; dense mats of small jointed things around warm vents; broad, folded creatures clinging beneath the bridges. Some have shells like wet ceramic. Others are all limbs and feelers, their shapes difficult to follow in the darkness. Few seem to have eyes. The largest animals move slowly along the ridge, their bodies hidden by cloud, while the smaller ones pour around their feet.

Their food is not green. It grows as a chemical film around hot cracks, a colorless abundance too thin to see unless the water catches it at a particular angle. The city’s engineers harvest a portion of it, carefully and at immense scale. The animals take the rest. They scrape it from the stone, filter it from the water, and feed upon one another in ways the local ecology has made efficient over deep time. The city’s relationship with them is less one of ownership than of negotiation. Certain corridors are left open during breeding migrations. Some buildings are sealed with flexible skins that animals can crawl across without entering. The old districts have names for the different kinds of noise that come through their walls, and people learn to tell an alarm from a courtship call.

At night—which is most of the time—the animals make the landscape audible. There is a constant shivering rasp from the masses in the drainage channels. Deep calls pass through the rock with a pressure like distant thunder. In the upper fog, something clicks its jaws in long, measured bursts. The sounds stack on one another until the silence between them seems less like quiet than a pause in a great machine. Human voices are small here. Doors are made thick, rooms are built inward, and public announcements are sent as vibrations through the rails beneath the streets.

Light is scarce by custom as well as circumstance. The cloud is nearly opaque, the sky hidden behind it, and the city keeps its illumination low so the animals will continue to use their ancient paths. Guide strips glow faintly at ankle height. Doorways are marked by dim bands of color; most windows are dark. On very clear nights, if “clear” can be said of this place, the black sky opens just enough to show a scatter of stars above the high rim. The city’s towers then appear as silhouettes within silhouettes, their wet edges briefly silvered. Beneath them, the plateau gives off no reflection.

The place changes slowly, but it is never still. A crack lengthens over decades. A new shelf of stone rises under a district, and the buildings ease apart around it. Water alters the route of a street by patiently undermining its lowest support. Animals migrate through the city in waves that may take months to pass, their bodies pressing against the undersides of bridges until the whole span trembles. In response, machines adjust the city’s balance, shift its anchors, and open or close old passages. Most changes are too gradual to feel in the moment. People notice them in the way they notice a familiar room after returning from a long journey: the door is slightly higher, the air has a different taste, and something large has begun calling from the next valley.

Beyond the densest construction, the city thins into platforms, observation pylons, and half-made districts. Then comes the open plateau: wet stone, dark water, new ridges, and the slow movement of innumerable animals. The built world ends not at a wall but in a diminishing series of agreements—where the walkways stop, where the lights stop, where human maintenance gives way to weather and migration.

From the far side of the basin, the settlement looks less like a city than a reef growing in cold air: layered, crowded, full of passages, with life moving through and over it. It is still being made. So is the ground beneath it. So, in their different patient ways, are the animals, the machines, and the water.
