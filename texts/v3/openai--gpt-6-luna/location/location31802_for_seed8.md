The land lies so low that the horizon seems to rest on it. It is not quite a marsh and not quite a plain, but an old basin in which water has had so long to work that almost every hard edge has been rubbed away. Pale stone shows through the shallows in broad, smooth backs. Here and there it is furrowed into shallow grooves, pocked with little basins, or undercut into low ledges no higher than a person’s knee. Nothing rises sharply. Even the distant ridges have dwindled to a blue-gray suggestion.

Water gathers without seeming to arrive. It seeps from cracks in the stone, beads on the undersides of ledges, and lies in connected sheets across the flats. Narrow channels wander among the pools, too shallow and slow to make much of a current. Their surfaces hold the sky in long, broken reflections. In the morning, warmth gathers over the basin and mist lifts from the water; by midday it has thinned to a faint shimmer close to the ground. After rain, the whole place darkens and gleams. Water fills old hollows, slides over the bare rock, and then remains there—sometimes for days, sometimes for a season, sometimes long enough that the distinction hardly matters.

The warmth is steady, more like a hand laid on the land than a blaze from above. Light is ordinary and generous. Clouds pass, the sun comes through, and the pale surfaces brighten until the pools look almost white. Under ledges and in the deeper water, greens and browns gather in muted layers. There is no dramatic weathering here, no sudden transformation. The basin has been settling into this shape for ages. Its changes are real, but patient: a bank slumps by inches; a pool silts up; a low passage between waters is opened or closed by a season’s rain.

Vegetation is sparse, but it has found its arrangements. Tough reeds stand in scattered margins, not in walls, leaving broad spaces of open water between their clumps. Small, salt-tolerant grasses occupy the slightly raised patches, their roots gripping thin seams of soil. Low mats of slick green growth spread on submerged stone, where the water is shallowest. A few widely spaced shrubs hold to islands of grit. They lean with the prevailing breeze and grow slowly, each one distinct against the emptiness around it. Nothing makes a continuous canopy. The sky remains visible almost everywhere.

The apparent emptiness is misleading. The water is full of life. Small fish thread through the channels in quick flashes, turning together when a shadow crosses them. Snails and little crustaceans cling beneath the shelves of stone. In the shallows, tadpoles gather in dark, quivering clouds, and frogs sit so still among the reeds that they seem at first to be bits of mud. Insects skim and hover above the pools. Their numbers are greatest at dusk, when the air becomes densely audible with wings.

Birds use the basin in loose, shifting flocks. Waders pick through the shallows, probing the soft margins with slender bills; larger birds pass overhead in deliberate lines, calling to one another across distances that make their voices seem to belong to the weather. At certain pools, the water is stippled with feeding birds. Elsewhere, one stands alone on a low rock, patient as the stone. Small mammals move through the drier patches and vanish into grass clumps. Their trails are faint and temporary, soon blurred by dampness.

The place is loud, though little of it moves quickly. Frogs pulse in overlapping choruses. Birds bark, chatter, whistle, and answer across the flats. Insects add a fine, continuous rasp. Water makes a quieter contribution: a soft ticking where drops fall from stone, a whisper where a narrow runnel crosses gravel, the occasional hollow plunk of something breaking the surface. Wind passes low over the pools and combs the reeds, but rarely enough to stir the whole basin at once. The noise gathers and thins in patches. From a distance, all of it merges into a broad living murmur.

There are few shadows and few hiding places. A creature crossing open ground is visible for a long time; a creature resting becomes part of the pattern of stone, water, and grass. The safest places are not enclosed ones, but the small irregularities: a crack under a shelf, a grass-covered hump, a shallow pool whose dark bottom conceals movement. Life here is adapted to exposure. It uses timing, stillness, numbers, and the countless narrow refuges worn into the ancient ground.

Nothing in the basin appears newly made. The stones have lost the memory of sharp fracture. The water does not hurry to the sea, or toward any clear destination; it spreads, gathers, evaporates, returns. The animals’ busiest hours pass over a land that scarcely changes beneath them. A visitor might mistake that slowness for silence or barrenness. Stay long enough, and the basin reveals itself as a settled, enduring system: wet, warm, open, and full of voices, with life scattered across an immense surface of old, smoothed earth.
