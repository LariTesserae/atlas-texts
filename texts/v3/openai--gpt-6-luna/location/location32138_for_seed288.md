The city lies beneath a plain so old that the land above has almost forgotten how to be uneven. From below, it is a continent of shallow roofs: a low ceiling of stone, pressed smooth by ages of settling, with long cracks where the roots come down. There are no shafts of sunlight, only a dim, mineral-colored glow gathered in the pale surfaces of the rock. Distance fades into gray-green haze. The air is cool and dry enough to tighten the throat.

Nothing here runs. No water crosses the floor, gathers in hollows, or taps from the ceiling. Moisture is locked in the stone and in the bodies of living things, released sparingly and taken up again. The ground is powdery in some places, hard as old bone in others. Underfoot lie chips of limestone, shed husks, seed cases, and the fine dust made by centuries of feet.

Yet the place is not barren. Pale mosses and tough, low plants spread wherever roots reach the ceiling’s buried soil. Their leaves are small and thick, gray-green or nearly black, and they grow in broad, soft mats over ledges and old walls. In the deeper lanes, pale fungi rise in close groves, their dry caps layered like roof tiles. They do not shine. They only catch the little light there is and return it as a faint, dull silver. The richest growth gathers around cracks in the rock, where the stone holds a little more of what the plants need. Everywhere else, life keeps a careful distance from life.

The settlement has no single builder and no beginning that can be found. Its streets are corridors worn into the stone, wide enough for the passage of many bodies, their bends softened by generations of traffic. Rooms open off them in clusters: feeding chambers, nesting hollows, long communal vaults where the ceiling rises and the sound can swell. Some walls are natural; others are packed from rubble, clayless dust, and the hard, resinous residue of roots. The work is patient and repetitive. A passage is widened by a little, then a little more. A roof is braced with stems and stripped branches. A chamber is abandoned when it grows too dusty, and another is begun nearby. The city grows by habit, not design.

There are no machines, no lamps, no cut metal. The inhabitants carry nothing that would last as a tool. They move stones with their bodies, rake loose grit aside, and shape the softest walls with teeth, claws, and the pressure of many repeated journeys. Their construction is not crude. It is simply slow enough to have become part of the terrain. A visitor might take the long, orderly lanes for the remains of a planned city, though no one alive remembers a plan.

The inhabitants are everywhere, though rarely gathered in a single place. Small pale mammals thread through the wall-roots. Beetles pack the seams between stones. Long-legged creatures pick their way over the ceiling and disappear into its cracks. There are birds, too, ground-nesting and nearly silent as individuals, with blunt wings that beat the dust from the air when a whole flock rises. Their eyes are adapted to the dimness; their coats are the color of chalk, bark, or old soil.

The sound is immense. It fills the chambers until there seems to be no room left for silence. Clicking, scraping, trilling, shuffling, and the rasp of wings pass from vault to vault, multiplied by stone. A small animal’s call can travel through an entire district. In the largest hollows, the voices of many species merge into a steady, harsh roar, like wind trapped inside a hollow hill. When a corridor empties, the quiet does not arrive all at once. It recedes by degrees, leaving the softer sounds behind: claws on grit, leaves rubbing together, the dry tick of an insect working its way across a wall.

The city changes almost too slowly to witness. A ceiling sags by the thickness of a root each century. A corridor fills with dust, then is opened again by the traffic of generations. Old chambers become gardens; old gardens become packed earth; the next community builds beside them, using their walls without knowing who made them. Nothing is ruined in a hurry. The place is not a relic so much as an old arrangement that life continues to inhabit.

Its scale is broad but not endless: a region of connected districts, scattered groves, worn avenues, and quiet expanses of untouched stone. Between settled areas lie stretches where the floor is nearly level and the ceiling low, with only a few roots dangling like cords. These empty tracts make the inhabited chambers seem more populous, and the city more deliberate. A traveler can walk for hours through corridors whose builders have vanished, hearing the living city far away before seeing it.

There is no grand center. Instead, the settlement gathers around what little sustains it: rootfall, fungus beds, sheltered nesting vaults, and the cooler cracks where buried life persists. The oldest lanes lead nowhere in particular. They are simply the ways that proved easiest, worn smooth before the present inhabitants were born and kept open because so many feet still follow them.

Here, civilization is not a departure from nature. It is one of nature’s older habits: a network of passages, shelters, and shared grounds, made by creatures that do not imagine themselves making a city. Above them, the plain remains almost flat. Below it, the stone holds its dimness, its dry air, its crowded noise—and the slow, green persistence of things that have learned to live without water in sight.
