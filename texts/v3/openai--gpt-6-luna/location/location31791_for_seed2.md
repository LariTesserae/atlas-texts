The first impression is of open water, not a city.

Below the surface, a pale blue plain runs so far in every direction that its far edge is lost in haze. The light is ordinary daylight, though softened and shifted by the depth: bright enough to show distance, cool enough to make every color seem rinsed clean. Sunlight moves overhead in broad, wavering panels. When clouds pass above, the whole landscape dims at once.

The plain is built. Its floor is a continuous skin of gray-white ceramic and poured mineral composite, laid in immense plates. Seams cross it in ruler-straight lines, disappearing into the blue; here and there they widen into deep channels, sluices, and trenchlike service ways. The material is still young. Corners remain crisp. Cut surfaces have not yet softened, and the occasional broken edge shows a clean, granular interior. There is little sediment, no old earth beneath the feet of the water—only more engineered foundation, descending beyond sight.

This is a vast managed basin, or a portion of one so large that it has its own weather. Currents turn slowly across the plain. In certain places they gather into steady rivers of water, guided between low walls and under the arching mouths of conduits. Far off, the basin floor rises into long ramps and terraces, then falls away again. Sparse structures mark the distances: intake towers with broad collars, skeletal gantries, rows of buoyant pylons anchored to the bottom. They are spaced widely apart. Between one and the next there may be kilometers of empty water.

The emptiness is not stillness. Flocks of fish pass in shifting sheets, bright sides flashing and vanishing as they wheel. Larger animals keep to the open channels: blunt-headed, powerful swimmers that travel in loose groups, sometimes turning together at a distant signal of motion. Small fish shelter in the narrow shadows beneath service ledges and around the bases of the towers. Their abundance gives the water a fine, constant grain of movement. It does not make the place feel crowded. There is simply a great deal of water, and life distributed through it.

Vegetation is scarce, planted rather than rooted. Short fields of kelp-like growth are tethered to rails along a few sheltered channels, their dark fronds held flat by the current. Elsewhere, a few strings of cultivation hang from floating frames far above the floor. Nothing spreads freely. The plants are harvested, trimmed, and kept within their allotted boundaries; beyond them, the clean built surfaces remain bare.

The machinery is loud. The sound travels through the water as pressure more than noise: a low, continuous engine-note, punctuated by the hard knocks of gates closing and the long shudder of pumps starting. Near an intake, the current carries a rolling metallic roar. Farther away, those sounds flatten into a broad hum that seems to come from the basin itself. Fish hear it and alter their routes around the working structures. On the floor, small maintenance vehicles crawl along marked tracks, their lamps pale in the daylight.

Now and then, a cycle passes through the landscape. Pumps change pitch. A line of gates opens, and the water begins to move with greater purpose down one of the channels. Suspended particles stream past in thin veils. The light ripples over the blank plates, over the pylons, over the moving backs of fish. Then the machinery settles into its regular pulse, and the basin returns to its immense, measured quiet.

There is no ancient wildness here, no ruin concealed beneath layers of mud. The place is too new for that. Its edges are sharp; its systems are plainly at work; its animals are alive within a world that has been designed to hold them. Yet at this scale, design becomes difficult to distinguish from geography. The channels are rivers. The pressure gates are passes. The endless, fabricated floor is the seabed.

Above it all lies the bright surface, trembling and remote. Below it, the constructed ocean continues.
