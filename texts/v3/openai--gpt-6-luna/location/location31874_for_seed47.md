The high country has been worn almost smooth.

It rises so far above the lowlands that the air thins to a clear, cold medium, and distance loses its usual measures. From one ridge, the next may look close enough to walk to in an hour; the walk can take three days. The horizon is not a line so much as a pale curvature, interrupted by the occasional summit—broad, rounded, and older-looking than mountains have any right to be. Their flanks descend in immense, shallow folds. Here and there the stone is cut through by chasms so deep that their floors never show, only layers of blue-grey darkening to black.

There is no water. No rivers have left their channels, no lakes their basins. The old valleys are filled with powder-fine mineral dust, sifted into drifts that look like snow until the wind lifts them. In places, exposed salt and mica catch the light in flat, subdued flashes. Most of the ground is a hard, dull stone: ash-coloured, iron-brown, or the grey of a blade left out for centuries. It has been smoothed by erosion until every edge seems considered. Even the cliffs are not jagged so much as immense and tired, their faces furred with ledges and shallow scallops.

The land is ancient. It has reached a shape it should have kept.

But it does not keep it.

Across the high shelf, whole regions shift by fractions of a degree. The change is too slow to see, then suddenly obvious: a ridge that was a horizon yesterday lies folded beneath itself; a basin has tilted and poured its dust in a long, pale spill; a plain has opened a hairline crack that runs farther than sight. The crust is restless at scales too large for ordinary weather. Stress travels through it in broad, invisible waves. Some days the ground trembles once, gently, as if a giant had turned in sleep. Other days distant escarpments rise and collapse in silence, raising a veil of mineral grit that takes weeks to cross the sky.

The storms are made of pressure, dust, and gravity. They gather without clouds. A light wind combs the plains, then stops; later, a whole horizon of dust advances at walking pace, lifting the smallest stones and leaving the larger ones untouched. On the edges of these storms, loose material moves uphill. It pours up slopes in branching streams, hangs briefly against the wind, and settles in new places. The thin atmosphere carries little sound. The largest movements arrive as a faint tremor through the soles, or as a change in the shadows.

Light is ordinary here, and for that reason it can seem strange. The sun is a plain white disc. It casts clean shadows beneath the few standing objects, while the enormous distances remain washed in a cool, metallic haze. At midday, the land is almost colourless. Toward evening, the dust above the far ridges turns the light the colour of old bone. Night is very dark. Stars hang with uncommon sharpness over the high country, and sometimes a thin band of light passes between them—not an aurora, not a satellite, but a slow distortion in the sky, as if something immense has briefly altered the depth of the dark.

Life is scarce and unobtrusive. Low mineral growths cling to protected cracks, dark mats no larger than a handspan, drawing what they need from light and trace compounds in the stone. They survive for centuries, then vanish in a single storm. Small pale animals are sometimes seen at the mouths of fissures: six-legged, nearly transparent, so still that they may be mistaken for fragments of crystal. They leave no obvious tracks. Farther out, the occasional larger creature moves along the lee of a ridge, keeping to the shallow shadows. Nobody sees them often enough to agree on their shape.

The structures are rarer than the life. A black tower stands alone on a saddle between two uplands, narrow as a needle and without a visible entrance. It has no weathering, though it may be older than the surrounding mountains. Elsewhere, rings of pale material lie half buried in the dust. They are too precise to be natural and too worn to be recent. Some are anchors, according to the few who still come here: devices set into the crust to distribute strain. Their surfaces do not conduct heat, and their seams are finer than any tool could cut. A few remain active. Near them, the ground is quieter—not still, but less likely to break in sudden and wasteful ways.

These machines do not tame the country. They give its violence somewhere to go.

There are long intervals in which nothing appears to happen. Dust settles. Shadows cross the stone. The ancient slopes hold their shape. Then, with no warning that a human body can recognize, the highlands rearrange themselves. A plain buckles. A cliff pours down in a silent sheet. The wind reverses across thousands of miles, and every loose grain travels toward the same unseen destination.

Afterward, the land looks old again. It smooths its scars. It settles into broad, finished forms. The empty distances return.

To stand here is to feel that the world has almost ended—not in fire or ruin, but by spending its shape down to its simplest expression. And still, beneath that near-final calm, the whole country is in motion.
