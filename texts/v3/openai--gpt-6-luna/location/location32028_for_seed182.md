The district fits inside a single hollow in the earth, though it has been built upward until there is hardly any hollow left. Its ceiling is low and uneven: a fresh roof of black stone, newly shouldered away from the deeper rock, with sharp shelves and pale seams that still look fractured rather than worn. Heat gathers beneath it. Water does not fall so much as arrive in fits—beads on the ceiling, a brief spill from a crack, a silver thread running down a wall and vanishing into a drain.

There are perhaps four streets, if the narrow passages between buildings deserve the name. They twist around a central cistern and climb over one another on iron steps, brick arches, and little bridges of bolted plate. Houses are packed so close that their walls share warmth. Pipes run outside them in tangled bundles: hot-water lines, condensate gutters, cables wrapped in tarred cloth. Some pipes knock once in a long while, then settle. The oldest engines are kept in low rooms beneath the street, where flywheels turn at a measured pace and pumps draw moisture from the stone. Their work is more felt than heard—a slight shiver in a railing, a tremble in a hanging cup.

The light is always late. It comes from hooded lamps, from orange furnace doors left barely ajar, and from greenish growths that cling to damp mortar. The plants have taken every ledge no one could use: broad leaves in the gutters, pale stems threaded through roof cracks, mats of soft moss around the cistern’s lip. Their roots push into new masonry without haste. In places the city seems to have been built around a garden; elsewhere, the garden has quietly entered the city and made itself at home. The leaves are warm to the touch. When the air grows wetter, they bead with condensation and release a smell like crushed herbs, hot dust, and rain that never reached the surface.

Water is precious here, but not scarce enough to be precious in the usual way. It collects in shallow pans beneath the walls, in the hollows of stair treads, in the dark between floorstones. The cistern is fed by a fissure that runs intermittently: days of dryness, then a thin trickle that lasts an hour, then silence again. The water tastes faintly of iron and has a skin of mineral color where it stands. People have built their habits around these small returns. Gutters are angled with care; basins are nested beneath drips; no one wastes a leaking pipe until it has truly stopped.

The district is too full for much animal life. Now and then a white, many-legged thing moves through the drains, and once in a season a blind moth appears around the lamps, beating softly against the glass. Most movement belongs to leaves, vapors, and the slow turning of machinery. Even the air seems crowded: warm and close, carrying smoke, damp stone, oil, and the green breath of plants. Sound does not travel far. A conversation disappears around the next corner. A tool dropped on a metal stair gives one small, clear note that hangs in the passages longer than seems reasonable.

Nothing here changes quickly. The stone is young, but its making is patient: a crack extends by the width of a hair; a mineral rind grows over a bolt; a new shelf of rock presses out from the wall and waits. Buildings are repaired against these slow intrusions rather than cleared away. A stair may end in a fresh black bulge of stone. A doorway may narrow over several years until someone cuts it wider. The inhabitants mark these shifts with chalk lines, and the lines remain for a long time.

There is one peculiarity that no one agrees how to explain. At certain hours, when the cistern is full and the pumps have stopped, every damp surface briefly reflects a little more light than it should. The reflected passages do not quite match the real ones. They show an extra landing, a corridor continuing behind a wall, or a small patch of open ground where there is only brick. The images last just long enough to be noticed, then go dark as the water settles.

The district is bounded by the rock on every side. It has no horizon, only a succession of near things: a warm wall, a dripping arch, a tangle of leaves, a lamp in its iron cage. It is a town reduced to the size of a room and grown dense enough to fill it—quietly industrious, damp at the edges, and still being formed.
