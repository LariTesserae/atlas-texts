The low country is all made.

There is no exposed earth beneath it: no mud, no gravel, no buried root-mat. The ground is a continuous work of hands—broad fired-clay plates, pale lime plaster, woven reed panels sealed with pitch, and blocks of black riverstone fitted so tightly that even the smallest seams seem deliberate. It extends farther than a day’s walk, a level of roofs and platforms and shallow basins, with occasional rises no taller than a house. Everything has the crispness of something recently assembled. Corners are still sharp. Walls have not slumped. Stairs descend to water that seems not to have had time to wear them.

The builders are nowhere to be seen. Their methods were simple: mallets, wooden levers, baskets, kilns, cord, and an astonishing patience. A whole district may be made from one repeating unit, yet no two units quite match. Clay bears the thumbprints of its makers. Reeds are bound in bundles so thick they resemble timber. Stone has been split along straight, pale scars. The structures are not ruins, though they have the stillness of ruins; nor are they inhabited in any ordinary sense. Doorways open onto empty rooms whose floors are bright with standing water. Roofs shelter only sunlight.

Water lies everywhere, but never deep. It fills the long channels between platforms and shines in shallow courtyards, held in low troughs and saucer-shaped basins. Here and there it weeps from the joints of walls, clear as glass, without stain or mineral trace. The surfaces are so still that reflections often seem more substantial than the things reflected. A roofline in the water may continue downward farther than the building itself, repeating in a dim, inverted country below the tiles.

Heat presses down from a white, relentless sky. The air is wet enough to bead on the plaster and gather in the hollow of every stone. Sunlight scatters from the water, the lime, the pale ceramic roofs; there are few shadows, and those that do form are thin and strangely cool. The horizon is washed out. Across the open expanse, small groves rise from constructed islands, their canopies a dense, dark green against the glare.

The vegetation grows with extravagant health, but it has no natural ground to claim. Roots thread through cracks in brick and coil around drain-spouts. Broad leaves emerge from jars set into walls; vines cross courtyards on purpose-built trellises, then continue over them as if trellises were simply the first part of a longer stem. Trees grow from shallow masonry beds, their roots visible beneath translucent floors, pale and branching like hands suspended in water. Flowers open in drains. Moss makes green cushions on steps that have never been trodden smooth.

Everything living seems well watered. Frogs crowd the cistern mouths. Long-legged wading birds stand on roof ridges and watch the pools without blinking. Small fish gather in the channels, including some that swim through the reflections rather than the water; they pass beneath the image of a bridge, though the bridge’s shadow does not touch them. Insects hover in the heat, but their wings make no audible movement. A large, soft-shelled animal—perhaps a turtle, perhaps a seedpod that has learned to walk—rests beneath one of the groves. Its patterned back is covered in tiny flowering plants. Nothing flees. Nothing appears hungry.

The place is abundant without feeling crowded. Groves and animal clusters are separated by long, bare reaches of bright paving. A flock may occupy one courtyard and leave the next utterly empty. The silence is not complete, but sound travels poorly here. There is a faint ticking from water in the channels, a hushed brush of leaves, an occasional hollow note from somewhere inside the walls. Sometimes a bird opens its beak and a sound arrives several moments later from a different direction. Mostly the landscape holds its breath.

Its oddities are modest at first. A narrow canal runs uphill for several hundred paces, its surface perfectly level. A doorway, viewed from one side, is too small for a person; seen from the other, it frames a sunlit court large enough to contain the horizon. In certain pools, reflections show clouds that are not overhead. Once in a while, a reed bends toward a passerby before the passerby has moved. The strangeness is not violent. It has settled into the place like another kind of weather.

Nothing seems to alter. Water does not evaporate in the heat, nor does it rise with the rain. Leaves remain fresh, neither growing larger nor browning at the edges. The bright sky holds no visible sun, only a fixed, even radiance. No crack widens. No wall collapses. The pools keep their exact outlines; the birds return to the same ledges; the same beads of moisture cling to the same seams in the masonry.

At the far edge of the constructed country, the terraces end abruptly in a low parapet. Beyond it is a blank brightness that may be sea, mist, or simply more of the same world waiting to be built. The final channel pours toward that brightness without spilling. Its water reaches the lip, turns into a flawless sheet, and hangs there—neither falling nor retreating—while the quiet green country behind it remains unchanged.
