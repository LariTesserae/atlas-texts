The city lies beneath a low country of black spruce and sedge, in a cavern so broad that its far wall fades into the blue-grey air. The roof has fallen in several places—not into neat shafts, but in raw, fresh-edged wounds. Daylight pours through them in pale columns, catches on suspended moisture, and settles over the basin with the quality of an overcast afternoon. Beyond the openings there is only a glimpse of the weather above: a moving scrap of cloud, the dark comb of treetops, once in a while a bird crossing the light.

Everything here is wet. Water threads down the rock in shining seams, beads on ledges, and gathers in hollows before spilling from one terrace to the next. The pools are clear at their edges and ink-dark toward their centers. They reflect the roof, though the reflection trembles constantly with falling drops. In some places the water stands just under the soil; the ground yields slightly beneath a foot, and cold seeps through moss and packed earth. In others, narrow streams have cut channels through the new stone, their banks still raw and angular, strewn with pale chips.

The cavern is young enough that its shapes have not yet softened. Broken shelves jut from the walls. Fresh slopes of rubble descend from the roof-falls. Here and there, a pale mineral crust outlines the path of water that has only recently found its way down. But life has already taken possession of every surface it can reach. Ferns uncurl from cracks. Moss thickens over stone steps and roofs. Low trees grow in the larger patches of soil, their branches crowded with glossy leaves, bent toward the daylight wells. Lianas and rootlets lace the old fractures in the ceiling. In the deepest reaches, where light thins but does not quite fail, the vegetation becomes smaller and darker: mats of liverwort, pale grasses, and tiny flowering plants that open only when the cavern brightens.

There is almost no bare ground. The city is packed into the basin wherever stone can hold a foundation. Its houses are built close together, with shared walls of fitted rock and dark timber taken from the buried forest that grew before the roof collapsed. Their roofs overlap like scales, shaggy with moss and sprouting little gardens. Narrow lanes bend around pools, butt against ledges, climb stairways cut directly into the cavern’s sides, then pass beneath low arches where water drips steadily onto the paving. Whole neighborhoods occupy natural terraces. Others cling to the walls in stacked rows, connected by bridges of wood and stone. From far away, the settlement resembles a second growth of the cavern: roofs like shelves of fungus, windows sunk into rock, living green filling every gap.

There are no engines, lamps, or humming wires. After dusk—or when cloud covers the openings—the city simply grows dim. People know the daylight by its arrival at particular walls and pools; they tend their plots accordingly, and move their gatherings toward whatever terrace is brightest. The city’s order is old and practical: channels are kept open, steps are shared, and the lowest houses are built to accept water when the pools rise. Rain above can swell the cavern in a few hours. Then the streams quicken, the drains sing, and residents lift baskets, stools, and sleeping mats onto higher floors. No one tries to stop the water. The city has been laid out to make room for it.

The inhabitants are not alone. Small pale fish hold in the clear channels. Newts, broad-headed and still as carved stone, rest beneath dripping steps. Bats roost in the high fractures and pass through the light wells at twilight, nearly soundless. There are birds too, drawn in through the roof-falls: they nest in the tall growth and disappear into the darker reaches of the cavern. Insects gather around the damp gardens, and the occasional fox slips through the outer lanes, thin and careful, following the smell of rodents. The people accept these neighbors as part of the place. Food scraps go to the water, the gardens attract what they attract, and even the houses have small openings left for the creatures that lived here first.

Sound does not travel as it ought to in such a crowded place. Water is everywhere, but its voice is subdued: a steady ticking from the roof, a hush in the channels, a low rush from the deepest pools. Footsteps soften on moss. Voices seem to stay close to the speaker, absorbed by wet stone and leaves. At noon, when daylight fills the basin, the city can be nearly silent. Then a roof-drop strikes a hollow pot; somewhere a child calls; a bird answers from high above, and the sound vanishes into the wide ceiling.

The air is cold, though not cruel. It smells of wet stone, crushed leaves, and wood that never fully dries. Mist gathers over the largest pools and lifts in thin veils when the light reaches them. Nothing here feels finished. The rock is still breaking loose from the ceiling; streams are still choosing their beds; roots are still finding cracks in the city walls. The settlement has been built not against that change, but within it—dense enough to fill the cavern, open enough to leave the water its routes, and patient enough to let the young landscape keep becoming.
