The basin is almost level, though nothing in it quite agrees on what level means.

It opens wider than a town should: streets, canals, terraces, and rooflines spread across a low plain until they thin into dark water and heat haze. Beyond them, the land remains visible for a long way, a rim of low hills softened by vapor. The place is hot at every hour. Heat rises from the ground as if the basin were still cooling from some ancient fire, and the air holds so much water that distant buildings look half-submerged even when they stand on dry stone.

There is no clear sun. The sky is a dense, unlit gray-black, brightest near the horizon and utterly without stars. Darkness settles into the streets, but it is not cool darkness: walls sweat, paving stones give off warmth, and the canals carry a faint sheen that seems to come from nowhere. Water gathers along lintels and under bridges, beads on the undersides of arches, and falls upward in slow, wavering threads. Rain comes often, though sometimes it rises out of the canals first and patters against the clouds.

The city is old enough to have stopped looking newly made. Its buildings are broad, low, and crowded in districts, but each district has breathing space around it: a flooded square, a stretch of bare paving, an empty plot filled only with silt. Pale stone, dark brick, and black volcanic rock have been patched together over generations. Their edges are worn smooth. Corners have slumped; foundations show where water has worried away the ground. The architecture feels practical and repeated—stairs, courtyards, drainage cuts, covered walks—until it doesn’t. A street may pass beneath its own roof. A stairwell may descend into a room that is plainly above it. Some façades have rows of windows looking onto other façades at a distance of only a handspan, with darkness in the gap between them.

There are no machines, no lamps, no cables or engines. The city’s order is made of stonework and channels. Water is directed through gutters and basins, but it does not always obey the routes laid for it. It pools on roofs while the streets below remain dry; it runs downhill into high places; it disappears beneath a bridge and emerges from a wall several blocks away. The channels are still maintained—not by anyone visible, but by the stubborn continuation of their shape. They have been used for so long that the water seems to remember them.

Most of the ground is bare. Here and there, sparse growth has taken hold in cracks: a few wiry reeds with pale, translucent stems; mats of dark moss that feel warm to the touch; small, waxy leaves growing singly from the joints between stones. Nothing flowers. Nothing rustles. There are no birds on the roofs, no insects in the damp, no fish turning beneath the canal surfaces. Even the scattered plants seem to make no sound. Their presence is less an ecology than a set of exceptions—life persisting in the spaces where the city’s rules have worn thin.

The quiet is not complete. Water ticks from eaves into courtyards. Far away, a long, soft rush moves through the drainage system, though no channel appears to be filling. Now and then a building gives a muted creak, not from settling but from the sound of its own proportions shifting: a low groan that seems to come from the whole block at once. Footsteps, if there are any, carry strangely. A sound made in one square may arrive in another before it reaches the nearest wall.

The basin changes without seeming to move. After rain, the waterline rises along the sides of buildings but leaves the doorways untouched. In the driest interval, a thin film of water appears on every horizontal surface. Some streets become longer in darkness; others shorten, bringing distant courtyards abruptly close. The effect is not a grand collapse of the world. It is more like a city whose habits have outlasted the geometry that once supported them.

At the edge of town, construction thins into isolated walls and platforms standing in shallow water. Past those, the plain stretches on under the same dim sky. The hills remain low and remote, and the warm wet air keeps their distance uncertain. From here the city looks settled, almost peaceful: a wide, dark arrangement of stone, water, and empty space. Yet its canals continue their quiet circuits, the walls continue to bead with moisture, and somewhere in the basin rain is falling upward.
