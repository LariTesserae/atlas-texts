The place is small enough to cross in an hour, if you know where the crossings are. It occupies a notch in the crown of a very high mountain: a tilted shelf of dark stone, cupped against a sheer wall on one side and falling away on the other into cloud. The shelf is no wider than a hamlet. Beyond its edges, the world becomes distance.

Water is everywhere, but never in great quantity. It threads down the rock in silver seams, gathers in thumb-deep basins, and spills from ledges in narrow curtains that vanish before they reach the unseen slopes below. Rain comes often and lightly, more a persistence than a storm. Even between showers the air beads on eyelashes and sleeves. Moss makes soft green maps across the stone, while small, tough plants hold themselves in cracks: starry flowers, red-stemmed grasses, cushions of pale lichen. They grow close to the ground, bent by wind and cold, but the shelf is lush for its size. Every warm patch of soil seems to have been found and occupied.

The elevation announces itself less through grandeur than through absence. There are no distant trees to soften the horizon, no roofs, no roads, no lower slopes in view—only cloud moving through the chasm, opening now and then onto a long fall of cliffs, then closing again. On clear moments, the far peaks appear like islands in a white sea. They seem close enough to touch and impossibly far away. A person standing at the edge can hear water dropping for a long time before it reaches anything.

The stone is old and settled. Its ledges have been rounded by centuries of freezing, thawing, and rain; nothing here looks newly broken. Yet the mountain is not still. Water ticks and gurgles under thin skins of ice. Wind combs the grasses, changes its note as it passes through cracks, and sometimes comes up from below with a deep, hollow moan. On clear mornings the whole shelf rings with falling droplets. During rain, the sound grows broad and loud: hundreds of little impacts on stone, punctuated by the steady roar of a waterfall hidden just beyond the wall.

For all its emptiness, the place is alive. Small mountain birds wheel out of the cloud in bursts, their wings beating hard in the thin air. They nest in holes in the cliff, where scraps of grass and feather protrude from the dark. Their cries are sharp and insistent, often heard before the birds appear. Insects shelter under stones and among the moss; on the rare sunlit hours, they lift in glittering swarms above the shallow pools. A pale fox—or something fox-shaped, lean and high-stepping—keeps to the outer rim. Its tracks appear in wet grit and disappear where the rock turns sheer. The animals do not crowd one another. Each has a route, a perch, a pocket of shelter, and a great deal of open air between.

A handful of human marks occupy the safest part of the shelf. There is a low stone shelter with a roof of overlapping slabs, built against the cliff to escape the worst wind. Its entrance faces away from the drop. Nearby, a narrow channel has been cut by hand to lead seepage into a basin. The channel is old, repaired in places with fitted stones and in others left to the patient work of water. A few wooden pegs, blackened by weather, remain in cracks along the wall. No metal gleams here; tools are simple, and the work is careful. Whoever uses the shelter understands that the mountain permits little waste.

Nothing in the place feels enchanted, exactly. Still, its rules are strict. Sound carries strangely: a whispered word beside the shelter may vanish, while a stone knocked loose far below can seem to strike beside your ear. Clouds can rise without warning and erase the edge of the shelf. Wet stone is slickest where it looks dry. The birds go silent shortly before the wind changes. And when the cloud thins, the sun does not warm the land so much as reveal how cold it has been all along.

At evening, the shelf becomes a small dark room suspended above the world. Water keeps moving through its channels. Birds settle into the cliff. The shelter holds a little warmth, the moss gives off the smell of rain and crushed leaves, and below everything the unseen falls continue their long, steady descent.
