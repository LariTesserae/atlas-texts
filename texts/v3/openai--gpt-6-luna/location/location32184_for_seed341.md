The district lies low enough that the water never quite leaves.

It occupies a shallow basin between old embankments, no larger than a few city blocks. From above it might look like a neglected square of town, but there is no clean edge between street and wetland. Canal water seeps through brickwork. Moss and broad-leafed plants grow from the joints of stairways. The paving has settled into long, shallow bowls, each holding a skin of rainwater that reflects the lamps as blurred amber smears. Even in dry weather, the walls sweat.

The heat is close and persistent. It rises from the ground, from the canal, from warm pipes laid beneath the streets. Mist gathers under bridges and in the narrow gaps between buildings, not enough to conceal the place, only to soften its distances. The upper windows vanish into a low grayness. By midday the light is dim and greenish, filtered through cloud, steam, and the heavy foliage that has taken the roofs. At night, a few industrial lamps burn behind caged glass. Their glow travels only a short way before the wet air swallows it.

The buildings are old, practical, and settled into their dampness. Brick workshops stand beside concrete pump houses, their lower stories darkened by years of water. Some have been abandoned; others still operate, though it is hard to tell which from outside. Pipes cross the alleys at several heights, patched and repatched, beaded with condensation. Narrow metal walkways link upper doors. Their railings are furred with green. A tram line survives on one street, but its rails are mostly submerged, and every so often a maintenance car passes through the water with a grinding, deliberate clatter.

There is a small, orderly infrastructure beneath the disrepair. Water moves through sluices, gutters, cisterns, and buried channels. The pumps do not drain the district so much as keep its water circulating. At intervals, a bell sounds from some unseen control room; then a gate shifts, a pipe bucks, and a fresh surge goes whispering under the streets. Water is always on its way somewhere. It laps in cellar mouths. It ticks from leaves onto sheet metal. It threads down stairwells that lead to doors no one uses.

Vegetation has found every stable surface. Ferns crowd the cracks around drainage grates. Vines descend from rooftops and gather in the loops of abandoned cables. Trees grow in courtyards where the paving has broken, their trunks bent toward pale openings in the fog. Their leaves are large, waxy, and dark, and they hold the damp in trembling droplets. The growth is lush but not crowded: there are bare stretches of slick stone between green masses, whole walls left empty, alleys where the plants have stopped at the threshold as if respecting a line.

There are no animals. No birds pick through the runoff, no insects stipple the lamps, no movement in the water except the movement of the water itself. The district’s abundance is entirely vegetable and mechanical. Leaves unfold; roots widen cracks; pumps turn; metal cools and contracts. These are the local forms of life and change. The absence of animals makes every sound seem more deliberate.

And it is loud. Fans pulse behind louvered walls. Engines start and idle somewhere below street level. Water rushes through a channel beneath a grating, then dwindles to a thin, uneven hiss. A loose sign knocks against its bracket. Deep in the workshops, machinery beats at different tempos, never quite falling into rhythm. The sounds overlap until they resemble weather. In the quiet between pump cycles, the whole district seems to hold its breath—then a valve opens and the noise returns.

Nothing here appears newly ruined. The edges are worn smooth; the structures have had time to become what they are. The streets do not fight the water anymore. They guide it, imperfectly. The plants do not conquer the buildings so much as occupy the parts the buildings cannot keep dry. Even the machinery seems to belong to the landscape, like a second kind of stone: noisy, warm, and necessary.

The district’s rule is simple, though it takes a while to notice it: water may pass through anything, but nothing passes out unchanged. Brick darkens. Iron blooms orange. Concrete grows soft-edged with mineral stain. Green things root where the damp pauses. The pumps can delay the basin’s filling, but they cannot make it dry. So the town persists in a suspended condition—neither drowned nor reclaimed, neither abandoned nor fully inhabited—turning over its water and its heat, block by block, beneath the low light.
