The city stands on a high shelf of black stone, above a country that is mostly cloud.

From the rim, the land seems to fall away in broad terraces: pale mineral flats, drowned basins, and long ridges softened by ages of rain. The air is hot enough to blur the horizon. Water gathers everywhere, though rarely in rivers. It beads on ledges, slips from roofs, seeps through the stone in silver threads. In the lower hollows it lies ankle-deep over smooth white ground, reflecting the bright sky so perfectly that the pools look like openings cut into the world. By afternoon, the water lifts again as vapor, and the whole shelf breathes.

Nothing grows here. No grasses, no scrub, no moss darkening the walls. The bare rock has colors enough in its place: chalk, rust, greenish glass, the bruised blue of wet slate. In the warmest places, the mineral crusts form fine branching patterns, like the memory of roots. They are not alive. They simply precipitate from the water, layer by layer, then crack and flake when the sun dries them.

Yet the place is full of animals.

They move in the shallows and through the vapor, most of them pale or translucent, their bodies adapted to a world without cover. Flat, many-legged skimmers drift over the pools. Long-necked things stand perfectly still on the exposed flats, drawing moisture through slits along their throats. At night—when the heat eases but never quite leaves—small bright creatures climb the city walls in slow, orderly streams, feeding on salt and the residue of warm machinery. Larger animals pass between the basins in the distance, their shapes visible through the haze: heavy backs, narrow heads, the occasional lifted fan of membranes. They make almost no sound. The inhabitants say the beasts are not silent, exactly; they hear one another through pressure, through the water underfoot, through vibrations too low to reach the ear.

The city is built to meet the wetness rather than keep it out. Its blocks of dark, glazed stone sit on raised piers. Channels run openly beneath the streets, carrying seepage toward cisterns and heatworks. The roofs are shallow and broad, each one tilted to guide condensation into pipes. Between buildings, long covered walks cross the pools, and narrow bridges lead to outlying pumps, towers, and settling yards. There is space between everything. Whole squares stand empty except for a single machine or a pool, bright and still beneath the high sun.

Industry here is quiet by design. The factories do not roar; they turn slowly, wrapped in thick mineral insulation. Their great flywheels move behind walls. Their engines draw heat from the rock and use it to separate clean water from the salt-laden seepage. Now and then, a pressure valve sighs, or a chain ticks once as it settles. The loudest regular sound is the soft tapping of droplets in the drain channels, though even that disappears when the wind drops.

The wind seldom rises. When it does, it travels low across the open shelf and combs the vapor into long, level sheets. The light changes then—not dimmer, but stranger. The city’s edges blur. Pools lose their reflections and become flat white surfaces. Animals disappear into the haze, leaving only the faint movements of ripples where they have passed.

The shelf has been weathering for a very long time. Its cliffs are neither sharp nor collapsing, but rounded in deliberate-looking steps, as if the stone had been shaped by a patient hand. Rain has worn shallow bowls into the high ground. Water has polished the paths between them. The city follows these old channels, bends around the basins, and leaves the tallest natural ridges bare. From above, its streets resemble another system of runoff: measured, branching, and slightly imperfect.

At midday the brightness is nearly total. Sunlight strikes the wet roofs and comes back from the pools beneath them. Shadows collect only under bridges and inside the narrow mouths of the cisterns. The people move slowly, dressed in pale layers that conceal the skin from glare and heat. They speak close to one another. Their voices seem to vanish as soon as they are made.

Nothing in the landscape hurries. Water climbs, condenses, falls, and climbs again. Machines turn. Mineral skins form and break. Animals cross the exposed ground without disturbing its immense quiet. The city persists among these processes—not large enough to dominate the shelf, but old enough to feel like one more of its natural arrangements. A settlement made not against the wilderness, but in a place where the wilderness has no leaves, no wind-song, and no need to announce itself.
