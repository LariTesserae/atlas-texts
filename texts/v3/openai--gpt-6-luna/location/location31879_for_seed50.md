The place is inside a mountain that has not finished becoming one.

It is a pocket of water high on the mountain’s shoulder, sealed in a hollow no larger than a room. Outside, the peak rises above a pale, wind-scoured sea; inside, everything is submerged. There is no shore, only a rough little chamber of dark stone, and a wavering skin of light far overhead where a narrow crack admits the day. The light looks ordinary until it enters the water. Then it breaks into clean, shifting planes, bright enough to show the whole pocket at once—and how little there is in it.

The floor is a scatter of fresh rock: black glassy seams, chalk-colored crusts, and loose grains that haven’t decided whether to be sand or stone. The walls are unfinished. Some sections feel newly pressed together, their edges still sharp; elsewhere, a mineral veil hangs in folds, as if the mountain is slowly learning to close its own wounds. Thin white threads of crystal grow from the ceiling. They tremble when the water moves, though nothing touches them.

The water never settles. It draws inward, then shoves back, turning over itself in tight, violent circuits. A surge can pass through the chamber without seeming to come from anywhere. It lifts grit from the floor, spins it once in a bright cloud, and drops it somewhere else. The rock is not much worn yet. Instead, new matter keeps arriving: pale sediment sighing through cracks, beads of metal-bright mineral forming and vanishing, a soft plume of warmth that gathers in one corner and is pulled away before it can make the place comfortable.

The sound is far too large for the space. Every movement of water becomes a roar, a hammering, a deep animal bellow—though the chamber is empty enough that a single bubble seems important. The noise comes in pulses. Between them, there is no true silence, only a low, steady pressure that can be felt in the jaw. At times the whole pocket gives a short, resonant shudder, like a cup struck with a wet finger. The sound continues after the walls have stopped vibrating.

A few living things have found their way here. Small translucent grazers cling to the mineral threads, nearly invisible until they turn and catch the light. A pale tuft of vegetation grows from a crack in the floor, no taller than a thumb, its fronds folded close against the current. Nothing hunts openly. Now and then a dark shape passes behind the stone, too large to belong in this little chamber and gone before it can be seen clearly.

There are marks of habitation, though it is hard to tell who made them. Three flat stones have been stacked against one wall. A short length of braided fiber is wedged into a seam, swollen and stiff with salt. Near the floor sits a shallow clay cup, intact, with a thin skin of mineral deposited inside it. These things do not drift. They remain exactly where they are put, even when the current sweeps hard enough to send gravel rattling across the chamber.

The pocket has rules of its own. Water enters through a crack in the lower wall, but never in a stream; it arrives as a pressure, a darkening of the stone, a change in the pitch of the roar. The upper fissure gives out light but does not seem to give out water. If a bubble rises to it, the bubble vanishes without breaking. Objects dropped near the center travel down for a while, then sideways, then return to the hand that released them. They may take a few seconds, or a day.

The mountain is still rising. You can feel it in the way the stone makes and unmakes its seams, in the fresh grains that appear where the walls meet, in the tiny tremors that pass through the water. The chamber is hidden within all that immense, unfinished weight: a small hollow filled with a whole, raging ocean, loud as a storm and scarcely wide enough to turn around in.
