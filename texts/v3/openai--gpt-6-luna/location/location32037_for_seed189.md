Below the ground, under a shallow skin of ordinary soil, there is a pocket where the earth has split open recently. Its entrance is no wider than a root hole. The edges are raw: pale clay, sharp flakes of stone, roots cut cleanly as if the ground had opened around them rather than been worn away. The opening leads down only a little. Then the space begins to misbehave.

The chamber is small enough to be hidden beneath a patch of grass, but its interior has no reliable measure. A person could cross it in a dozen steps, or spend an hour walking toward the same dark green stem. The ceiling is close overhead in some places and lost at a height the eye insists should not fit beneath the soil. There is a great deal of room here, and very little in it.

Plants occupy almost everything without filling much of the space. Their growth is less a thicket than a few immense, delicate claims: a smooth green blade spanning the chamber like a bridge; a cluster of roots hanging in midair, fine as hairs, each tipped with a bead of moisture; a single pale flower whose petals seem to extend beyond sight when viewed from the side. Between them lie broad intervals of emptiness. The plants are the chamber’s walls, its floor, its weather, and perhaps its only inhabitants. They have no need to crowd one another.

The stone is young. It has not yet settled into a familiar shape. Ridges end abruptly; hollows have crisp rims; a gravel slope holds together at angles too steep to last. The earth smells warm and mineral, with a green scent underneath, like leaves crushed between wet hands. Moisture comes in brief spells. Sometimes a fine rain descends from the ceiling, though there is no water above it. Sometimes the plants bead over all at once, then shed their droplets upward. The water gathers in shallow dishes of stone and vanishes before it can soak in.

Light is plain and ample, like daylight on an overcast morning. It reaches every visible surface, but casts few dependable shadows. A plant may shade a stone without dimming it; a shadow may remain after its plant has shifted away. There is no obvious opening through which the light enters. It simply belongs to the place.

The chamber is loud. A distant sound—perhaps a drop of water landing, perhaps a leaf flexing—arrives with the force of a struck drum. Stems creak and click as they grow. The roots whisper against the rock, then suddenly roar, as though a forest were moving just beyond a thin wall. In the long pauses, even silence has a pressure to it. Small noises travel farther than they should, while a shout can be swallowed after a few paces.

Animal life is scarce. Once in a while, a translucent grub will cross a stone and disappear into the stone’s shadow, not into a crack. Tiny pale flies gather above the wet hollows, but their reflections are sometimes visible where there is no water. Nothing hunts openly. The plants respond to movement, though not always to the thing that moved: a leaf may turn toward a person before they arrive, or continue following the warmth of someone who has already left.

The rules are simple until they are not. Water seeks the plants, except when it seeks the ceiling. Roots enter stone, but only where the stone has not yet been looked at. A stem that bends away from you may be pointing toward your back. If you stare down a passage, it tends to become a wall; if you turn away, it may open behind you. The chamber does not seem hostile. It seems unfinished, and unable to distinguish between growing, remembering, and making room.

Nothing here appears built. There are no paths, tools, or signs of habitation—only the occasional smooth notch in a stone, too regular to be erosion and too small to be useful. A few flat shards lie nearby, as if something once tried to pry the place open with a crude edge and stopped. The plants have grown over none of them. They have grown around them with the wary care of something that has not decided what a tool is.

Above, the surface continues to receive its ordinary rain and sun. Below it, this hidden pocket keeps widening in ways that do not move the soil. A new shoot may appear in empty air. A sharp wall may soften overnight, then return sharper than before. The place is active, but its activity is quiet in the way of a world assembling itself: a drop forming, a root testing stone, a space learning how large it can be.
