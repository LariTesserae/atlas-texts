The plateau stands high beneath the sea, a broad shelf raised above the dark country around it. From its outer rim the bottom falls away in long blue steps, each one fading before it reaches the next. The water is not deep everywhere, but it is deep enough that the sky has become only a pale rumor overhead. A little light comes down, thinned and shaken into long grey bands. It catches the upper edges of the plateau and leaves the lower streets in dusk.

Nothing here is naturally laid. The ground is a fitted skin of stone: broad slabs, close-set and subtly curved, running in paths across the shelf and down into its gullies. They are old beyond any visible mark of their making. Their surfaces have been worn smooth by water, but not softened; corners remain where the currents have scoured them clean. Some slabs are dark as wet ash, others pale and faintly translucent, like the inner face of a shell. Between them, narrow seams descend into blackness. No soil gathers in those seams. No plant takes hold.

The construction does not look like a city, though it has streets, terraces, walls, and doorways. It feels more like a landscape taught to hold its shape. Long ridges of masonry divide the plateau into shallow basins. Towers, where they rise, are low and blunt, their tops rounded by ages of passing water. Some stand alone on the open shelf; others lean together in scattered clusters. There are arches with their spans intact, and courtyards open to the sea, every surface exposed to the same cold current. Nothing glows, turns, or signals. There are no lamps, mechanisms, or instruments. The place is built, but it is not working.

The cold is even and old. It lies in the stone as much as in the water, and makes the open spaces feel larger. Along the rim, where currents strike the plateau head-on, the water is restless. It pours over the edge in sheets, spirals through clefts, and recoils from the higher walls in ragged veils. In the lower basins it slows for a moment, only to be seized by a cross-current and swept away. The flow changes direction without warning. A quiet avenue can fill in seconds with racing water; a turbulent channel can settle briefly to a glassy stillness, while the stone beneath it seems to shudder.

The shuddering is not an earthquake. It is the whole sea pressing and releasing. The plateau makes a low, intermittent sound as the currents pass through its passages: a hollow note, like breath drawn through a vast mouth. Most other sounds are small. The faint click of stone grit crossing stone. The soft rasp of a shell against a wall. The muted rush of distant water arriving from beyond sight. There is so much movement, yet little noise. Sound is swallowed almost as soon as it is made.

Life is plentiful here, though it does not soften the place. Fish gather in the lee of walls in dense, shifting clouds, then scatter into the open water when a current turns. Pale, long-bodied animals drift through the high arches, trailing fine feelers that sense the water before the body follows. Small scavengers cling to the undersides of bridges and comb the seams in the paving. There are broad, flat creatures that rest on the terraces like fallen pieces of the architecture, their outlines visible only when they move. Some animals travel in streams along the avenues, using the currents as roads. Others hover in the open, suspended in dim water, each one separate from the next.

Their abundance never quite becomes a crowd. They gather in pockets, in eddies, under ledges, around the mouths of shafts. Between those concentrations are long, empty stretches of stone and cold water. Nothing grows. Nothing nests in the old chambers. The animals pass through, feed, shelter briefly, and move on.

The ancient works are still being worn down, but at a pace difficult to perceive. A lip of stone loses a grain. A wall is polished on one side and roughened on the other. Whole sections of the plateau have slumped into its outer ravines, leaving clean breaks that look almost deliberate. Yet the main forms endure, so old that their damage has become part of their design. The currents make and unmake small arrangements constantly: siltless drifts of pale fragments gather in one hollow, then are swept away; loose stones roll through an arch and come to rest elsewhere; an abandoned basin fills, empties, and fills again.

From above, the place might seem settled—an immense, spare pattern laid across the seabed. Near the stone, it is never settled at all. Every surface is being crossed by water. Every passage has its own weather. The plateau holds its shape against the sea, but the sea is forever running through it, pulling at its joints and finding new ways to pass.
