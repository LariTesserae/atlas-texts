The first impression is of a mountain range that has been built rather than raised.

It stands high enough for the clouds to pass through its valleys, but the air is hot and close. Moisture beads on every surface, then gathers into runnels, sheets, and sudden falling curtains. Rain is not the only source of water: the dark walls weep from their seams, the broad terraces sweat, and clear pools appear in shallow hollows where no spring seems to enter. The light is ordinary daylight, pale through vapor, made brighter where it catches the water and dimmer beneath the roofs of leaves.

There is almost no bare stone. What looks like rock is usually compressed plant matter: layers of old stems fused into ledges, root-fiber packed hard as fired clay, broad green plates grown into retaining walls. The forms are young and sharp-edged. Their corners have not softened; their slopes break off in clean steps, and channels cut through them still seem newly made. Yet nothing here looks temporary. The mountain’s surfaces have the deliberate regularity of a place arranged for a purpose.

The vegetation is not a covering over the landscape. It is the landscape. Tall stalks brace against one another to form arches and open-sided chambers. Flat leaves overlap into roofs. Thick, corded roots cross ravines as bridges, tightening whenever they are loaded with water. In some places, the growth has been drawn into long pale terraces, each one edged by a low lip that catches the runoff and guides it onward. Farther up, solitary towers of bundled stems rise from broad, empty platforms. They are spaced widely apart, with whole valleys of wet, unoccupied green between them.

The emptiness is part of the design. There are no herds moving through the openings, no birds circling the towers, no insects stitching the air with sound. Nothing animal breathes or calls. The gaps between the great structures are large enough to make the place feel abandoned, but the plants are never still. A wall tightens its weave by a fraction. A roof lifts and settles. New shoots appear along a seam, then bend away from one another as though obeying an invisible plan. The construction is alive, though there are no builders.

Water makes the loudest music. It bangs through elevated channels, pours down narrow chutes, and strikes hollow basins with a deep, drumlike note. Beneath this is a continuous rustling and creaking: leaves sliding over leaves, roots flexing in their sockets, long stems knocking together in the wind. Now and then a whole terrace gives a low shudder as water shifts inside it. The sound travels a long way across the open valleys. On a still day, the mountain seems to speak from several ridges at once.

The heat gathers under the plant roofs. Mist hangs among the pillars, and the damp air smells green, mineral, and faintly sweet, like crushed stems left in warm water. Above the roofs, the ridgelines are exposed to a harder sun. Their upper surfaces shine with wetness; their shaded faces are dark and slick. Clouds snag on the highest growth, unravel into rain, and spill down the constructed slopes. A storm does not simply pass over the range. It enters the channels, fills the basins, and makes the architecture move.

The landscape is turbulent, but not indiscriminately so. When a channel overflows, water cuts a new notch through a terrace. The cut does not remain raw for long: fibrous edges extend across it, catch the runoff, and draw it into a different course. A bridge may sag under a sudden flood, then thicken from below as roots knot together. A tower sheds a wet outer skin in long strips; new layers rise behind it. Change here has the briskness of construction. Erosion and repair are almost the same event.

There are no tools, hinges, or engines. Nothing is assembled in the human sense. The rules are written in growth: water seeks the lowest open channel; stems lean toward pressure; roots stitch the breaks; leaves turn to shelter whatever lies beneath them. The plants do not appear to be reaching for anything. They do not flower for pollinators or fruit for mouths. Their work is in the forms themselves—in the stairways, reservoirs, bridges, and high hollow halls that make up the range.

At a distance, the whole place resembles a sparse city spread across a green upland: towers set far apart, roofs gathered in clusters, broad causeways connecting one high basin to another. Up close, there are no streets in the usual sense, only passages that open when the leaves part and close when they sag with rain. There are no signs of habitation, yet every surface has the finish of use. A basin has been shaped to receive water. A ledge has been shaped to hold a roof. A wall has been shaped to turn wind aside.

As evening approaches, the vapor thickens and the structures darken to deep green and black. Water continues to thunder through the upper channels. In the valleys, the open pools hold the last daylight, while the tall living constructions stand above them like unfinished monuments—still making themselves, still directing the rain, with no creature in sight to witness or command the work.
