The place is called the Throat, though it has no opening large enough to admit a person.

It lies below the foundations of an ordinary city, sealed inside a pocket of stone no wider than a few streets. From outside, there is nothing to mark it: no spring, no sinkhole, no dampness in the basements above. Inside, the whole hollow is full of warm water. Its ceiling is low by the standards of caverns, lost in a brown-black dimness; its floor drops away in shelves and pits worn smooth over a very long time. The water is clear only where machinery stirs it. Elsewhere it carries a faint mineral haze, like breath held in a glass.

A small city has been built in the hollow—not crowded, but arranged with purpose. Its towers are squat pressure housings, riveted drums, and narrow stacks of ceramic conduit. Walkways span the gaps between them, though the gaps are mostly water. Some streets are pipes wide enough for a service trolley; others are open channels where the current runs freely around the buildings. There are inspection lamps behind thick glass, their amber light falling only a few meters before the dark takes it. The settlement’s shape is easiest to understand from above: a handful of connected work-sites clustered around a central pump, with long empty reaches between them.

The pump is the Throat’s loudest feature. It does not so much run as batter the water into motion. Its old turbines turn unevenly, taking up slack with a clank, then catching again in a shriek of vibration. Sound travels through the water and stone together. A person inside the hollow feels it in the teeth, in the joints of doors, in the soft tissue behind the eyes. There are quieter sounds, but they exist only in the brief troughs between surges: cables clicking against their guides, bubbles collecting under a roof, a valve’s thin metallic complaint. Even silence here would have weight. It never quite arrives.

The water is warm enough to fog a mask and soften the tarry seals around old hatches. Currents cross and recross the chamber, driven by the pump, by pressure changes in the deeper cracks, and by the slow breathing of the stone. One moment a channel draws hard toward the machinery; the next, a broad eddy turns loose sheets of silt through the streets. The city’s fittings are built for this: handrails doubled with chains, doors that open only against pressure, warning bells wired to gates that no longer close reliably. The arrangement is old and understood, but not safe. The Throat has settled into its habits, and those habits can still change without warning.

There is almost no life. Pale flecks sometimes gather in the lee of a warm exhaust pipe—tiny, translucent things that might be animals, or mineral crusts disturbed from the walls. They vanish when the current turns. Nothing green grows here. Nothing branches. The nearest thing to a garden is a rack of mineral filters, grown thick with orange and white deposits, which workers scrape clean and feed back into the plant.

Erosion has rounded the oldest stonework until it seems poured rather than cut. New metal braces cross those softened surfaces in straight, practical lines. Here the distinction between natural hollow and constructed space is difficult to keep: a pipe follows a fissure, a walkway is bolted to a shelf that may once have been a streambed, and the pump’s foundation disappears into rock worn smooth by water that may have been moving before the city above had a name.

The Throat is hidden, compact, and nearly empty by volume. Its buildings occupy only small islands in a great dark body of water. Yet everything in it is busy: currents, rotors, alarms, pressure gauges, the pulse of cables carrying power upward through stone. The hollow does not feel like a drowned city so much as a machine placed inside a quiet geological thought—one that has been running for centuries, loud enough to keep the rest of the world from hearing it.
