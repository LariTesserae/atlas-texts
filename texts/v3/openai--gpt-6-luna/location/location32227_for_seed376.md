The plateau begins where the last dark rock shelves lift above the cloud line. It is not a summit so much as a broad, tilted country, open enough to see weather coming from far away and crowded enough that the ground is seldom visible. Its old contours are softened by time: ridges rounded by ice, gullies worn deep and green, long slopes folded around shallow basins. Nothing here looks newly made. Everything looks as though it has been changing for ages and has simply never finished.

The vegetation owns the place.

It grows over stone in thick, overlapping layers: blue-black mats of moss, stiff grasses with glassy blades, cushion plants packed so tightly they form springy banks, and low shrubs whose branches knit into walls. In hollows, pale stalks rise shoulder-high, their dry heads combed sideways by the wind. Farther across the plateau, stands of slender, dark plants gather into groves. Their trunks are segmented and faintly ribbed, more like bundled reeds than trees, and their crowns spread in flattened fans that catch the thin light. The tallest groves bend together over the ravines, making green roofs beneath which the slopes are almost entirely alive.

The plants don’t merely occupy the land. They make it. Their roots bind the crumbling edges of terraces, then slowly pry them apart. Their fallen stems accumulate in the wet hollows, compressing into dark, fibrous ground. Underfoot, the surface yields, tightens, and shifts: a quilt of living pads over ancient scree. Here and there, a patch has drawn back from the rock, revealing a smooth seam of stone slick with condensation. Water is scarce in the open air, but it is never absent. It beads on leaves at dawn, gathers in the hollows of folded fronds, and runs in narrow, intermittent threads through the vegetation. After a passing shower, the plateau darkens in patches, as if something enormous had breathed against it.

The cold is steady, but not absolute. It lives in the shade under the groves and in the damp that clings to the lowest plants. Sunlight reaches the plateau normally, yet it seems to warm the surfaces rather than the air. On clear days, the land is all sharply legible: pale rock, green-black growth, the distant white backs of mountains. Then a cloud crosses, and the whole country goes soft and close. Mist slips into the gullies, beading on stalks. It may lift in minutes, or thin out over hours, leaving the plants glittering as if coated with ground glass.

There are few animals. Sometimes a small, pale creature will appear at the edge of vision, moving quickly between the stalks and vanishing before its shape can be understood. More often there are signs: a narrow channel pressed through the moss, a cluster of leaves cleanly stripped, a shallow hollow warmed by something that has just left it. Birds are almost never seen. The plants have filled so much of the available space, and so much of the available life, that other creatures seem to visit rather than belong.

And yet the plateau is in constant motion.

Whole patches of vegetation lean and flatten, then recover in slow waves, though the wind is too slight to account for it. A dark grove shivers from crown to crown, its leaves turning their pale undersides outward and then back, as if responding to a pressure passing underground. In some places, the growth seems to advance over a slope in a single night: not by visibly sprouting, but by changing its arrangement, closing gaps, raising a new ridge of tangled stems where open ground had been. Elsewhere, a mat of plants loosens and slides downslope with the soil beneath it, exposing a crescent of wet stone. The change is dramatic at a distance, but close up it happens with hardly a sound.

The quiet is remarkable. There is no continuous rush of wind, no running water, no insect chorus. The plants swallow most small noises. A person crossing the plateau would hear the dull press of their own steps and, occasionally, the dry tick of a stalk touching another. Even the weather arrives silently. Clouds cast their moving shadows; rain stipples the leaves; a whole basin may empty itself of mist. None of it announces itself. The silence makes the motion seem deliberate.

Across the broader country, certain formations repeat. Low ridges of dense vegetation run roughly parallel to one another, enclosing long corridors where the ground lies flatter and the growth is shorter. In the corridors are circular depressions, some shallow, some deep enough to hold pools. Their rims are lined with stiff, dark plants that lean inward. At intervals, the ridges divide around clusters of towering stalks. These have hollow bodies, polished by rain, and broad, cupped crowns. When clouds gather, water condenses along their ribs and travels downward in fine lines, feeding the root beds around them.

From a distance, these formations look engineered. There are the impressions of channels, reservoirs, production lines: orderly runs of growth feeding into deeper basins, repeated chambers, carefully divided slopes. But nothing is built. No stone is stacked, no metal placed, no straight edge holds its line for long. The apparent order comes from plants making use of the same pressures over and over—wind, gravity, cold, moisture, the weight of other plants. The plateau has arrived at a kind of industry without tools. It gathers water, stores it, divides it, and moves it through living tissue.

A low vibration can sometimes be felt through the soles rather than heard. It travels beneath the mat of growth, passes through a ridge, and fades under a basin. Then the stalks in the nearest grove turn by a fraction. Moisture gathers on their surfaces. Far off, a patch of pale fronds folds closed, while another opens. The movement is too coordinated to be random and too broad to belong to any one organism. Nothing visible drives it. The plateau seems to process its own weather.

At the edge of the high country, the groves thin and the slope falls away into valleys that are mostly hidden beneath clouds. The plateau does not end cleanly there. Strands of vegetation hang over the precipices in long, heavy curtains. Water drips from their tips. Now and then a whole section tears free—not with a crash, but in a silent cascade of green that unravels down the rock face and disappears below.

The place is old, settled into its own arrangements, but never still. Its turbulence is not a storm of sound or spectacle. It is a steady, immense rearranging: roots tightening, mats sliding, crowns turning, water changing course. The plateau wears no marks of human presence and requires none. It has the density of a forest, the exposure of a highland, and the uncanny patience of a machine that has been running for so long that no one can tell what it was made to do.
