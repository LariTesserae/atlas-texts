The dark is not complete, but it is thick enough to have weight.

At ground level, the country is a low spread of black mud, warm pools, and newly hardened earth. It seems to have risen only recently: ridges end abruptly, banks crumble in fresh slabs, and the channels between them have not yet decided where to go. A stream may divide around a mound of raw stone, vanish under a mat of roots, then return a hundred paces away in a rush of brown water. Here and there the ground still steams. The air smells of wet leaves, bruised fruit, and minerals newly uncovered.

Above it, vegetation packs every gap. Broad leaves overlap in tiers; reeds stand shoulder to shoulder in the shallows; climbing vines stitch one thicket to another. The plants are warm-weather exuberant, though not gentle. New shoots spear through old foliage. Thorned creepers drag at anything that passes. Flowers open in the dimness with heavy, pale faces that seem to drink what little light reaches them. Moss and soft growth coat the rocks, but the rocks beneath are sharp-edged, dark, and young.

There is enough sky to show that the place is wider than a forest clearing. Between the high tangles, one can glimpse a low horizon of broken wetland, scattered islands of brush, and farther ridges barely raised above the plain. Clouds pass overhead, but their movement is difficult to see. The darkness comes from more than the canopy: soot-colored haze, storm cloud, and the sheer density of living things all seem to press it down. A few dim glimmers move among the leaves—wet reflections, insect bodies, the occasional pale fungus—but no glow holds steady. The world gives back light sparingly.

Sound fills what sight cannot. Water chatters, sluices, and suddenly roars through narrow cuts in the earth. Leaves rasp against leaves in the wind. Insects pulse in shrill, overlapping waves; frogs answer from the pools; unseen animals bark, cough, whistle, boom, and scream from the thickets. The calls are so numerous that distance becomes hard to judge. A sound that seems close may come from across a flooded basin; a soft rustle beside the ear may be a heavy body passing just beyond the leaves. Even when rain stops, the place does not become quiet. It only changes instruments.

Fauna is everywhere—not merely abundant, but layered into the landscape. Small, quick things cloud the air above the water. Long-backed swimmers wrinkle the pools. Heavy-footed grazers force paths through the reeds, and hunters use those paths soon after. Birds, or creatures much like them, perch in branches too thin to hold them, while broad, many-limbed animals cling to trunks and wait for the sudden movement of prey. Most are hard to see. Their presence is betrayed by a bending stalk, a wake crossing a pool against the current, a patch of leaves that trembles after the rest have stilled. Some calls seem to be answered by other species; others draw no reply at all, though the answering animal may already be moving.

The weather is warm, and the damp comes in fits. Rain may strike hard for a brief while, rattling the upper foliage and swelling the channels, then pass without clearing the heavy air. Moisture collects on every surface. It beads along the underside of leaves and drips steadily from them long after the rain has ended. In sheltered hollows, water stands dark and still, filmed with pollen and tiny moving lives. Elsewhere it pours through the land in sudden, forceful surges, carrying fronds, branches, and fresh soil downstream.

Nothing here feels settled. The banks are too new, the roots too recently exposed, the channels too changeable. A pool becomes a creek; a creek cuts a new notch in the mud; a fallen tree turns briefly into a bridge, then into a dam, then into a nursery for insects and fungi. Vegetation closes over scars almost as soon as they open, but the scars keep shifting beneath it. The land is not being slowly worn smooth. It is still assembling itself, quickly and noisily, with water, growth, and animal movement all tugging at its shape.

There are no roads, fences, clearings, or old stones arranged by hand. A path is only the temporary absence of growth, and it rarely stays open. The few signs of tool use one might imagine—cut stems, split branches, a sharpened length of wood—would look small and vulnerable against the abundance around them. This is a place that makes no accommodation for visitors. Its rules are simple only at a distance: follow high ground when the water rises; do not trust a still pool; do not mistake silence for emptiness; and never assume that a trail made by one animal is safe from another.

When darkness deepens, the forest does not disappear. It becomes more crowded. The water keeps moving, the foliage keeps shaking, and the calls multiply until the whole low country seems to breathe through a thousand mouths. The land is young, lush, and almost too alive—a raw, warm basin where every fresh opening is already being filled.
