The plateau begins above the weather.

From below, it looks like a single pale wall, sunlit even when the lowlands are under cloud. Up here the land opens into a country of ridges and shallow basins, all broad enough to hold their own horizon. The stone is cream-colored where freshly exposed, ochre in its old seams, and dark as tea where water has found a way to linger. Wind has spent a long time smoothing the exposed faces. It has not made them gentle so much as settled: ledges rounded at their edges, gullies deepened into familiar courses, whole hillsides leaning in the same direction as if they had agreed, ages ago, how to stand.

The air is warm and thin. Sunlight has a hard clarity to it, but the warmth is interrupted by little, passing dampnesses. Rain comes in brief veils, often from a sky that still looks bright. It stipples the rock, gathers in hollows no wider than a hand, and vanishes from the open ground within an hour. Some basins keep water beneath their crusts. There, the stone sweats at dawn. Fine beads shine along the undersides of overhangs, and a person walking at first light might hear tiny drops falling into pools they cannot see.

Vegetation keeps mostly to such places. Tough silver grasses trace the seams where water runs underground. Low plants with folded, waxy leaves make dark mats around the seep-holes, opening only after rain. Here and there a thorn tree stands alone, its branches bent flat by years of wind, casting a neat, thin shadow. The green is never spread evenly. It appears in small, deliberate-looking clusters, then gives way again to bare stone and pale dust.

The animals are everywhere, though not always visible. At the edges of the basins, long-legged grazers move in loose groups, their coats nearly the color of the land. They lower their heads to feed among the grasses and lift them together when something changes in the air. Small bright-eyed creatures live in the cracks and under the old ledges. They leave trails of fine prints through dust, then disappear into stone so narrow it seems impossible they could have passed through. High overhead, wide-winged scavengers circle without calling. In the heat of midday, pale insects rise from the basin floors in slow, glittering columns; from a distance they look like dust suspended in the sunlight.

The plateau is quiet, but it is not still. Sound travels a long way here, and so every creature seems to have learned economy. A hoof placed on a flat slab makes a single hollow note. Grass whispers when the wind passes over it. Somewhere below the surface, water ticks and shifts through the rock. Occasionally a ridge gives off a low, sustained vibration, like a glass being touched with a wet finger. It fades before anyone can locate its source.

The old works are scattered across the open ground. They are not ruins in the usual sense: no walls collapsed into heaps, no broken roads running toward an absent city. A few narrow towers stand at intervals along the ridges, each built from dark material that reflects the sky without quite reflecting the sun. Some are no higher than a person. Others rise above the thorn trees, smooth and unjointed, with no visible doors. Near them, the stone has been shaped into shallow channels, circles, or long, perfectly straight cuts that ignore the natural slope.

These structures still do things. A tower may cast its shadow in two directions at once. A flat stone may remain cool through the hottest part of the day, then release warmth after sunset in a slow, even pulse. On certain mornings, a basin’s loose stones lift a finger’s width from the ground and hold there until the air dries. None of this happens with spectacle. The place does not flash or roar. Its engineering seems to work by making small, exact agreements with the land.

In the largest basin stands a low ring of pale pillars. They are spaced far apart, with enough room between each for a grazing animal to pass. Rainwater gathers inside the ring though the floor is no lower than the surrounding ground. For a little while after a storm, the water forms a shallow, unbroken sheet. The pillars then show a second landscape in their surfaces: a dim reflection of the plateau under a different sky, with cloud banks moving from a direction the local wind never takes. By noon the water is gone, and the pillars are only pillars again.

Nothing marks the boundaries of the place. Beyond one ridge, the ground rises into another set of basins, then another. The far distances soften in the heat, but they do not seem empty. Every so often a moving line appears against the horizon—animals migrating, perhaps, or some immense mechanism crossing a slope too far away to make a sound. The plateau has room for such uncertainties. It does not crowd them with answers.

Toward evening, the light turns the old stone pink. Dampness gathers again in the cracks, and the small plants uncurl their leaves. Herds descend toward the hidden water, moving between the scattered towers without fear of them. Above, the great circling birds fold their wings and drop toward unseen roosts. A thin vibration passes underfoot, crosses the basin, and continues into the ridge beyond. The animals pause until it is gone.

Then the plateau resumes its quiet work: holding a little water, sheltering many lives, and keeping its ancient, impossible instruments in patient balance with the wind.
