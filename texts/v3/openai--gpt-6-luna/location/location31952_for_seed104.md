The high country lies beneath a warm, dim sea.

It is not a range of peaks so much as a continent that has spent ages being worn down underwater. Its ridges have softened into long, rounded backs; its valleys are broad enough to gather their own weather. Here and there, old stone rises toward the surface in pale shelves, but none breaks through. Above them hangs a distant, wavering brightness: not daylight exactly, but the memory of it, filtered through so much water that it seems to come from everywhere and nowhere.

The plateau is ancient beyond the measure of anything living there. Its rock has been smoothed almost to a final shape. Great hollows hold still, warm currents; cliffs descend in slow terraces, each step furred with growth. Some walls are honeycombed with openings too regular to be caves and too worn to be ruins. Their edges are gentle as river stones. They might have been made by water, or by something that understood water and then disappeared.

Vegetation crowds every ledge. Long fronds comb the currents, their tips brushing one another in slow, continuous motion. Dense gardens of soft coral-colored growth spread over the stone, interrupted by dark stands of stalks that rise like drowned groves. They do not sway with the current so much as lean toward it, as if listening. Higher up, where the water is brighter, thin floating leaves gather in mats. Their undersides shine faintly, and their roots descend for astonishing distances, threading through the terraces and vanishing into blue-green shadow.

The water is warm enough to feel inhabited, but the place is strangely spare of animals. Now and then a pale creature crosses an open basin—long, nearly transparent, difficult to tell from a crease in the water. Small things hide among the growth, though they leave little sign of themselves. No calls carry. No chorus rises from the beds. The landscape is dense with life and yet almost silent, a crowded world holding its breath.

Currents move through the highlands in overlapping layers. One passes along the slopes with the steady pull of a tide; another rises out of a valley and travels upward, carrying leaves and silt toward the light. In certain places, the water turns without turning. A strand of weed will drift east while its shadow drifts west. Fine sediment hangs in the airless water, falling for days and then, without warning, lifting toward the seabed. The currents meet and make broad, invisible boundaries. Cross one, and the warmth changes. Cross another, and distance becomes unreliable: a ridge that seemed near recedes for hours, while a remote shelf appears suddenly beneath you.

The strangest places are the basins. There the floor is so smooth and pale that it resembles a sky. Shapes above it are reflected downward, though there is no surface to reflect them. Visitors see the terraces below their feet, and then see themselves there too—smaller, older, sometimes standing where they have not yet arrived. The image does not ripple when touched. After a while, it is hard to say which landscape is the reflection.

Scattered across the plateau are remnants of building: a narrow stone arch on a ledge, a few upright pillars grown through with fronds, a half-buried stair that descends into solid rock. There are no machines, no lights, no marks that explain their making. The structures are too sparse to form a town and too deliberate to be natural. Their surfaces have been rounded by the same patient erosion as the rest of the land. Some are coated in living growth; others remain bare, as though the plants have agreed to leave them untouched. Where a doorway stands open, the current avoids it.

The high country has weather, though nothing here has a sky. Warm surges travel across it for weeks, stirring the groves and setting the suspended silt in motion. Then come long spells of near-stillness, when the fronds hang straight down and even the water seems to have weight. In the deepest valleys, slow storms of bubbles rise from fissures in the stone. They do not burst. They collect in ceilings beneath overhangs, forming shining chambers that drift, intact, through the water.

At the edges of the plateau, the land falls away. The shelves end in immense slopes descending into blue darkness, and the vegetation thins to isolated ribbons. Far below, the old country continues in fragments: another bench of stone, another basin, another drowned ridge, each one softened and overgrown. The scale defeats the eye. A whole day of swimming may carry you only from one small detail of the landscape to another.

Yet the place is never still. Plants turn by fractions. Sediment rises and settles. Currents pass through the valleys, carrying warmth, leaves, and the occasional pale animal. Somewhere, a stone arch sheds a grain of sand. Somewhere else, the water flows uphill for a season.

Nothing announces these changes. The highlands simply continue, ancient and lush, holding their dim light in layers—while the world above, or the world below, slowly forgets which way it is.
