The high country begins where the ground loses its color.

Below, perhaps, there is a world of ordinary weather. Up here the plateau rises into cold brightness, a great shelf of pale stone and ash-colored mineral stretching beyond the curve of sight. Its surface is not bare. It is covered by a living crust so extensive that from a distance it seems like another kind of rock: a dull green-gray skin, ribbed and furred, broken by broad seams of exposed ground. Nothing pools in those seams. There is no water to gather. The plants draw what they need from the air, from mineral salts, from the deep slow chemistry of the plateau itself. Their tissues are dry, tough, and faintly metallic. They do not grow upward toward rain. They grow outward, gripping the land.

The vegetation is almost everything here, but it does not make a forest. It makes a continent’s worth of intervals.

Across the open slopes, low mats of branching growth lie tight against the stone, their leaves reduced to overlapping scales. Between them stand scattered towers: tall, hollow stalks fluted like organ pipes, or swollen trunks split into many stiff arms. Some rise a hundred meters above the plain, solitary enough to have their own weather. Their crowns are open cages of black-green filaments. When the wind reaches them, the filaments flex and comb the sky.

The sound is immense.

It comes first as a low, continuous pressure, like distant machinery heard through the ground. Then the separate voices emerge: the rasp of millions of leaves sliding against one another; the booming of hollow stems; the shrill, almost electrical whistle of seed-capsules turning on their hinges. Every gust sets off a different section of the plateau. A ridge begins to howl, then falls silent as the wind passes on. Farther away, a field of thin stalks rattles like a factory full of loose tools. The great towers answer in long bass notes that travel for miles. On certain days the whole country is audible before it is visible.

The ground has been shaped by this sound for ages. Wind and dry grit have sanded the exposed stone into shallow basins, hard terraces, and long fluted corridors. The edges are rounded, not fresh. Nothing here looks newly made, even when it is moving violently. The plateau is old in its habits: it sheds a little mineral dust each season, grows over its own scars, and settles back into a form it has occupied for longer than any animal can remember.

Yet its calm is only apparent. The air is always busy. Bright, cold winds run at several levels, crossing and colliding above the plain. Dust lifts in sheets, glitters, and drops somewhere else. The tall growth releases clouds of dry spores that stream sideways like smoke. There are sudden fronts where the sky goes white with airborne seed, and the light turns so sharp that every stem casts a clean black shadow. The storms do not bring rain. They bring abrasion, pressure, spores, and the tremor of distant plant-masses shifting their weight.

Some of the plants are not rooted in the usual way. Vast mats creep so slowly across the mineral floor that their movement is measured in years, but the plates at their edges can buckle and travel during a storm. Their undersides grip the rock with thousands of hair-fine anchors. When the wind tears one loose, it rolls over itself in a long, rattling wave, flattening smaller growth and exposing pale stone before catching again on a ridge. It may travel for days. Behind it, new shoots rise from the fragments it leaves scattered in its wake.

Other growths operate like living machines. A cluster of ribbed chambers opens and closes against the wind, pumping air through a bed of mineral dust. Narrow vanes rotate on dry joints, grinding salts from the rock and feeding them into the colony’s buried tissues. The mechanism is crude and ancient, but it works at industrial scale: a whole hillside turning, clicking, filtering, storing. There are no factories in the landscape, no walls or towers put there by people. The production belongs to the plants. Their systems are too widespread to look designed and too regular to seem entirely wild.

Animals are abundant, though seldom seen all at once. They move through the spaces between the living masses. Pale runners with many jointed legs skim across exposed stone, vanishing beneath the mats at the slightest change in vibration. Heavy grazers browse the high filaments of the stem-fields, moving in slow herds that seem to drift apart and gather again across the ridgelines. Tiny winged creatures cling to the lee sides of the towers, their bodies bright as fragments of foil. At dusk—which is a brief dimming rather than darkness—they lift in sheets, making the air flicker.

The largest animals are heard before they are found. Their calls enter the plateau’s continuous roar as blunt pulses, spaced far apart. Sometimes a column of dust walks across a distant basin and resolves into a herd crossing the open ground, each creature trailing a veil of ground-up mineral. The plant life gives them shelter and food, but little concealment. In this brightness, everything is exposed. Survival depends less on hiding than on reading the wind, the tremors, the minute changes in the great collective noise.

There is no single horizon. The plateau rises and falls in immense shallow folds. Ridges appear pale through the haze, then sharpen as the air clears; farther off, immense shelves of high ground are lost in the glare. The world’s scale is difficult to judge because the growth has no familiar reference points. A solitary stalk may be the height of a tower, or a tower may only be the near edge of a forest of stems. Dust curtains erase distance, while the long, low sound makes far-off things feel close. A storm can occupy the entire view and still be only one moving part of a weather system that spans the continent.

At night the brightness drains away, but the place does not become quiet. The stems cool and contract with a sound like distant metal settling. Seed-capsules knock against their stalks. Beneath the ground, the mats make tiny dry clicks as they tighten their hold on the rock. The animals continue moving. Somewhere on the plateau, a ridge begins to sing as the wind shifts, and the note passes from one hollow plant to another until the dark itself seems to be vibrating.

Morning returns without softness. Light pours across the bare stone and catches on the dry foliage, turning each scale and filament briefly silver. The immense plain resumes its work: wind over mineral, roots into stone, stalks pumping air, herds crossing the open seams. Nothing here is still, and nothing is new. The whole country is an old machine made of living matter, running hot with motion beneath a cold, clear sky.
