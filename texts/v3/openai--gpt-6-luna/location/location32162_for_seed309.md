### The Sump Orchard

The Sump Orchard lies beneath a district that has forgotten it exists. Its whole extent is perhaps a few streets’ worth of ground: a low, irregular chamber where the ceiling dips within reach of the tallest machinery and the floor rises and falls in old, rounded ridges. Nothing here is truly bare. Roots have softened the masonry, moss has furred the iron, and thick green mats cover the places where water once stood. The Orchard has been settling into itself for a very long time.

It is warm below ground, but not evenly so. Near the pumps the air is close and oily; among the root columns, cool beads of condensation gather and fall at long intervals. There is no river, only water remembered in pockets: black cisterns under the walkway, shallow pools caught in the hollows of stone, damp seams where the wall sweats after rain above. The pools shrink, return, and shrink again. Each time they do, something small and numerous takes advantage.

Plants make the terrain. Their trunks are not quite trunks, but bundled stems braided together until they can bear weight. They arch over old service roads and thicken around pillars, lifting the ceiling in places or pressing it down beneath curtains of leaves. Long root-fans cross the floor like low fences. A person can walk the Orchard, though never straight through it: the passage bends around a tree, narrows between a boiler and a bank of reeds, opens suddenly into a little green square, then pinches into a slot beneath hanging roots. The growth is lush, yet oddly spaced. It leaves broad pockets of dim air between its crowded structures, as if the plants have built rooms and know not to fill them.

The oldest human works remain in place, though none remain in charge. There are squat brick sheds, a loading platform, a pair of rusted rails, and the squat black cylinders of industrial pumps. A small settlement has grown around these: lean-to rooms of sheet metal and timber, a workshop bolted to a retaining wall, a few lamps protected in wire cages. The machinery still runs. Belts turn slowly under mats of leaves; a turbine makes the water tremble in its cistern. The Orchard’s inhabitants maintain the engines, and the plants maintain the inhabitants: roots hold up collapsing walls, broad leaves shed droplets into drinking jars, and pale runners creep into warm housings to draw heat from the motors.

No one agrees whether the pumps are keeping the Orchard alive or keeping it from drowning. They start and stop according to a schedule that predates anyone’s memory. When they engage, the entire chamber gives a low shudder. The roots tighten against the masonry. Water stirs in the cisterns, and thousands of tiny animals answer.

Fauna is everywhere, but rarely all in sight at once. Small pale frogs sit in the damp folds of leaves. Beetles stipple the walls. Long, many-legged things slip beneath the walkways. Bats roost in the machinery’s high cavities, while blind fish nose through the cisterns and vanish under mats of submerged roots. Most conspicuous are the winged insects: clouds of them gather where the lamps glow, then break apart like shaken ash. Larger animals move through the outer tunnels, their weight announced by a ripple through the growth and the sudden silence of everything smaller.

The noise never quite stops. Pumps thrum. Water ticks from the ceiling. Beetles rasp in the wall-roots; frogs make sharp, hollow calls from the pools. Wings flicker against lampshades. Somewhere, something knocks rhythmically inside a pipe, though the pipe has been disconnected for years. The inhabitants speak close to one another’s ears. Even then, the Orchard seems to hear them: leaves turn, slowly, toward a voice; a hundred little bodies cease their clicking, then start again.

Its strangest rule is that nothing here wastes warmth. The plants grow thickest around motors and warm pipes. The animals nest in the root-wrapped spaces between them. Fallen leaves are broken down almost as soon as they land, and even the old machinery has become a kind of habitat, its housings lined with nests and its exhaust vents feathered with green. When an engine goes cold, the growth withdraws from it, and the animals leave. When it starts again, they return in stages: first the small things, then the bats, then the heavy, quiet movement in the tunnels.

At night—if it is night—the lamps show only a little way into the green. Beyond their reach the Orchard is not dark so much as occupied. Things rustle, breathe, hunt, and feed in the spaces between trunks and walls. The settled paths remain underfoot, worn smooth by years of passage; the roots continue their slow revisions around them. Nothing is growing fast enough to be seen growing. Nothing, here, is ever quite still.
