### The Upper Works

The place occupies the crown of a mountain too narrow to be called a plateau. From below, it looks like a brass cap pinned to the sky: a little arrangement of terraces, pipes, sheds, and green, high above the cloudline. There is no exposed mountain beneath it. The summit has been faced over entirely—every ledge poured, plated, bolted, tiled, or sealed beneath a skin of pale ceramic. Even the cliff edge is engineered, its sheer drop held in a ribbed collar of black metal that disappears down the mountain’s sides.

The whole of the Upper Works is small enough to cross in a few minutes. Yet it feels immense. The air falls away on every side, and the narrow paths run between structures that leave most of the sky unobstructed. There are long distances here, but not much ground.

Warm moisture arrives in fits. A cloud brushes the summit, beading on handrails and collecting in the seams between slabs; then the wind takes it away, leaving the paving dry except for dark runnels. Water is rationed by gravity and architecture. It drips from condenser coils into narrow gutters, passes through settling tanks, and is lifted again in a slow, steady circuit. Here and there, the builders have allowed it to spill over a lip—not enough for a stream, just a shining thread that disappears through a grate.

The vegetation is lavish in its allotted places. Vines braid the vent housings. Ferns and broad, waxy leaves crowd the mouths of stairwells, where warm exhaust makes a sheltered climate. Small trees grow from deep, fabricated basins, their roots disappearing into pipes and mineral-rich packing. They seem less planted than installed: green machinery, quietly using what the Works sheds. In the harshest wind, the leaves turn their silver undersides outward. At night they fold, though night here is never entirely dark.

The structures are industrial, but old enough to have softened into their own order. The oldest tower has settled a fraction of an inch; its pipes bend around that change instead of correcting it. Rivets are stained rust-red. Ceramic panels have worn smooth where rain repeatedly crosses them. Nothing is collapsing. Repairs are slow, thorough, and apparently performed over generations by someone who never needs to hurry. A crane stands permanently at the edge of the highest terrace, its long arm angled over emptiness. It moves perhaps once a year, turning just far enough to keep its joints from seizing.

The Works is loud. Its turbines make a continuous roar that fills the chest and flattens ordinary speech. Pressure valves hammer at intervals, each burst of steam so sharp that birds rise from the gardens in a dark flurry. The sound seems to belong not to any one machine but to the whole summit, as if the mountain’s hidden interior were being ventilated through the buildings. Yet between the great mechanisms are empty courts and narrow passages where the noise thins to a trembling hum. A dropped screw can be heard skittering a long way.

There are many animals, though scarcely any seem to live in the open. Small birds nest in the hollow cross-braces. Fat moths cling to the warm sides of control cabinets. Pale geckos hunt in the condensation channels, and a colony of bats occupies an abandoned cooling chamber. Once, visitors found a shallow tank crowded with tiny translucent things that had no eyes and gathered wherever the water trembled. The Works’ caretakers—if there are caretakers—do not remove them. The local rule appears to be that anything which fits into the system may remain.

Other rules are harder to learn. No one has found where the water first enters the cycle. The lowest pipe descends through the summit’s armored floor and gives off a mild warmth, even in winter. The loudest turbine never turns, according to its painted gauge; still, its housing shivers with a steady pulse. If a leaf is torn from one of the high garden beds, it is usually back by the following morning, attached to the plant or caught nearby in the mesh.

Most things change very slowly. The trees grow around their supports. Mineral bloom gathers on the shaded sides of the tanks. The crane’s shadow inches across the terrace over the course of a day. Below, clouds pass in immense white currents, while the Upper Works remains fixed above them, damp in patches, warm at its vents, green in its seams, and deafeningly patient.
