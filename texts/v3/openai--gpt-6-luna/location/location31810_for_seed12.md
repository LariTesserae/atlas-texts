The high country is under water.

It rises from a broad, unseen seabed in long steps of black stone and pale, still-soft ash. Its upper ridges nearly reach the surface, though nowhere does the land break through. Instead the sea thins overhead to a wavering ceiling of tarnished silver. Sunlight comes down reluctantly, green and diffuse, and the highest pinnacles cast shadows that drift for hours across the slopes below. From the summit, if anything here could look upward, the surface would seem less like sky than a second, distant landscape: bright, moving, and unreachable.

The plateau is young. Its cliffs have not yet learned to crumble into proper cliffs; their edges are rounded, swollen, and unfinished, as though the stone is still deciding where to harden. Here and there, hot seams breathe a faint shimmer into the water. Mineral veils gather around them, clouding the deep blue into tea-brown, then clearing again over the course of a season. New shelves of rock push out from the old ones. They are easily mistaken for terraces until one notices how little they match anything around them.

Water occupies nearly every hollow. It lies in high basins among the ridges, pours through narrow ravines, and hangs in curtains from overhanging ledges where warmer currents climb. These falls do not roar. They make a soft, continual hush, like cloth drawn slowly across stone. In the deeper channels, the water is heavy and nearly black. Above, it is warm enough that the pale fronds growing along the exposed ridges curl and open with the passing of the current.

Vegetation crowds the slopes in layers. Broad bladder-leaves cling to the rock; long grasses stream sideways from cracks; red and amber fans grow in sheltered hollows, their edges trembling though the water seems still. Rootlike cords stitch ledges together. In places, they have caught silt and rubble and made new ground—mattress-soft banks that lift slightly each year. The plant life is lush but not cheerful. Its colors are subdued by the dimness: deep bottle green, bruised violet, yellow like old bone. The thickest groves hold darkness even at midday.

There are settlements among these growths, built on the stable shoulders of the plateau. From a distance they look like additional reefs: low roofs, narrow bridges, pale retaining walls furred with moss and weed. Up close, their hand-made order appears. Stones have been fitted into gaps in the young rock. Walkways are lashed from flexible stems. Small chambers, wedged beneath overhangs, are sealed with layered bark and resin. A few towers rise above the rest—not high enough to approach the surface, but high enough to catch the light first. Their inhabitants use no great machines. They shape, tie, wedge, and wait.

The town is dense, yet the open country between its clusters is wide. Paths wind along ridges and vanish into vegetation, then reappear on the far side of a ravine. Old steps end in water. New ones are being set into soft stone by patient hands. Some streets are not streets at all but corridors cleared through waving fronds, kept open by constant cutting. Others pass beneath the settlement, through chambers where the water is still and the walls gleam with minerals.

There are few animals. A dark, many-finned shape may pass far below and never be seen clearly enough to name. Small translucent creatures gather around warm vents, pulsing faintly like thoughts that have not yet become words. On quiet days, tiny mouths open in the moss and close again as the currents carry nourishment past. Nothing here hunts loudly. The rare calls are low, spaced far apart, and difficult to locate. A stone may click once in the dark, and the sound may travel through the ridge until it seems to come from everywhere.

Time behaves gently here, but not evenly. The water moves. The plants grow. The plateau continues to rise by a measure too small to notice from one lifetime to the next. Silt settles in the basins, and the basins slowly fill. A passage that was waist-deep becomes shoulder-deep; a roof once touched by every high current begins to dry beneath a warmer layer. The town adapts by inches. No one can say whether the land is ascending toward the surface or the surface is descending toward it.

There is an odd rule to the light: the highest ridges are not always the brightest. Sometimes a valley glows while the summit above it goes dark, as if the water has remembered an older depth and folded it over the land. In those hours, the settlement lamps show through the vegetation as muted amber points, each one doubled by the wavering water overhead. The whole plateau seems to float beneath another, hidden plateau made of shadow and reflection.

Nothing arrives quickly. New rock swells out of the earth. Greenery takes hold. A bridge sinks into a mat of roots and becomes part of it. The sea fills the space left behind. Beyond the town, the ridges continue into blue distance, each softer and less finished than the last. This is not yet a complete landscape. It is a place still being assembled—warm, drowned, and quietly crowded—where stone, water, plant, and dwelling have not settled on which of them is making the ground.
