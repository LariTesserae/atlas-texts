The plateau begins where the lower country gives up its color.

It rises in long, weathered steps, each one a different shade of iron red, bruised violet, or pale mineral yellow. The uppermost shelf lies so high that the horizon has the clean, hard edge of a cut. Heat stands over it in visible layers. Far ridges waver, though nothing here is wet enough to shimmer; the air itself seems to have been heated until it softened. By day the light is ordinary and merciless. It shows every crack, every grain of black grit, and gives no relief from them.

There is no water. Not a pool, not a seep, not even the blue suggestion of one. The old channels are still legible in the stone, branching across the plateau like the veins of some enormous exhausted body. They lead nowhere now. Their beds are packed with salt, metallic dust, and smooth gray nodules that ring faintly when the ground shifts underfoot. The land has been dry for so long that dryness is no longer an absence. It is a material: crusted in the hollows, powdered on the slopes, compressed into pale shelves at the edges of the old channels.

Nothing grows.

At first the eye calls the scattered dark forms rocks, because rocks are what the plateau has in abundance. Then one of them unfolds. The forms are animals—some the size of a fist, some larger than a house—and there are so many that the distinction between animal and ground is only temporary. They lie motionless for years at a time, their hard shells collecting dust until the landscape incorporates them. They open along seams when the temperature changes, exposing intricate interiors of plates, bellows, and damp-looking metallic membranes. From within comes a low, clattering breath.

The smaller creatures travel in great sheets. They pass over the old channels in dense, glittering drifts, not walking so much as pouring. Their jointed bodies grind against one another with the sound of an enormous factory working far below the earth. When they reach a slope, the whole mass takes hours to descend. When they reach level ground, they stop. The front ranks stop first; the rear ranks continue arriving for days, settling over them in layers. The drift may remain in place for a season, or a century. At last some pressure, too slight for a human to detect, passes through it, and the whole congregation lifts its heads together.

The large animals are more widely spaced. One may stand alone for many kilometers, braced against the heat on a dozen articulated legs. Its back carries a comb of hollow vanes, each one thick with mineral scale. When the wind strikes those vanes they sound like metal doors shuddering in a storm. The animal does not seem to feed. It holds still until dust accumulates along its flanks, then loosens its plates and lets the dust fall through its body in a steady stream. What it takes from the plateau, and what it gives back, is not clear. The smaller creatures gather in its shadow. Sometimes they climb into the gaps between its plates and disappear for years.

There are no roads, no towers, no factories. Yet the place is loud with industry.

A distant thudding travels through the rock at irregular intervals. Nearer, the air is full of grinding, ticking, and long metallic groans. The sound has the scale and confidence of machines too large to stop for anything. In some valleys it becomes a continuous roar, though nothing moves there that can be seen. At night—if night is the word for the dimming of this pale, hot sky—the noise changes pitch. It does not grow quieter. It seems instead to move farther inside the ground.

The plateau’s inhabitants perform the work of engines without having been made as engines. Their movements are slow, collective, and difficult to distinguish from geology. A migration can take a generation to cross the region. A great animal may spend centuries aligning itself to the prevailing wind. The smaller swarms circulate through the old drainage beds in patterns that have survived the beds themselves: tributaries without water, deltas made of living bodies. Their passage abrades the stone. Their excretions—if they excrete—leave thin, dark seams in the dust. Erosion continues, but at a pace so patient that it feels less like destruction than a method of keeping the plateau in its proper shape.

The empty spaces are immense. Between one moving congregation and the next, there may be a hundred kilometers of apparently uninhabited stone. Then the dust rises, or the ground rings, and the emptiness is revealed to be full of things waiting. There are burrows below the crust, though they do not have entrances in the usual sense. A surface may be unbroken one day and, the next, subtly convex, warm to the touch, and humming with something passing underneath. The creatures travel through the plateau as much as over it. A ridge can contain a migration. A plain can be hollow.

Distance is not reliable here. A peak seen at dawn may remain the same apparent size until midday, when it is suddenly close enough to cast a shadow across the viewer’s feet. Sometimes the sun is reflected in a black rock before it reaches that part of the sky. Sometimes an animal’s shadow arrives first, extending over the ground long before the animal can be made out. These are not treated as events by the fauna. They continue their patient work, adjusting by increments too small to see.

The wind crosses the high country carrying no scent of soil or rain. It tastes of hot stone and pennies. In the exposed places it strips loose scales from the animals’ backs and drives them in bright little spirals along the ground. In sheltered basins it falls away completely. Dust hangs there in motionless columns, each grain suspended at a different height. At the center of some columns, sound bends. A footfall may come from behind. A roar may seem to be spoken directly into the bones of the skull. The animals avoid those basins, or perhaps they gather beneath them where they cannot be seen.

The oldest ridges have been worn smooth, but not gently. Their edges are scalloped and grooved, shaped by centuries of abrasion from migrating bodies and driven mineral dust. On certain slopes, the stone has a polished sheen, as though an immense machine has passed over it repeatedly. It has. It is still passing, though the machine is alive, distributed across miles of shifting shells and clicking legs. The plateau’s industrial rhythm is not an interruption of its natural order. It is the order.

Nothing here is hurried. The heat has been established; the dryness has settled in; the great migrations keep to their ancient courses. Even the reality-breaks seem worn into the place. A shadow falls the wrong way and remains wrong for a month. A distant swarm appears to climb vertically into the sky, though its members are plainly crossing level ground. A ridge echoes before it is struck. Then these things pass—or do not pass—and the animals continue.

From a high overlook, the whole region looks empty. Rust-colored shelves reach toward a flat horizon. Old channels branch and fade. A few dark heaps interrupt the plain. Above them, the air wavers in the heat.

Then one heap opens.

The plateau begins to move.
