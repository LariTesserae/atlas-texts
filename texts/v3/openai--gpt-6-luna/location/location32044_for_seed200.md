The basin is low enough to feel held in the earth’s palm. It extends farther than it first appears: beyond the nearest screen of leaves, another green wall rises, then another, until the whole region seems to be one continuous, crowded room with no ceiling.

The ground is young. Dark mineral mud lies in broad, unbroken sheets, firm in some places and yielding in others, with shallow water shining in its footprints. Here and there, low bulges of raw stone push through the soil—rounded, pale, and almost smooth, as if the land has only just learned how to be solid. Nothing has had time to cut deep channels or wear the rock into familiar shapes. Water collects in hollows, spreads thinly through the roots, and goes no farther. The earth looks unfinished, but not unstable. It has settled into its first arrangement and stays there.

Heat fills the basin without a visible source. The air is close and wet, beading on leaves and gathering in the hollows of stone. A dimness hangs beneath the vegetation: not night, exactly, but a green-gold dusk that persists even when the sky above is bright. The canopy is woven from large, overlapping leaves, glossy with moisture. Fernlike fans and thick-stemmed plants crowd the gaps below. Their roots make a dense net across the mud, holding it in place. Flowers are scarce, but small pale growths bloom along trunks and stones, each one giving off a faint, cool gleam.

Life is everywhere, and rarely alone. The vegetation trembles with insects—beetles tucked beneath leaves, winged things hovering in the humid air, soft-bodied creatures clinging to every damp surface. Larger animals move through the undergrowth in short, deliberate passages: long-necked grazers nosing the water films; broad, low-backed animals carrying mats of moss; small bright-eyed scavengers slipping between their legs. They do not flee at the sight of one another. Each seems to have a place in a settled order, a route through the plants, a patch of shade or shallow water it returns to.

The sound is immense but strangely even. Insects rasp in a continuous blanket. Birds and other, harder-to-place callers answer from different distances, their cries overlapping until no single voice dominates. Water ticks from leaf to leaf. Somewhere in the vegetation, a heavy animal gives a deep, slow call at regular intervals. The basin is loud in the way a full room is loud: not with commotion, but with every space occupied. If something falls, or a creature moves quickly, the sound is swallowed back into the larger chorus almost at once.

Nothing seems to be advancing. The same droplets cling to the same curled leaves; the same shallow pools hold the same dark reflections. Plants do not noticeably reach or wither. Animals feed, rest, and pass along their habitual paths, but the patterns neither multiply nor collapse. Even the clouds, glimpsed through occasional gaps in the canopy, appear fixed above the basin. It is not frozen—there is breath in it, and hunger, and a thousand small motions—but all motion returns to its place.

The basin feels less like a wilderness still becoming and more like a world caught at the moment just after its beginning: abundant, warm, and crowded, yet not yet worn down by time. The stones are new to the air. The water has not chosen a direction. And beneath the unending chorus, the land holds steady, as if it has found a form it intends to keep.
