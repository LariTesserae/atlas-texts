The hollow lies high in the mountain, where the air ought to be thin and cold. Instead, the stone gives off a steady heat. It rises through the soles of your feet and gathers beneath the low roof of the basin, turning the darkness close and feverish. No flame burns here. The warmth comes from deep seams in the rock, and the rocks have been warming this place for longer than anything living can remember.

The hollow is small: a bowl of black basalt, bounded by walls worn smooth in some places and fluted in others. The floor is not quite level. It is crusted with pale mineral scales that crack under weight, revealing darker, softer layers beneath. There are no pools, no trickles, no damp margins. Only, in the deepest cracks, a faint slickness that may be brine or may be something the stone sweats and takes back. Dust hangs in the air but never travels far. It settles into every crease and softens every edge.

It is nearly dark even when the sky outside is bright. The roof overhangs the bowl, and the walls drink what little light reaches them. Across the floor, the mineral crust catches a dull, intermittent gleam—not enough to see by, just enough to suggest that the ground is freckled with eyes.

The eyes belong to the animals.

They fill the hollow. They pack the cracks, lie layered beneath the scales of mineral, cling in dark fans to the walls. Some are no bigger than seeds, many-legged and pale, with bodies so thin they seem pressed from the rock. Others have heavy, jointed shells and forelimbs like hooked stones. A few are tall as a person, though their legs fold close beneath them and their heads are hidden under ridged plates. Nothing here is solitary. Where one creature moves, the surface trembles with the movements of hundreds beneath it.

At first the basin seems still. Then a slow migration passes through it: not a wave exactly, but a change in pressure, a leaning of bodies toward one of the warm seams. The crowd parts without opening, each creature sliding into the space vacated by another. The pale crust rises and settles as if the floor were breathing. High on the wall, a dense black veil peels loose and descends, creatures stepping over one another so quietly that the fall makes no sound.

They make almost no audible noise. Their lives are written in contact instead: claws tapping through stone, shells brushing, a faint shiver transmitted from wall to floor. Put a hand against the basalt and you can feel the hollow speaking through your bones. Now and then a single dry click carries across the basin. Everything stills around it. After a moment, the crowd resumes its patient rearrangement.

There is no vegetation. Nothing takes root in the crust or softens the stone. The animals feed on mineral films, on one another, on the thin residue left behind by older generations. A dead creature does not remain dead for long. Its shell is picked clean, scraped, and fitted into some crevice; its softer parts vanish beneath the floor. The living have made an ecology out of heat, salt, and the bodies of the living. They are not flourishing in spite of the barrenness. The barrenness is part of their abundance.

Scattered among them are a few human-shaped constructions, old enough that the hollow has grown around them. Low stone pillars, half-buried lintels, a little chamber with its roof collapsed inward. They are not machines and do not seem to have been built for the creatures. Their surfaces are worn smooth by heat and by the passage of bodies, though nothing has carved them. In the chamber’s shadow, animals have made a dense, shifting nest of themselves, leaving one narrow doorway open. No one knows whether the opening is an accident. It has stayed open for ages.

The hollow keeps its own order. Heat rises through certain seams and fades at others; the creatures follow it, gathering until the stone is crowded beyond belief, then thinning as the warm pulse moves elsewhere. When the mountain cools, they climb the walls and press together beneath the roof. When it warms again, they pour down—not rushing, never scattering, but flowing as a single multitude that happens to be made of many bodies.

Stand at the edge and the place feels sealed from the world above: a high, dry chamber without sky, quiet as ash. Stay longer and you begin to sense the scale of its motion. Every crack is occupied. Every shadow is busy. The whole basin is a single animal’s nest, and the nest is listening.
