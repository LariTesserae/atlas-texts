The district is called the Fold, though nothing in it visibly bends. It occupies a cavity in a low shelf of new basalt, just beneath the seafloor: a hollow no wider than a town square, packed so tightly with streets, ducts, towers, and retaining ribs that there is scarcely any open water between one structure and the next.

Everything is submerged. Water fills the streets and stairwells, rests behind windows, courses through the walls. It is cold enough to keep the stone’s color—slate, iron-black, the occasional blue shine of fresh fracture. The rock has not had time to soften. Its edges are sharp, its surfaces ridged with the first slow scars of erosion. Here and there, the city’s builders have anchored a building directly into a raw basalt tooth, as if the settlement grew from the wound in the shelf.

The buildings are small, but numerous: narrow façades stacked into one another, balconies facing only a few meters of water, bridges threaded through gaps too tight for any ordinary street. Some rooms are sealed and dry; others are deliberately flooded, with their furnishings fixed to the walls and their doors opening downward into the current. The district has a municipal hall, a market, a turbine court, and what may once have been a plaza. The plaza is now a knot of pillars and conduits, all surfaces claimed by useful things. Pipes cross above cables; cables pass through the stems of structural braces. There is no vacant corner. Even the spaces between buildings have been given a purpose: water storage, heat exchange, pressure control, light.

The light is startlingly ordinary. It arrives in a pale, even wash from nowhere that can be found. It brightens the upper stone and leaves the undersides of bridges in soft shadow; it gleams along the metal rails and makes the water look clear rather than black. Occasionally the whole district dims by a fraction, then returns to its former brightness. The residents say this is the Fold breathing. The engineers say the light is being bent through the rock by a system whose diagrams never quite agree with one another.

There are no animals here. Nothing flutters away from a lamp, nothing noses through the silt. The water holds a faint mineral haze, but it is not alive. In a few protected seams, sparse vegetation has taken hold: flat, dark fronds pinned to the stone, and threadlike green growth gathered around warm outflows. It barely moves. The city’s machines do most of the moving for it.

And the Fold is never quiet. Pumps knock in a steady, heavy rhythm. Valves slam shut with a sound like a distant door in a vast building. Water rushes through narrow channels, whining as it passes, and the whole cavity carries every vibration from one end to the other. Voices travel poorly, but tools, motors, and the shifting stone are loud enough to make conversation unnecessary. At intervals, a deep note rolls through the district. It seems to come from the basalt itself.

The note marks a change in pressure. The Fold responds by degrees: shutters turn, walkways pivot, storage bladders swell against their frames. A surge enters through a fissure at the district’s lowest point, passes through the old turbine court, and leaves by a higher vent. The cycle lifts silt, flexes the cables, and makes the small plants comb flat against the rock. Then the water settles, though it never becomes still.

Nothing here is very high above anything else. There is no skyline—only layers. From certain windows, one can see through three buildings and emerge, impossibly, at the outer wall of the cavity, where open sea presses against a transparent pressure membrane. Beyond it, the water darkens at once. The Fold’s lights do not reach far enough to reveal what lies outside, and the sea gives no sign that anything lives there.

The district feels both recently made and impossibly old. Its stone is new, its surfaces unfinished; its infrastructure has been repaired so often that the original design has become a rumor. The Fold is a hidden place that has learned to be crowded without ever becoming large. It holds itself together by adjusting—pumping, flexing, sealing, brightening—while the ocean leans against it from every side.
