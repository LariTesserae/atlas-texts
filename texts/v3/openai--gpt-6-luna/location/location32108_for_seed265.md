The dark here is complete enough to have weight. It lies on the skin like warm wet cloth, and no amount of waiting makes it thin. There is no moon behind it, no distant town-glow, no pale seam at the horizon. Eyes open and remain open; the world does not arrive.

The ground is low and newly made. It may be a coast, though there is no visible sea—only shallow water spread over a plain in sheets and channels, warm as bathwater, with firmer ground rising between them in blunt, broken ridges. The ridges have sharp edges, as if the earth lifted them only recently and has not yet learned how to wear them down. Mud gathers in their hollows. Here and there, a crust of mineral breaks underfoot, thin and brittle as old glaze. Beneath it the soil is soft, still settling.

The water is everywhere, but not in a hurry. It laps quietly at the ankles of the land. It gathers in long, shallow basins and seeps from cracks in the ridges. Sometimes a person can hear it dripping from a rock that has no overhang; sometimes water runs uphill along a groove, pauses, and sinks into the ground. Rain falls often, though its arrival is known less by sound than by the sudden coolness of drops on the face. It is warm rain. It leaves the air close and smelling of iron, wet stone, and something faintly animal.

Vegetation is sparse and low. A few tough reeds stand in the channels, their stems packed close together; flat mats of dark growth cling to the mud. There are places where the ground feels furry under the soles, though nothing there rises high enough to brush a knee. Most of the plain is open, yet never empty. Sounds and movements are crowded into it: little bodies slipping through water, the soft collapse of mud, a tremor traveling along a ridge, the touch of something passing near the hand.

The animals are abundant. They are seldom seen, and here “seen” may be the wrong word. Their presence accumulates in other ways. Small, many-footed things cross the wet ground in close, dry pattering bursts. Larger animals move through the channels without making a wake. Once in a while, something breathes beside you with a breath much too deep for its size, then continues on. The water carries clicks, taps, and brief shivers of sound, but no birdsong. Nothing calls to announce itself. The creatures seem to know the dark too well to waste noise on it.

Far across the plain—though “far” is unreliable—stand scattered industrial structures. Their shapes are easiest to understand by touch: a low wall of warm, riveted metal; a cylinder sunk halfway into mud; rails that run straight for a while and then curve back toward their beginning. Most of the buildings are small or half-lost, with their machinery exposed. A few engines are still turning somewhere inside them. Their slow rotation comes through the ground as a faint, regular pressure, like a pulse felt through a mattress. There is no clatter, no roar. The mechanisms work with the patience of things that have never been switched off and no longer remember what they were built to do.

These structures do not make the plain feel inhabited. They feel like marks left by an absent intention. One pump draws water from the earth and sends it through pipes that return to the same place. A gantry hangs over a channel, lifting a chain one link at a time; the links vanish into darkness above and reappear below. Rust gathers on the machinery, but never flakes away. It stays soft and damp, as if the metal is slowly producing it.

Distance behaves oddly. A ridge reached after an hour of walking may be the same ridge that was just beyond the first pool. A sound made behind you can arrive ahead, softened and old, as if it had crossed a great plain before the breath that made it. Sometimes footsteps seem to continue for several paces after you stop. Sometimes the ground under a person’s palm is dry on one side and submerged on the other, though the hand has not moved. These are not great upheavals. Nothing flashes or tears open. The place alters its agreements by degrees, and then keeps them.

Time is similarly damped. Watermarks remain on the walls for years, perhaps, while a newly opened crack in the earth can take a season to widen by the thickness of a hair. The machines turn. The animals pass. The rain comes and comes again. The plain changes so slowly that it is possible to mistake change for memory: to remember a channel where no channel lies, or to be certain that a structure has always stood at your shoulder.

There are long stretches in which the only sound is a low murmur from the water and the minute shifting of life in the reeds. It is not silence. It is a crowded quiet, full of small events that do not need to be heard by people. Once, from somewhere beneath the flooded ground, comes a single heavy knock. The animals pause. The machinery keeps its slow rotation. Then the water closes over the sound, and the plain waits again in its warm, lightless openness.
