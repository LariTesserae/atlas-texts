The High Fold is a country lifted abruptly out of the lowlands, a broad shelf of dark rock with its upper rim lost in the weather. From below, it looks like a single wall. Above, it opens into a world of long plateaus, split basins, and ridges that recede one behind another until distance takes on the quality of weather. The air is cold and thin. Daylight is ordinary, clear when the clouds break, but the sun seems to warm only the exposed faces of stone.

The rock is young. Its broken edges still look newly made: black basalt shelves, pale seams of crushed stone, angular talus that has not yet settled into gentler slopes. Ravines cut sharply through the plateau, some scarcely wider than a street and deep enough to hold their own darkness at noon. Water appears unevenly. Rain passes in brief, fine sheets; cloud settles against the high ground and leaves beads of moisture on walls and ledges. Meltwater and condensation find the faults, then run underground or gather in shallow pools where the stone dips. In the driest stretches, people walk from cistern to cistern. In the ravines, narrow streams thread between boulders and vanish beneath the next fall of rock.

Vegetation keeps low. Grey-green mats cling to cracks, and wiry grasses grow in sheltered hollows, bending flat under frost and rising again when the air softens. There are a few stunted shrubs along the watercourses, their branches combed bare by wind. Most color belongs to the stone: iron-red scars, white mineral crusts, the bruised blue of deep shade. After rain, the whole plateau darkens and shines briefly, as if it had been polished; by morning, the moisture is gone from the open ground, though it remains in joints, cellars, and the folds of clothing.

The Fold is not empty. Its settlements crowd the places where water and shelter coincide. They are built low and close, often in clusters so dense that roofs nearly touch across the lanes. Houses are assembled from fitted slabs, rubble, packed earth, and whatever timber can be carried up from below. Flat roofs are weighted with stone. Doorways are small; windows are narrower still, shuttered against the cold. Stairs climb the slopes between houses, and covered passages run beneath them where the streets cross exposed ground. A town can occupy an entire basin, with its outer walls following the curve of the rock and its highest roofs disappearing into fog.

There is little machinery. People move water by hand, haul stone on sledges, and mend their walls with simple tools. Rope bridges span some of the smaller clefts; elsewhere, paths have been cut directly into the cliff face, with shallow footholds worn smooth by generations of boots. At the larger settlements, low stone channels carry runoff toward communal tanks. The channels are carefully made, though their courses bend around older buildings and follow the accidents of the ground. Nothing here is quite straight for long.

Life gathers densely around these constructions. Small, thick-furred animals nest in the warm gaps beneath roofs and stairs. Pale cliff-birds pack the ledges beyond the towns, leaving the rock stippled with nests; in the morning, they lift together and drift over the ravines in quiet, wavering flocks. In the basins, long-legged grazers move between sparse patches of grass, stopping to nose at the damp earth. They are not tame, but they know the settlements well and come close for scraps or shelter from sudden weather. At night, there are tracks in the dust of courtyards and prints along the cistern rims. The people share their cramped ground with more animals than they could ever count, and have built their daily habits around that fact.

For all this life, the Fold is mostly quiet. Sound does not carry easily through cold air and broken terrain. A hammer strikes somewhere among the houses, a door shuts, a flock shifts on a ledge; each noise arrives softened, then is taken by the stone. The ravines keep small sounds. Water ticking into a basin can be heard across a room. Wind is less a constant roar than a pressure against walls, rising now and then through a notch in the ridge and stirring loose grit across the roofs.

The settlements are crowded, but the country around them is immense. Beyond the last roof, paths divide and dwindle among ridges whose far slopes are still unmapped by the people who live nearest them. Weather travels across the plateau in broad, patient fronts. A cloud may linger against one escarpment for days while the next basin remains bright. The ground changes slowly: a block slips from a wall, a ravine widens by a hand’s breadth, a channel shifts after an unusually wet season. The Fold is still becoming itself, but its making is measured in generations.

From a high ridge, the towns appear as dark knots in a pale expanse, too compact for their surroundings and too numerous to be temporary. Their fires make faint amber marks at dusk. Then the light leaves the stone, and the buildings merge with it: one more set of ledges, cavities, and sharp-edged formations in a land that has not yet softened.
