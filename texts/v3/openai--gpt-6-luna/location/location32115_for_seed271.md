The hollow is small enough to cross in a few minutes, though its enclosing slopes make it feel self-contained. It lies low between rounded shoulders of pale stone, a shallow basin worn smooth by water that has long since found its way elsewhere. The ground is mostly exposed: grey-beige limestone, flattened shelves, and fine grit collected in the seams. Nothing here looks newly broken. Edges have softened, hollows have filled, and the land seems to have arrived at a shape it intends to keep.

Moisture comes and goes without much ceremony. After a brief rain, dark patches spread across the stone and shallow water gathers in a few depressions. In dry spells, the basin is not quite dry: coolness lingers in cracks, and a faint damp sheen sometimes forms beneath the overhanging rock. The air is warm, but shaded. Light reaches the hollow as a dull, indirect wash, paling the stone without ever making it bright. The deepest recesses stay the color of old ash.

Vegetation is scattered rather than absent. A few tough, low plants root in the grit-filled seams: wiry grasses, small grey-green rosettes, and tufts of something almost mosslike where water collects longest. They leave wide spaces between themselves. There are no trees to break the outline of the slopes, only an occasional shrub held close to the rock. Nothing flutters or stirs among them. No birds call from above; no insects rasp in the grass. The plants stand in a quiet that feels ordinary, not enchanted—simply a place where few things find enough reason to live.

Near one edge of the basin are a handful of low stone remnants. They are not grand enough to suggest a town: a short wall, a roofless enclosure, several blocks set in a rough curve. Their stone matches the surrounding rock, weathered into the same muted tones. They might once have sheltered people from rain or divided one patch of ground from another. Now they are almost indistinguishable from the natural shelves, except where a line is too straight to have been made by water.

Sound is sparse. A drop occasionally ticks into a shallow pool. Wind, when it reaches the hollow, passes over the rim and seems to lose its force before touching the floor. Footsteps would carry plainly across the stone, then fade against the slopes. Most of the time there is only the low hush of air in the shaded places and the faint, intermittent sound of water finding its way through cracks.

Nothing in the basin presses toward change. Rain darkens it; warmth dries it; the same small plants persist in the same sheltered seams. The old stones settle without quite collapsing. Water returns to the same hollows, and the hollows hold it until it disappears. The place is not frozen. It is simply settled: a quiet, modest landscape whose few signs of life and human presence have worn down toward the same patient stillness.
