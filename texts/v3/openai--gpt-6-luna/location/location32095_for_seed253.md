The plateau rises beneath the sea like a mountain range that has forgotten the sky.

Its highest ridges are still far below the surface. Water fills every valley, every hollow, every chamber in the green-black architecture. From above there would be no island, no coast—only a long swell in the dark, a broad back lifting out of the abyss. The land is high by the measure of the deep: shelves and escarpments climb through layers of cold water, while the seafloor falls away on either side into distances the eye cannot hold.

Nothing here is quite natural, though everything is alive. The ground is built from plant matter: braided rootstone, compressed stems, old fronds fused into walls, arches, terraces, and causeways. It has the look of a city after all its builders have vanished, except the builders have not vanished; they have become the city. Thick trunks emerge from the slopes like pillars. Their crowns spread through the water in immense, slow fans, dimly green even in the darkness. Between them are open halls, broad enough to be called plazas, where the floor is a mat of fine, ancient fibers and the ceiling is a canopy of overlapping leaves.

The oldest parts have been worn almost smooth. Water has passed over them for ages, carrying silt and the powdered remains of leaves. Corners have rounded. Stairs descend into blank walls; arches have been softened until they resemble natural tunnels. Yet the place is not ruined. It has settled into a final form, and nothing appears to be breaking down. The structures hold their shape as if growth and erosion reached an agreement long ago: a little building, a little wearing-away, no change large enough to notice.

There is room between the great masses. A terrace may stretch for a long way without crossing a trunk or meeting a wall. In these open spaces, pale water drifts through the darkness. The sparse light comes from the vegetation itself—not bright enough to illuminate the whole landscape, only enough to gather along an edge or catch the underside of an arch. Here and there, blue-green pinpricks hang in the water like stars seen through deep cloud. Beyond their small reach, the structures become silhouettes, then disappear.

The airlessness of the place is complete. Its silence is not. Water presses sound everywhere. Across the plateau comes a steady, low thunder, the far-off voice of currents moving through the immense plantwork. Closer by, leaves scrape against one another with a dry, papery rasp, though they are saturated through. Long stems groan where they rest against their neighbors. The sound has no clear beginning and no conclusion. It is a continuous weather, loud enough to fill the great halls, constant enough that it seems part of the stone—or the thing that has replaced stone.

Animals are plentiful, but they do not crowd the open ground. They live in the architecture’s many seams. Schools of narrow, translucent fish thread through root lattices. Larger bodies cruise above the terraces, turning slowly when they meet a column, their bellies faintly lit. Small creatures graze the walls in dense, ordered bands, leaving clean paths through the darker growth. In the high branches, broad-mouthed animals hang motionless and sift the water. Their stillness is so complete that they can be mistaken for knots in the wood until they blink.

The largest animals seem to belong to the plateau as much as its plants do. They pass through the built corridors without disturbing them, using the broad avenues as though they remember who made them. No chase, hunt, or migration is visible. Life is abundant here, but not hurried. Every creature occupies its old place. The plants lean in their fixed directions; the animals return to familiar hollows; the current pours through the same open channels. There is no season beneath this much water, only an enduring coolness and the slow pressure of the deep.

The landscape gives the impression of a civilization with no tools and no separate builders: a world made by growth, whose inhabitants never had to distinguish house from sheltering plant, road from root, monument from habitat. The whole plateau may be one organism, or a multitude whose work has become indistinguishable. It offers no signs of intention that a human visitor could read. Its passages go somewhere, but not necessarily to a destination. Its towers rise, but not toward the sun. They rise because rising is what their ancient living material has always done.

At the highest ridge, the vegetation thins into a broad, dark meadow of short fronds. The plants there are close-cropped by feeding, so the terrain opens into long views: a succession of terraces dropping away, each edged with black trunks, each vanishing into water. Far off, another green-built height emerges from the dark, equally enormous and equally quiet. Between them, the abyss is crowded with sound.

Nothing announces a change in this place. No current turns sharply; no growth visibly unfolds; no old structure collapses. The sea, the plants, the animals, and the constructed ground persist together in an equilibrium so old it has passed beyond patience. It is not waiting for something to happen.

It has already become itself.
