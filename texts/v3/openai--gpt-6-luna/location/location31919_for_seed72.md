The district occupies a single hollow beneath the city, not large enough to be called a cavern so much as a room the earth has not finished making. Its ceiling is low and uneven. In places, black rock presses down close above the roofs; elsewhere, the stone opens into damp, raw pockets where mineral crust is only beginning to gather. The ground is soft at its edges, a mixture of clay, gravel, and newly fallen fragments. Nothing here looks ancient. The walls seem recently exposed, and the floor still has the tentative shape of something settling.

Water stands just below the surface. It seeps through seams in the rock and gathers in shallow channels that run between the buildings. The water is warm enough to breathe from. It carries a faint metallic taste, and wherever it pools, the air above it trembles. Condensation beads on pipes, rails, and doorways, then drops with patient regularity into the channels. Some walls weep continuously; others remain dry for days before darkening all at once.

The settlement is built around this wetness. A handful of squat structures—pump sheds, a boiler house, storage rooms, a small assembly hall—sit on raised concrete pads. Their walls are patched with sheet metal and old tile. Narrow walkways bridge the channels, and thick pipes run overhead in bundled lines, disappearing into the rock or looping back into the machinery. There are lamps, but not many. Their weak amber light leaves whole corners unseen, and turns the water beneath the grates the color of old tea.

The machinery works without hurry. Pumps thud through long cycles; valves open with a low groan, and somewhere behind the walls an engine maintains its steady, laborious rotation. The noise is substantial, but not frantic: metal knocking, water moving through pipes, the hum of current. Sounds travel strangely in the close air. A small clink can seem to come from the far end of the district, while a large machine may sound as though it is operating just behind one’s head.

Then the animals fill every silence.

They occupy the channels in pale, braided masses: blind fish no longer than a finger, slick eels coiling under the grates, soft-bodied things that cling to the warm undersides of the walkways. Tiny crustaceans pulse through the shallows in such numbers that the water appears to be moving even when it is still. They gather around warm outlets and spread away from cold ones, making slow, living maps of the hidden heat. At night—though night has no clear meaning here—they climb the damp walls and cluster along the pipes. Their faint clicking and rasping mingle with the pumps until it becomes difficult to tell which parts of the district are mechanical.

There is little vegetation. A few stubborn green films stain the stone near the lamps. Thin threads of fungus hang from a crack in the ceiling, pale and nearly transparent. They do not make the place feel fertile. Instead, they suggest that life has found the smallest possible footholds and is waiting to see what happens next.

The heat is constant, but not oppressive in the way of a furnace. It is the heat of a place that has never quite cooled: wet air pressed between stone and machinery, warm water moving underfoot, bodies packed close in the dark. The district smells of mineral water, hot iron, oil, and the faint animal musk of the channels. Its boundaries are easy to find. Beyond the last walkway, there is only low rock, darkness, and the sound of seepage.

Over time, the channels widen by small degrees. Sediment shifts. A ledge slumps; a new pocket collects water. The inhabitants repair what they can, raising another walkway or moving a pipe a few inches higher, but the place is not yet stable enough to keep a final shape. The machines hold the district together for now. The animals fill what the machines leave open. Beneath the city, among raw stone and warm water, an underground neighborhood continues to come into being—slowly, noisily, and almost entirely in the dark.
