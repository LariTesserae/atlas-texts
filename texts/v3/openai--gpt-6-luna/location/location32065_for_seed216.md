The country is called the High Quiet, though no one agrees whether it is one place or many places joined by a mistake.

It lies above the weather of the lower world. Warm cloud drifts beneath it, snagged on the underside of its terraces; rain arrives already softened into mist. Here, at the heights, the air is mild and close. Water beads on every surface and gathers in dark seams. It drips for a long time before reaching anything that could be called ground.

There is no natural ground.

The High Quiet is built from end to end. Its floors are layered mats of living root, woven and thickened until they bear the weight of forests. Its ridges are braided trunks, its cliffs are walls of interlocked stalk and resin, its distant mountains are immense, sloping constructions of pale timber, packed moss, and old green growth. Nothing looks newly made. The work has been weathered into itself over ages: edges rounded, joints swollen shut, stairways grown into shelves, shelves into valleys. Yet the hand of making is everywhere. Roots have been trained around doorways large enough for a river. Vines are knotted into bridges that cross distances too great to see across. The occasional exposed stone is squared, drilled, or set in a repeating pattern, then mostly swallowed by plants.

The builders used simple things. Hand-cut wedges. Bone needles. Fire, perhaps, though the High Quiet seems to have forgotten what fire is for. Its oldest constructions are so vast and so patient that they can be mistaken for geology. A wall runs along the horizon for half a day’s walk, then bends upward and becomes a canopy. A flight of stairs ascends through mist, passes behind a hanging garden, and comes out beneath the same sky it left. There are no machines. There are no engines. The great works move because they grow, settle, and take on water.

Vegetation is not on the landscape. It is the landscape. Every exposed surface is a skin of leaf, fiber, lichen, flower, or tightly layered frond. In the wide open places, grasslike plants lie in immense rippling sheets, each blade taller than a house and thin enough to show the dim light through it. Forests grow in rooms, across ceilings, and down the sheer outer faces of the world. Some trees have been trained into arches; others have grown through the arches and made them part of their trunks. Their leaves are waxy and dark, with fine pale veins that hold droplets like strings of beads.

The scale makes ordinary distances unreliable. A traveler may cross a bridge for three days without seeing its far anchorage. The bridge may turn out to be a single root, or a series of roots pretending to be one. From certain ridges, the land appears to fall away into a vast, green interior: galleries, groves, open courts, and terraces descending through haze. The eye cannot tell whether the lowest levels are near or simply small. Sometimes a cloud moves between two distant shelves and takes a week to pass.

For all this immensity, the place feels empty. There is too much room between its presences. A single flowering tree may stand alone on a terrace as wide as a province. A flock may cross the sky from one horizon to the other, then leave no movement behind it. Animals are abundant, but they are scattered through a wilderness of chambers, hollows, high corridors, and inaccessible crowns. One hears of whole herds living in the upper gardens, though a traveler might walk for months without meeting one.

The animals are quiet, too. Long-legged birds roost upside down beneath bridges. Pale burrowers tunnel through the woven floors, leaving brief, warm depressions in the moss. Little climbing mammals feed on the soft growth inside old beams. In the upper air, enormous drifting creatures sail without wings, their bodies translucent and veined like leaves; they pass so high above the terraces that they resemble stains moving through the dimness. Predators are present, but rarely seen. Sometimes the birds in a far grove turn all at once toward an empty patch of shade. The shade does not change. After a while, the birds turn back.

Sound travels oddly. Rain can be seen falling a long way off but makes no sound when it reaches the roof overhead. A person’s voice carries only a few paces, then seems to enter something soft and be held there. Water makes the clearest noise: a slow, hollow plink from a gutter, the faint whisper of a stream behind a wall, the sigh of a reservoir emptying into unseen roots. At night—or what passes for night—the structures creak very gently as they take on dampness. The whole country seems to breathe through its leaves, but the sound is too small to hear.

The light is dim at every hour. There may be a sun beyond the layered cloud, but the High Quiet receives it only as a mild blanching of the mist. Leaves glow at their edges. Pools hold a weak, bronze-colored brightness that does not seem to come from above. In deep interiors, pale flowers open when no light reaches them. Their petals show a color the eye cannot keep: blue for a moment, then the memory of blue.

Water collects in shallow basins made by the builders, in cupped leaves, in the joints of the living floors. It runs through narrow channels cut into the construction, sometimes down, sometimes sideways, sometimes upward for a little way along the underside of a beam before it falls. This is not considered remarkable. The water follows the old routes, and the old routes are what the water knows. In some places, a stream vanishes through a doorway and emerges from a tree on another terrace. In others, rain pools on a ceiling, reflecting a garden that is not there. The people who know the High Quiet say that water is only lost when it forgets what it was meant to connect.

Space has similar habits. Corridors lengthen when nobody is using them. A distant tower may become reachable after years of travel, then recede again once it has been visited. A traveler can pass through a grove at dawn and find, at dusk, that the trees have arranged themselves around the path in a different order—not moved, exactly, but remembered differently. Maps are made as songs, knots, or sequences of turns, and are never trusted twice in the same way.

Still, the place is settled. Its strangeness is old enough to have become part of its balance. Terraces slump, and new roots bind them. A gallery disappears under growth, and another opens in its place. Whole sections of the High Quiet sink by inches over generations, while the gardens above continue to flower. Nothing seems to be in a hurry. Even the erosion has a finished quality, as if the country has already passed through its collapse and is now holding the shape left behind.

In the broadest openings, the emptiness becomes almost like weather. Mist gathers there without clouding anything. A person may stand at the edge of a green plain, surrounded by an architecture of leaves and old handwork, and feel that the land is waiting for something too large to arrive.

Then, far away, a bell sounds once.

No one can tell whether it came from a tower, an animal, or the country itself.
