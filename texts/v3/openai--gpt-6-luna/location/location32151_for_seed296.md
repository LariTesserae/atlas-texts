The country stands above the weather.

Its old mountains have been worn down almost to shoulders: long, rounded backs of dark stone, broad enough to carry whole districts, then abruptly cut apart by ravines so deep that their bottoms are seldom visible. From the high ground, the land seems nearly level. Walk toward its edge, though, and the plateau falls away in a sequence of immense shelves, each one furred with green and half-lost in drifting cloud. Far below, water shines now and then in the dark—never a river for long, just a brief thread appearing between folds of rock before it disappears again.

The stone is ancient, gray-black and smooth where rain has worried it for ages. In places it wears a skin of pale mineral crust, like ash. The ravines are full of old shapes: arches left standing after the cliffs around them softened; narrow stone bridges that were once whole ridges; hollows worn deep enough to hold a pocket of mist all day. Nothing here looks newly broken. Even the landslides seem settled into the landscape, their edges rounded, their scars already mossed over.

Moisture arrives irregularly. A fine rain may drift across the plateau for an hour, too light to bead on a coat, yet enough to darken every stone. More often the damp comes from below: cloud climbing the ravines, condensation gathering on the undersides of overhangs, a slow seep through cracks in the rock. Water collects in shallow basins and iron gutters, then spills over the lip of the plateau in silver veils. The sound of it is usually distant. Up close, it is a soft, steady threading through channels.

Vegetation takes every place the stone allows. Dark moss fills the joints in the pavement; thick mats of small, waxy leaves soften the ledges. Low wind-bent trees grow in the sheltered hollows, their roots exposed and twisted over the rock like old cable. In the wettest clefts, tall ferns crowd the paths and bead with cold water. There are flowering plants, but they keep their colors subdued—smoke-blue, bruised violet, a deep rust-red glimpsed beneath the leaves. The green is lush, but not bright. It seems to absorb what little light reaches the plateau.

The settlement is built along one of the broadest shelves, where an ancient basin holds rain and mist. From a distance it resembles a dark seam in the stone: roofs pressed low against the wind, stacks and towers rising only where the ground permits. The oldest buildings are made from the plateau itself, fitted blocks worn smooth at the corners. Newer structures cling to them in layers—metal walkways, pipes, lift towers, enclosed channels for hauling water and ore. Whole streets pass under overhangs or through tunnels cut in the rock. The town does not spread so much as thicken.

Its machines are practical and old-fashioned in scale: water wheels set in narrow falls, engines housed in squat brick sheds, winches hauling freight up from ledges below. Their metal is dark with damp. Steam escapes from vents and hangs low, mingling with the cloud. The works run intermittently, responding to the water: a wheel begins to turn after rain; a cable groans under load; a distant pump starts, stops, starts again. Most of the machinery is quiet enough to be mistaken for the mountain settling.

Light has trouble here. The sky is often the color of unpolished tin, and the sun, when it appears, comes through as a pale disk behind cloud. At midday the streets can feel like evening. Lamps burn in windows and along the covered ways, their amber glow caught in wet stone. At night, the settlement is a scatter of low lights under a ceiling of mist. The heights beyond it vanish first; then the ravines; then even the nearest slopes seem to withdraw.

There are few animals. A small, dark grazer may be seen on a far shelf, standing motionless against the wind. Pale moths shelter under eaves. In the deepest clefts, something occasionally moves through the ferns, but no one agrees on what it is. Birds are rarer still, and those that cross the plateau fly low, following the ravines rather than the open sky. Their calls carry a long way when they do come, startling in the quiet.

The place is not still, despite its age. Water rearranges itself from week to week. Moss creeps across abandoned steps. The cloud banks rise and drain from the valleys in slow cycles, exposing a ledge here, swallowing a tower there. In certain hollows, the air flows upward even when the wind is falling; leaves tremble without a sound. People say the plateau breathes through its cracks. The older residents make no mystery of it. They simply avoid building over the openings.

At the edge of town, a stairway descends to a lower shelf and then ends in a wall of cloud. The stone steps continue beyond sight, polished by generations of feet, though no one can say where they lead. Water runs down them in a thin, intermittent line. Sometimes, after rain, that line appears to climb.
