The place lies beneath a low, unremarkable stretch of ground. From above, it might be mistaken for a shallow valley, or for land that has not yet decided what shape to take. Below, the earth opens into a long basin of raw stone. Its walls are broken in planes, not worn into curves: fresh fractures, shelves that end abruptly, blocks still lodged where they split. Dust gathers in the angles. Nothing here has had time to become smooth.

The basin runs farther than the eye can follow. It is not a single cavern so much as a succession of connected hollows, joined by narrow necks and sudden broad chambers. The ceiling hangs low in some places, high in others; here and there, cracks reach upward through soil and root. Daylight falls through them in pale shafts, ordinary and steady, though its source is hidden. It touches the upper roofs first, then the ground in scattered squares. In the deeper passages, lamps of oil or tallow carry the light onward. The air is cool, with a dampness that comes and goes. Water beads on stone after a change in the weather, trickles briefly through seams, and vanishes into the rubble. There are shallow catch-pools in the hollows, some clear, some clouded by fresh grit.

The settlement fills nearly every part of the basin that can be made habitable. Houses stand close enough to share walls; stairways pass over lanes, and lanes duck beneath storage platforms. Timber frames brace stone fronts. Clay bricks, split slabs, woven screens, and bundles of reed are pressed into gaps where the rock is too irregular to build against. There is little ornament. A doorway is often simply a dark opening under a lintel; a window, a cloth-covered notch. Roofs are burdened with water jars, drying racks, baskets of gravel, and stones set in place against the tremor of settling ground.

The construction looks permanent from a distance, but up close it is all adjustment. Walls are newly raised, then shifted when the floor beneath them drops a hand’s breadth. A stair ends at a ledge that was not there last season. People carry stone away from one passage and stack it in another, widening a market nook or shoring up a low ceiling. No one has fully mastered the shape of the place. Its streets are less laid out than negotiated, built along the dry ribs of the basin and rerouted around fresh cracks. The city grows by filling openings, then discovering that the ground has made new ones.

There are workshops, though few machines: hand-saws, mallets, drills turned with a bow, stone mortars, simple kilns vented toward the daylight fissures. The sound of work remains low. A hammer’s note travels a long way through the stone and is quickly muffled by cloth, timber, and the close press of buildings. Most of the time the settlement makes only small sounds: water ticking from a seam into a jar, sandals on grit, a shutter tapping once in a draft, voices softened by low ceilings. From somewhere far off comes the brief scrape of a block being dragged into place.

Vegetation is scarce, and valued where it takes hold. Thin roots descend through ceiling cracks; pale shoots grow in the dampest courtyards beneath the light shafts. Pots of herbs line a few steps, set where the sun lingers longest. Moss gathers along the rims of cisterns. There is no enclosing forest below, only the occasional green mark on stone. Animals are rarer still: small burrowers in the rubble, a few insects drawn to lamps, birds that enter through the larger fissures and do not always find their way out again.

The place is busy without being loud. Water arrives irregularly. Dust falls from the ceiling after hard rain above. The ground settles in small increments, and the inhabitants have learned to notice its warnings: a new grit line beneath a wall, a jar trembling on a shelf, a crack that has turned pale at the edges. They build lightly where they can and heavily where they must. A lane kept clear for a year may be closed in a week; a raw cleft may become the next market street once someone finds a way to brace it.

Nothing here feels ancient. The stone has not been polished by rivers or softened by long passage. The buildings have not yet gathered the layers of age that make a city seem inevitable. This is a settlement inside an unfinished land: dense with lives and structures, but still being shaped by the blunt first motions of rock, water, and hands. The earth has opened; the people have moved in; neither has quite finished making room for the other.
