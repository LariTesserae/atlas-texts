The high country begins where the clouds get caught.

From below, it looks like a green wall rising out of the lowlands: a succession of cliffs, tilted shelves, and narrow terraces, all climbing toward a plateau so high that its far edge is lost in ordinary daylight. The ascent is not sharp everywhere. In places the mountain has opened into broad hanging valleys, old and settled, their floors carpeted in wet vegetation. Elsewhere, the rock rises sheer for thousands of meters, dark with seepage and striped by mineral colors—copper green, iron red, the milky white of salts slowly returning to the surface.

It is hot here, even at this height. Heat gathers in the stone and is given back after sunset; rain falls warm on the leaves. The air is thick with water, and the cliffs are always sweating. Mists form in ravines, but rarely hide the whole landscape. They cling in layers: a low river of vapor sliding through the trees, a veil caught on a ledge, a thin wreath drifting around the summit of a distant tower of rock. Sunlight passes through them without becoming golden or dim. Everything remains plainly visible, just softened at the edges, as if the country has been seen through a wet eye.

Water does not so much flow here as find its many patient ways downhill. It beads on leaves, runs in clear threads down cracks, gathers in dark bowls, then spills over the lip of a terrace into another bowl far below. The rivers are often only a few meters wide, but they fall for such distances that their sound reaches everywhere. The whole plateau murmurs and roars at once: close water underfoot, distant waterfalls in the rock, the broad hiss of rain passing through a canopy. Some falls seem to hang in the air for a moment before striking the basin beneath them. Their spray rises and darkens the cliff above as reliably as weather.

The forest is lush without being impenetrable. Trees grow in crowded stands along the wet slopes, but they leave long sightlines across the open valleys. Their trunks twist around mineral pillars and old stone, their roots descending over ledges like bundled cables. Broad leaves overlap in several tiers; smaller plants cover the gaps, and ferns, flowering mats, and pale creepers fill every damp shelf. At the highest elevations the trees become shorter and more widely spaced, but the ground does not grow bare. Mosses, tough grasses, and low shrubs hold the dark soil in place. Every exposed surface seems to be hosting something.

The animals are numerous, though most are heard before they are seen. High in the branches, flocks of black-glass birds click and whistle, their calls thrown back and forth between cliffs until a single bird seems to have multiplied into a crowd. Long-legged grazers move in herds across the plateau’s open meadows. Their hides are patterned like wet rock, and they pick their way through the grass with the caution of creatures accustomed to sudden edges. In the streams, bright amphibians cling to stone under the falls. At night, which is never wholly dark, small animals flicker in the foliage like sparks reflected in moving water.

There are stranger residents, too. On certain ledges, pale shapes can be seen standing motionless against the sky, too tall and narrow to be trees. They are rooted organisms, but their surfaces resemble polished mineral. During the heat of the day they gather moisture from the wind and drip it into shallow basins around their feet. The basins attract insects, the insects attract birds, and the birds scatter seeds into the cracks. In this way the peculiar growths have become part of the forest’s ordinary arrangements.

The plateau’s oldest feature is a ring of stone ridges around its central basin. They may once have been a crater, or the rim of something lifted from beneath the earth; time has made the difference difficult to see. The ridges are worn smooth, not crumbling, and their slopes have acquired the composure of ancient hills. In the basin, water gathers into a long, dark lake. Its surface is often perfectly calm despite the wind, reflecting the clouds with such accuracy that the sky seems to continue downward beneath it. Only the waterfalls disturb the reflection, making slow, circular bruises that spread toward the shore.

Scattered across the high country are structures. There are never many in one place: a narrow arch on a ridge, a low building half-grown into the roots of a tree, a series of white posts where a trail passes between two cliffs. Some appear ancient and weathered; others have no visible marks of age at all. They do not dominate the land. A traveler might walk a day without seeing one, then notice that a distant waterfall runs through the center of a structure without wetting it, or that a stone doorway stands alone in a meadow with no wall attached.

The technology here is quiet, and difficult to separate from geology. A few bridges cross ravines without hanging from anything. They are thin, translucent planes that gather dew and shine faintly at dawn, though the light at dawn is no different from the light everywhere else. Some paths alter their slope as a person walks them, not abruptly, but by a degree so slight the body accepts the change before the mind notices. In the lake, submerged lines of blue-white illumination can be seen at certain angles. They do not pulse or blink. They hold steady, like another kind of mineral deposit.

Nothing appears to be in a hurry. The bridges do not move visibly. The stone ridges have held their shape for ages. The forest advances a little at a time, its roots finding weaknesses in the rock, and the rock yields on a scale too slow for the eye. Even the clouds seem to cross the plateau reluctantly. Changes happen, but they are measured in seasons, generations, the gradual filling of a basin with silt, the slow migration of a waterfall from one notch to another.

The sound, by contrast, is large. Water makes the baseline, a constant layered noise that never quite resolves into silence. Above it are the calls of animals, leaf-friction, the hollow knocks of falling fruit, and the occasional deep note from somewhere inside the mountain. The deep note comes only a few times a year. It lasts several minutes, audible across the whole plateau, and leaves the surface of the lake vibrating in fine rings. No one knows whether it comes from the rock, from the structures, or from something living below the water.

People who cross the high country learn not to trust distance. A cliff that looks near may take half a day to reach; a waterfall that seems to fall from the sky may begin in a stream only a few meters behind the ridge. The paths are marked, but not always with signs. A particular stand of red-leaved trees means there is safe water nearby. A certain kind of birdcall means the next shelf is narrow. The scattered structures offer shelter, though they do not always open to the same side of the landscape twice.

The place holds height, heat, and water together without resolving them. It is a mountain country, but not a barren one; a wet forest, but not a lowland; a technological place, though its mechanisms rarely announce themselves. Its cliffs have been there long enough to become hospitable. Its forests have had time to learn the shape of the stone. And beneath the steady roar of the falls, the whole plateau seems to be listening for something that moves at the pace of mountains.
