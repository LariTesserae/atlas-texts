The place is called the Upper Quiet, though there is no air here in which a name could be spoken.

It occupies the crown of a drowned structure, a small, steep district suspended high above the unseen bottom. Below it, the water continues downward into darkness; above it, the water closes without a surface. Its highest terraces are still submerged. They rise in steps and narrow ramps around a central tower, each ledge only a few paces wide, so that the whole landscape feels less like a city than a single elaborate object. There is nowhere natural to stand. Floor, wall, arch, ridge, and hollow are all made.

The oldest builders used clay, fired stone, and a pale, shell-rich mortar. Their work has been worn almost smooth. Corners have softened into long curves; doorways have become keyhole-shaped from the slow passage of water. The terraces are furred with mineral crusts, not growth: thin layers of yellow-white deposit that ring each basin and seam. Here and there, a broken parapet shows the layered body of the place, packed and fired until it became harder than the water could easily unmake. The structures have not collapsed so much as settled into their final shapes. Erosion has taken the gestures out of them.

The water is warm and heavy. It presses gently against the face and gathers in the sleeves of every passage. It is clear only nearby; beyond the next arch, it becomes a dense blackness. The darkness is not absolute. A dim, brownish glow seeps from high cracks in the tower, though no opening reveals a sky. It may be light filtering down through many layers of water, or a remnant of the old lamps—small cups set in niches, their mineral surfaces still holding a dull gleam. Nothing shines brightly enough to cast a shadow.

Life crowds every sheltered surface. There are pale, soft-bodied animals packed into the mortar seams, and flat blind things folded beneath the ledges like wet leaves. Long, many-jointed swimmers thread through the archways in constant near-contact, turning their narrow bodies aside at the last possible moment. Some cling in dense colonies to the undersides of stairs; others gather in the empty rooms, suspended upright as if attending to something. Their colors are mostly ash, tea-brown, and the faint translucent pink of the inside of a shell. There are no plants, no fronds, no green. The animals feed on one another, on drifting matter, on the thin mineral films and the smaller lives hidden within them. The food chain is close and old, confined to this handful of constructed terraces.

Despite their abundance, they make almost no sound. A fin brushing a wall gives a whisper. Small claws tick once against fired stone and stop. The loudest noise is the water itself, though even that is more pressure than sound: a slow, almost imperceptible tightening in the ear as the currents shift through the structure. The inhabitants seem to know when those shifts are coming. When the water changes direction, they turn together—not suddenly, but over several breaths, until the whole district appears to be facing the same way.

There are passages that lead nowhere now. Some end in doors swollen shut by mineral deposits; others open into chambers so narrow that only the smallest creatures can enter. The old builders made everything by hand, repeating the same curves, fitting blocks into seams, smoothing surfaces with patient tools. Their marks remain in places the water has not reached: a thumb-shaped hollow beside a threshold, a row of shallow cuts around a basin, a small figure pressed into the wall with no face left to it. The work is primitive in its means and total in its ambition. Every visible inch was intended.

Nothing changes quickly here. Sediment settles in the bowls. Mineral rims thicken. Creatures hatch, drift, and are eaten. Once in a long while, a piece of the tower gives way and falls only a short distance before lodging against another piece of the tower. The sound may take a long time to arrive, if it arrives at all.

The Upper Quiet is not empty, and it is not alive in any familiar, generous sense. It is a made place slowly being inhabited by the water’s smallest hungers. It hangs high in the black, warm and crowded, its ancient terraces holding still while a thousand soft bodies pass through them without hurry.
