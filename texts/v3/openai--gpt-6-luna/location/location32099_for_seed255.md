The place is called the **Unfinished Reach**, though nothing here looks as if it could ever be finished.

It spreads across a low basin in close-packed layers: ridges of brick, sunken courts, narrow causeways, shallow roofs, and long channels running between them. From a little distance, the whole region resembles a dark, lopsided floor laid in overlapping tiles. There are no hills in the ordinary sense, only heaps of construction raised just high enough to interrupt the basin’s shallow fall. A walkway disappears behind a parapet, reappears as the roof of a passage, and continues on the far side of a courtyard. Every surface is made. The ground beneath your feet is no more natural than the walls around it: clay pressed into slabs, gravel bound in crude mortar, fired fragments fitted together by hand.

The workmanship is primitive and patient. Some blocks are no bigger than a loaf; others are broad, bowed plates that must have taken many hands to lift. Finger marks remain in the clay. Here and there, a line of thumb-sized hollows runs along a wall, as if someone counted out the courses while the material was still soft. The Reach is not ruinous. Its edges are too sharp for that, its broken faces too fresh. The place seems to be arriving at its own shape by small increments. Ramps stop abruptly against blank walls. Stairways descend to sealed floors. New courses have been laid across older roofs, leaving the older doors stranded halfway up the sides of rooms.

There is no vegetation. No green has found a crack, and no roots have loosened the masonry. Nothing grows in the courts. The material of the Reach is clay, ash, crushed ceramic, and pale mineral paste, worked into an immense density of ledges, troughs, culverts, cells, and retaining walls. Even the few open areas are carefully edged. They look less like clearings than like places where construction has not yet caught up.

The air is cool and damp, but never wet for long. Moisture gathers in the seams between slabs, darkening them to the color of old ink. A brief rain passes over the basin now and then. You hear it first as a far-off pattering, then as thousands of taps on the roofs. Water runs down channels worn too recently to be called worn, gathers in shallow cisterns, and vanishes into covered drains. Between rains, condensation beads beneath low overhangs and drops at irregular intervals. Some courts hold a film of water so thin it reflects the surrounding walls without quite appearing to be a pool.

There is little open sky. Roofs and bridges crowd overhead, but they do not make a single continuous ceiling. Through gaps, the darkness above shows a dim, cold brightness with no obvious source. In the deepest lanes, the only light is the faint grayness reflected from wet surfaces. A broad avenue may be black at one end and faintly visible at the other, though no lamp stands there. Shadows do not sharpen so much as accumulate, gathering beneath ledges and in doorways. In some places they seem to occupy a little more space than the architecture allows.

Sound is constant, and much louder than the distances suggest. Water ticks into hidden hollows. Clay plates give small, dry clicks as the temperature changes. From beneath the paving comes a low, irregular clatter, like a line of tools being carried through a room one floor below. Voices, if there are voices, never resolve into words. A sound made in one courtyard can travel through the drains and emerge nearby, changed in pitch and apparently coming from inside a wall. The basin carries noise well; its many surfaces break it up without quieting it.

Movement is rare, but the Reach is not still. Water redistributes itself after every shower. Fine powder slips from fresh joints and collects in corners. Every so often a whole section makes a soft settling groan, and somewhere a new crack appears, pale against the fired dark. The changes are small enough to miss if you are not watching, yet on returning to a place you may find a passage narrowed, a step sunk, or an open channel bridged by a newly slumped slab. The construction does not collapse so much as keep negotiating with itself.

There are animals, though not many. Small, pale creatures shelter in the wet joints, difficult to distinguish from scraps of broken ceramic until they uncurl and scuttle away. They have smooth backs and too many thin legs, and they are never seen far from water. A few larger shapes move through the covered drains. Their passage is marked by brief tremors in the tiles, followed by a series of quick knocks receding into the dark. Nothing flies overhead. Nothing calls from the roofs.

The Reach’s rules are easiest to learn by staying quiet. Water always takes the route laid out for it, except when it doesn’t. A channel may remain dry through a storm and then fill hours later from a source higher in the maze. Doors do not open onto rooms so much as onto changes in the thickness of the place: a door in a wall may lead to a narrow passage, or to a recess so shallow it seems to have been made for a person standing sideways. The same courtyard can appear larger from its entrance than from its center. No one has discovered whether this is a trick of the joined angles, or a property of the Reach itself.

Nothing here looks old enough to be ancient. Nothing looks new enough to be under construction. The whole basin holds the tense, unfinished quiet of a thing being made by a maker who has not yet decided what it is for. And all the while, in the dark between its crowded walls, the water keeps finding its way.
