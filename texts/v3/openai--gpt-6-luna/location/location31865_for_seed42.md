The town stands on a high shelf beneath a mountain lake, though “stands” is only an old word for it. Water covers everything: roofs, lanes, courtyards, the shallow steps leading up to the central rise. Beyond the shelf, the lake floor drops away into blue-green depth. The settlement is a small island of habit in a much larger, silent volume.

Light reaches it plainly, but softened by the water. It falls in broad, shifting panes, bright on the upper walls and dim beneath bridges and overhangs. There is no true sky here—only the trembling underside of the lake, far above, where wind writes wavering marks on the surface. On clear days, the whole place seems suspended inside a pale, cool glass.

Plants have taken the town without destroying it. They are its pavement, its roofing, its mortar. Thick mats of moss and fine-leaved water plants soften the stone lanes; long fronds hang from lintels; rooted fans spread over the terraces, holding the old masonry together. In some streets, vegetation fills the space from wall to wall, leaving only a narrow channel for passage. Elsewhere it grows in scattered clumps, exposing broad, bare stretches of worn stone. The buildings are simple, low, and numerous enough to suggest an orderly town: square rooms, stepped foundations, retaining walls, narrow causeways, a few larger halls. Their builders used hand-cut stone, woven stems, pegs, and braided root. Nothing is polished. Every edge has been rounded by years of water and growth.

The town is not crowded. Its structures sit apart, with open water between them. A house might have a whole courtyard of lake around it, except for the plants rooted across the floor. The spaces feel intentional, like streets widened into plazas, though the plants have blurred every boundary. There are no great towers, only the suggestion of height in the terraces climbing the shelf toward a small central knoll. Above that, the slope disappears into waving growth.

Life is abundant. Small fish move through doorways and under stone benches in loose, glittering schools. Broad-backed, slow animals graze the plant mats, leaving pale trails that close over behind them. Shell-creatures cling to the walls; pale worms thread through the soft sediment; tiny, many-legged scavengers stir wherever old leaves collect. Larger fish pass in the outer water, glimpsed between buildings like moving shadows. None seem startled by the settlement. They use it as cover, feeding ground, and nursery.

The sound is unexpectedly strong. Water carries every small movement far. Fronds knock together with a steady, papery clap; fish flick and rattle through the reeds; bubbles rise from crevices in long, low chains. The stonework gives off a deep hum when the lake shifts above it. In the narrow lanes, these sounds gather and overlap until the town seems full of distant voices, though there are no voices at all. Now and then, a heavy animal brushes a wall, and the whole terrace answers with a hollow wooden groan.

The cold here is even and patient. It does not bite; it settles into the stones and keeps them cool through every season. Changes arrive slowly. Plants lengthen by almost imperceptible degrees. Sediment gathers in sheltered corners. The upper lake brightens and darkens as clouds pass, and the inhabitants—if any still live here—would measure time by the gradual closing of a doorway, the slow burial of a stair.

Nothing in the place looks newly ruined. It has been submerged long enough to become itself. Its paths remain where people laid them, though roots now determine where one can walk. Its rooms remain recognizably rooms, although fish occupy their floors and soft growth hangs from their ceilings. The lake has not erased the town; it has made the town into habitat.

At the edge of the shelf, where the last retaining wall gives way to open water, the plants thin. The ground drops steeply into a darker blue, and the town’s sounds diminish there, one by one, until only the faint pulse of the lake remains. On the high terrace, meanwhile, sunlight slides across the roofs, the fish keep moving through the old streets, and the green city waits without hurry.
