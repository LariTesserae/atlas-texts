The city of Vey has been built across a plain that is still deciding what shape to be.

It lies low beneath a dim, pewter-colored sky. The ground is dark, warm, and newly exposed: broad plates of black clay split by sharp ridges, shallow hollows, and seams of pale mineral glass. Nothing here has had long to soften. Cut banks stand crisp at the edges of the canals; the stone around foundations looks snapped rather than weathered. In places the plain rises only a few feet, but those small changes make entire districts disappear from view.

Water comes intermittently. A brief rain may sweep across the city, stippling roofs and filling every groove in the ground, then vanish into the porous earth before the sun—if it can be called a sun—has moved perceptibly. Most days are merely damp. Condensation beads on cables, leaves, and the undersides of bridges. Thin water gathers in the shaded channels and in hollows lined with moss, while the open spaces remain dry enough to dust the shoes. The canals are not quite rivers: they are narrow, dark cuts through the plain, sometimes empty, sometimes carrying a slow ribbon of water toward a distant basin.

Vey is spacious for a city. Its inhabited districts are separated by large intervals of open ground: a mile of wetland, a field of tilted stone, a garden grown over an abandoned transit platform. Towers stand in loose constellations instead of walls. They are tall and slender, made from pale composite material that seems to have been poured rather than assembled. Between them, bridges cross the air in long, level strokes. Some end at buildings that are no longer there. Others pass through empty space and arrive, without apparent support, at the far side of a canal.

The infrastructure is too precise to feel entirely mechanical. Transit lines run above the plain as faint silver threads. Vehicles travel along them without wheels or visible engines, gliding so quietly that people notice them chiefly by the shadows they cast on the ground. At intersections, the lines bend around one another without meeting, though their routes seem to occupy the same space. The city’s power comes from immense buried structures that do not produce heat or vibration. Now and then, a low ring passes through the foundations and every puddle in Vey takes on the same pattern of concentric circles.

Vegetation thrives in the gaps. Thick, glossy growth rises wherever water lingers: fernlike fans along the canals, low trees with translucent bark in the courtyards, mats of small red leaves clinging to cracks in the pavement. Some plants grow in deliberate beds; others have found their own routes through the city. They climb the blank sides of buildings and vanish into vents, appearing again weeks later on rooftops blocks away. Their leaves hold drops of water long after the rain has passed. At dusk, the green deepens until it is almost black, and the few pale flowers look lit from within.

The city’s inhabitants have learned not to clear every plant. A vine crossing a doorway is usually trained aside, not cut. Certain species are said to keep the ground stable, though no one agrees what “stable” means here. The plain shifts by small amounts over time. A path may end a little farther from the river than it did the year before; a stairway may acquire an extra step overnight. Surveyors mark these changes on soft metal tablets, but their maps are treated as suggestions.

There is little animal life. Small, long-legged creatures sometimes stand beneath the bridges, perfectly still, their narrow heads turned toward the canals. No one has seen them feed. In the wet months, tiny insects gather around the dim lights in the lower districts, but they leave no bodies behind. Birds are nearly absent. Once in a while, a flock crosses high above the city in a formation so broad it seems to be a moving flaw in the cloud cover. The sound arrives much later, if at all.

Vey itself is quiet. Its residents move through covered walks and open plazas without much conversation. Doors seal without clicks. Public notices appear as shallow changes in the surface of walls, visible only when the light strikes them at an angle. At night—though night is more a deepening of the dimness than an arrival—the city’s windows glow gently, not enough to illuminate the streets. The brightest places are the canal crossings, where strands of blue light hang in the air above the water and sometimes drift a few inches from their anchors.

The strangest rule is that reflections do not always belong to what they reflect. A person crossing a still pool may see the buildings correctly, and also a second skyline beneath them: lower, darker, filled with structures of unfamiliar design. The image usually disappears when the water moves. Once, during a season of prolonged condensation, the second city remained visible for three days. The transit authority suspended service across the southern plain, citing a “temporary conflict of routes.” No further explanation was given.

Outside the last residential clusters, Vey opens into a wide, green, mostly empty landscape. Here the new ground has broken into shelves and fins, each edge sharp against the haze. Warm air carries the smell of wet stone and crushed leaves. Rain can be seen approaching from far away as a gray curtain, but it does not always fall where it appears to. Sometimes it passes over the plain and settles instead on the roofs of the city, though the clouds remain beyond the horizon.

Nothing in Vey is completely still. Water shifts beneath the crust. Plants extend toward structures whose lights they cannot see. The buried engines—or whatever lies below—make their slow, inaudible corrections. Yet the place gives little sense of haste. It is active in the way a sleeping body is active: breathing, mending, adjusting to pressure. The city has been built here because the ground is young, the water scarce but dependable, and the distances still large enough to allow for uncertainty.

From a high bridge, Vey looks less like a city than a careful arrangement of pauses: towers separated by gardens, districts separated by dark plain, one thin river reflecting a light that has no visible source. Far away, at the edge of sight, the land rises into a clean black ridge. It was not there the previous year.
