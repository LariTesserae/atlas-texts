The high country begins above the clouds, though the clouds are seldom far below it. From a distance it looks less like a range of mountains than a dark green sea caught in the act of rising: ridges rolling away beneath a pale sky, every slope crowded with trees, reeds, moss, and the broad leaves of plants that grow in the cold. Water threads through all of it. It drips from branches, seeps from the stone, gathers in shallow bowls of peat, and runs under the ground in such abundance that the earth seems to be holding its breath around it.

The land is old in its present shape. Its ridges have been rounded by ages of rain and frost, but the valleys are not gentle. They are deep, narrow, and so densely occupied that there is hardly an open view from any one place. A fallen trunk does not lie bare for long. Moss covers its upper surface; water darkens its underside; seedlings root in the soft wood. Ferns and shoulder-high grasses fill the gaps around it. Higher on the slopes, trees grow close enough for their crowns to press together, making a second, dimmer terrain above the ground. Beneath that canopy, the light is green and even. It has the ordinary brightness of day, but it arrives filtered through layers of leaf, fog, and hanging water.

The coolness is steady rather than severe. Frost appears on exposed stone in the mornings, then vanishes into beads of water before noon. Fog pours over the crests and sinks into the hollows, lifting again when the sun warms the upper branches. Rain is frequent but rarely theatrical: a long, soft sift that continues for hours, almost too quiet to distinguish from the dripping that follows. On clear days the air still feels wet against the skin. The rocks weep from seams and hairline cracks, and in some places a person can watch a thin film of water spread over stone without any rain falling at all.

There are broad basins where the water lies just under the surface. The ground gives slightly beneath the foot, springing back with a muted sigh. Pools form among the roots, black and still, and are often so crowded with floating leaves that their water is visible only in small, shifting windows. Streams pass through these basins without making much sound. They slide beneath mats of vegetation, disappear into gravel, and reappear lower down, cold and clear. Only at the steepest drops do they speak aloud, and even then the sound is quickly swallowed by the wet forest.

Life is abundant, but the abundance is close-packed and watchful. Small animals move mostly beneath leaves or along the braided tunnels between roots. There are pale, long-bodied grazers that feed on the tender growth at dawn and vanish into the underbrush at the first change in wind. Dark hunters follow their trails without leaving much trace. Birds are present, though seldom seen; their calls are brief, low notes from within the canopy, spaced far apart. Insects thrive in the warmer hollows and along the water, but the high, cool ridges are quieter. Everything seems adapted to conserve movement. A leaf trembles, a ripple crosses a pool, a cluster of seedheads bows—and the rest of the landscape remains still.

Stillness here is not inactivity. The slopes are working constantly, though their labor is slow. Water loosens mineral salts from the rock and carries them downhill. Roots divide boulders along old fractures. Peat grows over pools, then collapses into them, making new channels and temporary islands. The whole highland feeds itself through cycles of soaking, draining, freezing, and thawing. It produces immense quantities of fibrous matter, dark soil, clear water, and metal-rich sediment. Some valleys are threaded with natural seams of iron and copper, their edges stained red, blue, or green. In the seepage below them, mineral crusts grow in delicate scales. They have a processed look—layered, sorted, almost machined—but no hand has made them. Chemistry does the work with industrial patience.

In a few ravines, the landscape makes its most unusual impression. There, underground water drops through narrow shafts and turns vast stone chambers into slow, natural engines. The pressure drives pulses through the ground: not an earthquake, but a recurring, barely perceptible thrum. It travels through roots and rock, setting hanging pools to tremble and making the leaves along certain slopes quiver in sequence. The force is gathered and spent through channels of stone, mineral deposits, and living root-mass, like a system of pumps built over geological time. Nothing in the ravines is constructed. There are no towers, walls, roads, or cut surfaces. Yet the arrangements of falls, conduits, and mineral terraces can seem almost mechanical, as if the mountain has learned to manufacture its own weather.

The sound of this place is low. Water clicks from leaf to leaf. A stream murmurs under a bank. Far off, a tree gives way with a soft, heavy crash, and the forest closes around the gap. The subterranean pulses are felt more often than heard. On certain nights, when the fog is thick and the air has settled, there is a faint metallic singing from the mineral ravines—a resonance produced by moving water passing through stone. It is so quiet that it seems to belong to the listener’s own bones.

Nothing here is built, but nothing is empty. Every ledge has a plant gripping it; every pool has a skin of growth; every fallen tree is already becoming habitat. The highland has reached an old equilibrium that is not peace so much as continuous adjustment. Water changes its course by inches. Roots take advantage. Animals shift their routes. A storm passes through, redistributing the weight of the canopy, and within days the altered ground has been absorbed into the usual pattern.

From the highest ridge, one can see the land extend for what seems an impossible distance: an unbroken expanse of cloud-shadowed green, broken only by pale watercourses and the occasional gleam of wet stone. It is vast enough to have its own weather, its own slow industry, its own quiet laws. The clouds arrive from below, climb through the forests, and spill over the far side. The whole country breathes them in and gives them back as rain.
