The plain has no horizon at first. It is too broad and too level for the eye to understand as land. Light runs over it in an unbroken sheet, warm and pale as metal left in the sun. Far away, the surface rises—not into mountains, but into long, low ridges, each one so gradual it might be the curve of a world.

Nothing here is quite stone. The ground is made of fused ceramic, dull silver alloys, and mineral glass, laid in enormous interlocking scales. Their seams are narrow as pencil lines, though each seam could swallow a city. In places, the old surface has worn down until its buried layers show: dark hexagons beneath white plates, fine gold mesh beneath those, then a depth of patterned material that descends beyond sight. There is no raw bedrock under it. The planet, or whatever this place is, was built all the way through.

Age has made its works gentle. Towers have slumped into ramps. Walls have weathered into terraces. Bridges have settled into the ground and become long, shallow valleys. Every edge that once declared itself has been softened by ages of abrasion, until the architecture resembles the final shape of a landscape rather than something placed upon one. The tallest remnants barely interrupt the low sky. Their faces are worn smooth, but their shadows remain sharp in the fierce brightness.

The air is warm and nearly dry. It carries a fine suspension of bright mineral dust that never quite settles. When the ground heats, the dust rises in slow veils, revealing faint bands of color: rose, brass, bone-white. From far off, these veils look like water. They are not. There is no water anywhere in the open plain—not in pools, not in clouds, not in the dark hollows between structures. A few sealed channels run through the land, their lids marked with tiny symbols that may once have indicated rivers. Now they carry heat, pressure, or something too subtle to see.

The wind has a strange patience. It does not gust so much as lean. It moves across the plain in vast, barely perceptible fronts, combing dust from one district and laying it in another. Over the old ridges, the air bends and shivers. The shimmer is not quite mirage: it reveals the landscape’s hidden geometry, long arcs and nested grids that are invisible from the ground. Sometimes the light itself seems to move through the air in measured pulses, as if a colossal machine were taking slow breaths beneath the surface.

The constructed ground is crowded, though the crowding is horizontal and low. There are billions of close-set forms: domes no higher than a person, towers worn down to stumps, slender antennae packed into groves, rows of apertures, vents, sealed mouths, and shallow basins filled with pale grit. Some structures touch. Others stand separated by finger-width lanes, with the narrow spaces between them running farther than the eye can follow. From above, the plain would look dense as a woven fabric. From within it, one sees only fragments: a corridor opening into a courtyard, a courtyard descending through several older floors, a ramp vanishing beneath a roof of stone-colored plates.

Very little grows. In protected cracks, sparse black filaments cling to the seams. They resemble dry roots or the fine wiring of a long-abandoned instrument. Once in a while, a tuft of pale fronds appears in the lee of a wall. The fronds turn toward warmth rather than light, folding flat when a thermal front passes over them. They do not make the place green. They only mark that something is still able to answer the world.

Animals are rarer still. A small, many-jointed shape may cross an exposed plate and disappear into a vent before the eye has followed it. There are no calls, no wings, no rustle in the dust. The few creatures here seem adapted to passages and seams, living in the gaps the builders left—or the gaps that time made for them. They are brief signs of appetite and movement in a landscape otherwise too vast to feel inhabited.

The quiet is immense. Sound does not travel far across the smooth ground. A footfall, if one were to make it, would die almost immediately, absorbed by the layered material below. Yet the place is not silent. Beneath the surface there are low vibrations: a pressure shift, a distant click passing through thousands of joined plates, a faint metallic murmur that can be mistaken for one’s own pulse. At long intervals a whole region gives a single soft tone, like a glass rim touched by a finger. The note may last for hours and be heard nowhere else.

The world is active, but never hurried. Beneath the old works, systems continue to redistribute heat, repair fractures, open and close buried gates. A ridge may rise by the width of a hand over a century. A field of plates can subtly rearrange itself as the temperature changes, the seams darkening and brightening in an orderly sequence. Some collapsed towers slowly turn, not by wind or weight, but by a mechanism that has not stopped recognizing its purpose. The landscape changes at a scale too slow to watch and too regular to call dead.

There are rules here. Open ground stores heat; enclosed ground returns it. The bright dust gathers where old boundaries once separated districts. Narrow passages are warmer than the broad plain, even when they are shaded. Certain surfaces will not hold a footprint. Others preserve every touch for ages, gradually filling it with a thin coat of glass. And if the buried systems detect a new weight, they sometimes part without noise, making a dark, clean passage into the depths.

The plain stretches on beneath a clear, high brightness. Its low ridges repeat until they become indistinguishable from the planet’s curve. Whatever once lived in the structures has been gone for an almost unimaginable time. Whatever built them is not entirely gone. It persists as pressure, orientation, repair, and an occasional note moving through the ground—a last, vast intention, still working its way through the world.
