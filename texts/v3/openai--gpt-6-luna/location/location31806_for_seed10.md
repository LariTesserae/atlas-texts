The plateau does not rise so much as remain after everything around it has worn away.

It lies high beneath a low, bruised sky, a broad country of dark stone shelves, shallow basins, and long slopes polished smooth by ages of weather. The edges of the land are difficult to see. They drift in and out of cloud, and sometimes the cloud opens onto another range of pale ridges that seems much nearer than it should be. Water is everywhere, though rarely in the form of a clear stream. It seeps from seams in the rock, gathers in black bowls, beads on every surface, and hangs in the air as a fine, cold wetness. The pools are shallow enough to show their bottoms, but the bottoms are often farther away than the pools are deep.

The stone has been eroded into a final, patient shape: rounded ledges, worn channels, low knobs like knuckles beneath a blanket. Nothing here looks newly broken. Even the cliffs have softened, their faces scalloped into broad, quiet curves. The old rock carries bands of gray, green-black, and the faint rusty color of iron. In places, those bands appear to run uphill, or continue through the air for a short distance before becoming cloud.

Vegetation fills the plateau without making it crowded. It grows in immense, separate tracts, each one a country of its own: mats of dark cushion-plants spread over the wet stone; waist-high forests of hollow stalks combed flat by the wind; shaggy shelves of moss clinging to the sides of ravines. Between them lie long expanses of bare rock and shallow water, open enough that a person could see weather arriving from far away. The plants are dense wherever they take hold. Their roots knit the ground so tightly that the earth seems less like soil than a single living skin. But they leave great distances between one patch and the next.

The tallest growths are pale and jointed, with broad heads that bow almost to the ground. In calm weather they stand still. In storms they flex in waves, each stalk bending at a slightly different moment, so that whole fields appear to be listening to separate instructions. Their insides are full of water and fine metal-colored fibers. When the wind passes through them, they sound like vast machinery coming awake: a rhythmic clanking, a deep shuddering hum, the occasional thin shriek of pressure releasing. There are no mills, towers, tracks, or engines on the plateau. Still, it has the noise of a factory heard from very far away.

The work is done by the plants themselves. Root-bands tighten under the ground and draw water uphill. Hollow stems pump it from one basin to another. Once in a while, under a particular combination of cold, cloud, and electrical charge, an entire slope begins to pulse. Water moves through the plants with a sound like belts running over rollers. Seed cases snap open in sequence. Clouds of dark spores lift, hang suspended, then travel sideways against the wind. For an hour, a hillside can seem to be manufacturing its own weather.

The animals are abundant, though scattered across the open distances. Small white grazers move through the cushion-plants in loose bands, their feet making almost no mark in the saturated ground. Large, long-necked birds gather at the pools and stand with their heads tucked beneath one wing, even while awake. There are burrowing things that travel under the plant mats and raise slow, traveling humps in them. The larger animals are rarely seen all at once. Their calls carry across the plateau—a hollow barking from a ravine, a chorus of clicks out in the fog, a booming cry that seems to come from the stone itself.

Nothing keeps exactly the same distance. A call can sound nearby for several minutes without growing louder, then suddenly be behind you. From certain ridges, a pool on the far side of a valley is visible as if seen from directly above; walk down toward it and the same pool proves to be miles away. Shadows sometimes fall toward the sunless side of the sky. Rain rises from the basins in narrow, vertical threads and vanishes into the clouds. These are not dramatic events here. They occur as routinely as mist, and the plants have adapted to them. Some face the wrong way. Some root into the air above the water and hang there, drawing nourishment from a reflection rather than from the pool beneath it.

The light is dim even at noon. Clouds press low, thinning to a cold pearl-gray that gives no clear direction. At intervals the plateau brightens from below: the wet stone reflects a diffuse, greenish light from the plant mats, and every puddle briefly contains a sky that is not the one overhead. At dusk, the hollow stalks grow faintly luminous along their seams. The glow is weak and uneven, like light seen through closed eyelids.

Weather is rarely settled. Wind pours through the high passes, collides with the damp air, and breaks into gusts that travel in different directions across the same slope. Rain may fall on one tract of plants while the bare rock beside it remains dry; then, without warning, the water drains upward through the stems and begins falling again as fine mist. The plateau’s loudness comes in layers: wind in the stalks, water in the channels, birds calling across the basins, and beneath everything a persistent industrial thrum, a vibration too low to hear cleanly but strong enough to feel in the teeth.

In the widest open stretches, there is room for the eye to rest. The stone runs away in smooth gray miles, crossed only by thin lines of standing water. A solitary island of vegetation may occupy a whole ridge. Far off, another gathers around a steaming cleft, its edges rippling in the wind. The emptiness is not barren. It is simply the space the living things leave between their operations.

And the whole place feels old—not ruined, not dead, but finished in a way that resists improvement. The water has found its hollows. The stone has given up its sharpness. The vegetation has learned to move, pump, store, and release. Animals cross the distances along paths worn so faintly they appear only when the rain changes direction. Whatever once made the plateau is no longer separate from it. The engines have become roots; the weather has become a kind of labor; the landscape continues to turn over its immense, quiet production, without buildings, without hands, and with no need to explain what it is making.
