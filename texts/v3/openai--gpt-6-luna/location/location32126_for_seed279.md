The basin is so low that the horizon seems to rest on it. Beyond the nearest ridges, the land does not rise so much as gather itself into a gray distance: shelves of newly exposed stone, shallow flats, and dark seams where water lies just beneath the surface.

Nothing grows here. There are no reeds to mark the channels, no lichen softening the rock, no green or brown anywhere. Instead, the ground is crowded with mineral forms: blunt spires, knuckled ridges, little towers with hollow centers, and plates of stone tilted against one another like scales. Their edges are sharp, not yet worn into gentleness. Some have been lifted from the mud only recently; their pale undersides remain clean, while their tops are already stained dark by rain. The basin feels unfinished, though it may have been this way for a very long time.

Water gathers in every depression. It seeps from cracks, stands in thin sheets over the flats, and beads along the undersides of overhanging rock. The stone itself seems to sweat. On colder mornings, low mist pools between the formations, concealing the channels and making the crowded ground appear to float. The air smells faintly of wet slate and something metallic, like a stone held too long in the mouth.

The loudness comes from everywhere, though nothing seems to be moving much. Water falls from narrow ledges in steady threads, striking hollows below. Each hollow answers with a different note. Some make a hard, bright knocking; others give out a deep, throatlike moan. The sounds overlap until the basin becomes a continuous clamor—a roar assembled from thousands of small, patient impacts. When the wind crosses the flats, it passes through the hollow stones and draws out wavering tones. No one sound dominates for long. The whole place speaks at once, but never seems to be saying anything urgent.

Near the center are a few scattered constructions: low walls of fitted stone, half sunk in mud, and one freestanding arch whose opening faces the waterlogged plain. They are too small to explain the scale of the basin and too deliberate to be natural. Their surfaces are worn smooth where hands might have touched them, though no tool marks can be seen. Water collects along their foundations, and the walls make the sound change—dampening it on one side, sharpening it on the other. Nothing indicates whether the structures were shelters, markers, or simply things people once tried to make stand against the weather.

Life is rare enough to be mistaken for a flaw in the stone. Now and then, a dark shape glides under a shallow film of water. It leaves no wake. On a ledge, a bead of moisture may tremble against gravity for several minutes before slipping upward into a crack. There are faint impressions in the wet silt, widely spaced and without a clear direction. By the time they are noticed, their edges have softened.

The light is dim even at midday, filtered through a sky the color of pewter. Shadows are not black but blue-gray, and they gather beneath the stone shelves in long, still bands. At dusk the water and ground become difficult to distinguish. Only the scattered walls, the darker channels, and the hollow formations retain their shape. In that half-light, the basin seems densely inhabited—not by animals, but by presences of stone and water, each close to the next, each making its small contribution to the noise.

The place changes slowly. Water raises and lowers its skin across the flats; new cracks open; small shelves collapse into the mud. The sharpest features keep their edges for a while, then lose a grain or two at a time. There is no sudden flood, no storm that clears the basin of its old arrangements. The land is still becoming, but it does so with an immense reluctance.

At night, the clamor continues. In the darkness, it is possible to hear that some of the notes come from beneath the ground. They travel through the stone in low pulses, arriving a moment before or after the water’s own voice. The basin does not answer back. It merely holds the sound, the damp, and the dimness together, as if this were the shape the world had made for itself.
