The basin is low enough that the horizon never quite rises. It travels outward in a long, shallow curve: slate-colored flats, knuckles of old stone, narrow cuts where water has worried at the ground for ages. Nothing here is newly broken. The edges have been rounded by innumerable small floods, and the hollows polished by traffic. The whole region seems worn smooth by repetition.

There are no plants. No stalks, fronds, or roots break the stone. In their place, the land is crowded with animals.

They lie packed into every depression and seam: pale, many-jointed crawlers that knot over one another like spilled cords; blunt-bodied things with wet, lidless eyes, wedged beneath overhangs; small hard-shelled skitterers tracing the same grooves until the rock shines. Larger animals move through them slowly, shouldering aside the living carpet. Their backs are ridged with mineral crust. Their mouths open and close without urgency, sifting the basin’s thin, dark water for smaller lives.

Above, the air is thick with wings. Some creatures beat through it in dense, wavering sheets; others hang from the low shelves of stone, trembling in place. Their wings brush the rock and each other with a dry, papery rasp. When they rise together, the sound swells until the sky seems to be tearing. The air smells of wet stone, salt, and the faint sourness of countless bodies.

Water arrives intermittently. Cool rain passes over the basin in fine, brief veils, collecting in shallow pans and the hollows left by old erosion. Condensation beads under the rock ledges and drops steadily from their rims. The animals drink, bathe, feed, and lay their eggs in the gathered water; soon it is clouded with movement. Then it sinks into cracks or dries from the exposed flats, leaving dark outlines and a glitter of salt behind. The basin is damp, but never generous.

The light is nearly absent. A low, opaque ceiling of cloud keeps the day dim and flat; at other times, darkness settles without any visible transition. Stone and creature alike lose their color in it. Shapes announce themselves by motion: a shifting bank of backs, a sudden ripple through the wing-cloud, the silver wink of an eye turned toward you. Here and there, living membranes give off a very faint bluish sheen—not enough to illuminate the ground, only enough to make the nearest bodies appear briefly submerged in deep water.

The sound never falls away. It is made from many scales at once: the tick and scrape of small feet, throaty calls from the darker hollows, the booming pulse of larger animals communicating through the ground. Wingbeats layer over everything. Rain on the stone makes a hiss beneath the chorus, and water moving underground answers with a low, sustained vibration. At certain places the basin’s walls throw the noise back so densely that no single call can be separated from the whole. The sound becomes a pressure in the chest.

There is food here, though none of it grows toward the light. Thin films of microbial life coat the wettest stone, nearly invisible until the animals disturb them. Mineral-rich seepage feeds these films; droppings and shed skins feed the seepage in turn. The smallest creatures graze, filter, or feed on one another. The larger ones follow the migrations of the smaller ones, and the largest follow whatever moves most abundantly. Death is not a pause in the system. A body is covered, opened, and reduced within hours, leaving little but clean bone and a temporary darkening of the stone.

Despite the crowds, the basin has an old, settled order. The migrations pass through familiar channels. The water gathers in the same hollows. The creatures avoid certain ridges, where the rock holds a temperature too low even for them, and mass together in sheltered cuts when the rain comes hard. Their paths have polished the basin into a map no eye can read all at once.

Nothing built interrupts it. No tool marks, no walls, no trace of a hand arranging the ground. There is only stone shaped by water and time, and life so abundant that it seems less to inhabit the place than to be its weather. From a distance, the flats look still. Then the surface ripples, the air darkens with wings, and the whole landscape begins to sing.
