The town of Serein occupies a shelf of newly risen stone no wider than a few streets. Beyond its edge, the mountain drops into cloud. The shelf is pale and sharp-edged, still raw from its emergence: split faces, fresh scree, seams of black glass where the rock cooled too quickly. Rain comes rarely, but the place is never quite dry. Mist beads on walls and gathers in cracks; water threads through the stone for a few minutes at a time, then vanishes into hidden hollows.

Serein was built to fit the shelf, not tame it. Its houses are squat, close-set, and made of stacked stone, timber, and whatever can be tied or wedged into place. Narrow lanes run between them, often stopping abruptly at a wall of rock or a patch of growth too dense to pass. Bridges of lashed poles cross the deeper fissures. At the center is a little square with a well, though the well fills only when the clouds press low enough to comb water from the roofs. No one builds high. The wind takes upper floors first.

The settlement seems empty until it moves.

At dusk, the walls ripple with tiny feet. Pale moths crowd the shutters from the inside, having found their way into the houses through pinholes in the stone. Long-legged birds stand on chimney rims, motionless, their heads turned toward the mountain. In the gardens, beetles roll through the leaf litter in shining knots. Something large but light-footed crosses the roofs overhead, making no sound at all. The people of Serein leave bowls of water under the eaves and never ask what drinks from them.

Vegetation thrives wherever the stone holds a little damp. Ferns push between paving slabs. Moss hangs in thick green pads beneath windowsills, and small trees root in abandoned cisterns, their branches bent sideways by the wind. In sheltered courtyards, creepers climb the walls and produce white flowers that open only in fog. Their scent is clean and faintly metallic. The town’s few cultivated plots grow more leaves than food; most of the harvest comes from gathering, trapping, and exchanging with the many creatures that pass through.

The animals are not merely numerous. They are organized in ways the people can’t quite read. Every morning, the same black beetles line the lane leading to the eastern gate. At midday, the birds lift from the rooftops all at once, though no one hears a call or sees what startled them. On certain nights, small furry shapes gather beneath the bridges and face outward, toward the drop. They remain there until first light, then scatter into cracks too narrow to contain them.

Sound in Serein is sparse and therefore difficult to ignore. A drip falling into a bucket. The soft rasp of a hand saw. The muted knock of someone testing a loose stone. There is no bell in the town, because sound carries strangely over the shelf: a whisper may travel for a mile along the rock, while a shout dies against the nearest wall. The animals are quieter still. Even the birds seem to hold their breath between movements.

The place is active without being loud. Clouds drag across the roofs; water appears and disappears; roots prise stones apart by imperceptible degrees. Fresh fractures open after cold nights. The townspeople patch them with moss, clay, and narrow wedges of wood, then mark the date beside the repair. If the crack grows, they move the nearest door. If it closes, they leave the mark where it is.

No one in Serein agrees on whether the shelf is a mountain ledge or the roof of something buried beneath the clouds. They do agree that it is still rising. Every year the eastern edge stands a little higher, and every year more animals arrive from below. The town keeps its lamps hooded, its paths narrow, and its gardens untended at the borders. In the morning, the fresh stone is usually covered with tracks: too many to count, all pointing in different directions.
