The place is called the Fold, though it is hardly wide enough to unfold in. A saddle of newly risen black stone holds a cluster of terraces, lanes, and tightly packed dwellings, all pressed into the shallow lee of a ridge. From the edge of the basin you can see almost all of it: roofs close together, roofs over roofs, the whole settlement no larger than a small hillside village. Beyond it, the ground drops steeply into cloud.

The stone is young. It breaks in plates and sharp little fins, still raw at the edges, with pale mineral seams showing where the rain has worried them open. But very little rock remains uncovered. Plants have entered every crack and laid themselves across every ledge: dark mats of moss, stiff silver grasses, low shrubs whose branches shine with water. Where the stone slopes too sharply to hold soil, roots hang in thick braided curtains, catching grit and fallen leaves until the cliff face becomes a wall of living fiber.

The buildings are not built so much as persuaded. Thick-stemmed plants grow in ribs and arches, their woody stalks bent together and fused where they meet. Roofs are overlapping leaves, waxy and nearly black, with water gathering at their tips before dropping into narrow channels of root. Rooms open under these roofs like hollows in a hedge. Some are little more than warm, dry pockets in the vegetation; others have several chambers grown around a central trunk. No two are quite alike, but every surface has been made useful. A wall is also a trellis. A stair is also a root. A lane is a living seam kept open by the steady passage of the settlement’s few inhabitants.

There is no metal, no glass, no smoke. Nothing here clicks or turns. The Fold’s order comes from growth and pressure. A bridge thickens where many feet cross it; a doorway slowly narrows where no one passes. Roof leaves overlap to direct meltwater toward a common cistern of hollow stems, then loosen and slide aside when the plants beneath them need light. The inhabitants do not seem to prune so much as to guide: a shoot is bent while it is young, a passage held open by walking, a room kept dry by living inside it.

Cold settles into the basin and stays there. The air is wet but not warm, full of fine mist that beads on every leaf. Stone sweats. The undersides of the roofs gather a dull shine. In the morning—if it is morning, beneath the low cloud—the uppermost leaves are edged with frost, while lower down water runs in slow threads through moss and root. The light is weak and greenish, filtered through cloud and the dense canopy of roofs. It never quite becomes dark, but neither does it become day with conviction.

The vegetation fills the Fold so completely that the lanes feel like tunnels through it. There is no bare square, no open garden, no clear ground around the buildings. Even the central meeting place is paved in flattened, springy growth, with only a small circle of exposed stone at its center. Rain collects there, and the dark stone reflects a wavering patch of sky. In the wet months the whole settlement seems to float on a thin skin of water. In colder weather the channels crust over, then thaw from below as the roots continue their quiet work.

Few animals live here, or at least few show themselves. Now and then a small pale insect moves under a leaf. A bird may pass over the ridge without landing. The people of the Fold speak softly, when they speak at all, and their presence is mostly evident in the paths they keep clear and the warm breaths fogging certain openings. The loudest sounds are water ticking from roof to roof, stems creaking under their own weight, and the faint dry rasp of frost forming along a leaf’s edge.

The Fold is not still. A new ledge of stone appears each year where the ridge breaks; each year, roots catch it before it can fall. One roof slowly rises, another sinks beneath a gathering of wet leaves. The lanes shift by inches. A room that once held two people may close around a single occupant, while a fresh hollow opens near the outer wall. The whole place is young enough to be changing, but dense enough that change cannot happen quickly or cleanly. Every new growth presses against something already living.

From outside, the settlement looks like a single dark clump of vegetation caught on the mountain. Only at close range can its streets be found: narrow, sheltered passages where the air smells of cold water, bruised stems, and stone newly split. There is no view from within the Fold, only glimpses—of a higher roof, a bead of water suspended under a leaf, a gray strip of cloud through an arch of roots. The place is small, crowded, and almost entirely alive. It keeps its inhabitants warm not by excluding the mountain, but by growing so tightly around them that there is scarcely room for the cold to enter.
