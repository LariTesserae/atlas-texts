The plateau begins above the clouds and continues until distance loses its meaning.

It is not a mountain range so much as a raised country, lifted abruptly out of the lower world: a pale, newly fractured shelf of black stone, green seams, and immense level reaches. Its edges fall away in cliffs too long to see from end to end. The rock is young. It has not yet softened into familiar shapes. Ridges run sharp as broken glass; ravines end in clean, unfinished cuts. Here and there, the stone has buckled into broad slabs, as if the earth were still settling into the idea of land.

Water is everywhere, though seldom in abundance. It beads on the rock, gathers in shallow sheets, and seeps from cracks in the cliffs. The air holds a fine cold dampness that settles on every surface. In hollows, silver pools lie so still they seem cut from the same material as the sky. Narrow falls descend from ledges, but they make little valleys; they strike the ground and spread thinly, disappearing into moss or into drains made by human hands. Mist gathers low over the plateau, not as a moving bank but in long, fixed veils. It hangs around towers, spans empty avenues, and blurs the far edges of the city.

The green is low and close to the stone. Thick mats of moss occupy the sheltered faces of walls. Dark grass grows in cracks, and small, blunt-leaved plants crowd the mouths of water channels. In some districts, whole roofs have become level gardens of sedge and pale lichen. There are no trees, or almost none: only a few tall, jointed growths that resemble trees at a distance, their rigid stems wrapped in green felt. Nothing flies above them. Nothing stirs beneath them. The plateau is without animal life. Its quiet spaces contain no calls, no tracks, no sudden movement in the grass.

The city is built in islands.

From above, its settlements look less like a continuous sprawl than a set of dark knots threaded together by infrastructure. Blocks of stone and metal stand around immense, empty squares. Elevated railways cross plains where there is nothing to carry. Pipelines, gantries, retaining walls, power lines, and culverts travel for hundreds of miles between districts, vanishing into fog before they reach another one. The buildings are practical and old-looking, though the land beneath them is young: heavy factories, tall narrow stacks, covered reservoirs, workshops, and housing arranged in grids. Some structures seem sized for a population far greater than the one that remains. Others are too large to have been built for people at all.

The machinery never stops.

A deep electrical hum fills the open ground, joined by the pounding of pumps, the slow clank of lifts, the vibration of distant presses. The sound carries unusually far across the bare plateau. One factory can be heard from another before it can be seen; whole districts announce themselves through layers of metallic thunder. Near the rail lines, the noise is almost physical, pressing against the body like weather. But there are no crowds to soften it, no traffic, no voices. The machines work into empty buildings and across empty distances. Their measured repetition becomes the chief sign of life.

Everything appears to be in motion, yet nothing changes.

Water runs down the same channels in the same thin streams. Mist occupies the same hollows. The machines repeat their cycles without wear or interruption. Even the weather has settled into a permanent arrangement: cold light, wet stone, cloud above the far rim, and a low ceiling of gray that never quite lowers or lifts. Rain may be falling in one part of the plateau and suspended as beads on wires in another, but the boundary between these conditions does not advance. A sheet of water spills over a cliff and is always just about to break apart at its base. In some places, drops hang beneath pipes without falling. They tremble faintly in time with the engines.

The light is ordinary, almost disappointingly so. It comes from a pale sky with no visible sun, reaches the ground evenly, and throws soft shadows beneath the towers. At certain hours—if hours can be distinguished—the stone takes on a blue cast and the green darkens toward black. There are no dramatic sunsets. The horizon simply dims, then returns to its former brightness, as if the light has completed a quiet circuit overhead.

Beyond the last buildings, the land is wider than it is empty. Distances seem to stretch without changing the view: wet plain, sparse green, a line of infrastructure, and then another enormous plain. The plateau’s scale cannot be comfortably measured. A person might travel for days along a service road and remain within sight of the same distant ridge. The sky has its own geography. Cloud bands lie above the land like additional ranges, and weather gathers at a scale that makes the city seem like a small mechanism left running on a table.

At the rim, the world drops away. The lower clouds press against the cliffs and conceal whatever lies beneath. Water passes over the edge in narrow, shining threads. Some disappear into fog before they have fallen far; others can be heard long after they are out of sight. The cliffs are fresh and unweathered, marked by the straight scars of uplift. Metal stairways cling to a few faces, descending to platforms that vanish into cloud. No one is seen on them.

The plateau has the quality of a place waiting to begin. Its land is raw; its green is taking hold; its industry is already complete. All around, the pumps keep the water moving, the lights remain on, and the immense empty intervals hold their silence beneath the machinery. Somewhere in the mist, a bell or an impact sounds at steady intervals, too regular to be an alarm. Nothing answers it. The sound travels out across the wet stone, passes through the vacant squares, and goes on until the plateau absorbs it.
