The place is a high, shallow hollow in the shoulder of a mountain, small enough to cross in an hour. Its rim is not a sharp ridge but an old, softened lip of dark stone, worn down by weather into ledges and shelves. Beyond it, the land falls away through veils of cloud. Inside, the ground rises and dips gently, as if the hollow had once been a single basin of water and had only slowly remembered how to be land.

Water lies close beneath everything. It wells through cracks in the stone, gathers in clear pools, and spills from one shallow depression to the next without making a stream. The pools are warm. Their surfaces barely tremble, though now and then a bubble appears far from shore and vanishes without a sound. In the morning, mist lifts only a little way above them, caught among the plants like a second, thinner kind of foliage.

Vegetation grows in separated islands. There are mats of small, thick-leaved plants, pale green at their edges and deep green where water runs beneath them. Tall stems rise in scattered clumps, each crowned with a fan of narrow leaves that hold beads of condensation. Between these growths lie broad stretches of bare stone, smooth and dark, with fine seams of mineral color: rust-red, yellow-white, a blue so faint it looks like shadow. The greenery is lush where it has taken hold, but it does not spread evenly. It leaves room around itself.

Nothing moves as an animal moves. There are no wings breaking the air, no bodies in the pools, no tracks in the soft margins. The plants open and close by such small degrees that their motion is usually noticed only by its result: a little more shade on the stone, a bead of water sliding down a leaf, a pale flower showing where yesterday there was only a folded bud. Some of the growths seem to lean toward the pools, but whether they follow light, warmth, or the faint pressure of water below the ground is hard to tell.

The light is ordinary and steady, though the place makes it feel filtered. Even at midday, the stone is not glaring. The water reflects the sky without reflecting the clouds clearly; each pool holds a washed-out version of the weather, as if looking upward through deep glass. Shadows are soft and slow to shift. At dusk, the hollow does not darken all at once. The bare stone keeps a little warmth, and the wet surfaces continue to shine long after the surrounding heights have gone blue.

Sound is scarce. Water threads under the stone with a low, nearly imagined hush. Now and then a droplet falls from a leaf into a pool, making a single clear note that seems too large for its source. There is no echo, or only the suggestion of one, swallowed by moss and mist. When wind reaches the rim, it passes over the hollow rather than through it.

The older stones show the place’s long patience. Their edges have been rounded, not broken; their hollows are polished smooth by water that must have traveled here for ages. The pools have no obvious shores, only gradual transitions from wet stone to dark soil to roots. Nothing looks newly formed. Even the oddest features—the narrow grooves that end in mid-rock, the shallow basins arranged like overlapping rings, the little ridges that curve against the slope—have the composure of things that have been there a long time.

There is no path and no sign of a hand at work. No walls, cuttings, or discarded tools interrupt the natural shapes. Yet the hollow feels bounded, almost kept. Water does not drain quickly from it. Mist settles here and lifts reluctantly. Plants grow in their chosen patches and scarcely encroach on the bare stone between them. The whole place has the quiet order of a room without a ceiling, though nothing in it has been made.

Over years, the pools deepen by the width of a finger. Over decades, a ledge softens. A new green patch appears, perhaps, and remains the same size for longer than anyone would expect. The hollow is not still; it is simply moving at a scale that asks to be mistaken for permanence.
