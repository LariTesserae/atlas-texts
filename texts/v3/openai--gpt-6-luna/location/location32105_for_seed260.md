At first the land looks like a mountain range seen through smoke: immense, pale, and unfinished. Its ridges rise too sharply from the plain, as though the ground has been lifted by a hand that has not yet decided where to set it down. Between them stretch broad distances of gray mineral crust, interrupted by shelves, spires, and long exposed seams that catch almost no light.

There is no visible water. Not in the gullies, not in the hollows, not in the dark grooves between the rocks. The basins are dry down to their oldest layers: chalk, powdered mica, salt that has never dissolved. Yet the place is lush.

Its vegetation grows in quiet extravagance wherever the stone allows a grip. Pale, jointed stalks emerge from cracks and climb the slopes in loose thickets. They are not green so much as green remembered: a muted color under the dim sky, threaded with silver veins. Some spread as soft, felted mats over ledges; others hang from overhangs in long tassels, stirring without wind. The plants have no flowers, or perhaps their flowers are too dim to distinguish from the leaves. They seem to feed on the mountain’s warmth, though the mountains are cold to the touch. Their roots disappear into the stone and return elsewhere, sometimes many yards away, passing through solid rock without breaking it.

The scale is open but not endless. From one high shelf, the next valley is visible, and beyond it another range, each one a little less finished than the last. Some peaks are clean-edged and severe. Others trail thin skirts of rubble that have not yet fallen. A cliff may end in a smooth vertical face, then continue several hundred feet farther down as a faint outline in the air, as if the world has drawn the idea of a cliff before committing to its weight.

Erosion is only beginning here. The land has scarcely learned how to weather. Rock flakes away in neat, dry curls, like bark, and the new surfaces beneath are lighter than the old ones. A landslide may remain poised on a slope for generations, every stone held just short of motion. Sometimes, without warning, a whole ravine shifts a few inches sideways. The plants accommodate this slowly, their stems bending toward the new angle. Nothing rushes to fill the space. The place is patient, still assembling itself.

The sky is low and dark, although there is no cloud cover. Light arrives thinly, without a clear source. It settles on the peaks in cold bands and leaves the valleys almost unlit. Shadows do not always agree with the objects that cast them. A narrow tower of rock may have a shadow that bends uphill; a stand of pale plants may cast none at all until someone looks away. At the edge of sight, distances occasionally fold together. A far ridge seems to press close enough to touch, then withdraws when approached.

The sound is enormous.

It is not wind, though everything that can tremble does. A continuous roar fills the landscape, low enough to be felt in the teeth and ribs, with a higher, glassy keening laid across it. It comes from the stone itself—or from whatever lies beneath the stone, where the world is still being made. The sound does not swell or fade. It is simply there, deafening and even, like a sea heard through several miles of earth. Small noises cannot survive in it. A footfall has no distinct report. A shout vanishes before it is spoken. Still, the plants’ hanging fronds quiver in precise rhythms, as if receiving a message within the roar.

There are no animals. No tracks, nests, bones, or startled movements in the brush. Nothing watches from the cliffs. The only living things are the plants, and even they give the impression of being less inhabitants than part of the terrain’s slow growth. Here and there, a patch of vegetation will lean toward a person standing nearby, not quickly and not with purpose, but over the span of a few hours, as a hillside might gradually settle.

Human marks are few. On a ridge stands a small structure of stacked black stones, low and roofless, built with hand tools—or with hands and stones alone. Its entrance faces the wrong direction: not toward the valley, but toward a blank wall of rock. Inside, a ring of white grit has been laid carefully on the floor. The grit remains undisturbed, though the structure has stood through enough cold seasons for its outer stones to soften at the edges. Elsewhere, a line of waist-high markers crosses a plateau and stops abruptly at an empty patch of ground. The markers continue on the far side, farther apart, their spacing impossible to measure consistently.

The rules here are not entirely stable. A stone dropped from a ledge may fall normally, or remain suspended a little below the lip, as if reconsidering. Sometimes the same mountain can be seen from two valleys at once, each version carrying different scars. The horizon is mostly reliable, but not always in the same place. Nothing announces these failures. They occur with the composure of ordinary weather.

And still, the landscape feels neither ruined nor hostile. It feels young in a way that has nothing to do with softness. These heights are ancient-looking, but they have not yet been worn into their final shapes. The silent-seeming plants are spreading. The dry gullies are waiting for some principle other than water to teach them how to flow. The mountains continue to rise by nearly imperceptible degrees, while the deafening sound of their making fills every hollow.

To cross the plateau is to walk through a place that has already become vast, and is not yet finished becoming real.
