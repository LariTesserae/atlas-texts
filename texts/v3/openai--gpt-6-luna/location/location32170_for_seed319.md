The city stands on a high shelf of dark stone, above the lower country and its moving weather. From the rim, the land drops away in long, worn steps: terraces of basalt and pale mineral soil, old ravines softened at their edges, distant ridges fading in the warm haze. The elevation gives the place a broad sky, but not a cool one. Air comes up from the valleys damp and mild, carrying the smell of wet rock and hot metal.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in great open reaches. It beads on the walls, threads through cracks in the paving, and gathers under bridges in black, shallow channels. The stone seems to sweat. After rain, which comes often but seldom dramatically, the whole city shines as if freshly quarried. Gutters are deep and well-used; stairways have narrow runnels cut down their centers; square drains sit at every crossing, their iron grates polished by generations of water. Beyond the built edge, the plateau breaks into marshy shelves and weeping cliffs. Here, thin pools occupy hollows in the rock, and the ground gives slightly underfoot—not mud so much as a sponge of mineral grit.

Vegetation has made only a sparse claim. A few tough reeds stand in the drainage basins. Low gray-green mats cling to ledges where water seeps steadily, and small trees have been trained into courtyards, their roots boxed in by masonry. On the outskirts, plants gather wherever a crack holds enough soil: bent grasses, thorned shrubs, pale moss. They do not soften the city so much as mark the places its builders have not yet covered. Animal life is just as tentative. Now and then a dark-winged bird crosses the high air, or a small, damp-colored creature slips between the retaining stones. Most days, the noise belongs to machines.

The settlement is old, but not ruinous. Its districts have accumulated in broad layers, each following the contours of the shelf: low, heavy foundations near the rim; blocks of apartments and workshops farther in; smokestacks, lifts, and waterworks rising above the roofline. The oldest buildings are built from the plateau itself, thick-walled and blunt, with rounded corners worn by weather. Later construction is more precise—riveted bridges, pipe racks, steel gantries, glazed inspection towers—though the damp has darkened everything to the same family of colors. Rust blooms in seams and under bolts. Copper channels turn green. Windows are narrow, often clouded with condensation.

The city’s great industry is the handling of water. It catches the rain, draws seepage from beneath the shelf, and pumps it through a network of reservoirs, filters, turbines, and elevated tanks. Some of the channels are open, so that water runs alongside streets behind waist-high parapets. Others plunge below the paving and can be heard but not seen. At intervals, pressure gates release with a deep mechanical shudder. The sound travels through the foundations and into the soles of the feet.

There is almost always a roar. Turbines thrum in enclosed halls; pumps hammer in cycles; freight lifts grind between levels; water drops through tall shafts where the rock has been cut away. Near the central works, speech becomes a movement of lips and hands. Bells and warning horns are used not because the streets are loud, but because the machinery can drown out the weather. Yet the noise is strangely steady. It has settled into the place like a second atmosphere, so constant that a sudden silence would seem like a disaster approaching.

The landscape changes slowly. The ravines deepen by fractions, the stone rounds under the same persistent seepage, and new districts grow only when an old retaining wall is judged reliable enough to bear them. There are maintenance crews everywhere, but they seem to be tending a condition rather than fighting decay. A cracked surface is patched; a pipe is replaced; a channel is cleared of mineral scale. The city accepts wear and incorporates it. Water has made its marks here for centuries, and the builders have learned to construct around those marks rather than erase them.

There is something slightly wrong in the way the water behaves, though not enough to stop anyone from working. In certain narrow channels, the current hesitates before a bend, gathering into a smooth, trembling sheet, then continues on. Moisture forms on the undersides of some bridges. In one district, rain can be heard falling on rooftops long after the sky has cleared, a soft tapping carried through the drainage pipes from somewhere deep beneath the plateau. People know which sounds indicate ordinary pressure and which mean a gate must be opened. They do not agree on where the excess water goes.

At dusk, the city remains bright enough to see clearly. Lamps come on under the bridges and in the wet tunnels; factory windows cast amber rectangles onto the paving. The plateau’s outer edge disappears into vapor, while the rooftops and towers stand above it in hard silhouette. From the lower land, the settlement must look less like a city than a band of machinery bolted to the mountain. From within, it feels spacious and sparsely occupied: long streets between dense blocks, empty platforms over the ravines, broad squares built to hold more people than usually arrive.

The place is loud, warm, and permanently damp, yet unhurried. It does not feel newly made or still becoming. It feels as though it has found a workable arrangement with the stone, the seepage, and the weight of its own machines—and expects that arrangement to last.
