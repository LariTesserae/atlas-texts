The basin lies so low that the horizon is mostly a line of water and dark, vapor-blurred ground. It is warm without being bright. A ceiling of cloud holds the light close to the earth, turning the day the color of wet ash; at night, the clouds keep even the stars from seeming far away.

Nothing here has had much time to wear smooth. The land is newly gathered: broad plates of black stone, pale mineral crusts, and expanses of gray mud still marked by the pressures that raised them. Their edges are sharp, their surfaces scarcely scored by streams. Water stands in shallow channels and seeps through cracks. It beads on walls, shines in footprints, and gathers under the skin of the ground. Rain comes often, but gently; the basin is already so full of water that it has little room left to receive it.

There are no trees, grasses, or familiar green things. In their place, low films of color cling to stone: rust-red, chalk-white, bruised violet. They are slimy, almost invisible in dim light, and may be living, or only the first simple work of life. Nothing rises from them. Nothing casts a leafy shadow.

Yet the basin is crowded with animals. Shallow pools flicker with small, quick bodies; shell-backed things rasp at the mineral film, and pale fish nose through channels too narrow to look like rivers. Larger, smooth-skinned animals bask on warm shelves of mud, packed close together where the water is deepest. They call continuously. Some bark, some click, some produce long, wavering cries that seem to pass from one side of the basin to the other. Their many voices combine with the slap of water against stone and the hollow booming of surf beneath the settlement. Silence is not a condition the place knows.

The town has been built low, in close ranks along the slightly higher ground. Its oldest walls are blocks of raw stone fitted together without mortar; newer ones are shaped from mud and shell, pressed by hand into heavy courses. Roofs are slabs, hides, or layered shells held down against the damp. Narrow covered passages connect rooms and courtyards, so that people can cross the town without wading through the channels. Drainage gutters run everywhere. They do not carry water away so much as persuade it to gather in the places already chosen for it.

There is no great machinery, no metal framework reaching above the roofs. Tools are stone, bone, fired clay, and shell. The town’s order comes from repeated labor: a wall raised here, a channel deepened there, a stair cut into a shelf of rock. Even its densest quarters have a handmade look, as if the settlement is still negotiating with the ground about where it may stand.

The basin feels new, but not restless. Its landforms have the unfinished bluntness of something recently made, yet they do not visibly shift. Pools keep their outlines. Walls do not sink. The same warm seepage follows the same seams in the rock, and the animals return each day to the same ledges. The place is emerging from the world’s raw materials, but emergence here is not a spectacle. It is a stable fact, old enough to have routines.

From the upper roofs, the town appears to continue beyond itself: more dark walls, more shallow water, more crowded colonies of animals, all fading into the dimness. The whole basin sounds alive, though it has scarcely begun to grow.
