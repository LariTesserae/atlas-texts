The land begins where the ground has been opened from below.

A shallow roof of dark soil and frost rests over a region of excavated chambers, old cuts and broad low galleries. In places the ceiling is only a few metres above the floor; elsewhere, the workings widen into long, dim hollows whose far walls disappear behind a grey haze of dust and breath. The rock is newly exposed: pale seams, sharp ledges, raw faces still shedding grit. Nothing here has had much time to soften. A trickle may draw a narrow stain down a wall, but most of the stone remains dry and cold to the touch.

Plants have taken the floors.

They form continuous, dark coverings over the spoil and benches: mosses, low sedges, creeping groundcover, and tough little shrubs rooted in pockets of old soil brought down with the roof. The growth is dense at ground level, almost a second skin on the land, but it rarely rises high. Long open stretches remain between the scattered clumps of taller stems. Here and there, roots descend through cracks from the world above, pale and exposed where the rock has been cut away. They branch over the walls like bundled cable, then vanish into the earth.

Light reaches the chambers indirectly. It seeps through narrow roof breaks, service shafts and the occasional grating, weak as late afternoon in winter. Dust floats in those shafts of grey. Beneath them, the plants are a deeper green, though the color is subdued everywhere by the cold. Most of the galleries lie in shadow. Work lamps hang above the routes still in use, their light flat and yellow, catching wet leaves, metal rails and the sharp edges of the excavation.

Water arrives in brief, local ways. Condensation gathers beneath pipes and drops steadily into shallow hollows. A leak will make a dark patch in the plant cover and a thin runnel through the grit; farther on, the ground is dry again. After rain above, the cracks in the roof bead and drip for a day or two. Small pools collect in abandoned wheel ruts, then slowly shrink. Their margins are rimmed with green, while the water itself reflects only ceiling, lamp, and stone.

The works are still running, though not everywhere. A conveyor crosses one gallery on heavy legs, carrying cut vegetation and soil toward a distant processing hall. Ventilation fans turn behind iron screens. Pumps thud at intervals, and somewhere beyond the visible chambers a crusher gives the rock a steady, blunt roar. The sound fills all the spaces. It reaches through walls and along the floor, so that even a quiet pocket beneath a collapsed beam seems to tremble with machinery. When one engine stops, the sudden absence is startling; the smaller sounds emerge—the tick of cooling metal, water falling into a pool, a stem rubbing softly against a post—before the next motor takes up the noise.

The built things are few and practical: a pump house, a control booth, rail lines, a scatter of cable drums, a rusted stair descending to a lower level. Most have been left where they failed or became unnecessary. Their surfaces are filmed with mineral dust and plant matter. A vine has entered an open junction box; moss has spread across the concrete footings of a compressor that no longer turns. Nothing is concealed or ceremonial here. The machines were placed to move air, water, rock and harvested growth, and the traces they leave are plain.

Animals are scarce. A few small insects gather near the dampest seams. Now and then a pale moth, descended through a shaft, rests on a lamp housing. The galleries are too cold and too loud for much else. The plants make the place feel inhabited, but not crowded: a continuous low green over a wide emptiness, with human works separated by long distances and machinery heard long before it is seen.

Change comes slowly. A fresh cut remains raw for years. Dampness advances only a little beyond a leak. The plants spread along the floor and up the abandoned rails, taking their time, while the pumps continue to lift water and the fans draw the same cold air through the passages. Above, the surface may be moving through seasons. Below, the buried landscape holds its dim light, its scattered green, and its immense, mechanical thunder.
